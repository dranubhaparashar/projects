Beyond the Gaussian

Student's t, von Mises, Gamma & Wishart

The Gaussian is everyone's first guess for the shape of data, and it quietly assumes three things: the tails are thin, the data live on a straight line, and someone has handed you the precision. Real data break all three. Sensors glitch, wind blows from a direction, and nobody tells you how noisy anything is. This chapter builds the distributions for those cases. In the figure, drag the red point away from the crowd: the Gaussian's mean (orange) chases it, while Student's t (teal) shrugs and stays with the data.

The idea

Where the bell curve breaks

Fitting a Gaussian by maximum likelihood means paying a penalty, the negative log-density, for every point. For the Gaussian that penalty is a parabola: a point twice as far from the mean costs four times as much, and a point ten units out costs a hundred times as much as one at a single unit. One wild reading can therefore outvote dozens of sensible ones. Student's t, which we build in a moment, charges a logarithmic penalty instead: far points still cost more, but only a little more. Distances below are in units of the scale, 1/√λ. Slide the distance out and compare what the two models charge, how hard each point pulls on the mean, and how surprised each model is.

\[ \begin{aligned} &-\ln \mathcal{N}\big(x\mid\mu,\lambda^{-1}\big) \\ &\quad = \tfrac{\lambda}{2}(x-\mu)^2 + \text{const} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: λ is the precision, one over the variance. The Gaussian's penalty grows with the square of the distance, so its pull on the mean (the slope of the penalty) grows in a straight line, without limit.
Say it“Minus log N of x is lambda over two times x minus mu squared, plus a constant.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &-\ln \mathrm{St}(x\mid\mu,\lambda,\nu) \\ &\quad = \tfrac{\nu+1}{2}\ln\!\Big(1+\tfrac{\lambda (x-\mu)^2}{\nu}\Big) + \text{const} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same squared distance, but passed through a logarithm. Close in, the two penalties agree; far out, the t's penalty barely rises, and its pull on the mean falls back towards zero. ν, the degrees of freedom, sets where the bend happens: small ν bends early.
Say it“Minus log St is nu plus one over two, times the log of one plus lambda x-minus-mu squared over nu.”

Thin tails are the first of three ways the Gaussian lets us down. The second is the shape of the space: an angle of 359° is next door to 1°, but on a straight line they are as far apart as they can be. The third is the precision itself. Bayesian inference needs a prior over every unknown, and a precision is a positive number (or, in several dimensions, a positive-definite matrix), so it needs a distribution that lives only there. The rest of the chapter takes these in turn: the Gamma for precisions, Student's t for tails, von Mises for circles, and the Wishart for precision matrices.

Priors over precision

Gamma: learning how noisy the data are

Chapter 0.9 met the Gamma as the conjugate prior for a Gaussian's precision. It is worth a second look, because it is the brick the rest of this chapter is built from. A Gamma lives on positive numbers, its shape a sets how peaked it is and its rate b sets where. Pair it with a Gaussian prior on the mean whose width scales with the precision and you get the Gaussian-Gamma (or Normal-Gamma) prior, which a Gaussian likelihood maps to another Gaussian-Gamma. The cloud below is that joint belief about the mean μ and the precision λ. Add data points and watch it close in on the star, the truth that generated them.

\[ \mathrm{Gam}(\lambda\mid a,b) = \frac{b^{a}}{\Gamma(a)}\,\lambda^{a-1}e^{-b\lambda} \]
Read it as: a density on λ > 0. Its mean is a/b, its variance a/b², and for a ≥ 1 its peak is at (a−1)/b. Large a with a/b fixed means “I am sure the precision is near a/b”. Γ(a) is the gamma function, (a−1)! for whole numbers.
Say it“Gam of lambda given a and b is b to the a over gamma of a, times lambda to the a minus one, times e to the minus b lambda.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mu,\lambda) &= \mathcal{N}\big(\mu\mid\mu_0,(\beta\lambda)^{-1}\big) \\ &\quad\times\mathrm{Gam}(\lambda\mid a,b) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: first draw a precision from the Gamma, then draw the mean from a Gaussian around μ0 whose precision is βλ. The two are tied: if the data are very noisy (small λ) we are also less sure of the mean. β counts how many “pretend observations” the prior mean is worth. Here μ0 = 0 and β = 1.
Say it“p of mu and lambda is a normal on mu with precision beta lambda, times a gamma on lambda.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_N &= \frac{\beta\mu_0+N\bar x}{\beta+N}, \qquad \beta_N = \beta+N \\ a_N &= a + \tfrac{N}{2} \\ b_N &= b + \tfrac12 \textstyle\sum_n (x_n-\bar x)^2 \\ &\quad + \dfrac{\beta N (\bar x-\mu_0)^2}{2(\beta+N)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the posterior after N points is the same family with updated numbers. The mean is a weighted average of the prior guess and the sample mean; a grows by one half per point; b collects the scatter of the data, plus a charge for any disagreement between the sample mean and the prior guess. The posterior mean of the precision is aN/bN. Integrate λ out and the belief about μ alone is a Student's t, which is where the next section begins.
Say it“mu-N is beta mu-zero plus N x-bar over beta plus N; a-N is a plus N over two; b-N is b plus half the sum of squared deviations plus a disagreement term.”

Heavy tails

Student's t: a crowd of Gaussians

Here is a recipe for outliers. For each data point, first roll a random precision scale η from a Gamma whose mean is 1, then draw the point from a Gaussian with precision ηλ. Most points get η near 1 and land where a Gaussian would put them. Now and then a point gets a tiny η, a very wide Gaussian, and lands far away. Average over every possible η and you get Student's t, named after the pen-name William Gosset used when he published it in 1908 from the Guinness brewery. Lower ν to make the precisions more varied, and raise the number of Gaussians to watch their average settle onto the exact t.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathrm{St}(x\mid\mu,\lambda,\nu) \\ &\quad = \int_0^\infty \mathcal{N}\big(x\mid\mu,(\eta\lambda)^{-1}\big) \\ &\qquad\quad\times\mathrm{Gam}\big(\eta\mid\tfrac{\nu}{2},\tfrac{\nu}{2}\big)\,d\eta \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the t is an infinite mixture of Gaussians that share a centre μ but differ in precision. The mixing weights come from a Gamma with shape and rate both ν/2, so η has mean 1 and variance 2/ν. Small ν: precisions vary wildly, tails are heavy. Large ν: every η is close to 1, and the t becomes the Gaussian.
Say it“St of x is the integral over eta of a normal with precision eta lambda, weighted by a gamma on eta with shape and rate nu over two.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathrm{St}(x\mid\mu,\lambda,\nu) \\ &\quad = \frac{\Gamma(\frac{\nu+1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{\nu}{2})}\Big(\frac{\lambda}{\pi\nu}\Big)^{1/2} \\ &\qquad\quad\times\Big[1+\frac{\lambda(x-\mu)^2}{\nu}\Big]^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: doing the integral gives a closed form. The bracket decays like a power of x rather than like e−x², which is what “heavy tails” means. With ν = 1 it is the Cauchy distribution, whose mean does not even exist; as ν → ∞ it becomes N(x | μ, λ−1).
Say it“A ratio of gamma functions, times root lambda over pi nu, times one plus lambda x-minus-mu squared over nu, to the minus nu-plus-one over two.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{St}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Lambda,\nu) &\propto \Big[1+\frac{\Delta^2}{\nu}\Big]^{-\frac{\nu+D}{2}} \\ \Delta^2 &= (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)^{\top}\boldsymbol\Lambda(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: in D dimensions, mix multivariate Gaussians N(x | μ, (ηΛ)−1) the same way. Δ is the Mahalanobis distance. The mean is μ (when ν > 1), the mode is μ, and the covariance is ν/(ν−2) Λ−1 (when ν > 2): wider than Λ−1 alone, because of the occasional wide component.
Say it“St of a vector is proportional to one plus delta squared over nu, to the minus nu-plus-D over two, where delta squared is the Mahalanobis distance.”

Robust fitting

Outliers: let the t decide what to ignore

The t has no closed-form maximum-likelihood fit, but the crowd-of-Gaussians picture gives an elegant one. Pretend we knew each point's hidden precision scale ηn: the fit would be a weighted Gaussian fit. We don't, so we alternate. The E-step works out each point's expected ηn from how far it sits from the current fit; the M-step refits the mean and covariance with those weights. A point far out gets a small weight, so it loses its say. This is the EM algorithm, which chapter 1.12a treats in general. Click to add outliers, then step the algorithm and watch the teal ellipse slide back onto the crowd while the Gaussian's stays dragged out.

\[ \begin{aligned} w_n &= \mathbb{E}[\eta_n] = \frac{\nu+D}{\nu+\Delta_n^2} \\ \boldsymbol\mu &= \frac{\sum_n w_n\mathbf{x}_n}{\sum_n w_n} \\ \boldsymbol\Sigma &= \tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_n w_n\,\mathbf{d}_n\mathbf{d}_n^{\top} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first line is the E-step, the other two the M-step. Δn is point n's Mahalanobis distance from the current fit, using Σ = Λ−1, and dn = xn − μ. A point at the centre gets weight (ν+D)/ν, a little more than 1; a point at distance Δ = 10 gets roughly (ν+D)/100. Each round can only raise the t likelihood, so the fit settles. A Gaussian fit is the special case where every weight is 1.
Say it“Each weight is nu plus D over nu plus the point's squared distance. The mean is the weighted average; Sigma is the weighted scatter divided by N.”

Periodic data

Angles: the average of 350° and 10°

Wind directions, the hour a patient arrives, the day of the year a flower opens: these are periodic variables, and the ordinary average gets them badly wrong. Average two winds from 350° and 10° and you get 180°, a wind from due south, when both came from just either side of north. Worse, the answer depends on where you cut the circle to turn it into a line. The cure is to treat each angle as a point on the unit circle, average the points, and read off the direction of the result. Its length, r̄, says how tightly the angles cluster: 1 if they all agree, near 0 if they are spread all round. Drag the points around the circle, and move the cut to see the ordinary average jump while the circular mean stays put.

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar{\mathbf{x}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta_n\\ \sin\theta_n\end{pmatrix}, \\ \bar\theta &= \operatorname{atan2}(\bar x_2,\ \bar x_1), \qquad \bar r = \lVert\bar{\mathbf{x}}\rVert \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: turn every angle into a unit vector, average the vectors, and take the angle and length of the average. Nothing depends on which direction we call zero. For compass bearings (measured clockwise from north), cos θ is the northward part and sin θ the eastward part; the formulas are unchanged. atan2 is the two-argument arctangent that returns the correct quadrant.
Say it“x-bar is the average of the unit vectors cos theta-n, sin theta-n. The mean direction is atan2 of its two parts; r-bar is its length.”

The circle's bell curve

The von Mises distribution

A density for an angle must be positive, integrate to one over a full turn, and come back to the same value after 360°. A neat way to build one: take a two-dimensional Gaussian with equal spread σ in every direction, centred at distance r0 from the origin in direction θ0, and look at it only on the unit circle. What you see along the circle is the von Mises distribution, which Richard von Mises introduced in 1918. Move the Gaussian with the sliders and watch the density on the circle, and its unrolled version below, sharpen as the Gaussian tightens or moves outward.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\theta) &\propto \exp\big\{-\lVert \mathbf{u}_\theta - r_0\mathbf{u}_{\theta_0}\rVert^2/2\sigma^2\big\} \\ &\propto \exp\big\{r_0\cos(\theta-\theta_0)/\sigma^2\big\} \\ &= \exp\{m\cos(\theta-\theta_0)\} \\ m &= r_0/\sigma^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: uθ = (cos θ, sin θ) is the point on the circle at angle θ. Expand the squared distance: it is 1 + r0² − 2r0 cos(θ − θ0). The 1 and the r0² do not depend on θ, so they fold into the normalising constant, and all that is left is a cosine. Only the ratio m = r0/σ², the concentration, matters.
Say it“p of theta is proportional to e to the m cos of theta minus theta-zero, with m equal to r-zero over sigma squared.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(\theta\mid\theta_0,m) &= \frac{\exp\{m\cos(\theta-\theta_0)\}}{2\pi I_0(m)} \\ I_0(m) &= \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}e^{m\cos\theta}\,d\theta \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: I0 is the zeroth-order modified Bessel function, which simply makes the density integrate to one. The mean and the mode are both θ0. The circular variance, 1 − I1(m)/I0(m), runs from 1 (m = 0, the uniform distribution) down to 0. For large m the peak is narrow and the density is almost exactly a Gaussian in θ with variance 1/m, because cos u ≈ 1 − u²/2 near the peak.
Say it“One over two pi I-zero of m, times e to the m cos theta minus theta-zero.”

Fitting a von Mises by maximum likelihood comes straight back to the circular mean. Differentiate the log-likelihood with respect to θ0 and the best direction is the circular mean θ̄; differentiate with respect to m and the best concentration is the one whose mean resultant length matches the data's r̄. There is no closed form for m, but the function A(m) only rises, so a few Newton steps find it.

\[ \begin{aligned} \theta_0^{\text{ML}} &= \bar\theta \\ A(m_{\text{ML}}) \equiv \frac{I_1(m_{\text{ML}})}{I_0(m_{\text{ML}})} &= \bar r \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: A(m) is the length of the mean vector that a von Mises with concentration m would produce on average; it rises from 0 (m = 0) towards 1. Invert it at the data's r̄ to get m. I1 is the first-order modified Bessel function, the derivative of I0.
Say it“The best theta-zero is the circular mean; the best m is the one where I-one over I-zero equals r-bar.”

One von Mises has a single peak. Data with several preferred directions (Boston's winds, in the project, come off the sea in the afternoon and off the land in winter) call for a mixture of von Mises, fitted by EM just like a mixture of Gaussians. Another route is to wrap a distribution on the real line around the circle, adding up its density at θ, θ ± 2π, θ ± 4π and so on; the wrapped Gaussian and the von Mises are almost indistinguishable in practice, but the von Mises has the simpler maths.

Priors over precision matrices

Wishart and Gaussian-Wishart

In D dimensions the precision is a matrix Λ, the inverse of the covariance. A prior over it must produce only symmetric, positive-definite matrices, which is to say, only valid ellipses. The Wishart distribution does exactly that, and it is the Gamma's grown-up sibling: in one dimension it is a Gamma. Paired with a Gaussian on the mean it gives the Gaussian-Wishart, the conjugate prior for a multivariate Gaussian with unknown mean and precision. Each ellipse below is one draw of (μ, Λ), drawn as the 1σ ellipse of the covariance Λ−1 centred on μ. Lower ν to see how wild an uncertain prior is, then switch to the posterior and add data.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathcal{W}(\boldsymbol\Lambda\mid\mathbf{W},\nu) \\ &\quad = B(\mathbf{W},\nu)\,\lvert\boldsymbol\Lambda\rvert^{(\nu-D-1)/2} \\ &\qquad\times\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{W}^{-1}\boldsymbol\Lambda)\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: W is a D×D scale matrix and ν > D − 1 the degrees of freedom. The mean is E[Λ] = νW, so W sets the shape and ν how sure we are: larger ν, draws closer to νW. B(W, ν) = |W|−ν/2 / (2νD/2 πD(D−1)/4 ∏i Γ((ν+1−i)/2)) is the normaliser. With D = 1 this is Gam(λ | ν/2, 1/(2W)).
Say it“W of Lambda is a normaliser times the determinant of Lambda to the nu minus D minus one over two, times e to the minus half trace of W-inverse Lambda.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Lambda) &= \mathcal{N}\big(\boldsymbol\mu\mid\mathbf{m}_0,(\beta_0\boldsymbol\Lambda)^{-1}\big) \\ &\quad\times\mathcal{W}(\boldsymbol\Lambda\mid\mathbf{W}_0,\nu_0) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: exactly the Gaussian-Gamma pattern in more dimensions: draw a precision matrix, then draw the mean from a Gaussian whose precision is β0 times it. Here m0 = 0, β0 = 1, and W0 = (ν0Ψ)−1, so that E[Λ] = Ψ−1 whatever ν0: Ψ is our guessed covariance, with the correlation you set.
Say it“A normal on mu with precision beta-zero Lambda, times a Wishart on Lambda.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{m}_N &= \frac{\beta_0\mathbf{m}_0 + N\bar{\mathbf{x}}}{\beta_0+N} \\ \beta_N &= \beta_0+N, \qquad \nu_N = \nu_0+N \\ \mathbf{W}_N^{-1} &= \mathbf{W}_0^{-1} + \mathbf{S} \\ &\quad + \tfrac{\beta_0 N}{\beta_0+N}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{m}_0)(\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{m}_0)^{\top} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: S = Σn (xn − x̄)(xn − x̄)⊤ is the data's scatter matrix. The degrees of freedom simply count the data; the inverse scale adds up the prior's scatter, the data's scatter and the disagreement between their means. The posterior's typical covariance is (E[Λ])−1 = WN−1/νN, which tends to S/N, the sample covariance, as N grows.
Say it“m-N is the weighted average of m-zero and x-bar; nu-N is nu-zero plus N; W-N inverse is W-zero inverse plus S plus a disagreement term.”

Choosing

Choosing a likelihood that matches the data

Picking a likelihood is a modelling decision, and two questions do most of the work. What values can the data take? Any real number, only positive ones, angles, counts, categories? And how do they misbehave? Occasional wild values, skew, several clumps? The table pairs common answers with a likelihood and, where one exists, its conjugate prior.

Then let the data vote. Each candidate below is fitted by maximum likelihood to the same sample, and we compare the log-likelihood per point (higher is better; the densities are all in the same units, so the numbers are comparable). Candidates whose support doesn't match the data are ruled out before any fitting: a Gamma can't explain a negative number. Pick a dataset, then shrink the sample to see how easily a small sample is fooled.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\ell &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ln p(x_n\mid\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}) \\ \text{AIC} &= -2N\hat\ell + 2k \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: θ̂ is each model's maximum-likelihood fit and ℓ̂ its average log-density on the data. A model with more parameters (k) can bend to fit better by luck, so AIC charges 2 per parameter; lower AIC wins. Chapter 1.2e meets a fully Bayesian version of this vote, the model evidence.
Say it“ell-hat is the average log-density at the fitted parameters; AIC is minus two N ell-hat plus two k.”

Real-world project

Project: a year of wind at Boston Logan

Airports report the weather every hour in a coded message called a METAR, and NOAA's Integrated Surface Database archives them for thousands of stations (the data are US-government work, in the public domain). We take every routine hourly report from Boston Logan International Airport (station 725090-14739) for 2023: 8,754 reports. Each gives the direction the wind blows from, to the nearest 10°, and its speed in whole knots, which we convert to metres per second. Calm and “variable” hours have no direction, leaving 8,323 directions. Everything below is fitted live in your browser.

Where the wind comes from: von Mises and its mixtures

The rose shows how often the wind came from each 10° sector. A single von Mises says “the wind has one favourite direction”. Boston disagrees: there is a broad north-westerly in winter, a narrow south-westerly and a sea breeze off the Atlantic from the east in summer. Switch seasons and add components; the log-likelihood gain is measured against a uniform direction, which knows nothing.

Gusty changes: Gaussian or Student's t?

The database records a gust only when one is notable (1,147 of the hours), so we study something every hour has: the change in wind speed from one hour to the next, for the 8,733 pairs of reports about an hour apart. Most hours the speed barely moves; occasionally a front or a squall swings it by 8 m/s or more. A Gaussian fitted to these changes has to stretch to cover the jumps and still gets their frequency wrong. The t is fitted by the EM steps of the robust-fitting section, with ν chosen by maximum likelihood too.

The wind as a vector: a Gaussian-Wishart posterior

Finally, treat each hour's wind as an arrow: how fast it moves the air east and north (calm hours are the zero arrow). A Gaussian-Wishart prior (m0 = 0, β0 = 1, ν0 = 3, W0 = I/75, so the prior's typical covariance is 25 I, a 5 m/s spread) is updated with N hours spread evenly through the season. The same project in Python

import json, numpy as np
from scipy import stats, optimize, special

D = json.load(open("x010-data.json"))          # NOAA ISD, Boston Logan, 2023, hourly METARs
mon = np.array([int(c, 16) for c in D["mon"]])
d10 = np.array([-1 if c == "." else int(c, 36) for c in D["dir"]])
spd = np.array(D["spd"]) / 10                   # m/s, -1 = missing
gap = np.array([int(c) for c in D["gap"]])      # 1 = previous report was ~1 h earlier
SEASONS = {"all year": np.arange(1, 13), "winter (DJF)": [12, 1, 2], "summer (JJA)": [6, 7, 8]}

# ---- A. wind direction: von Mises, then mixtures of von Mises by EM ----
def A_inv(R):                                    # solve I1(k)/I0(k) = R for k
    return optimize.brentq(lambda k: special.i1e(k) / special.i0e(k) - R, 1e-9, 1e4, xtol=1e-14)
def vm_logpdf(th, mu, k):
    return k * np.cos(th - mu) - np.log(2 * np.pi * special.i0e(k)) - k
def vm_mix(th, K, iters=3000):
    C, S_ = np.cos(th).mean(), np.sin(th).mean()
    mu = np.arctan2(S_, C) + 2 * np.pi * np.arange(K) / K
    kap, pi = np.ones(K), np.ones(K) / K
    old = -np.inf
    for it in range(iters):
        L = np.log(pi) + np.stack([vm_logpdf(th, mu[k], kap[k]) for k in range(K)], 1)
        m = L.max(1, keepdims=True); ll = (m[:, 0] + np.log(np.exp(L - m).sum(1))).sum()
        r = np.exp(L - m); r /= r.sum(1, keepdims=True)
        n = r.sum(0); pi = n / len(th)
        c, s = (r * np.cos(th)[:, None]).sum(0), (r * np.sin(th)[:, None]).sum(0)
        mu = np.arctan2(s, c); kap = np.array([A_inv(np.hypot(c[k], s[k]) / n[k]) for k in range(K)])
        if ll - old < 1e-10: break
        old = ll
    return pi, mu, kap, ll, it
compass = lambda a: np.degrees(a) % 360
print("A. Wind direction (radians; compass bearings, 10-degree steps)")
for name, ms in SEASONS.items():
    th = np.radians(10 * d10[(d10 >= 0) & np.isin(mon, ms)])
    kap, loc, _ = stats.vonmises.fit(th, fscale=1)
    ll1 = stats.vonmises.logpdf(th, kap, loc).sum()
    print(f"  {name:13s} n = {len(th)}  R-bar = {np.hypot(np.cos(th).mean(), np.sin(th).mean()):.4f}"
          f"  von Mises: mean = {compass(loc):.2f} deg, kappa = {kap:.4f}, log-lik = {ll1:.2f}"
          f"  (uniform {-len(th) * np.log(2 * np.pi):.2f})")
    for K in (2, 3):
        pi, mu, kp, ll, it = vm_mix(th, K)
        o = np.argsort(-pi)
        comp = ", ".join(f"{pi[k]:.3f}@{compass(mu[k]):.1f}/k={kp[k]:.3f}" for k in o)
        print(f"      mixture K={K}: log-lik = {ll:.2f}  [{comp}]")

# ---- B. hour-to-hour change in wind speed: Gaussian versus Student's t ----
ok = (gap[1:] == 1) & (spd[1:] >= 0) & (spd[:-1] >= 0)
x = (spd[1:] - spd[:-1])[ok]
mu, sd = x.mean(), x.std()
llg = stats.norm.logpdf(x, mu, sd).sum()
nu0, loc0, sc0 = stats.t.fit(x)
nll = lambda p: -stats.t.logpdf(x, np.exp(p[2]), p[0], np.exp(p[1])).sum()
res = optimize.minimize(nll, [loc0, np.log(sc0), np.log(nu0)], method="Nelder-Mead",
                        options={"xatol": 1e-10, "fatol": 1e-10, "maxiter": 20000})
loc, sc, nu = res.x[0], np.exp(res.x[1]), np.exp(res.x[2])
print(f"\nB. Hour-to-hour speed change, n = {len(x)} pairs (m/s)")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • The Gaussian's penalty is quadratic, so one far point can drag its fit anywhere; Student's t charges a logarithmic penalty and its pull on the mean fades for far points.
  • Student's t is an infinite mixture of Gaussians whose precisions are drawn from Gam(η | ν/2, ν/2): ν = 1 is the Cauchy, ν → ∞ the Gaussian. EM fits it by re-weighting points by (ν+D)/(ν+Δ²).
  • Angles live on a circle: average them as unit vectors. The von Mises, ∝ exp{m cos(θ−θ0)}, is a 2-D Gaussian seen on the unit circle; its ML fit is the circular mean and A(m) = r̄.
  • The Gamma is the prior for a precision and the Wishart for a precision matrix (a Gamma when D = 1); with a Gaussian on the mean they give the conjugate Gaussian-Gamma and Gaussian-Wishart priors.
  • Choose the likelihood by the values the data can take and how they misbehave, then let the data vote: Boston's wind changes favour the t by 224 nats, and its directions need a mixture of von Mises.

Check your understanding

Why does a single outlier move a Gaussian's mean so much more than a t's?

The Gaussian's maximum-likelihood mean is the plain average, so a point d units away shifts it by d/N however large d is. In the t fit each point is weighted by (ν+1)/(ν+λ(x−μ)²), and that weight shrinks like 1/d², so the point's pull, weight times distance, falls like 1/d.

The precision scale η in the t's mixture is drawn from Gam(ν/2, ν/2). What are its mean and variance, and what happens as ν grows?

Mean (ν/2)/(ν/2) = 1 and variance (ν/2)/(ν/2)² = 2/ν. As ν grows the variance goes to zero, every component has precision λ, and the mixture collapses to a single Gaussian.

Winds come from 340°, 350°, 10° and 20°. What are the ordinary average and the circular mean?

The ordinary average is 180°, due south, which is nonsense. The angles sit symmetrically either side of north, so the sines cancel and the cosines are all positive: the circular mean is 0°, with r̄ = (cos 20° + cos 10°)/2 ≈ 0.96.

Where does the von Mises concentration m come from in the 2-D Gaussian picture, and what does m = 0 mean?

m = r0/σ²: the Gaussian's distance from the origin over its variance. m = 0 means the Gaussian is centred on the origin, so every point of the circle is equally likely: the uniform distribution on angles.

Why does a precision matrix need a Wishart prior rather than independent Gamma priors on its entries?

The entries are not free: the matrix must be symmetric and positive-definite (a valid ellipse), and off-diagonal entries can be negative. Independent priors would often produce impossible matrices. The Wishart puts all its mass on valid matrices and is conjugate to the Gaussian, so the update is a few additions.
