Bayesian Linear Regression

Error bars that know where the data are

Least squares hands you one set of weights and one curve, and it is equally sure of that curve everywhere. The Bayesian alternative treats the weights as unknown, puts a Gaussian belief over them, and lets every data point sharpen that belief. Predictions then come with honest error bars: narrow where you have data, wide where you don't. On the right, click anywhere to add a point and watch the band pinch around it.

The idea

Curve fitting, the Bayesian way

Go back to the polynomial curve fit of chapter 1.2: noisy points drawn from a sine wave, and a polynomial y(x, w) = w0 + w1x + … + wMxM. Maximum likelihood picks one weight vector wML, estimates the noise level from the leftover scatter, and predicts every new target with the same error bar, whether x sits among the data or far from them. Adding a penalty on the weights (ridge) tames the wiggles but still gives one curve.

A Bayesian keeps every weight vector in play, each weighted by how plausible it is after seeing the data, and predicts by averaging over all of them. This averaging, called marginalising over w, is the step that maximum likelihood and MAP skip. For a Gaussian prior and Gaussian noise the average can be done exactly, and it is again a Gaussian, whose mean and variance both depend on x. Bishop's classic setting is a 9th-order polynomial with α = 5×10−3 and β = 11.1 (noise sd 0.3). Add points one by one, then hide the right-hand side of the data: maximum likelihood shrugs, while the Bayesian band balloons where there is nothing to learn from.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(t \mid x, \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{t}) &= \int p(t \mid x, \mathbf{w}) \\ &\qquad \times p(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{t})\, d\mathbf{w} \\ &= \mathcal{N}\big(t \mid m(x),\ s^2(x)\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: to predict the target t at a new input x, ask every possible weight vector for its prediction and average them, each weighted by its posterior probability given the training inputs x and targets t. With Gaussians throughout, the result is a Gaussian with a mean m(x) and a variance s²(x) that both change with x.
Say it“The predictive of t given x and the data is the integral of the model's prediction times the posterior over w; it is a Gaussian with mean m of x and variance s squared of x.”
\[ \begin{aligned} m(x) &= \beta\, \boldsymbol\phi(x)^{\top} \mathbf{S} \textstyle\sum_{n} \boldsymbol\phi(x_n)\, t_n \\ s^2(x) &= \beta^{-1} + \boldsymbol\phi(x)^{\top} \mathbf{S}\, \boldsymbol\phi(x) \\ \mathbf{S}^{-1} &= \alpha \mathbf{I} + \beta \textstyle\sum_{n} \boldsymbol\phi(x_n)\, \boldsymbol\phi(x_n)^{\top} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: φ(x) = (1, x, x², …, xM) lists the polynomial's features at x. S is the covariance of the weights after seeing the data: the prior precision α plus β times the data's own “spread” matrix, inverted. The variance has two parts: the noise 1/β that no amount of data removes, and the weights' remaining uncertainty seen through φ(x), which is large wherever the data say little.
Say it“m of x is beta, phi-x transpose, S, times the sum of phi-x-n t-n. s squared is one over beta plus phi-x transpose S phi-x. S inverse is alpha I plus beta times the sum of phi phi-transpose.”

Before any data

A Gaussian prior over the weights

To see the machinery clearly, shrink the model to a straight line, y(x, w) = w0 + w1x, so that each weight vector is a single point in a flat weight space and each point there is a whole line in data space. The model has two ingredients. The likelihood says how targets scatter around the line: Gaussian noise with precision β (precision means one over the variance). The prior says which lines we find plausible before seeing anything: a round Gaussian centred on w = 0, with precision α. Large α means “I'm sure the weights are small”; small α means “almost anything goes”.

Every weight vector drawn from the prior is a line. Turn α and draw again; click in the weight-space panel to pick your own w and see which line it is.

\[ p(\mathbf{w} \mid \alpha) = \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{0},\ \alpha^{-1}\mathbf{I}\big) \]
Read it as: each weight is an independent bell curve around 0 with variance 1/α, so its standard deviation is 1/√α. The contours are circles in weight space. Bishop's straight-line example uses α = 2.
Say it“The prior on w given alpha is a Gaussian with mean zero and covariance alpha-inverse times the identity.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &p(\mathbf{t} \mid \mathbf{w}, \beta) \\ &\quad = \textstyle\prod_{n} \mathcal{N}\big(t_n \mid \mathbf{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol\phi(x_n),\ \beta^{-1}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: given the weights, each target is the line's value at xn plus independent Gaussian noise of variance 1/β. Here φ(x) = (1, x), but nothing changes if φ holds polynomials, bumps or any other fixed basis functions: the model stays linear in w.
Say it“The likelihood of the targets is the product over points of a Gaussian in t-n, centred on w-transpose phi of x-n, with variance one over beta.”

After the data

The posterior: prior times likelihood

Bayes' rule multiplies the prior by the likelihood. Both are exponentials of quadratics in w, so their product is too, and the posterior is Gaussian again: the Gaussian prior is conjugate to this likelihood. Finding its mean and covariance is a matter of completing the square, and the result has a memorable shape: precisions add. The prior contributes αI; each data point contributes βφ(xn)φ(xn)⊤. The mean is a compromise between the prior mean and the least-squares answer, weighted by those precisions.

Three special cases are worth knowing. With no data the posterior is the prior. With a very broad prior (α → 0) the posterior mean becomes the least-squares solution wML. And because a Gaussian's peak is its mean, the most probable weights, wMAP, are simply mN. Drag the points and watch the three ellipses in weight space: the grey prior, the orange likelihood, and the blue posterior that sits where they agree.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{t}) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{m}_N, \mathbf{S}_N) \\ \mathbf{m}_N &= \mathbf{S}_N\big(\mathbf{S}_0^{-1}\mathbf{m}_0 + \beta\,\boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\mathbf{t}\big) \\ \mathbf{S}_N^{-1} &= \mathbf{S}_0^{-1} + \beta\,\boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\boldsymbol\Phi \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for any Gaussian prior N(m0, S0). Φ is the design matrix, one row φ(xn)⊤ per point. The posterior precision SN−1 is the prior precision plus the data's precision. The mean blends the prior mean and the data, each multiplied by its precision, then divides by the total precision.
Say it“The posterior is Gaussian with mean m-N equals S-N times S-nought-inverse m-nought plus beta Phi-transpose t, and precision S-N-inverse equals S-nought-inverse plus beta Phi-transpose Phi.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{m}_N &= \beta\, \mathbf{S}_N \boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\mathbf{t} \\ \mathbf{S}_N^{-1} &= \alpha \mathbf{I} + \beta\, \boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\boldsymbol\Phi \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same with our zero-mean, round prior N(0, α−1I). As α → 0, mN → (Φ⊤Φ)−1Φ⊤t, the least-squares weights. The posterior's tilt (the correlation between w0 and w1) comes entirely from Φ⊤Φ, that is, from where the inputs sit.
Say it“m-N is beta S-N Phi-transpose t; S-N inverse is alpha I plus beta Phi-transpose Phi.”

Online learning

Learning one point at a time

Because precisions simply add, the order in which you see the data does not matter, and you never need to keep the data: after each point, today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior. This is Bishop's famous figure, played live. The hidden truth is the line y = −0.3 + 0.5x, observed with noise sd 0.2 (β = 25), and the prior has α = 2.

The left panel is the likelihood of the newest point alone. One point cannot pin down two weights: every line that passes near it is about equally good, so its likelihood is a long ridge in weight space. Multiply that ridge into the current belief and the posterior (middle) shrinks across the ridge only. The right panel shows six lines drawn from the posterior. Press “Add a point” a few times, or click the right panel to add a point of your own.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{S}_n^{-1} &= \mathbf{S}_{n-1}^{-1} + \beta\, \boldsymbol\phi(x_n)\boldsymbol\phi(x_n)^{\top} \\ \mathbf{m}_n &= \mathbf{S}_n\big(\mathbf{S}_{n-1}^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{n-1} + \beta\, \boldsymbol\phi(x_n)\, t_n\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the general posterior formula with the previous posterior N(mn−1, Sn−1) playing the prior and a single new point (xn, tn) as the data. Each point adds precision only along its own direction φ(xn) = (1, xn): a rank-one update. After all N points you get exactly the same posterior as processing them in one batch.
Say it“The new precision is the old precision plus beta phi phi-transpose; the new mean is the new covariance times the old precision times the old mean, plus beta phi t.”

A familiar face

Ridge regression is the MAP estimate

Take the logarithm of the posterior and something familiar appears. The log-likelihood is minus a sum of squared errors; the log of the Gaussian prior is minus a squared length. Maximising the posterior is therefore the same as minimising squared error plus a weight penalty: ridge regression, with penalty λ = α/β. So the “regulariser” of chapter 1.2 was a prior all along, and λ is the ratio of how sure you are that weights are small to how sure you are about each data point.

Other priors give other penalties. A Laplace prior, with density falling like e−|w|, gives the lasso, whose diamond-shaped contours have corners on the axes, so the most probable weights are often exactly zero. But notice what MAP throws away. Under the Laplace prior the posterior mean is not sparse at all; only its peak is. Switch between the two priors and slide λ. The plot shows weight space for a model with two correlated features and no intercept.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln p(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{t}) &= -\tfrac{\beta}{2}\textstyle\sum_{n} e_n^2 \\ &\quad - \tfrac{\alpha}{2}\,\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{w} + \text{const} \\ e_n &= t_n - \mathbf{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol\phi(x_n) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: en is the model's error on point n. The log posterior is the log-likelihood (a scaled sum of squared errors) plus the log prior (a scaled squared length), up to a constant that does not depend on w. Divide by −β and maximising it becomes minimising ½Σ(error)² + ½λ‖w‖².
Say it“The log posterior is minus beta over two times the sum of squared errors, minus alpha over two times w-transpose w, plus a constant.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{w}_{\text{MAP}} &= \big(\lambda\mathbf{I} + \boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\boldsymbol\Phi\big)^{-1}\boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\mathbf{t} \\ &= \mathbf{m}_N \\ p(\mathbf{w} \mid \alpha) &\propto \exp\Big(-\tfrac{\alpha}{2}\textstyle\sum_{j}|w_j|^{q}\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the ridge solution with λ = α/β is exactly the posterior mean mN. The second line is a family of priors: q = 2 is the Gaussian (ridge), q = 1 the Laplace (lasso). With q = 1 the MAP minimises ½Σ(error)² + ½λΣ|wj|.
Say it“w-MAP is lambda I plus Phi-transpose Phi, inverted, times Phi-transpose t, which equals m-N. The general prior is proportional to e to the minus alpha over two times the sum of the absolute weights to the power q.”

The payoff

The predictive distribution and its error bars

We rarely care about the weights for their own sake; we want to predict t at a new x. Averaging the model's prediction over the posterior gives the predictive distribution. Its mean is the posterior-mean curve, and its variance has two parts that are worth keeping apart: the noise 1/β, which would remain even if we knew w exactly, and the uncertainty about w, which shrinks as data arrive. Adding a point can only lower the variance everywhere (σ2N+1(x) ≤ σ2N(x)), and with unlimited data the second part vanishes.

Russell and Norvig make the point with a plain straight line: its uncertainty grows with the squared distance from the middle of the data, so the error bars flare out like a trumpet. Bishop uses nine Gaussian bumps. Here the bumps hide a trap: far from every bump, φ(x) ≈ 0, the weight term disappears, and the band collapses back to the noise level, most confident exactly where it has seen nothing. Switch models, add points, and move the probe outside the data.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(t \mid x, \mathbf{t}) &= \mathcal{N}\big(t \mid \mathbf{m}_N^{\top}\boldsymbol\phi(x),\ \sigma_N^2(x)\big) \\ \sigma_N^2(x) &= \underbrace{\frac{1}{\beta}}_{\text{noise}} + \underbrace{\boldsymbol\phi(x)^{\top}\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol\phi(x)}_{\text{weight uncertainty}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the prediction at x is Gaussian, centred on the posterior-mean curve. Its variance is the noise variance plus the variance of the curve's height at x, which is φ(x)⊤SNφ(x) because the height w⊤φ(x) is a weighted sum of Gaussian weights.
Say it“The predictive is a Gaussian in t, with mean m-N-transpose phi of x and variance sigma-N squared of x, which is one over beta plus phi-transpose S-N phi.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{var}\big[y(x)\big] &\approx \frac{1}{\beta}\Big(\frac{1}{N} + \frac{(x-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}\Big) \\ S_{xx} &= \textstyle\sum_n (x_n-\bar x)^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for a straight line with a nearly flat prior, the line's height at x is known best at the mean input x̄ (to within the noise over N) and gets worse with the squared distance from it, relative to how spread out the inputs are. It is exact for a flat prior (α → 0); the lab's α = 2 pulls it in a little, as the running example shows.
Say it“The variance of the line at x is about one over beta times one over N plus x minus x-bar squared, over the sum of squared input deviations.”

Another view

The equivalent kernel

Substitute mN = βSNΦ⊤t into the prediction and regroup. The prediction at x turns out to be a weighted sum of the training targets, y(x) = Σn k(x, xn) tn. The weights k(x, xn) form the equivalent kernel, sometimes called the smoother matrix. They depend only on the inputs, never on the targets, and they are largest for training points near x, even when the basis functions themselves are not local at all: polynomials and sigmoids give local kernels too.

This flips the point of view. Instead of choosing basis functions and solving for weights, we could choose a kernel and predict directly from the data: that is the idea behind Gaussian processes, and the kernel is also the covariance between the curve's values at two inputs. Pick a basis, move x, and turn α; the stems below show the weight each of the 41 training points gets.

\[ \begin{aligned} y(x, \mathbf{m}_N) &= \sum_{n=1}^{N} k(x, x_n)\, t_n \\ k(x, x') &= \beta\, \boldsymbol\phi(x)^{\top}\mathbf{S}_N\, \boldsymbol\phi(x') \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the posterior-mean prediction is a linear smoother: it mixes the observed targets with weights k(x, xn). The kernel is built from the basis and from SN, so it depends on the training inputs xn but not on their targets.
Say it“y at x is the sum over n of k of x and x-n times t-n, where k of x and x-prime is beta phi-x transpose S-N phi-x-prime.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{cov}\big[y(x), y(x')\big] &= \boldsymbol\phi(x)^{\top}\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol\phi(x') \\ &= \beta^{-1}k(x, x') \\ \textstyle\sum_{n} k(x, x_n) &\approx 1 \\ k(x, z) &= \boldsymbol\psi(x)^{\top}\boldsymbol\psi(z) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: nearby predictions move together, by exactly the kernel's amount. The weights sum to about 1 (exactly 1 when the basis contains a constant and α → 0), so the prediction is close to a weighted average of nearby targets. And k is an inner product of the new features ψ(x) = β½SN½φ(x), which is what makes it a valid kernel.
Say it“The covariance of y at x and y at x-prime is phi-x transpose S-N phi-x-prime, which is k over beta. The kernel weights sum to about one, and k of x and z is psi-x dot psi-z.”

Honest error bars

When the noise level is unknown too

Everything so far assumed we knew β. Guess it wrong and the error bars are wrong in proportion, however good the curve. The Bayesian fix is to treat β as unknown as well. The conjugate prior for the pair (w, β) is the Normal-Gamma: a Gamma prior on the noise precision, and a Gaussian on w whose spread is measured in units of the noise. The posterior keeps the same form; the Gamma's parameters simply add half a count per data point and half the leftover squared error. Integrating out both w and β turns the Gaussian predictive into a Student-t, whose heavier tails admit that the noise level itself is uncertain, and which becomes Gaussian again once N is large.

Both models below share the same curve (ridge with λ = α/β = 0.08); only the bands differ. Each band is checked against 2,000 fresh points from the truth. Set the noise you assume far from the true noise, then change N.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{w} \mid \beta &\sim \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{0}, (\beta\lambda)^{-1}\mathbf{I}\big) \\ \beta &\sim \operatorname{Gam}(a_0, b_0) \\ a_N &= a_0 + \tfrac{N}{2} \\ b_N &= b_0 + \tfrac12\big(\mathbf{t}^{\top}\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{m}_N^{\top}\mathbf{S}_N^{-1}\mathbf{m}_N\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: here SN−1 = λI + Φ⊤Φ and mN = SNΦ⊤t, ridge once more. The noise precision's Gamma posterior gains half a count per point (aN) and half the penalised residual sum of squares (bN), so bN/aN is an estimate of the noise variance. We use a vague a0 = b0 = 0.01.
Say it“Given beta, w is Gaussian with covariance beta-lambda inverse; beta itself is Gamma with a-nought and b-nought. a-N is a-nought plus N over two; b-N is b-nought plus half of t-transpose-t minus m-N-transpose S-N-inverse m-N.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(t \mid x, \mathbf{t}) &= \operatorname{St}\big(t \mid \mu(x), s^2(x), \nu\big) \\ \nu &= 2a_N \\ \mu(x) &= \mathbf{m}_N^{\top}\boldsymbol\phi(x) \\ s^2(x) &= \tfrac{b_N}{a_N}\big(1 + \boldsymbol\phi(x)^{\top}\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol\phi(x)\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a Student-t with the familiar centre μ(x), a squared scale s²(x) equal to the estimated noise variance bN/aN times (1 + the weight uncertainty), and ν = 2aN ≈ N degrees of freedom. With few points the t's quantiles are far out (for 3 degrees of freedom the 95% point is 3.18 scales, not 1.96).
Say it“The predictive is a Student-t in t, centred on m-N-transpose phi, with squared scale b-N over a-N times one plus phi-transpose S-N phi, and two a-N degrees of freedom.”

Real-world project

Project: forecasting Mauna Loa CO₂

Since March 1958 the observatory on Mauna Loa, Hawaiʻi, has measured the carbon dioxide in the air every day, first by Charles David Keeling of Scripps and now by NOAA's Global Monitoring Laboratory. Its monthly means, of them through , form the Keeling curve: a steady, accelerating rise with a yearly breath as the northern forests grow and shed their leaves. NOAA publishes the data freely.

We fit a Bayesian basis-function regression: a straight-line start, a hinge max(0, t − κk) at every knot κk (every 5 years from 1960 to 2040) so the growth rate can bend, and two harmonics for the seasons. Each hinge weight vk has a Gaussian prior with sd sv: how much, in ppm per year, we believe the growth rate can change at a knot. The trick that makes the bands widen honestly: knots after the training cut-off have no data at all, so their weights keep their prior uncertainty, and the forecast fans out. The noise sd σ is set by refitting until it equals the root-mean-square training residual (the next chapter shows the principled way). Move the cut-off and watch the forecast meet the months it never saw.

\[ \begin{aligned} y(t) = {}& w_0 + w_1(t - 1958) \\ &+ \textstyle\sum_{k} v_k \max(0,\ t - \kappa_k) \\ &+ \textstyle\sum_{h=1}^{2} a_h \sin 2\pi h t \\ &+ \textstyle\sum_{h=1}^{2} b_h \cos 2\pi h t \\ v_k &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, s_v^2) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: t is the date in years. The level starts at w0 with slope w1 ppm per year; at each knot κk the slope changes by vk. The sines and cosines with periods of 12 and 6 months draw the seasonal cycle. The model is still linear in its weights, so every formula on this page applies, with a diagonal prior (very broad for w0, w1, ah, bh; sd sv for each vk).
Say it“y of t is w-nought plus w-one times t minus 1958, plus the sum of v-k times the hinge at kappa-k, plus two harmonics; each v-k is Gaussian with sd s-v.”

Backtest: five cut-offs

One cut-off can be lucky. Here the current settings are refitted at five cut-offs and scored on the following 10 years (6.75 years for 2020, as the data end in September 2026).

The same project in Python

import numpy as np, json
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge

J = json.load(open("co2_mm_mlo.json"))                 # NOAA GML monthly means, Mauna Loa
y = np.array(J["co2"]); k = np.arange(len(y))
t = J["y0"] + (J["m0"] - 1 + k + 0.5) / 12             # mid-month, in years

def basis(t, gap=5):
    knots = np.arange(1960, 2041, gap)                 # a hinge every `gap` years, 1960..2040
    cols = [np.ones_like(t), t - 1958] + [np.maximum(0, t - c) for c in knots]
    cols += [f(2 * np.pi * h * t) for h in (1, 2) for f in (np.sin, np.cos)]
    return np.column_stack(cols), len(knots)

def fit(cut, gap=5, s_v=0.2):
    tr = t < cut
    P, K = basis(t[tr], gap)
    prior_var = np.array([1000.0**2, 10.0**2] + [s_v**2] * K + [10.0**2] * 4)
    s2 = 1.0
    for _ in range(100):                               # noise: refit until sigma^2 = mean residual^2
        S_inv = np.diag(1 / prior_var) + P.T @ P / s2
        m = np.linalg.solve(S_inv, P.T @ y[tr] / s2)
        new = np.mean((y[tr] - P @ m) ** 2)
        if abs(new - s2) < 1e-12: s2 = new; break
        s2 = new
    S_inv = np.diag(1 / prior_var) + P.T @ P / s2
    S = np.linalg.inv(S_inv); m = S @ P.T @ y[tr] / s2
    return dict(m=m, S=S, s2=s2, prior_var=prior_var, P=P, tr=tr, gap=gap)

def predict(F, tq):                                    # predictive mean and sd
    Q, _ = basis(tq, F["gap"])
    return Q @ F["m"], np.sqrt(F["s2"] + np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", Q, F["S"], Q))

F = fit(2010.0); te = t >= 2010.0
mu, sd = predict(F, t[te]); err = y[te] - mu
print(f"noise sigma = {np.sqrt(F['s2']):.4f}; held out {te.sum()} months: "
      f"RMSE {np.sqrt(np.mean(err**2)):.3f}, inside 95% band {np.mean(np.abs(err) < 1.96*sd):.3f}")
for cut in (1980, 1990, 2000, 2010, 2020):             # backtest, next 10 years
    G = fit(float(cut)); h = (t >= cut) & (t < cut + 10); mu, sd = predict(G, t[h]); e = y[h] - mu
    print(f"{cut}: sigma {np.sqrt(G['s2']):.3f}  RMSE {np.sqrt(np.mean(e**2)):.3f}  "
          f"coverage {np.mean(np.abs(e) < 1.96*sd):.3f}  half-width {np.mean(1.96*sd):.3f}")
A = fit(2100.0); mu, sd = predict(A, np.array([2030 + 1/24, 2040 + 1/24]))
print(f"all data: Jan 2030 {mu[0]:.2f} +/- {1.96*sd[0]:.2f}, Jan 2040 {mu[1]:.2f} +/- {1.96*sd[1]:.2f} ppm")
sc = np.sqrt(F["prior_var"])                           # ridge on rescaled columns = posterior mean
r = Ridge(alpha=F["s2"], fit_intercept=False).fit(F["P"] * sc, y[F["tr"]])
print(f"max |ridge - posterior mean| = {np.max(np.abs(r.coef_ * sc - F['m'])):.1e}")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Put a Gaussian prior N(0, α−1I) on the weights of a linear model with Gaussian noise of precision β, and the posterior is Gaussian too: SN−1 = αI + βΦ⊤Φ, mN = βSNΦ⊤t.
  • Precisions add, so you can learn one point at a time: each posterior is the prior for the next point, and the order does not matter.
  • The posterior mean is the MAP, and the MAP is ridge regression with λ = α/β; a Laplace prior gives the lasso instead, whose MAP is sparse but whose posterior mean is not.
  • The predictive variance 1/β + φ(x)⊤SNφ(x) is noise plus weight uncertainty: it shrinks near data and grows away from them, unless the basis functions vanish there, which makes the model falsely confident.
  • The prediction is a weighted sum of the training targets through the equivalent kernel k(x, x′) = βφ(x)⊤SNφ(x′), the first step towards Gaussian processes; learning β too turns the Gaussian predictive into a Student-t.

Check your understanding

A model with one weight, φ(x) = x, has prior precision α = 1 and noise precision β = 4. You observe one point, x = 1, t = 2. What is the posterior?

S1−1 = 1 + 4·1² = 5, so the posterior variance is 0.2, and m1 = βS1xt = 4 × 0.2 × 2 = 1.6. The prior pulls the least-squares answer, 2, back towards 0.

Why does a single data point make the posterior over a line's (w0, w1) shrink in one direction only?

It adds precision βφφ⊤, a rank-one matrix: it constrains w0 + w1xn (the line's height at xn) but says nothing about how the line pivots around that point. A second point at a different x fixes the pivot.

Your colleague fits ridge regression with λ = 0.1 and says “no priors here”. What prior is she using?

A zero-mean Gaussian on the weights with precision α = λβ, that is, variance 10/β: ten times the noise variance. Ridge is the MAP (and the posterior mean) under that prior.

Far outside the data, a model built from Gaussian bumps predicts with an error bar equal to the noise sd. Why is that a problem, and what fixes it?

Far from every bump, φ(x) ≈ 0, so the weight-uncertainty term φ⊤SNφ vanishes and only 1/β is left: the model is most confident exactly where it knows least. Covering the whole input range with basis functions, adding a constant or trend, or switching to a Gaussian process (where the kernel's prior variance does not vanish) fixes it.

Why does the CO₂ forecast band widen with the horizon, and why would it stay narrow if all the knots were in the training period?

Knots after the cut-off have no data, so their slope changes keep their prior sd sv: each one adds a possible bend, and these add up as the horizon grows. Without future knots the forecast just extends the last fitted slope, whose uncertainty is small, so the band would stay narrow and miss real changes in the growth rate.
