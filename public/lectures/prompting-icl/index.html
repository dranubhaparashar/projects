Prompting & In-Context Learning

How a frozen model picks up a new task from a few examples

Show a large language model three solved examples and a fourth unsolved one, and it often solves the fourth. Nothing in it was retrained: the “learning” happened inside a single forward pass, from the prompt alone. This page can’t run a large language model, so it runs something you can inspect completely: a small transformer, trained offline to learn straight lines from its prompt. Its weights never change below. On the right, drag the examples and watch it change its mind, next to the least-squares line a statistician would draw.

The idea

Learning without changing a weight

In every earlier chapter, learning meant changing weights: compute a loss, follow its gradient, repeat. In 2020, Brown and colleagues showed that GPT-3 could often do a task it was never trained for when the prompt simply contained a few worked examples, with no gradient step at all. They called this in-context learning. The model’s weights encode a general skill; the prompt supplies the particular task.

Our stand-in makes the idea concrete. A tiny transformer was trained, offline and once, on 768,000 prompts, each built from a different random straight line y = a x + b plus noise. Since no line ever repeats, memorising is useless: the only way to predict well is to work out the line from the examples in front of it. Below, the prompt is literally a sequence of numbers, x₁, y₁, x₂, y₂, …, and then a query x. Add examples one at a time and watch the prediction home in on the truth.

\[ \begin{gathered} \text{training:}\ \ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}(\theta) \\[4pt] \text{in context:} \\ \hat y_q = f_{\theta}\big(x_1, y_1, \dots, x_k, y_k,\ x_q\big) \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: during training the weights θ move downhill on the loss. Once training is over, θ is fixed. To “learn” a new task the model is simply run on a prompt holding k solved examples (xi, yi) and a question xq; its answer ŷq depends on the examples only through the forward pass.
Say it“Training moves theta down the gradient. In context, y-hat-q is f-theta applied to the k examples and the query, with theta frozen.”

Vocabulary

Zero-shot, one-shot, few-shot

Brown et al. (2020) named the settings by the number of solved examples, or shots, in the prompt: zero-shot (only a description of the task), one-shot and few-shot (typically 10 to 100 examples, as many as fit GPT-3’s 2,048-token window). On the TriviaQA question set, for instance, the paper reports 64.3% accuracy zero-shot, 68.0% one-shot and 71.2% few-shot for the 175-billion-parameter model, and it reports that the benefit of examples grew with model size.

Garg et al. (2022) turned this into a clean experiment, the one reproduced here at small scale: measure the error of a transformer’s prediction after k examples, on fresh tasks, and compare it with classical algorithms given the same k examples. Slide k and compare the curves. The chart is computed live: 200 new prompts, each one 40 examples long, run through the network in your browser.

\[ \text{MSE}(k) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Big(\hat y^{(n)}_{k+1} - y^{(n)}_{k+1}\Big)^2 \]
Read it as: take N prompts, each from a task never seen in training. After k examples, ask for the (k+1)-th y and square the miss. Average over the prompts. With k = 0 nothing is known about the line, so the best guess is 0 and the error is Var(a x + b) + σ² = 1 + 1 + 0.25 = 2.25. No algorithm can go below the noise floor σ² = 0.25.
Say it“MSE at k is the average, over N prompts, of the squared miss on the next point after seeing k examples.”

Under the bonnet

What is it computing?

The network was trained to minimise squared error over a known family of tasks: slopes and intercepts drawn from a standard normal, inputs from a standard normal, noise with σ = 0.5. For that family the best possible predictor is known exactly. It is the posterior mean, and for Gaussian lines it is ridge regression with penalty λ = σ²/τ² = 0.25 (τ = 1 is the spread of a and b). So a well-trained in-context learner should behave not like plain least squares but like ridge with λ = 0.25, hedging towards a flat line when examples are few.

That is a testable claim. Akyürek et al. (2023) asked the same question of larger transformers and found them tracking ridge and least-squares solutions; von Oswald et al. (2023) showed transformers can implement steps of gradient descent in their forward pass; Xie et al. (2022) framed in-context learning as implicit Bayesian inference. Here you can check it directly. Slide λ and find the ridge line that agrees best with the network.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf w}_{\lambda} &= \big(X^{\top}X + \lambda I\big)^{-1} X^{\top}\mathbf y \\ X_{i} &= [\,x_i,\ 1\,], \quad \lambda^{\star} = \sigma^2/\tau^2 \\ \Rightarrow\ \hat{\mathbf w}_{\lambda^{\star}} &= \mathbb{E}\big[(a, b) \mid \text{examples}\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ridge regression is least squares with λ added to the diagonal, which pulls the slope and intercept towards zero. λ = 0 is plain least squares. When the true lines are drawn with spread τ and the noise has spread σ, choosing λ = σ²/τ² makes ridge return exactly the posterior mean of (a, b): the Bayes-optimal answer under squared error.
Say it“w-hat-lambda is X-transpose-X plus lambda I, inverted, times X-transpose y. With lambda equal to sigma squared over tau squared, it is the posterior mean of the slope and intercept.”

Stress tests

What do the examples teach?

If the network really infers the task from the examples, then damaging the examples should damage its answers, and in ways we can predict. Three experiments, each run on 100 fresh prompts with 10 examples: wrong labels (a share of the y’s replaced by random values), unfamiliar inputs (x spread wider than anything seen in training) and shuffled order (the same examples, rearranged). Pick an experiment and move the slider.

Compare what the papers report for real language models. Min et al. (2022) found that replacing the labels in classification demonstrations with random ones “barely hurts performance” across twelve models, GPT-3 among them: the demonstrations mostly conveyed the label space, the kind of inputs and the format, while the task itself was already known from pre-training. Wei et al. (2023) then found that the largest models can follow deliberately flipped labels, overriding what they knew. Lu et al. (2022) showed that the order of the same few examples can swing accuracy from near state of the art to near chance. Our toy has no pre-training on language to fall back on, so its behaviour is the clean baseline these findings are measured against.

\[ \begin{aligned} X^{\top}X &= \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathbf x_i \mathbf x_i^{\top}, \\ X^{\top}\mathbf y &= \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathbf x_i\, y_i \\ \Rightarrow\ \hat{\mathbf w}(\pi D) &= \hat{\mathbf w}(D) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: least squares (and ridge) only ever use sums over the examples, and a sum does not care about order: any permutation π of the examples D gives the same line. A transformer sees the examples at different positions, so nothing forces it to be order-blind; it can only learn to be approximately so.
Say it“X-transpose-X and X-transpose-y are sums over the examples, so shuffling the examples leaves w-hat unchanged.”

Prompting for reasoning

Chain of thought: room to think

Wei et al. (2022) changed only the examples in a few-shot prompt: instead of question → answer, each example showed question → worked steps → answer. On the GSM8K maths word problems, PaLM (540 billion parameters) went from about 18% solved with standard prompting to about 57% with these chain-of-thought examples, as the paper reports; the gains appeared only in the largest models they tested. Kojima et al. (2022) then found that merely adding “Let’s think step by step” lifted InstructGPT (text-davinci-002) on GSM8K from 10.4% to 40.7%, with no examples at all.

Why could writing steps help? A transformer spends a fixed amount of computation on each token it produces: one pass through its layers. An answer blurted out as the first token gets one pass; every intermediate token written first buys another pass, and lets each pass do something easy. The toy below makes that concrete. It is not a language model: two identical small networks learn to give the last digit of a sum of n digits, from the same training lists. One must answer in one go. The other writes the running total after each digit, the scratchpad idea of Nye et al. (2021). Train both, then raise the number of digits.

\[ \begin{aligned} s_0 &= 0 \\ s_t &= (s_{t-1} + d_t) \bmod 10 \\ \text{answer} &= s_n \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the answer is the last digit of d₁ + d₂ + … + dn. Answering directly, a network must map all 10n possible lists to the right digit at once, and a change in any single digit changes the answer. Writing the steps, it only ever needs the 100-entry table “previous total plus next digit”, applied n times.
Say it“s-zero is zero; s-t is s-t-minus-one plus d-t, mod ten; the answer is s-n.”

Sampling many chains

Self-consistency: many chains, one vote

A chain of thought is sampled, so a second run may take a different route to a different answer. Wang et al. (2023) proposed sampling several chains and returning the most common final answer. With PaLM 540B on GSM8K they report a gain of 17.9 percentage points over a single chain of thought. The reason is arithmetic: correct chains tend to agree with one another, while wrong chains scatter over many different wrong answers.

The lab uses an idealised model so the maths is exact: every chain is independent, right with probability p, and when wrong it picks one of m wrong answers at random. Real chains from one model are correlated, so treat this as the best case. Set p below one half and compare one shared wrong answer with ten scattered ones.

\[ \begin{aligned} P_{\text{vote}}(k) &= \sum_{j=1}^{k} B_j\, W_j \\ B_j &= \binom{k}{j} p^{j} (1-p)^{k-j} \\ W_j &= \Pr\big(\max_{a} c_a < j\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: j of the k chains are right with the binomial probability Bj. Wj is the chance that the other k − j votes leave every wrong answer a with a count ca below j, so that the right answer wins (a tie for first place is settled by a coin toss and counts as a share of a win). With one shared wrong answer (m = 1) this is the classic majority rule, Σj>k/2; with many wrong answers, a minority of right chains can still win.
Say it“The vote is right with probability: sum over j of k-choose-j, p to the j, one-minus-p to the k-minus-j, times W-j, the chance every wrong answer gets fewer than j votes.”

Practice

Prompt patterns

Most prompting advice is a small set of recurring patterns, catalogued by White et al. (2023) and repeated in the prompting guides from model providers such as Anthropic and OpenAI: say who the model should be and what to do, give the context it needs, show examples of the exact input and output, fix the output format, fence off pasted data with delimiters so it is not mistaken for instructions, and for multi-step problems ask for the reasoning before the answer. These are recommendations, not laws: what helps varies by model and task, which is why the evaluation habits of chapter 4.10 matter.

The builder below assembles a real prompt from those parts. It does not send it anywhere (this page runs no language model); copy it into any model you use. Switch parts on and off and read the checklist.

\[ k_{\max} = \left\lfloor \frac{C - L_{\text{fixed}} - L_{\text{answer}}}{L_{\text{example}}} \right\rfloor \]
Read it as: examples cost context. With a context window of C tokens, after the fixed parts of the prompt (instructions, context, the question) and room for the answer, the number of examples that fit is the space left divided by the length of one example, rounded down. Lengths here are estimated as characters ÷ 4, a common rule of thumb for English; a real tokenizer (chapter 4.2) gives the exact count.
Say it“k-max is the floor of: the context size, minus the fixed part, minus the answer, over the length of one example.”

    Real-world project
    
    
    Project: prompting a transformer with Old Faithful
    
    
    
      
    Old Faithful in Yellowstone is famous for being predictable: the longer an eruption lasts, the longer the wait for the next one. The classic record of 272 eruptions (Härdle, 1991; analysed by Azzalini & Bowman, 1990) lists each eruption’s duration and the waiting time to the next. Our transformer has never seen a geyser, or any real data: it was trained only on synthetic straight lines. Can it predict real waiting times when its prompt holds a handful of real eruptions?
    
      
    One honest preparation step: the network only knows numbers on the scale it was trained on, so durations and waits are converted to standard units with a fixed change of units (subtract 3.488 min and divide by 1.139 min; subtract 70.90 min and divide by 13.57 min). That is like choosing to write minutes instead of seconds: it is the same for every prompt and every method, and predictions are converted back to minutes for scoring.

    One draw of k eruptions is luck. For each k, the page draws 20 random prompts of k eruptions, predicts the waiting time of every one of the other 272 − k eruptions, and averages the root-mean-square error in minutes. The same draws (the same seeded random numbers) are run in Python below.
    
    
    
    
    
    
    

    
    The same project in Python
    

    # pi-model.py: the numpy transformer (forward and backward passes) used for training;
# pi-load.py: reads the same float32 weights that the page embeds.
import numpy as np, json
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression, Ridge
exec(open("pi-model.py").read()); exec(open("pi-load.py").read())
P, cfg, D, flatw = load_weights("pi-weights.json")
F = np.genfromtxt("pi_dl/faithful.csv", delimiter=",", skip_header=1)
dur, wait = F[:, 1], F[:, 2]
x, y = (dur - 3.488) / 1.139, (wait - 70.90) / 13.57          # the fixed change of units
print(f"{flatw.size} weights, sum {flatw.sum():.6f}")

def draw(k, t):                                               # the same seeded draw as the page
    r = mulberry32(7000 + 100 * k + t); idx = list(range(272))
    for i in range(k): j = i + int(r() * (272 - i)); idx[i], idx[j] = idx[j], idx[i]
    return np.array(idx[:k]), np.array(idx[k:])

def net_predict(xc, yc, xq):                                  # k examples, then one query per row
    U = np.zeros((len(xq), 2 * len(xc) + 1, 4)); U[:, :-1] = tokens(xc[None], yc[None])[0]
    U[:, -1, 0], U[:, -1, 2] = xq, 1
    return forward(P, cfg, U)[:, -1]

rmse = lambda p, i: np.sqrt(np.mean((p * 13.57 + 70.90 - wait[i]) ** 2))
print("  k   transformer   least squares   ridge 0.25   mean of k")
table = []
for k in [1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 39]:
    res = []
    for t in range(20):
        c, q = draw(k, t); X1 = np.c_[x[c], np.ones(k)]; Xq = np.c_[x[q], np.ones(len(q))]
        ls = LinearRegression(fit_intercept=False).fit(X1, y[c])   # minimum-norm when k < 2
        rd = Ridge(alpha=0.25, fit_intercept=False).fit(X1, y[c])  # intercept penalised too
        res.append([rmse(net_predict(x[c], y[c], x[q]), q), rmse(ls.predict(Xq), q),
                    rmse(rd.predict(Xq), q), rmse(np.full(len(q), y[c].mean()), q)])
    m = np.mean(res, 0); table.append([k, *m])
    print(f"{k:3d}   {m[0]:9.4f}   {m[1]:13.4f}   {m[2]:10.4f}   {m[3]:9.4f}")
full = LinearRegression().fit(x[:, None], y)
print(f"best line through all 272: RMSE {rmse(full.predict(x[:, None]), np.arange(272)):.2f} min")
json.dump(table, open("pi-skref.json", "w"))

    The page and Python use the identical float32 weights and the identical random draws, so every number in the table above should agree with this output to the last printed digit (the forward passes differ by about 10−15, from the order of floating-point additions). What this does and does not show: a network trained only on synthetic lines can use a few real examples in its prompt as well as ridge regression can, because the geyser happens to be close to a straight line on the scale it knows. It shows the mechanism of in-context learning, at a scale where every weight can be checked. It says nothing directly about how a large language model reads a prompt in words.
    
  
    

    



    
  
    
    
    Wrap-up
    
    
    Five lines to remember
    
    
      
      
    • In-context learning is learning in the forward pass: the weights stay frozen and the examples in the prompt specify the task.
      • 
      
    • Zero-, one- and few-shot count the solved examples in the prompt; more examples usually help, and (as Brown et al. report) help larger models more.
      • 
      
    • A transformer trained on a family of tasks can approach the Bayes-optimal learner for that family: ours behaves like ridge regression with λ = σ²/τ², not like plain least squares.
      • 
      
    • Chain-of-thought prompts give the model intermediate tokens, and so more computation and simpler steps; self-consistency samples several chains and votes, which helps when wrong answers scatter.
      • 
      
    • Prompt patterns (role, instruction, context, examples, format, delimiters, reasoning first) are good defaults, not guarantees: order, format and labels can matter, so measure.
      • 
    
    
    
    Check your understanding
    
    
    
      
    A model answers a new task correctly after seeing three examples in its prompt. Which of its weights changed?
    None. In-context learning happens entirely in the forward pass; the examples change the activations, not the parameters. Remove the examples and the “learning” is gone.
    
      
    With two noisy examples, least squares does far worse than the transformer. Why?
    Two points determine a line exactly, so least squares passes through both and inherits all their noise; when the two x’s are close the slope can be wild. The transformer, like the Bayes-optimal ridge rule, shrinks towards a flat line until the evidence is strong.
    
      
    Randomising the labels in our toy’s examples ruins it, but Min et al. found random labels barely hurt real language models on classification. How can both be true?
    Our toy has no knowledge of any particular task; the labels are its only source of information. A pre-trained language model often already knows the task (say, sentiment) and uses the demonstrations mainly to learn the format, the label set and the kind of input, which random labels still convey.
    
      
    Each sampled chain of thought is right 40% of the time. When does a majority vote over 15 chains help, and when does it hurt?
    If the wrong chains all agree on one wrong answer, the vote is right less often than one chain, since 40% < 50%. If wrong answers scatter over many values, the right answer can still be the most common one, and voting helps. Correlated chains help less than the idealised model suggests.
    
      
    Your few-shot prompt has a 2,048-token window, 300 tokens of fixed text, 100 tokens reserved for the answer, and each example costs about 90 tokens. How many examples fit?
    ⌊(2048 − 300 − 100) / 90⌋ = ⌊18.3⌋ = 18 examples.
