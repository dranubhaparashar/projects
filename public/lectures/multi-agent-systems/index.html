Multi-Agent Systems

When several agents beat one, and when they only cost more

Give one hard job to a team of agents and three things can happen: they catch each other’s mistakes, they finish sooner by working in parallel, or they spend a fortune talking to each other and agree on the wrong answer. Which one you get is not luck; it follows from a few pieces of maths this chapter builds. It starts with real people. In the figure, 39 human annotators each looked at the same 108 photos of two very similar blue birds and answered yes or no. One by one they are mediocre. Do they become wise as a crowd? Tap the figure to step through the photos, then swap the real crowd for independent stand-ins of exactly the same skill.

The idea

Many minds: the jury theorem

In 1785 the Marquis de Condorcet asked when a jury is more reliable than a single juror. His answer is the oldest argument for multi-agent systems. Suppose each of n agents answers a yes/no question and is right with probability p, independently of the others, and the team goes with the majority. If p is above one half, the chance that the majority is right climbs towards certainty as n grows. LLM practice rediscovered this as self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023): sample several answers from a model and keep the most common one. Li et al. (2024) found that simply adding more sampled “agents” and voting keeps improving accuracy on many benchmarks, up to a point.

The bars on the left show how many of the n agents are right, a binomial distribution; the green bars are the outcomes where the majority wins. Move p and n, and try p below one half.

\[ \begin{aligned} P_{\text{maj}}(n, p) &= \sum_{k=(n+1)/2}^{n} \binom{n}{k}\, p^{k} (1-p)^{\,n-k} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: k is how many of the n agents are right. Each agent is right with probability p, independently, so k follows a binomial distribution: there are C(n, k) ways to choose which k are right, each with probability pk(1 − p)n−k. The majority is right when k is more than half of n; add up those cases. The sum needs n odd; with an even n a tie is possible, and the labs settle ties with a fair coin.
Say it“The chance the majority is right is the sum, from k equals n-plus-one over two up to n, of n choose k times p to the k times one minus p to the n minus k.”

The catch

When agents think alike

The hero figure broke the theorem: 39 annotators averaging 64% should, if independent, be right 97% of the time by majority. They manage 76%. The missing ingredient is correlation. Some questions are simply hard, and on a hard question most agents fail together. A simple, standard way to model this: give each question its own chance θ that an agent gets it right, drawn from a Beta distribution with mean p, and let the agents be independent only once θ is fixed. The spread of θ sets the correlation ρ between any two agents’ correctness (Ladha, 1992, studied the jury theorem with correlated votes). Five copies of one LLM given the same prompt are a high-ρ jury: they share training data, habits and blind spots. Raise ρ and watch the ceiling come down.

\[ \begin{aligned} \theta_q &\sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta) \\ \alpha &= p\,(1/\rho - 1) \\ \beta &= (1-p)(1/\rho - 1) \\ \lim_{n\to\infty} P_{\text{maj}} &= P\big(\theta > \tfrac12\big) \\ &= 1 - I_{1/2}(\alpha, \beta) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: question q has its own difficulty θq, the chance that any one agent gets it right. Across questions θ averages p, and two agents’ correctness has correlation ρ = 1/(α + β + 1). Votes then follow a beta-binomial distribution. With infinitely many agents the majority is right exactly on the questions where θ > ½, so that share is a ceiling no team size can break; I is the regularised incomplete beta function.
Say it“Theta is Beta alpha beta; in the limit the majority is right with the probability that theta exceeds one half.”
\[ n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{n}{1 + (n-1)\,\rho} \;\xrightarrow{\;n\to\infty\;}\; \frac{1}{\rho} \]
Read it as: the share of a correlated panel that votes right varies as much as the share of a smaller panel of neff independent agents would. Survey statisticians call 1 + (n − 1)ρ the design effect (Kish, 1965). However many correlated agents you add, they are never worth more than 1/ρ independent ones.
Say it“The effective number of agents is n over one plus n minus one times rho, which tends to one over rho.”

Pattern 1

Orchestrator and workers

Voting uses many agents for the same job. The other big reason for a team is to split one job into parts. In the orchestrator–workers pattern (Schluntz & Zhang, 2024), a lead agent plans the job as a graph of sub-tasks, briefs worker agents to do them, and merges what comes back; Anthropic’s research system works this way, with a lead agent that sends sub-agents to search in parallel. Below is such a plan for “Should I put a heat pump in my old house?”: eight searches, four analyses, two sections of writing, a critique and an answer. The durations are illustrative seconds of agent time. A greedy scheduler hands each free agent the ready job with the longest chain still behind it. Two quantities decide everything: the total work, and the critical path (red), the longest chain of jobs that must run one after another. Add agents, then click a job to make it three times slower.

\[ \begin{aligned} T_W &\ge \max\!\big(T_\infty,\; T_O + \tfrac{T_{\text{work}}}{W}\big) \\ T_W &\le \tfrac{T_{\text{work}}}{W} + T_\infty \\ S &= T_1 / T_W \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: TW is the time to finish with W worker agents. T∞ is the critical path: even infinitely many agents must wait for it. Twork is the workers’ total job time, which W agents can at best share evenly; TO is the orchestrator’s own planning and answering, which here comes first and last. Graham (1969) proved the right-hand bound for any greedy schedule, one that never leaves an agent idle while a job is ready. S is the speed-up over one agent doing all T1 seconds itself. Both bounds assume briefing a worker is free; the slider says otherwise.
Say it“The time with W agents is at least the critical path, and at least the orchestrator's time plus the work over W; and at most the work over W plus the critical path.”

Coordination costs

Amdahl’s law and the cost of keeping in sync

The briefing slider above showed the problem in miniature: work only the orchestrator can do does not shrink when you add workers. Gene Amdahl made the point about parallel computers in 1967: if a share α of a job is serial, no number of processors can make it more than 1/α times faster. Teams of agents have a second cost that Amdahl ignored. Agents that share a goal must keep each other up to date, and the number of pairs that could need syncing grows as N(N − 1). Neil Gunther’s universal scalability law adds that crosstalk term, with a striking consequence: past some team size, adding agents makes the team slower. Set the serial share and the crosstalk, then slide N.

\[ \begin{aligned} S_{\text{Amdahl}}(N) &= \frac{N}{1 + \alpha (N-1)} \\ S(N) &= \frac{N}{D(N)} \\ D(N) &= 1 + \alpha (N-1) + \beta N (N-1) \\ N^* &= \sqrt{(1-\alpha)/\beta} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: N agents, α the serial share (planning, merging, briefing), β the cost of each pair of agents keeping in sync. With β = 0 this is Amdahl’s law, which flattens towards 1/α. With β > 0 the N(N − 1) term eventually wins, and the speed-up peaks at N* (set the derivative to zero) and then falls.
Say it“Speed-up is N over one plus alpha times N minus one plus beta times N times N minus one; it peaks at the square root of one minus alpha over beta.”

Pattern 2

Debate: changing minds

Voting counts first answers. In a debate the agents read each other’s answers and reasons, and may change their minds over several rounds; Du et al. (2024) found this improved arithmetic and factual accuracy over a single model. Whether it helps depends on one thing: is the right answer more persuasive than the wrong one? This lab is a deliberately simple model of that. Each round, every agent reads everyone else’s answer. A wrong agent is won over with probability a × (the share who are right); a right agent is talked out of it with probability b × (the share who are wrong). Thin lines are 60 individual debates, coloured by how they end; the black line averages 2,000. Make the truth more, then less, persuasive than error.

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[x_{t+1} \mid x_t\big] = x_t + (a - b)\, x_t (1 - x_t) \]
Read it as: xt is the share of agents that are right after round t. The wrong ones, a share 1 − xt, switch to right with probability a·xt; the right ones switch to wrong with probability b·(1 − xt). The net expected change is (a − b)xt(1 − xt): the logistic curve. If a > b the truth spreads, if a < b error does, and the dashed line iterates the formula (a mean-field approximation of the average).
Say it“The expected share right next round is x plus a minus b times x times one minus x.”

Pattern 3

Critique: a checker in the loop

A cheaper team than a debate is two agents with different jobs: a generator proposes an answer and a critic (or verifier) accepts it or sends it back for another try. This is the evaluator–optimiser pattern, and close to Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023), where the critic is the same model. The critic is itself fallible: it catches a wrong answer with probability c and, wrongly, rejects a right one with probability e. Bayes’ rule then says exactly how much the filter is worth. Each dot below is one task; a number on a dot counts the attempts it took. Make the critic sharper, then make it reject right answers more often than wrong ones.

\[ \begin{aligned} a &= p(1-e) \\ &\quad + (1-p)(1-c) \\ P(\text{right} \mid \text{acc.}) &= \frac{p\,(1-e)}{a} \\ P(\text{none in } R) &= (1-a)^R \\ \mathbb{E}[\text{tries}] &= \frac{1 - (1-a)^R}{a} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a is the chance the critic accepts an attempt: right and not falsely rejected, or wrong and not caught. Among accepted answers, the right ones are the share p(1 − e)/a, which beats p exactly when c > e. The loop stops at the first acceptance or after R tries; then it either returns the last attempt anyway or hands the task to a human. Each try is assumed to be a fresh, independent attempt.
Say it“The chance of right given accepted is p times one minus e, over the acceptance rate a.”

Pattern 4

Hand-offs: passing the baton

In a hand-off one agent passes a conversation to a specialist: triage to bookings, bookings to payments. OpenAI’s Swarm and Agents SDK made hand-offs a first-class tool call. Every hand-off is also a place where context can be lost. If each agent passes on a free-text summary that keeps each fact with probability r, losses compound hop after hop, and the case below (an invented airline customer) is only as good as its weakest summary. The fix is the one tool calls already use (chapter 5.2): a structured hand-off, whose required fields are arguments checked against a schema, so a hand-off missing one is rejected and redone. Lengthen the chain, then switch to the structured hand-off.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_{\text{free}} &= m\,r^{k} \\ P_{\text{free}}(\text{all kept}) &= r^{mk} \\ \mathbb{E}_{\text{struct}} &= m_{\text{req}} + (m - m_{\text{req}})\, r^{k} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: E is the expected number of facts that reach the last agent: m facts, k hand-offs, each fact survives each summary with probability r, independently. After k hops a fact survives with rk, and all m together with rmk, which collapses fast: 0.940 is about 1.5%. A schema guarantees its mreq required fields; only the optional ones still erode.
Say it“Expected facts after k hand-offs are m times r to the k; all of them survive with r to the m k.”

Coordination costs, counted

    
Who talks to whom

    
The shape of a team, its topology, sets three costs. Links: if everyone may talk to everyone there are n(n − 1)/2 conversations to keep straight. Tokens: every message is read, and reading is what an LLM agent pays for; Anthropic reported that its multi-agent research system used about 15 times the tokens of a chat (2025). And hops: how many relays a fact needs to cross the team. Cemri et al. (2025), studying why multi-agent LLM systems fail, found many failures in exactly this layer: lost information, ignored messages, agents working at cross purposes. Compare the four shapes at the same n; numbers on the nodes are how many agents each one talks to.

    
\[ \begin{aligned} L_{\text{mesh}} &= \binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2} \\ L_{\text{star, chain, tree}} &= n - 1 \\ \text{tokens per round} &= 2L\,m \\ \text{largest context} &= d_{\max}\, m\, R \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: L is the number of two-way links. Each round, the two ends of every link read one message of m tokens from each other, 2L messages in all. The busiest agent, with dmax links, accumulates dmax·m tokens of others’ messages per round, R rounds in all. A mesh is quadratic in n; a star, a chain and a tree are linear, but they differ in who is busiest and how far apart agents are.
Say it“A full mesh has n choose two links, n times n minus one over two; a star, chain or tree has n minus one.”

Real-world project

    
Project: 39 annotators, one verdict

    

      
The crowd in the hero is real. For their NeurIPS 2010 paper The Multidimensional Wisdom of Crowds, Peter Welinder, Steve Branson, Serge Belongie and Pietro Perona asked 39 human annotators to label the same 108 photos, each showing one of two blue birds that are easy to confuse, the Indigo Bunting and the Blue Grosbeak: 4,212 yes/no answers, with expert labels to check them against. They released the data with their CUBAM code (BSD 3-Clause Clear licence); this page uses the file demo/bluebirds from commit 1db8c09 of 18 February 2011, unchanged. Swap “annotator” for “LLM agent” and nothing in the maths changes: what matters is each member’s accuracy and how their mistakes overlap. Everything below is computed in your browser from those 4,212 answers.

    


    
1 · Look at the votes

    

    
    


    
2 · Do bigger panels help?

    
Pick n of the 39 at random and let them vote. Averaged over every possible panel, the chance the panel is right on a photo that k annotators got right is a hypergeometric probability, so the real curve below is exact, not sampled. Compare it with section 1 (independent annotators of the same average skill) and section 2 (the correlated model, with ρ estimated from the data).

    
\[ \begin{aligned} \bar P_{\text{maj}}(n) &= \frac{1}{108} \sum_{j=1}^{108} \sum_{c > n/2} h_j(c) \\ h_j(c) &= \binom{k_j}{c}\binom{39 - k_j}{n - c} \Big/ \binom{39}{n} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: kj of the 39 annotators got photo j right. A random panel of n contains c of them with the hypergeometric probability hj(c); the panel is right when c is more than half of n. Average over the 108 photos.

3 · Learn whom to trust, without the answers

    
A majority vote treats a 32% annotator like an 89% one. If we knew the accuracies pi, the best rule for independent voters would weight each vote by ln(pi/(1 − pi)) (Nitzan & Paroush, 1982), which turns a worse-than-chance voter’s vote around. But an orchestrator rarely has an answer key. Dawid and Skene (1979) showed how to estimate each member’s reliability from the votes alone, by expectation–maximisation: guess the true labels (start from the majority), score each annotator against the guess, re-weigh the votes with those scores, and repeat. Here each annotator gets two numbers, how often they say yes to a yes photo and no to a no photo, since many annotators lean towards one answer. Step through the EM iterations and watch the estimates line up with the truth they never saw.

    
\[ \begin{aligned} P(y_j = 1 \mid \mathbf v_j) &= \frac{\pi A_j}{\pi A_j + (1 - \pi) B_j} \\ A_j &= \textstyle\prod_i s_i^{v_{ij}} (1 - s_i)^{1 - v_{ij}} \\ B_j &= \textstyle\prod_i (1 - t_i)^{v_{ij}}\, t_i^{1 - v_{ij}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: vij = 1 if annotator i said yes to photo j, and vj holds all 39 votes on photo j. si is i’s chance of saying yes when the truth is yes (sensitivity), ti of saying no when it is no (specificity), π the share of yes photos; Aj and Bj are the chances of photo j’s votes if it were a yes or a no. This E-step is Bayes’ rule; the M-step re-estimates s, t and π from these posteriors (with add-one smoothing), and the two alternate until nothing changes.
Say it“The chance photo j is a yes is pi times the product of each annotator's chance of voting as they did if it were a yes, over the same plus the same for a no.”

The scoreboard

    

    
Two centuries of statistics in one table: a plain majority adds 12 points to the average annotator and then stalls, because the annotators share their mistakes. Weighting by skill, or learning the skills with EM, adds another 12 to 13 and matches the best single annotator, who could only have been picked out with the answer key. Independent annotators would have done better still; that line is theory, not something this crowd can reach.


    
The same analysis in Python


import json, numpy as np
from math import comb
from scipy.stats import binom, betabinom, hypergeom
from scipy.special import betainc

d = json.load(open("ma-data.json", encoding="utf8"))          # Welinder et al. (2010) bluebirds, from the CUBAM repo
V = np.array([[int(c) for c in row] for row in d["V"]])        # 39 annotators x 108 photos, 1 = "yes"
y = np.array(d["y"]); W, N = V.shape
acc = (V == y).mean(axis=1); pbar = acc.mean()
kc = (V == y).sum(axis=0)                                      # annotators right, per photo
share = kc / W; v = share.var()
rho = (v / (share.mean() * (1 - share.mean())) - 1 / W) / (1 - 1 / W)
print(f"{W} annotators x {N} photos, {y.sum()} 'yes'; mean accuracy {pbar:.4f}, best {acc.max():.4f}, worst {acc.min():.4f}")
print(f"rho (method of moments) {rho:.4f}; photos most annotators get wrong: {(2 * kc < W).sum()}")

def maj(pmf):                                                  # majority right, ties split by a fair coin
    k = np.arange(len(pmf)); n = len(pmf) - 1
    return pmf[2 * k > n].sum() + 0.5 * pmf[2 * k == n].sum()
a, b = pbar * (1 / rho - 1), (1 - pbar) * (1 / rho - 1)
print(" n   real panels   independent   beta-binomial")
curve = {}
for n in range(1, 40, 2):
    real = np.mean([maj(hypergeom(W, k, n).pmf(np.arange(n + 1))) for k in kc])
    ind, bb = maj(binom(n, pbar).pmf(np.arange(n + 1))), maj(betabinom(n, a, b).pmf(np.arange(n + 1)))
    curve[n] = (real, ind, bb)
    if n in (1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 21, 39): print(f"{n:2d}   {real:.6f}      {ind:.6f}      {bb:.6f}")
print(f"ceiling P(theta > 1/2) = {1 - betainc(a, b, 0.5):.6f}; n_eff(39) = {39 / (1 + 38 * rho):.4f}")

pb = np.array([1.0])                                           # Poisson-binomial: independent, real accuracies
for p in acc: pb = np.convolve(pb, [1 - p, p])
pois = maj(pb)
mv = (2 * V.sum(axis=0) > W).astype(int)
print(f"majority of all 39: {(mv == y).sum()}/{N} = {(mv == y).mean():.4f}; if independent: {pois:.4f}")

pred = np.zeros(N, int)                                        # weighted vote, cross-fitted on odd/even photos
for h in (0, 1):
    tr, te = np.arange(N) % 2 != h, np.arange(N) % 2 == h
    p = ((V[:, tr] == y[tr]).sum(axis=1) + 1) / (tr.sum() + 2); w = np.log(p / (1 - p))
    pred[te] = (w @ (2 * V[:, te] - 1) > 0).astype(int)
print(f"weighted vote (cross-fitted): {(pred == y).sum()}/{N} = {(pred == y).mean():.4f}")

T = np.where(2 * V.sum(axis=0) > W, 1.0, np.where(2 * V.sum(axis=0) < W, 0.0, 0.5))
for it in range(1, 1001):                                      # Dawid & Skene (1979), two-coin, add-one smoothing
    s1 = T.sum(); pi = s1 / N
    se = (V @ T + 1) / (s1 + 2); sp = ((1 - V) @ (1 - T) + 1) / (N - s1 + 2)
    l1 = np.log(pi) + (V * np.log(se)[:, None] + (1 - V) * np.log(1 - se)[:, None]).sum(axis=0)
    l0 = np.log(1 - pi) + (V * np.log(1 - sp)[:, None] + (1 - V) * np.log(sp)[:, None]).sum(axis=0)
    T2 = 1 / (1 + np.exp(l0 - l1)); delta = np.abs(T2 - T).max(); T = T2
    if delta < 1e-12: break
ll = np.logaddexp(l1, l0).sum(); est = pi * se + (1 - pi) * sp
print(f"Dawid-Skene: {it} iterations, {((T > 0.5) == y).sum()}/{N} = {((T > 0.5) == y).mean():.4f}, log-lik {ll:.4f}, "
      f"corr(estimated, true accuracy) {np.corrcoef(est, acc)[0, 1]:.4f}")

print("checks: Condorcet n=5 p=0.7", round(maj(binom(5, 0.7).pmf(range(6))), 6),
      "| USL N=16 a=.05 b=.001", round(16 / (1 + 0.05 * 15 + 0.001 * 16 * 15), 6),
      "| critic p=.7 c=.8 e=.1: P(right|accepted)", round(0.7 * 0.9 / (0.7 * 0.9 + 0.3 * 0.2), 6))
json.dump({"pbar": pbar, "maj": (mv == y).mean(), "ds": ((T > 0.5) == y).mean(), "wcf": (pred == y).mean(), "best": acc.max(),
           "pois": pois, "rho": rho, "dsIt": it, "curve": {str(n): c for n, c in curve.items()}},
          open("ma-py.json", "w"), default=float)

Honest caveats: 108 photos is a small test, so one photo is almost a point of accuracy; the annotators are people, not LLM agents; and the simulations in sections 3 to 8 are stylised models with illustrative numbers, there to show the shape of each trade-off, not to predict a particular system.

  







  

    
Wrap-up

    
Five lines to remember

    
    
      
  • Voting turns many agents that are right more often than not into a reliable team, but only to the extent their mistakes are independent: n correlated agents are worth at most 1/ρ independent ones.
    • 
      
  • Splitting a job across workers is capped by the critical path and by everything only the orchestrator can do (Amdahl); the cost of keeping every pair in sync can make big teams slower than small ones.
    • 
      
  • Debate helps only when the truth is more persuasive than error; otherwise agents talk each other into confident, unanimous mistakes.
    • 
      
  • A critic pays off exactly when it rejects wrong answers more often than right ones, and every hand-off is a place to lose facts: make required context a checked, structured argument.
    • 
      
  • Count the conversations: a full mesh has n(n − 1)/2 of them; stars and hierarchies keep it linear, at the price of a busy hub and more hops.
    • 
    

    
Check your understanding

    

      
Five copies of the same model, same prompt, each 80% accurate, vote. Why might you get far less than the 94% Condorcet promises?
Because the copies make highly correlated mistakes: on the questions one gets wrong, the others tend to as well. The 94% assumes independence. With correlation ρ the vote is worth about n/(1 + (n − 1)ρ) independent agents and can never beat the share of questions most copies get right. Varying the model, prompt, tools or evidence lowers ρ.

      
A plan has 600 s of worker jobs and a critical path of 150 s. Roughly how fast can 4 workers finish, and what would 20 workers buy?
At least max(150, 600/4) = 150 s and, for a greedy schedule with free briefing, at most 600/4 + 150 = 300 s. Twenty workers cannot beat the 150 s critical path, so beyond a handful the extra agents mostly wait.

      
Under the universal scalability law with α = 0.04 and β = 0.002, what team size is fastest?
N* = √((1 − 0.04)/0.002) = √480 ≈ 22. Past that, adding agents lowers the speed-up because the N(N − 1) crosstalk term grows faster than the extra capacity.

      
Your critic rejects 30% of right answers and 25% of wrong ones. Is it worth running?
No. A critic improves the accuracy of accepted answers only if it rejects wrong answers more often than right ones (c > e). Here c = 0.25 < e = 0.30, so it filters slightly towards mistakes and costs extra attempts.

      
How did Dawid–Skene beat the majority on the bluebird photos without ever seeing the expert labels?
It estimated, from the agreement patterns alone, how reliable each annotator is for yes photos and for no photos, then weighed each vote by that reliability. Unreliable or biased annotators stop dragging the vote, and some below-chance annotators are even read in reverse. Majority voting counts every vote the same.
