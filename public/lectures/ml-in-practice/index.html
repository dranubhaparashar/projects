ML Systems in Practice

Formulation, data, explanations, fairness and monitoring: everything around the model

A trained model is the easy part. Before it you must decide what to predict and whether your data can teach it; after it you must explain what it does, check whom it fails, watch it in the world and write down honestly what it is for. This chapter does all of that for one real model: a logistic regression, trained in your browser on 32,561 people from the 1994 US census, that guesses whether a person earns more than $50,000 a year. Step 0 · before any data

Deciding what to predict, and why

Chapter after chapter we have been handed a dataset and a target. In real projects nobody hands you either. Problem formulation is the work of turning a goal (“offer our premium card to people who can afford it”) into a learning problem: who uses the output, what decision it feeds, which label stands in for the thing you care about, and what each kind of mistake costs. The label is almost always a proxy. The Adult data record whether a person’s 1993 income passed $50,000; they say nothing about ability to repay, so a lender using them would be optimising a stand-in, and any gap between proxy and goal becomes the model’s blind spot. It also pays to ask early whether you need machine learning at all, what the simplest baseline scores (here, “nobody earns over $50K” is right 76.4% of the time), and what you will measure once the system is live.

The output of a classifier is a probability; the decision needs a threshold, and the threshold comes from the costs, not from the model. Suppose the bank flags people for an offer. A false positive (offering to someone below $50K) costs cFP; a false negative (missing an eligible customer) costs cFN. Change the two costs and watch the best threshold move away from 0.5.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{flag } x &\iff p\,c_{\text{FN}} > (1-p)\,c_{\text{FP}} \\ &\iff p > t^{*} = \frac{c_{\text{FP}}}{c_{\text{FP}} + c_{\text{FN}}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: p = p(x) is the model’s probability that person x earns over $50K. Not flagging them risks cFN with probability p; flagging them risks cFP with probability 1 − p. Flag whenever the first expected cost is larger. That gives a threshold t* that depends only on the ratio of the costs, and only works if p is a calibrated probability.
Say it“Flag x when p times c-F-N beats one minus p times c-F-P, that is when p exceeds c-F-P over c-F-P plus c-F-N.”
\[ \text{cost}(t) = \frac{c_{\text{FP}}\,\text{FP}(t) + c_{\text{FN}}\,\text{FN}(t)}{n} \]
Read it as: at threshold t, count the test people wrongly flagged (FP) and wrongly passed over (FN), price each kind of error and average over all n people. The curve on the left is this cost for every t; its lowest point should sit near t*.
Say it“The cost at t is c-F-P times the false positives plus c-F-N times the false negatives, over n.”

Step 1 · the raw material

Data you can trust: collect, assess, manage

Most of the effort in a real project goes into data, and most failures start there. Collection: where did the rows come from, who is missing, and how were labels made? Adult was extracted by Barry Becker from the 1994 Current Population Survey, keeping only adults with a positive income and positive weekly hours; it is a sample of working adults, not of the population, and its labels are self-reported income. Assessment: look before you model. Count missing values, check the class balance and, crucially, check how many examples each group has, because a number measured on a small group is a noisy number. Management: record the source and version of every file, fix the train/test split before you start, keep anything that peeks at the test labels out of training (leakage), and store personal data as the law requires. The audit below is computed live from the data embedded in this page.

Now play the data collector. Below, the model is retrained on a sample of the training set. Shrink the sample, or keep fewer of the women, as a careless collection process might, and watch what happens to the error on women and on men in the untouched test set.

\[ \hat a \;\pm\; 1.96\,\sqrt{\frac{\hat a\,(1-\hat a)}{n}} \]
Read it as: a rate â (an accuracy, a true-positive rate) measured on n independent people is uncertain by about this much, with 95% confidence. The width shrinks only like 1/√n: four times the data halves the uncertainty. A group with few examples in the test set gets a wide interval however good the model is; a group with few in the training set gets a worse model.
Say it“a-hat plus or minus 1.96 times the square root of a-hat times one minus a-hat over n.”

Trust · the whole model

Reading the whole model

Why would anyone trust a model? Partly through testing, as in the chapter on evaluation, and partly through understanding. It helps to separate two words. A model is interpretable when a person can inspect it directly and see how it works: a short decision tree, or a linear model whose weights you can read. A model is explainable when, whatever its insides, some method can tell you why it produced a given output. Logistic regression is interpretable: each weight wj multiplies the odds by ewj when its input rises by one unit. But with 54 columns, standardised numbers, squared terms and one-hot categories, even this “transparent” model is hard to take in at a glance. A model-agnostic alternative asks a blunter question: permutation importance shuffles one attribute across the test set, breaking its link to the label, and measures how much worse the model gets. Click a bar to read that attribute’s weights as odds ratios.

\[ \frac{\text{odds}(x \text{ with } x_j{=}1)}{\text{odds}(x \text{ with } x_j{=}0)} = e^{\,w_j} \]
Read it as: for a one-hot column (say marital = Married-civ-spouse), switching it on and everything else held fixed multiplies the odds p/(1 − p) by ewj. An odds ratio of 3 is not “three times as likely”: going from odds 0.1 to 0.3 lifts p from 9% to 23%. Within one attribute, only differences between levels matter.
Say it“Switching x-j on multiplies the odds by e to the w-j.”
\[ \begin{aligned} I_j &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell\big(y_i, f(x_i^{\pi_j})\big) \\ &\quad - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell\big(y_i, f(x_i)\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: xiπj is person i with attribute j replaced by that of another, randomly chosen, test person (one shuffle πj of the column). ℓ is the loss: misclassification or log-loss. Ij is how much the loss rises. Zero means the model ignores j; it says nothing about direction, and correlated attributes can cover for each other.
Say it“Importance of j is the average loss after shuffling j minus the average loss before.”

Trust · one prediction

Shapley values: sharing out one prediction

A customer who is turned down does not want global importances; they want to know why their score is what it is. Lundberg and Lee’s SHAP (2017) borrows an answer from cooperative game theory. Treat the attributes as players who together move the prediction from the average, E[f], to this person’s f(x). How should the credit be shared? Lloyd Shapley showed in 1953 that exactly one rule is fair in four precise senses (it hands out the whole difference, treats interchangeable players alike, gives nothing to a player who never matters, and adds up across games): average each player’s marginal contribution over every order in which the players could arrive. With 12 attributes there are 212 = 4,096 coalitions, few enough for this page to evaluate them all, exactly. An attribute that is “absent” from a coalition is filled in from 100 background people drawn from the training set. Pick a person, then change their age, education, hours, marital status or sex.

\[ \begin{aligned} \phi_j &= \sum_{S \subseteq F\setminus\{j\}} w_S\,\Delta_j(S) \\ \Delta_j(S) &= v(S\cup\{j\}) - v(S) \\ w_S &= \frac{|S|!\,(M-|S|-1)!}{M!} \\ v(S) &= \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} f\big(x_S,\; z^{(b)}_{\bar S}\big) \\ f(x) &= v(\varnothing) + \sum_{j=1}^{M} \phi_j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: F is the set of M attributes and S a coalition of them. v(S) is the model’s average output when person x keeps their own values on S and takes the values of background person z(b) on the rest (S̄). φj is j’s marginal contribution Δj(S) = v(S ∪ {j}) − v(S), averaged with weights wS that count how many orderings put exactly S before j. The last identity, efficiency, says the φs add up exactly to f(x) minus the average prediction v(∅).
Say it“Phi-j is the weighted sum over coalitions without j of v of S with j minus v of S; and f of x equals v of the empty set plus the sum of the phis.”
average prediction v(∅)
this person, f(x)

Trust · around one prediction

LIME and counterfactuals: looking around one person

Two other ways to explain a single decision look at the model’s neighbourhood instead of sharing credit. LIME (Ribeiro, Singh & Guestrin, 2016) samples points near the person, asks the model about each, and fits a simple linear model that is weighted to be accurate close by: a local tangent to a curved surface. Its answer depends on what “close” means, the kernel width σ. A counterfactual explanation (Wachter, Mittelstadt & Russell, 2018) answers the question people actually ask: what is the smallest change that would have flipped the decision? It only helps if the change is something the person can act on; “be twelve years older” is a true counterfactual and a useless one. The map shows the model’s probability over two attributes, the person’s other ten held fixed. Drag the person, widen the kernel, and switch the map to education × hours.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat g &= \arg\min_{g}\, L(g) \\ L(g) &= \sum_{k} \pi_x(z_k)\big(f(z_k) - g(z_k)\big)^2 \\ \pi_x(z) &= \exp\!\big(-\Vert z - x\Vert^2 / \sigma^2\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: draw K points zk around x, weight each by its closeness πx, and fit the linear g = β0 + β1z1 + β2z2 that matches the model best where the weight is high. Distances are in standard deviations of each attribute. The full method also adds a penalty Ω(g) that keeps g short; with two inputs we do not need it.
Say it“g-hat minimises the closeness-weighted squared gap between the model and a line.”
\[ \begin{aligned} x' &= \arg\min_{x'} \sum_{j} \frac{|x'_j - x_j|}{s_j} \\ &\text{s.t. } \hat y(x') \ne \hat y(x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ŷ(x) = 1 if f(x) ≥ 0.5, else 0. Find the nearest point x′ that gets the other decision, measuring each change in units sj (here the attribute’s standard deviation), so a year of schooling and an hour of work are comparable. Only the two attributes on the map may change; the search tries every whole-number combination.
Say it“x-prime is the closest point, in scaled absolute distance, whose decision differs from x's.”

Fairness · the criteria

Three ways to be fair

A model can be accurate overall and still serve one group worse. Bias enters at every stage: in history (in 1994, 30% of the men in these data earned over $50K against 11% of the women), in collection, in labels, in the choice of attributes. Removing the sensitive attribute does not remove it: marital status and relationship (“Husband”, “Wife”) encode sex almost perfectly, so a model blind to sex can rebuild it. To argue about fairness you first need definitions, and the literature (summarised by Barocas, Hardt & Narayanan) boils most of them down to three statistical criteria for a decision Ŷ, a true outcome Y and a group A. Independence (demographic parity): both groups are flagged at the same rate. Separation (equalised odds; its half on Y = 1 is equal opportunity): among people who really earn over $50K, both groups are flagged at the same rate, and likewise among those who do not. Sufficiency (predictive parity, close to calibration): a flag means the same thing in both groups. Set a threshold per group, or press a button to satisfy one criterion, and watch the others.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{independence:} \\ &\quad P(\hat Y{=}1 \mid a) = P(\hat Y{=}1 \mid b) \\ &\text{separation:} \\ &\quad P(\hat Y{=}1 \mid y, a) = P(\hat Y{=}1 \mid y, b) \\ &\text{sufficiency:} \\ &\quad P(Y{=}1 \mid \hat Y{=}1, a) \\ &\qquad = P(Y{=}1 \mid \hat Y{=}1, b) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a and b are short for A = a and A = b, the two groups. The first criterion compares selection rates; the second compares true-positive rates (y = 1) and false-positive rates (y = 0), so it must hold for both values of y; the third compares precision, the share of flagged people who really earn over $50K (PPV). Each is a different promise: equal shares of the opportunity, equal error rates for people in the same situation, and equally reliable flags.
Say it“Independence: equal flag rates. Separation: equal flag rates given the truth. Sufficiency: equal truth rates given a flag.”

Fairness · the trade-offs

Why you can’t have all three

The buttons above never manage to satisfy everything at once, and this is not a failing of the model. Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan (2017) and Chouldechova (2017) proved that when two groups have different base rates (here 10.9% and 30.0% of women and men earn over $50K), no imperfect classifier can have equal PPV and equal TPR and equal FPR. The identity below, which follows from the definitions alone, shows why: fix PPV and TPR, and the FPR is pinned down by the base rate. So fairness requires a choice, made by people, about which promise matters in this application, and it usually costs some accuracy. Pick a criterion and tighten the allowed gap: the page searches all 9,801 pairs of thresholds for the most accurate one that satisfies it.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{FPR}_a &= \frac{p_a}{1-p_a}\cdot\frac{1-\text{PPV}_a}{\text{PPV}_a} \\ &\quad\cdot\,\text{TPR}_a \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: pa is group a’s base rate, the share who truly earn over $50K. Both sides count the false positives per negative: FP = TP(1 − PPV)/PPV and TP = TPR · pan. If two groups share PPV and TPR but have different p, the first factor differs, so their FPRs must differ. Only a perfect classifier (FPR = 0, PPV = 1) or equal base rates escape.
Say it“The false-positive rate equals the base-rate odds, times one minus PPV over PPV, times the true-positive rate.”

Step 4 · after launch

Operation and monitoring: watching for drift

A model is trained on the past and used in the future, and the world does not hold still. Data drift (covariate shift) is a change in who arrives: the inputs P(x) move, as when a population ages. Prior shift changes how common each label is. Concept drift changes the rule itself, P(y | x): with steady wage inflation, the same job and hours cross a fixed $50,000 line, so last year’s “no” is this year’s “yes”. In operation you log every prediction, compare the distribution of inputs and scores with the one at launch, and watch accuracy once the true labels arrive, which can take months. A common score-drift alarm is the population stability index; by a widely used rule of thumb, PSI < 0.1 is stable and PSI > 0.25 calls for action. Maintenance then means retraining on fresh data, re-validating, and re-running the fairness audit. Below, a year of deployment is simulated by drawing 1,000 people a month from the real test set, re-weighted (or, for concept drift, relabelled) more strongly each month. Choose a kind of drift and see which alarm rings.

\[ \text{PSI} = \sum_{b=1}^{10} (a_b - e_b)\,\ln\frac{a_b}{e_b} \]
Read it as: cut the model’s scores at launch into 10 equal-count bins (deciles), so each expected share eb is 0.1. ab is the share of this month’s scores in bin b. Each term is never negative, and PSI is zero only when the shares match. It is a symmetrised Kullback–Leibler divergence: KL(a‖e) + KL(e‖a). It needs no labels, which is its strength and its blind spot.
Say it“PSI is the sum over bins of actual minus expected share, times the log of actual over expected.”

Transparency

Model cards and datasheets

Trust also needs transparency: the people who use, buy, regulate or are judged by a model should be able to find out what it is, what it was tested on and where it fails. Two short documents have become standard. A datasheet for a dataset (Gebru et al., 2018/2021) answers fixed questions about why the data were collected, what they contain, how they were gathered and cleaned, and what they should and should not be used for. A model card (Mitchell et al., 2019) does the same for a model, and its heart is disaggregated evaluation: every metric reported separately for each relevant group, with its uncertainty, so a good average cannot hide a bad subgroup. Regulation is moving the same way (the EU AI Act requires technical documentation for high-risk systems). The card below is written by the page from the numbers it has computed. Change the grouping and the metric, then download the card.

\[ \frac{\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n} \;\pm\; z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}} \]
Read it as: the Wilson score interval for a rate p̂ = k/n, with z = 1.96 for 95%. Unlike “p̂ ± 1.96√(p̂(1 − p̂)/n)”, it stays inside [0, 1] and behaves sensibly when n is small or p̂ is near 0 or 1, exactly the situation of a small subgroup.
Say it“p-hat plus z squared over 2n, plus or minus z root of p-hat one minus p-hat over n plus z squared over 4 n squared, all over one plus z squared over n.”

Real-world project

Project: auditing an income classifier

The UCI Adult dataset (Becker & Kohavi, 1996; CC BY 4.0) holds 48,842 records from the 1994 US Current Population Survey, already split into 32,561 training and 16,281 test rows. It is the most used benchmark in the fairness literature, which is reason enough to audit a model on it carefully. The whole pipeline runs in your browser: decode the records, build 54 columns (age and hours with their squares, standardised; education as years; log capital gain and loss; one-hot workclass, marital status, occupation, relationship, race, sex and country), fit an L2-penalised logistic regression (C = 1, as scikit-learn) by Newton’s method, then evaluate, audit, explain and document it. import json, math, numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.preprocessing import OneHotEncoder, StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, roc_auc_score, log_loss

cols = ["age", "workclass", "fnlwgt", "education", "edunum", "marital", "occupation", "relationship",
        "race", "sex", "cg", "cl", "hours", "country", "y"]
tr = pd.read_csv("x034_dl/adult.data", names=cols, skipinitialspace=True)
te = pd.read_csv("x034_dl/adult.test", names=cols, skipinitialspace=True, skiprows=1)
for d in (tr, te):
    d["y"] = d["y"].str.startswith(">50K").astype(int)
    d["country"] = d["country"].map(lambda v: "Unknown" if v == "?" else "United-States" if v == "United-States" else "Other")
    for c in ("workclass", "occupation"): d[c] = d[c].replace("?", "Unknown")
    d["cg"], d["cl"], d["age2"], d["hours2"] = np.log1p(d["cg"]), np.log1p(d["cl"]), d.age ** 2, d.hours ** 2
print(f"train {len(tr)}, test {len(te)}; '?' in workclass/occupation/country (train): "
      f"{(tr.workclass == 'Unknown').sum()}/{(tr.occupation == 'Unknown').sum()}/{(tr.country == 'Unknown').sum()}")
for s in ("Male", "Female"):
    print(f"  {s:6s} train n={(tr.sex == s).sum():5d} P(>50K)={tr.y[tr.sex == s].mean():.4f}   test n={(te.sex == s).sum():5d} P(>50K)={te.y[te.sex == s].mean():.4f}")

GROUPS = {"age": ["age", "age2"], "workclass": ["workclass"], "education": ["edunum"], "marital": ["marital"], "occupation": ["occupation"],
          "relationship": ["relationship"], "race": ["race"], "sex": ["sex"], "capital gain": ["cg"], "capital loss": ["cl"],
          "hours": ["hours", "hours2"], "country": ["country"]}
NUM = ["age", "age2", "edunum", "cg", "cl", "hours", "hours2"]

def fit(groups):
    num = [c for g in groups for c in GROUPS[g] if c in NUM]; cat = [c for g in groups for c in GROUPS[g] if c not in NUM]
    sc, oh = StandardScaler().fit(tr[num]), OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore").fit(tr[cat])
    X = lambda d: np.hstack([sc.transform(d[num]), oh.transform(d[cat]).toarray()])
    m = LogisticRegression(C=1.0, solver="newton-cholesky", tol=1e-12, max_iter=200).fit(X(tr), tr.y)
    names = num + [f"{c}={v}" for c, vs in zip(cat, oh.categories_) for v in vs]
    owner = [next(g for g in groups if n.split("=")[0] in GROUPS[g]) for n in names]
    return m, X, names, owner

def rates(y, yh):
    tp, fp = ((y == 1) & (yh == 1)).sum(), ((y == 0) & (yh == 1)).sum()
    return yh.mean(), tp / (y == 1).sum(), fp / (y == 0).sum(), tp / max(yh.sum(), 1)

ALL = list(GROUPS); BLIND = [g for g in ALL if g not in ("sex", "race")]
for tag, groups in (("aware (12 attributes)", ALL), ("blind to sex and race", BLIND)):
    m, X, names, owner = fit(groups); p = m.predict_proba(X(te))[:, 1]; yh = (p >= 0.5).astype(int)
    print(f"\n{tag}: {len(names)} columns, intercept {m.intercept_[0]:.6f}")
    print(f"  test accuracy {accuracy_score(te.y, yh):.4f}  AUC {roc_auc_score(te.y, p):.4f}  log-loss {log_loss(te.y, p):.4f}")
    for col in ("sex", "race"):
        for v in (["Male", "Female"] if col == "sex" else ["White", "Black", "Asian-Pac-Islander", "Amer-Indian-Eskimo", "Other"]):
            k = (te[col] == v).values; sr, tpr, fpr, ppv = rates(te.y.values[k], yh[k])
            print(f"  {v:18s} n={k.sum():5d} selected {sr:.4f}  TPR {tpr:.4f}  FPR {fpr:.4f}  PPV {ppv:.4f}")
    if groups is ALL: keep = (m, X, names, owner, p)

# exact interventional Shapley values of P(>50K), by enumerating all 2^12 coalitions
m, X, names, owner, p = keep
data = json.load(open("x034-data.json")); bg, gl = data["bg"], data["gl"]     # the page's background and test indices
W = m.coef_[0]; b0 = m.intercept_[0]
def contrib(Xm): return np.stack([Xm[:, [i for i, o in enumerate(owner) if o == g]] @ W[[i for i, o in enumerate(owner) if o == g]] for g in ALL], 1)
Hbg, Hte = contrib(X(tr.iloc[bg])), contrib(X(te))
M = len(ALL); masks = (np.arange(2 ** M)[:, None] >> np.arange(M)) & 1
wt = np.array([math.factorial(s) * math.factorial(M - s - 1) / math.factorial(M) for s in range(M)])
def shap(i):
    v = (1 / (1 + np.exp(-(b0 + (masks @ Hte[i])[:, None] + (1 - masks) @ Hbg.T)))).mean(1)
    phi = np.zeros(M)
    for j in range(M):
        S = np.nonzero(masks[:, j] == 0)[0]; phi[j] = (wt[masks[S].sum(1)] * (v[S | (1 << j)] - v[S])).sum()
    return phi, v[0], v[-1]
phi, base, fx = shap(gl[0])
print(f"\nSHAP, test person {gl[0]}: E[f] = {base:.6f}, f(x) = {fx:.6f}, sum(phi) = {phi.sum():.6f}")
print("  " + ", ".join(f"{g} {x:+.4f}" for g, x in zip(ALL, phi)))
G = np.array([np.abs(shap(i)[0]) for i in gl]).mean(0)
print("mean |phi| over 200 test people: " + ", ".join(f"{g} {x:.4f}" for g, x in sorted(zip(ALL, G), key=lambda t: -t[1])))

# permutation importance: one seeded shuffle per attribute (mulberry32, as in the page)
def mulberry32(a):
    def r():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF; t = a
        t = ((t ^ (t >> 15)) * (1 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = (t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return r
logit = b0 + Hte.sum(1); acc0 = ((logit >= 0) == te.y.values).mean(); n = len(te)
out = []
for j, g in enumerate(ALL):
    r, perm = mulberry32(1000 + j), np.arange(n)
    for i in range(n - 1, 0, -1):
        k = int(r() * (i + 1)); perm[i], perm[k] = perm[k], perm[i]
    z = logit - Hte[:, j] + Hte[perm, j]
    out.append((g, acc0 - ((z >= 0) == te.y.values).mean(), log_loss(te.y, 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))) - log_loss(te.y, p)))
print("permutation importance (accuracy drop, log-loss rise): " + ", ".join(f"{g} {a:.4f}/{l:.4f}" for g, a, l in sorted(out, key=lambda t: -t[2])[:6]))

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Formulate first: who decides what, which label stands in for the goal, and what each error costs. The costs, not the model, set the threshold: t* = cFP/(cFP + cFN).
  • Audit the data before modelling: where it came from, what is missing, and how many examples each group has; a rate measured on n people is uncertain by about 1/√n.
  • Explain at two levels: globally (weights, permutation importance) and per person (SHAP shares f(x) − E[f] exactly; LIME fits a local line; counterfactuals give the smallest actionable change).
  • Fairness has several incompatible definitions (independence, separation, sufficiency); with unequal base rates you must choose, and dropping the sensitive attribute does not remove its proxies.
  • Ship with a model card and a datasheet, monitor inputs (PSI) and outcomes after launch, and remember that concept drift is invisible without fresh labels.

Check your understanding

A false negative costs four times as much as a false positive. Where should the threshold go, and what must be true of the model for that to work?

t* = cFP/(cFP + cFN) = 1/(1 + 4) = 0.2. It assumes the model’s scores are calibrated probabilities; if they are not, the threshold should be chosen by measuring cost on validation data instead.

You remove sex and race from the inputs. Why do the gaps between women and men barely change?

Other attributes are strong proxies: relationship (“Husband”, “Wife”), marital status, occupation and hours all correlate with sex, so the model reconstructs most of the same information. “Fairness through unawareness” neither guarantees any fairness criterion nor lets you measure the gaps, which needs the attribute.

The SHAP values of a person add up to 0.31. What is that number?

Their prediction minus the average prediction over the background data: f(x) − v(∅). This exact additivity (efficiency) is one of the four properties that make the Shapley value unique.

Two groups have base rates of 11% and 30%. Can a classifier have equal PPV, equal TPR and equal FPR in both?

Not unless it is perfect. From FPR = p/(1 − p) · (1 − PPV)/PPV · TPR, equal PPV and TPR force the FPRs to differ by the ratio of the base-rate odds (about 3.5 here).

The score PSI stays below 0.1 all year but accuracy falls from 85% to 75%. What is happening, and how would you have caught it?

Concept drift: P(y | x) has changed while the inputs, and therefore the scores, have not. Only labelled outcomes reveal it, so the monitoring plan must collect ground truth (even with a delay) and track accuracy and calibration, not just input statistics.
