Learning Bayesian Networks

Hidden variables and structure: counts, EM and search

A Bayesian network has two things to learn: the numbers in its tables and the arrows between its variables. When every variable is observed, the numbers are just counts. When one is hidden, the counts have to be estimated, and EM does that. The arrows are found by search: score a graph by how well it explains the data, minus a price for every parameter, then change one arrow at a time and keep the best change. The figure runs that search on 48,842 records from the 1994 US census, eight columns, starting with no arrows at all, and plays through once by itself. Each move is the single arrow change that raises the score the most. Click the graph to step through the moves yourself.

AIMA 20.2.1 & 20.2.5 · recap

Complete data: learning is counting

Start where the earlier chapters left off: the graph is known and every variable is observed in every row. Learning the parameters is then the easiest problem in this chapter. The probability of the data is a product over rows, and each row’s probability is a product of one entry from each node’s table. Regroup those factors and the log-likelihood falls apart into separate pieces, one for every node and every configuration of its parents. Each piece is the log-likelihood of an ordinary categorical distribution, and its maximum is at the observed frequencies. So the maximum-likelihood (ML) estimate of every table entry is a ratio of two counts.

Two warnings come with it. A parent configuration that never occurs in the data leaves its whole row undefined (0/0). And a value never seen in a row gets probability 0, so any new case that contains it becomes impossible. The Bayesian estimate fixes both: put a Dirichlet prior (a Beta prior, for two values) on every row. The posterior is Dirichlet again, and its mean simply adds a pseudo-count α to every cell before normalising. α = 1 is Laplace’s rule. The network here is the familiar sprinkler: Cloudy → Sprinkler, Cloudy → Rain, and both of those → Wet grass. Shrink the sample to 20 rows and compare plain counting with a pseudo-count.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\log P(\mathcal D \mid \boldsymbol\theta) = \sum_{i,j,k} N_{ijk} \log \theta_{ijk} \\ &\hat\theta_{ijk} = \frac{N_{ijk}}{N_{ij}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: i runs over nodes, j over the configurations of node i’s parents, and k over its values. Nijk counts the rows in which node i has value k while its parents are in configuration j, and Nij = ∑k Nijk. Each (i, j) row of the sum can be maximised on its own, and the answer is a frequency.
Say it“The log-likelihood is a sum of count times log-probability over nodes, parent settings and values; the best theta is the count over the row total.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol\theta_{ij} &\sim \mathrm{Dir}(\alpha, \dots, \alpha) \\ \mathbb E[\theta_{ijk} \mid \mathcal D] &= \frac{N_{ijk} + \alpha}{N_{ij} + r_i\,\alpha} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ri is the number of values of node i. A symmetric Dirichlet prior acts like α imaginary rows in every cell. With few real rows the estimate is pulled toward uniform; with many, the counts swamp the prior and the Bayesian and ML answers agree.
Say it“Add alpha to every count and r-alpha to the row total.”

AIMA 20.3.2

A hidden bag: parameters by EM

Now hide one variable. Two bags of sweets are tipped into one bowl. Every sweet has a flavour (cherry or lime), a wrapper (red or green), and either has a hole or not. The mix of these differs between the bags, but nobody wrote down which bag a sweet came from. The network is Bag → Flavour, Bag → Wrapper, Bag → Hole, with Bag never observed: a naive Bayes model whose class is hidden. It has seven parameters: θ = P(Bag = 1) and, for each bag, the chance of cherry, of a red wrapper and of a hole. (AIMA uses the same set-up; the numbers here are new.)

If we knew the bags, we would count. We don’t, so expectation–maximisation (EM) counts in expectation. The E-step uses the current parameters to work out, for each kind of sweet, the probability γ that it came from bag 1 (written γn for the n-th sweet), and so splits every sweet fractionally between the bags. The M-step re-estimates every parameter from those expected counts exactly as the previous section did from real ones. With three yes/no features there are only eight kinds of sweet, so the whole E-step is the eight rows of the table. Step EM one iteration at a time, then try the start where both bags begin identical.

\[ \begin{aligned} a(\mathbf x) &= \theta \textstyle\prod_m P_1(x_m) \\ b(\mathbf x) &= (1-\theta) \textstyle\prod_m P_2(x_m) \\ \gamma(\mathbf x) &= \frac{a(\mathbf x)}{a(\mathbf x) + b(\mathbf x)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the E-step. For a sweet with features x = (flavour, wrapper, hole), multiply bag 1’s prior by bag 1’s chance of each feature, do the same for bag 2, and normalise. This is Bayes’ rule with the current parameters, and the answer is that sweet’s share of bag 1.
Say it“Gamma of x is bag one’s prior times its feature probabilities, over the same for both bags.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \theta &\leftarrow \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n} \gamma_n \\ P_1(\text{cherry}) &\leftarrow \frac{\sum_n \gamma_n\,[F_n = \text{cherry}]}{\sum_n \gamma_n} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the M-step. The expected number of sweets from bag 1 is the sum of the γs; the expected number of cherry sweets from bag 1 is the sum over the cherry ones. Each parameter is a ratio of expected counts, exactly the counting rule with fractional rows. Bag 2’s parameters use 1 − γ instead.
Say it“Theta becomes the average gamma; bag one’s cherry rate becomes its expected cherries over its expected sweets.”
\[ \begin{aligned} Q(\boldsymbol\theta) &= \sum_n \sum_{b} P(b \mid \mathbf x_n, \boldsymbol\theta^{(t)}) \\ &\qquad \times \log P(\mathbf x_n, b \mid \boldsymbol\theta) \\ \boldsymbol\theta^{(t+1)} &= \arg\max_{\boldsymbol\theta} Q(\boldsymbol\theta) \\ \Rightarrow\; \ell(\boldsymbol\theta^{(t+1)}) &\ge \ell(\boldsymbol\theta^{(t)}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the M-step maximises the expected complete-data log-likelihood Q, where the expectation is over the hidden bag under the old parameters. Raising Q by some amount raises the true log-likelihood by at least as much (the gap between them is a KL divergence that is zero at the old parameters), so EM never goes downhill (ℓ is the log-likelihood of the data). It can stop at a local maximum or a saddle point, never guaranteed at the global one.
Say it“Q is the expected complete-data log-likelihood; maximising it can only raise the log-likelihood of the data.”

AIMA 20.2.7 · part 1 · scoring a structure

Why likelihood loves edges

Now the graph itself is unknown. The obvious plan is to fit each candidate graph’s parameters by counting and keep the graph under which the data are most probable. That plan fails, and the reason is worth seeing. The maximised log-likelihood of a network equals −N times the sum, over nodes, of the empirical entropy of each node given its parents. Conditioning never increases entropy, so adding a parent never lowers the maximised likelihood, and through sampling noise alone it nearly always raises it a little. Maximum likelihood therefore always ends at a complete graph, every pair of variables joined, which claims no independence at all and has the most parameters.

A usable score has to trade fit against size. The Bayesian information criterion (BIC; the same as minimum description length, up to sign) subtracts ½ log N for every free parameter. The Bayesian score instead integrates the parameters out under a Dirichlet prior: the marginal likelihood, here in its BDeu form, where an equivalent sample size α is spread evenly over each family’s cells. Both scores are decomposable (a sum of one term per node and its parents, which is what will make search cheap) and score-equivalent: graphs that encode the same independencies, such as A → B and B → A, get the same score. Click the small circles on the edges to edit the graph, and change N to see which score picks which graph.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{\boldsymbol\theta} \log P(\mathcal D \mid \boldsymbol\theta, G) \\ &\quad = \sum_{i,j,k} N_{ijk} \log \frac{N_{ijk}}{N_{ij}} \\ &\quad = -N \sum_i \hat H(X_i \mid \mathrm{Pa}_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: plug the counting estimates back in, and each family contributes N times minus the conditional entropy of the node given its parents, measured on the data. Adding a parent can only lower (or keep) that entropy, so this number only goes up as edges are added.
Say it“The best log-likelihood is minus N times the sum of each node’s entropy given its parents.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{BIC}(G) &= \log P(\mathcal D \mid \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}, G) \\ &\quad - \tfrac{1}{2} \log N \cdot \dim G \\ \dim G &= \textstyle\sum_i q_i\,(r_i - 1) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: qi is the number of parent configurations of node i and ri its number of values, so dim G counts the free parameters. Each one must earn ½ log N nats of likelihood to be worth having; that bar rises slowly with N, so bigger data supports bigger graphs.
Say it“BIC is the best log-likelihood minus half log N per free parameter.”
\[ \begin{aligned} P(\mathcal D \mid G) &= \prod_{i,j} \Big[ \frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{ij})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{ij} + N_{ij})} \\ &\qquad \times \prod_k \frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{ijk} + N_{ijk})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{ijk})} \Big] \\ \alpha_{ijk} &= \frac{\alpha}{q_i r_i}, \quad \alpha_{ij} = \frac{\alpha}{q_i} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the probability of the data averaged over all parameter values under a Dirichlet prior, which has this closed form because each table row is a Dirichlet–multinomial. Nothing is fitted, so nothing can overfit: a graph with many parameters spreads its prior thin and loses unless the data need it. BDeu uses the same α = 1 in every family.
Say it“The marginal likelihood is a product of gamma-function ratios, one per table row and cell.”

AIMA 20.2.7 · part 2 · searching for a structure

Hill climbing through the space of graphs

How many graphs are there to score? For n labelled variables the number of DAGs follows Robinson’s recurrence: 25 for three variables, 29,281 for five, about 7.8 × 1011 for eight and more than 1018 for ten. Exhaustive search stops being possible after a handful of nodes, and finding the best-scoring network is NP-hard in general (Chickering, 1996). So the search is local. Hill climbing starts from some graph, often the empty one, looks at every legal single change (add an arrow, delete one, reverse one, never creating a cycle) and makes the change that raises the score most. It stops when no change helps.

Decomposability makes each step cheap: a move changes the parent set of one node (two, for a reversal), so its effect on the score is the change in one or two family terms, and most terms can be cached from step to step. AIMA also describes a simpler scheme: fix an ordering of the variables first and let each variable choose its parents only among earlier ones, which removes the need to check for cycles (the K2 algorithm of Cooper and Herskovits works this way). Hill climbing can stop at a local maximum, so practical systems add random restarts or a tabu list. The data here come from a small student network with made-up tables: Difficulty and Intelligence → Grade, Intelligence → SAT, Grade → Letter. Step the search one move at a time, then shrink the data or switch to the log-likelihood score.

\[ \begin{aligned} a_n &= \sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k+1} \binom{n}{k} \\ &\qquad \times 2^{k(n-k)}\, a_{n-k}, \quad a_0 = 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: count DAGs by their k source nodes (nodes with no parents). Choose them, let each send or not send an arrow to each of the other n − k nodes, and fill in any DAG on the rest; the alternating signs correct for counting a graph once for every set of its sources.
Say it“a-n is an alternating sum over k of n-choose-k, times two to the k times n-minus-k, times a of n-minus-k.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta_{\text{add } a \to b} &= s(X_b, \mathrm{Pa}_b \cup \{X_a\}) \\ &\qquad - s(X_b, \mathrm{Pa}_b) \\ \Delta_{\text{rev } a \to b} &= \Delta_{\text{del } a \to b} + \Delta_{\text{add } b \to a} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: s(X, Pa) is one family’s term in a decomposable score. Adding or deleting an arrow changes only the head’s family; a reversal changes two. Every other term stays put, so a search step costs a few family scores, not a whole new fit.
Say it“The gain from adding a to b is b’s family score with a minus without it.”

PRML 8.4.8 · learning the graph structure

A posterior over graphs

Bishop puts structure learning in Bayesian terms. Treat each graph m as a model, give it a prior p(m), and rank graphs by their posterior, which is the prior times the marginal likelihood p(D|m). The marginal likelihood averages the likelihood over the parameters instead of maximising it, so it penalises complexity by itself: a graph with many parameters spreads its prior over many possible data sets and gives each one only a little probability (the Bayesian Occam’s razor met in the model-evidence chapter). Bishop names the two obstacles. The marginal likelihood needs an integral over the parameters (for discrete networks with Dirichlet priors it is the closed-form BDeu product of the previous section), and the number of structures grows faster than exponentially, so the posterior can be summed only for tiny problems; anything bigger needs heuristic search, or Monte Carlo sampling over graphs.

With three variables it can be summed: there are exactly 25 DAGs. They fall into 11 Markov equivalence classes, sets of graphs that claim the same independencies. A → B → C, A ← B ← C and A ← B → C all say only that A and C are independent given B, and BDeu gives them identical marginal likelihoods, so the data split their posterior evenly. The collider A → B ← C says something different (A and C independent until B is known) and is a class of its own. Instead of choosing one graph you can also average: the posterior probability of a feature, such as “A and C are directly joined”, is the total posterior of the graphs that have it. Switch the generating structure and grow N.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(m \mid \mathcal D) &\propto p(m)\, p(\mathcal D \mid m) \\ p(\mathcal D \mid m) &= \int p(\mathcal D \mid \boldsymbol\theta, m) \\ &\qquad \times p(\boldsymbol\theta \mid m)\, d\boldsymbol\theta \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the posterior of a graph is its prior times the evidence, where the evidence averages the likelihood over that graph’s parameter prior. Here the prior over the 25 graphs is uniform, so the posterior is the evidence, normalised.
Say it“The posterior of m is proportional to its prior times its evidence, the likelihood integrated over theta.”
\[ P(f \mid \mathcal D) = \sum_{m} f(m)\, p(m \mid \mathcal D) \]
Read it as: f(m) is 1 if graph m has the feature (an edge between A and C, say) and 0 otherwise. Averaging over graphs gives the probability of the feature without betting on any single structure; with little data that is far more honest than reporting the top graph.
Say it“The probability of a feature is the total posterior of the graphs that have it.”

AIMA 20.3.5

Structure with hidden variables

Hidden variables matter for structure in two ways. First, they can make a network much smaller. Suppose a hidden cause H sits between some observed causes and several observed effects. Leaving H out of the model does not remove its influence: every effect now depends on every cause and on every other effect, and the network that represents the same distribution without H needs arrows between all of them. AIMA makes the point with a hidden heart-disease node in a medical network. Change the numbers of causes and effects in the first figure to see how fast the gap grows.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{with } H&: \; p + 2^{p}(s-1) + c\,s \\ \text{without } H&: \; p + \sum_{m=0}^{c-1} 2^{\,p+m} \\ &= p + 2^{p}\,(2^{c}-1) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: p binary causes and c binary effects. With H (s states), each cause has one parameter, H has s − 1 per configuration of the causes, and each effect has one per state of H. Without H, the m-th effect must have all p causes and the m earlier effects as parents, which costs 2p+m parameters. Both are dim G = ∑i qi(ri − 1).
Say it“With the hidden node the count is linear in the effects; without it, it doubles with every effect.”
parameters with H
parameters without H

Second, learning such a structure means deciding whether a hidden variable exists, how many values it has and where it connects, with nothing observed to count for it. The direct approach scores every candidate structure with parameters fitted by EM, which means a full EM run for every move of the search. Structural EM (Friedman, 1998) is the shortcut AIMA describes: take one E-step under the current model, then treat the expected counts as if they were complete data and run ordinary score-based search on them, cheaply, because the expected counts make the score decomposable again. Refit the parameters of the new structure by EM and repeat. Friedman showed that each round cannot lower the real score.

The data here are 4,000 rows of five yes/no variables generated from a hidden H with two values and one direct arrow, X4 → X5, that H does not explain (made-up tables). The number of states of H is a structural choice too: BIC with k states compares models with different numbers of parameters. Fit H with k = 2 by EM, then run structural EM, and compare the BIC curves for k = 1–5. Structural EM is a local search too, so try other k: it can repair a wrong number of states with arrows that are not in the truth.

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar N_{ijk} &= \sum_{n} P(X_i = k, \mathrm{Pa}_i = j \\ &\qquad\qquad \mid \mathbf x_n, G^{(t)}, \boldsymbol\theta^{(t)}) \\ G^{(t+1)} &= \arg\max_{G} \textstyle\sum_i s\big(X_i, \mathrm{Pa}_i^{G};\, \bar N\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the expected counts N̅ijk add up, over rows, the posterior probability that node i and its parents take values (k, j); for families that include H they are fractional. The structure step scores the neighbouring graphs G of G(t) (one arrow added or deleted) with these counts in place of real ones (here BIC), exactly as hill climbing did, and keeps the best.

Deep Learning 16.5 · learning about dependencies

Latent variables instead of edges

Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville explain why deep generative models rarely search over graphs. The traditional route models dependent visible variables with direct edges between them; with many variables that means many edges, and a search in which every step fits and evaluates a model. Deep models take the other route. They fix a simple, generic structure, usually with every hidden unit connected to many or all visible units, and add latent variables h. Visible variables that are independent given h can still be strongly dependent once h is summed out, so the latent variables capture the dependencies indirectly, and fitting their parameters takes the place of structure search. As a bonus, the posterior over h is a learned description of each input, which is the representation-learning idea of chapter 15.

The smallest example of the idea is a mixture of Bernoullis: one hidden h with K values, and 16 binary pixels that are independent given h. It is trained by EM here on 4 × 4 “bars” images (each of the 8 rows and columns is switched on with probability 0.2, then 4% of pixels are flipped) and compared with two models that have no latent variables: independent pixels, and the best tree of direct edges between pixels (Chow–Liu, see the map below). Restricted Boltzmann machines and VAEs use many latent units instead of one categorical h, but the principle is the same. Raise K and watch the learned prototypes turn into bars.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf v) &= \sum_{h=1}^{K} \pi_h \prod_{d=1}^{16} \\ &\qquad \mu_{hd}^{\,v_d} (1-\mu_{hd})^{1-v_d} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: pick a hidden component h with probability πh, then switch each pixel d on independently with probability μhd. Inside one component the pixels ignore each other; the dependence lives in the choice of h.
Say it“p of v is a pi-weighted sum over components of independent Bernoulli pixels.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathrm{Cov}(v_a, v_b) \\ &\quad = \sum_h \pi_h\,(\mu_{ha} - \bar\mu_a)(\mu_{hb} - \bar\mu_b) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: μ̅a = ∑h πhμha is the overall rate of pixel a. Two pixels covary in the mixture whenever the components that switch one on tend to switch the other on too, even though no edge joins them.
Say it“The covariance of two pixels is the pi-weighted sum of the products of their deviations from the mean.”

PRML 8.4.8 · AIMA 20.2.7 · learning the graph in general

The structure-learning map

Score-and-search is one family of methods. AIMA mentions the main alternative in a sentence: instead of scoring whole graphs, test the conditional independences the graph would imply, and build a graph that matches the tests. That is the constraint-based family; its best-known member, the PC algorithm, starts from a complete undirected graph, deletes the edge between X and Y as soon as some set of neighbours Z makes them look independent in the data, then points arrows into every collider X → Z ← Y whose middle node was not in the separating set, and propagates the arrows that cycles and new colliders would otherwise contradict. Its answer is an equivalence class (a partly directed graph), because that is all independence tests can see.

A third family restricts the graph so that the best one can be found exactly. Chow and Liu (1968) showed that the best tree, the one maximising the likelihood, is the maximum-weight spanning tree when each pair of variables is weighted by its empirical mutual information, so it takes one pass over the pairs and no search. Grow N and change the test threshold to compare the three on the student data.

\[ \begin{aligned} G^2 &= 2 \sum_{x,y,z} N_{xyz} \log \frac{N_{xyz}\, N_{z}}{N_{xz}\, N_{yz}} \\ &\sim \chi^2_{(r_X-1)(r_Y-1)\,q_Z} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the likelihood-ratio test of X ⫫ Y | Z: compare the counts with what independence within each setting z would predict. If X and Y are independent given Z, it follows a chi-squared distribution with the degrees of freedom shown, approximately; PC keeps the edge only if the p-value is below the threshold.
Say it“G-squared is twice the sum of N log observed over expected, chi-squared under independence.”
\[ T^\ast = \arg\max_{T \text{ a tree}} \sum_{(i,j) \in T} \hat I(X_i; X_j) \]
Read it as: for a tree, the maximised log-likelihood is a constant plus N times the sum of the mutual informations along its edges, so the best tree is a maximum spanning tree. Prim’s or Kruskal’s algorithm finds it exactly in polynomial time.
Say it“The best tree maximises the sum of mutual informations over its edges.”

Two cautions apply to every method on the map. Without experiments, the data can identify at most an equivalence class: an arrow that can be flipped without changing the independencies is not supported by the data in either direction, whatever a learned DAG shows. And every method assumes the data are faithful to some graph over the variables you measured; a hidden common cause that the model does not include will show up as a spurious edge between its effects, which is exactly the situation the hidden-variable methods above try to repair.

def mulberry32(a):                       # the page's random-number generator
    st = [a & M32]
    def r():
        st[0] = (st[0] + 0x6D2B79F5) & M32; a = st[0]
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & M32
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & M32)) & M32) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M32) / 4294967296

def decode(codes, K):
    X = np.zeros((len(codes), len(K)), dtype=int); c = np.array(codes)
    for j in range(len(K) - 1, -1, -1): X[:, j] = c % K[j]; c = c // K[j]
    return X
dec64 = lambda s, K: decode([B64.index(ch) for ch in s], K)
def table(X, K):
    code = np.zeros(len(X), dtype=np.int64)
    for j in range(len(K)): code = code * K[j] + X[:, j]
    return np.bincount(code, minlength=int(np.prod(K))).reshape(K).astype(float)
def fam(T, i, pa):                       # counts N_ijk as a (q x r) array
    keep = sorted(set(pa) | {i}); other = tuple(a for a in range(T.ndim) if a not in keep)
    M = T.sum(axis=other) if other else T
    M = np.moveaxis(M, [keep.index(p) for p in pa] + [keep.index(i)], list(range(len(pa) + 1)))
    return M.reshape(-1, T.shape[i])
def score(T, i, pa, typ="bic", ess=1.0):
    M = fam(T, i, pa); N = T.sum(); q, r = M.shape; Nj = M.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    ll = float(np.sum(M[M > 0] * np.log((M / np.where(Nj > 0, Nj, 1))[M > 0]))); k = q * (r - 1)
    if typ == "ll": return ll
    if typ == "bic": return ll - 0.5 * math.log(N) * k
    if typ == "aic": return ll - k
    a, b = ess / q, ess / (q * r)       # BDeu
    return float(np.sum((gammaln(a) - gammaln(a + Nj))[Nj > 0]) + np.sum((gammaln(b + M) - gammaln(b))[M > 0]))
total = lambda T, par, typ="bic": sum(score(T, i, p, typ) for i, p in enumerate(par))
def path(par, s, t):                     # directed path s ~> t ?
    st, seen = [s], set()
    while st:
        v = st.pop()
        if v == t: return True
        for c in range(len(par)):
            if c not in seen and v in par[c]: seen.add(c); st.append(c)
    return False
def hill(T, typ="bic", maxpa=3, forbid=None, start=None):
    n = T.ndim; par = [list(p) for p in start] if start else [[] for _ in range(n)]; cache = {}
    def sc(i, pa):
        k = (i, tuple(sorted(pa)))
        if k not in cache: cache[k] = score(T, i, pa, typ)
        return cache[k]
    forbid = forbid or (lambda i, j: False); moves = []
    while True:
        best = None
        for i in range(n):
            for j in range(n):
                if i == j: continue
                cand = []
                if i in par[j]:
                    pj = [p for p in par[j] if p != i]; cand.append(("del", sc(j, pj) - sc(j, par[j])))
                    if not forbid(j, i) and len(par[i]) < maxpa:
                        P2 = [list(p) for p in par]; P2[j] = pj
                        if not path(P2, i, j): cand.append(("rev", sc(j, pj) - sc(j, par[j]) + sc(i, par[i] + [j]) - sc(i, par[i])))
                elif j not in par[i] and not forbid(i, j) and len(par[j]) < maxpa and not path(par, j, i):
                    cand.append(("add", sc(j, par[j] + [i]) - sc(j, par[j])))
                for m, d in cand:
                    if d > 1e-7 and (best is None or d > best[0] + 1e-7): best = (d, m, i, j)
        if best is None: return par, moves
        d, m, i, j = best; moves.append((m, i, j, d))
        if m == "add": par[j].append(i)
        elif m == "del": par[j].remove(i)
        else: par[j].remove(i); par[i].append(j)

Where the page and the reference differ. Nowhere that matters: the scores, the learned arrows, the sketch scores and the held-out log-likelihoods agree to the printed digits, and so do the lab values at their defaults. The page computes BDeu with its own Lanczos log-gamma (agreeing with scipy to about 13 significant digits) and its chi-squared tail with a continued fraction; numpy sums in a different order, so the last digits of a long sum can differ, which never changes a decision of the search because ties within 10−7 go to the first move tried. What the project does not show. The learned arrows are statistical, not causal: the table marks which directions the data fix and which are reversible, and even a fixed direction assumes there is no hidden common cause outside the eight columns. The columns are coarsened on purpose (fewer categories make the counts reliable), and a different coarsening gives a somewhat different graph. The three-parent limit binds for a few nodes, so the log-likelihood and AIC graphs are capped, not complete. Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • With complete data, a network’s log-likelihood splits into one piece per node and parent setting, so its parameters are conditional frequencies; Dirichlet pseudo-counts handle the zeros.
  • With a hidden variable, EM alternates expected counts (E-step) with counting on them (M-step); the likelihood never falls, but EM can stop at a local optimum or a saddle, and the hidden labels are only defined up to a swap.
  • Maximum likelihood always prefers more edges; BIC (½ log N per parameter) and the Bayesian marginal likelihood (BDeu) trade fit against size, and both are decomposable and score-equivalent.
  • The space of DAGs grows super-exponentially, so structure is found by local search over single-arrow changes, or by CI tests (PC), exact trees (Chow–Liu) or averaging over graphs; data alone fix only the equivalence class.
  • Hidden variables can make a network far smaller; structural EM searches structures on expected counts, and deep generative models go further, fixing a generic structure and letting latent variables carry the dependencies.

Check your understanding

Why can’t you choose a network structure by maximum likelihood alone?

Because the maximised log-likelihood is −N times the sum of each node’s conditional entropy given its parents, and conditioning on more variables never raises entropy. Adding any arrow therefore never lowers the score, and sampling noise makes it rise a little, so the winner is always a complete graph with no independencies, which overfits.

In the sweets example, EM started with both bags identical never moves. Why?

If the two bags have the same parameters, every sweet is equally likely under both, so γ equals θ for every sweet. The M-step then gives both bags the same overall frequencies again. That point is a stationary point of the likelihood (a saddle, not a maximum), and EM, which only follows its expected counts, cannot break the symmetry by itself.

Suppose hill climbing on the student data returned SAT → Intelligence instead of Intelligence → SAT. Would the search be wrong?

No. The two graphs are Markov equivalent (they claim the same independencies, and reversing that arrow creates no new collider), and BIC and BDeu are score-equivalent, so they score identically. The data cannot choose the direction; which one appears depends on the order in which the search tried the moves.

How does a hidden variable reduce the number of parameters? Give the count for 2 binary causes, 4 binary effects and a binary H.

With H, every effect needs only H as a parent: 2 + 22(2 − 1) + 4 × 2 = 14 parameters. Without it, the effects depend on the causes and on each other, so the m-th effect needs all causes and earlier effects as parents: 2 + 4 × (24 − 1) = 62.

What does structural EM gain over running EM for every candidate structure?

One E-step under the current model gives expected counts that act like complete data; with them the score is decomposable again, so many candidate structures can be compared by cheap family scores instead of a separate EM run each. Only the chosen structure is refitted by EM before the next round.
