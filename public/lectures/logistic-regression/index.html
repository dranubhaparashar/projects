Logistic Regression

A straight line, bent into a probability

Will this student pass? Is this email spam? Did this passenger survive? When the answer is yes or no, we want a probability between 0 and 1. Logistic regression takes the straight line from the last chapter and squashes it through an S-shaped curve. It's the most widely used classifier in medicine, finance and the social sciences, and it's the single neuron at the heart of every neural network.

Foundations

Yes or no needs a new line

Why not just use linear regression on 0/1 labels? Try it. Below, the dashed line is ordinary least squares fitted to pass (1) and fail (0); we classify as “pass” wherever it rises above 0.5. It sort of works — until three very hard-working students arrive. They passed, as expected, so they should change nothing. Watch what they do to each model's cut-off.

Two problems, one fix

  • Impossible outputsA straight line runs off to ±∞, so it predicts “probabilities” like −0.3 or 1.4. We need something squeezed into (0, 1).
  • Wrong pullSquared error punishes a confident, correct prediction far from the line (the hard workers), dragging the boundary to the wrong place. We need a loss that only cares about being right.
  • The fixKeep the line, z = w₀ + w₁x, but pass it through the sigmoid σ(z), and train it with log-loss. That is logistic regression.
The model

The S-curve that squashes

\[ \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \qquad\qquad p(x) = P(y = 1 \mid x) = \sigma(w_0 + w_1 x) \]
Read it as: any number z goes in; a number between 0 and 1 comes out. Big positive z → close to 1; big negative z → close to 0; z = 0 → exactly 0.5. The model first computes a straight line z, then squashes it.
Say it“Sigma of z is one over one plus e to the minus z. The probability of y equals one is sigma of w-zero plus w-one x.”
  • w₀ slides itChanging the intercept moves the whole S left or right.
  • w₁ sharpens itA bigger slope makes the S steeper: the model switches from “fail” to “pass” over fewer hours. w₁ < 0 flips it, so more x means less likely.
  • The 50% pointp = 0.5 exactly where z = 0, i.e. at x = −w₀ / w₁. That point is the decision boundary.
Interpretation

Odds: the hidden straight line

Betting shops don't say “80% chance”; they say “4 to 1”. Those are odds: p / (1 − p). Take their logarithm and you get the log-odds, which runs from −∞ to +∞. Here's the secret of logistic regression: the log-odds is exactly the straight line z. Drag the probability and watch the three scales move together.

\[ \text{odds} = \frac{p}{1-p}, \qquad \ln\frac{p}{1-p} = w_0 + w_1 x \]
Read it as: the sigmoid's inverse is the log-odds (also called the logit). So logistic regression is linear regression on the log-odds scale: each extra unit of x adds w₁ to the log-odds, which multiplies the odds by ew₁.
Say it“The log of p over one minus p equals w-zero plus w-one x.”

The odds ratio: what a weight means

\[ \frac{\text{odds}(x+1)}{\text{odds}(x)} = e^{w_1} \]
Read it as: the odds ratio. One more unit of x multiplies the odds by ew₁, whatever x was. This is how doctors read logistic models: “smokers have 2.3× the odds of…”
Say it“The odds ratio for one unit of x is e to the w-one.”
The objective

Punishing confident mistakes

How do we score a probability? If a student passed (y = 1) and we said p = 0.9, that's good; p = 0.01 is a disaster. Log-loss charges −ln p when the answer was 1 and −ln(1 − p) when it was 0. Being confidently wrong costs almost infinitely much; squared error would cap the cost at 1. Slide the prediction:

\[ \mathcal{L}(w) = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\, y_i \ln p_i + (1 - y_i)\ln(1 - p_i) \,\Big], \qquad p_i = \sigma(w_0 + w_1x_i) \]
Read it as: for each example, only one of the two terms is alive. If yᵢ = 1 we pay −ln pᵢ; if yᵢ = 0 we pay −ln(1 − pᵢ). Average over everyone. Also called binary cross-entropy.
Say it“The loss is minus the average of y log p plus one-minus-y log one-minus-p.”
Where does log-loss come from? (maximum likelihood, in four lines)

1. The model says example i has label 1 with probability pᵢ, so the probability it assigns to the label we actually saw is pᵢyᵢ(1 − pᵢ)1−yᵢ (this picks pᵢ when yᵢ = 1 and 1 − pᵢ when yᵢ = 0).

2. Assuming examples are independent, the probability of the whole dataset (the likelihood) is the product of these over all i.

3. Products of many small numbers underflow, and are hard to differentiate. Taking the log turns the product into a sum.

4. Maximising the log-likelihood = minimising its negative average = minimising log-loss. So “minimise log-loss” and “choose the weights that make the observed labels most probable” are the same instruction.

Training

Downhill on the log-loss bowl

Unlike linear regression, there is no closed-form answer: setting the derivative to zero gives equations with σ inside that can't be solved by hand. So we roll downhill. And the gradient turns out to be beautifully simple — the same shape as linear regression's, with p in place of ŷ.

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_j} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (p_i - y_i)\, x_{ij} \qquad\qquad w_j \leftarrow w_j - \eta\,\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_j} \]
Read it as: (pᵢ − yᵢ) is the prediction error for example i: positive if we said too high, negative if too low. Each weight moves against the error, scaled by its feature (xᵢ₀ = 1 for the intercept).
Say it“The gradient for weight j is the average of prediction-minus-label, times feature j.”
Why is the gradient so simple? (the sigmoid's derivative cancels)

The sigmoid has a lovely derivative: σ′(z) = σ(z)(1 − σ(z)) = p(1 − p).

For one example, ∂/∂z of −[y ln p + (1−y) ln(1−p)] = −[y/p − (1−y)/(1−p)] · p(1−p) = −[y(1−p) − (1−y)p] = p − y.

Then by the chain rule ∂z/∂wⱼ = xⱼ, so ∂loss/∂wⱼ = (p − y) xⱼ. The p(1−p) from the sigmoid exactly cancels the 1/p and 1/(1−p) from the logs. That cancellation is why sigmoid and log-loss are always used together.

Two features

A straight cut between classes

With two features, z = w₀ + w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ is a tilted plane, and the 50% boundary z = 0 is a straight line. The colour shows the probability, fading to white at 50%; the dashed lines are the 10% and 90% contours. Click to add points of the chosen class and watch the model refit instantly.

\[ P(y=1 \mid \mathbf{x}) = \sigma(w_0 + w_1x_1 + w_2x_2), \qquad \text{boundary: } w_0 + w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 = 0 \]
Read it as: the boundary is where the log-odds are zero. (w₁, w₂) points across the boundary, towards class 1; its length sets how sharp the transition is.
Say it“The decision boundary is the line where w-zero plus w-one x-one plus w-two x-two equals zero.”

Many classes

Softmax: more than two answers

Cat, dog or bird? Give each class its own line zₖ = wₖ·x — a “score”, or logit — then turn the scores into probabilities that add up to 1 with the softmax. With two classes, softmax is exactly the sigmoid again. This is also the very last layer of every language model: it turns scores for 100,000 possible next words into probabilities.

\[ P(y = k \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j}}, \qquad z_k = \mathbf{w}_k^\top \mathbf{x} \]
Read it as: exponentiate every score (so all are positive and bigger scores dominate), then divide by their total (so they add to 1). Trained with the same cross-entropy: −ln of the probability given to the true class.
Say it“The probability of class k is e to its score, over the sum of e to every score.”

At the probe

Real-world project

Project: who survived the Titanic?

On 15 April 1912 the RMS Titanic sank on her maiden voyage. The passenger list survives: 1,308 people with their ticket class, sex, age, family aboard and fare — and whether they lived. 500 did. Was it luck, or did “women and children first” and the class of your ticket decide? We fit logistic regression on 981 passengers and test it on 327 it has never seen.

A deliberate omission: the dataset also has boat (lifeboat number) and body (body identification number). Both were recorded after the sinking and would give the answer away. Using them would be a data leak — a model that is brilliant in the lab and useless in reality.

Choosing the threshold

The model outputs a probability; to say “survived” or “died” we must pick a cut-off. 50% is only a convention. Lower it and we catch more survivors but raise more false alarms. Every cut-off is one point on the ROC curve; the area under it (AUC) is the chance that a random survivor gets a higher score than a random victim.

The same project in scikit-learn

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import fetch_openml
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, roc_auc_score

df = fetch_openml("titanic", version=1, as_frame=True).frame
df = df[df.fare.notna()].reset_index(drop=True)            # 1 passenger has no fare
y = df.survived.astype(int).values
tr, te = train_test_split(np.arange(len(y)), test_size=0.25,
                          random_state=42, stratify=y)
age = df.age.astype(float).fillna(np.nanmedian(df.age.astype(float)[tr]))   # 28.0
X = np.c_[df.sex == "female", df.pclass == 2, df.pclass == 3, age,
          df.sibsp, df.parch, np.log1p(df.fare.astype(float))].astype(float)

m = LogisticRegression(penalty=None).fit(X[tr], y[tr])
p = m.predict_proba(X[te])[:, 1]
print("odds ratios:", np.round(np.exp(m.coef_[0]), 3))
print(f"accuracy {accuracy_score(y[te], p >= 0.5):.3f}   AUC {roc_auc_score(y[te], p):.3f}")

accuracy 0.807   AUC 0.875

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Logistic regression computes a line z = w·x and squashes it with the sigmoid into a probability p = σ(z).
  • The line is the log-odds: each unit of a feature adds w to the log-odds and multiplies the odds by ew — the odds ratio.
  • It is trained by minimising log-loss (= maximum likelihood), which punishes confident mistakes; the gradient is simply (p − y)·x.
  • The decision boundary is a straight line (a hyperplane); curved boundaries need engineered features such as x₁x₂, and separable data needs regularisation.
  • The threshold is a choice, not part of the model; judge the scores with AUC and pick the cut-off from the costs. Softmax extends it all to many classes.

Check your understanding

A coefficient for “smoker” is 0.83. What does that mean in plain words?

e0.83 ≈ 2.3: smokers have about 2.3 times the odds of the outcome, holding the other features fixed. Note: odds, not probability. If the base probability is small, the probability roughly doubles too; if it is large, much less.

Why do we not train logistic regression with squared error?

Through a sigmoid, squared error is not convex (plateaus and bends, as in the right-hand landscape) and it barely punishes confident mistakes, so learning is slow. Log-loss is convex, punishes confident errors hard, and gives the clean (p − y)x gradient.

On perfectly separable data, gradient descent never stops and the weights keep growing. Why?

A steeper sigmoid makes every training probability closer to 0 or 1, which always lowers log-loss a bit more; the optimum is at infinity. Add an L2 penalty (as in the boundary lab) or stop early.

The “women survive, men don't” rule scores 80.4% accuracy; the full model 80.7%. Is the full model pointless?

No. Accuracy at one threshold hides it; the AUC jumps from 0.79 to 0.875. The full model ranks passengers much better — it can tell a first-class girl from a third-class woman — which matters as soon as you choose a different threshold or need calibrated probabilities.

How is logistic regression related to a neural network?

It is a neural network with one neuron: a weighted sum followed by a sigmoid, trained with cross-entropy by gradient descent. Deep networks stack many of these, and end with a softmax layer exactly like the one above.
