Decoding & Sampling

How a machine that only gives odds ends up writing one particular text

A language model never writes anything. Given the words so far, it hands back a probability for every word it knows, and something else has to choose. That something is the decoder, and it matters as much as the model: the same probabilities can give a stuck record, a safe cliché or a wild ramble. Our model is a word-level trigram trained on twenty Sherlock Holmes stories, small enough to run in your browser, so you can watch every choice. On the right are the forking paths after a short prompt: the three likeliest words at each step, three steps deep. Click any word to follow that branch.

The missing half

One model, many texts

A language model is a function from a history to a probability distribution over the next word. To write, you call it, choose a word, append it, and call it again: autoregressive generation. The model fixes the odds; the decoding rule decides how to use them. Take the most likely word every time, search for the most likely whole sentence, or roll dice weighted by the odds, and the same model writes very different things. Below, four decoders continue the same prompt with the same trigram model. Type your own opening, or press Sample again; the two random decoders change, the two deterministic ones never do.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\log P(y_{1:n} \mid x) \\ &\quad = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \log P(y_t \mid x,\, y_{<t}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: x is the prompt and y1 … yn the words the decoder adds. By the chain rule (chapter 4.1), the probability of the whole continuation is the product of each word’s probability given everything before it, so its logarithm is a sum. Every log-probability is negative; a sum closer to zero is a text the model finds more likely. Our trigram only looks at the last two words of the history.
Say it“The log-probability of the continuation is the sum, over its words, of the log-probability of each word given the prompt and the words before it.”

The obvious rule

Greedy decoding, and why it loops

The simplest decoder takes the single most likely word, every time. It is fast, deterministic and often fine for short answers. But it never looks ahead, and a choice that is best now can lead somewhere poor. Worse, our trigram’s next word depends only on the last two words, so greedy decoding is a fixed function from a pair of words to the next pair. The moment a pair comes round again, everything after it repeats, forever. Step through it and watch for the loop.

\[ y_t = \arg\max_{w \in V}\; P(w \mid x,\, y_{<t}) \]
Read it as: at step t, look at the whole vocabulary V and keep the word with the largest probability; ties are rare and go to the more common word. Nothing random happens, so the same prompt always gives the same text. Greedy maximises each step separately, which is not the same as maximising the probability of the whole text.
Say it“Word t is the argmax over the vocabulary of its probability given the prompt and the words so far.”

Looking ahead

Beam search: keep the best few

Searching every possible sentence is hopeless: with 10,982 words there are over 1048 twelve-word continuations. Beam search is the compromise. Keep the B best partial texts (the beam); extend each by its likeliest next words; score every extension by its total log-probability; keep the best B again. When a hypothesis ends a sentence it leaves the beam and joins the finished list. B = 1 is greedy decoding. Then a surprise: the most probable sentences are usually the shortest, because every extra word multiplies in another probability below one. Widen the beam, then raise the length penalty α.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{score}(y) &= \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \log P(y_t \mid x,\, y_{<t}) \\ \text{rank}(y) &= \frac{\text{score}(y)}{|y|^{\alpha}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the beam keeps hypotheses by their raw score, a sum of negative numbers that only falls as a text grows. At the end, finished sentences are compared after dividing by their length to the power α. α = 0 is the raw probability, which favours short sentences; α = 1 is the average log-probability per word. Google’s 2016 neural translation system used a penalty of this kind, with a slightly different length formula.
Say it“The score is the sum of log-probabilities; finished hypotheses are ranked by score over length to the alpha.”

Why the most likely text is dull

The likelihood trap

If beam search finds more probable text, why not always use it? Because real writing is not the most probable writing. Holtzman and colleagues (2020) plotted the probability a model gives each word of human text, and of beam-search text, and found them nothing alike: beam search stays on a high, flat plateau of safe words, while people keep surprising the model, word after word. Here is the same experiment with our model, on passages from four stories it never saw. Each beam continuation starts from Doyle’s own words and runs for 40 tokens. Switch passages and compare the two lines.

\[ \begin{aligned} s_t &= -\log_2 P(y_t \mid x,\, y_{<t}) \\ \text{PPL} &= 2^{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n} s_t} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: st is the surprisal of word t in bits: 1 bit for a word the model gave probability ½, about 6.6 bits for one in a hundred. Its average is the cross-entropy, and 2 to that power is the perplexity from chapter 4.1: the number of equally likely words the model was, in effect, choosing among.
Say it“The surprisal of a word is minus log base two of its probability; perplexity is two to the average surprisal.”

Rolling the dice

Temperature: one knob for boldness

So sample instead: roll a die weighted by the model’s probabilities. Sampling reproduces the model’s own uncertainty, surprises included. Temperature lets you sharpen or flatten the die first. It divides every log-probability (or logit) by T before the softmax, a trick borrowed from the Boltzmann distribution of statistical physics. Low T piles the probability onto the favourites; T → 0 is greedy decoding. High T spreads it out towards a uniform guess over all 10,982 words. Drag T in a confident context and in an open one.

\[ \begin{aligned} P_T(w) &= \frac{\exp(z_w / T)}{\sum_{v \in V} \exp(z_v / T)} \\ &= \frac{P(w)^{1/T}}{\sum_{v \in V} P(v)^{1/T}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: zw is the score of word w: a neural network’s logit, or here simply log P(w). Dividing by T and renormalising raises every probability to the power 1/T. The order of the words never changes, only how unequal their shares are. T = 1 leaves the model as it is.
Say it“The tempered probability of w is e to the z-w over T, divided by the sum of the same over the vocabulary.”

Cut the tail

Top-k sampling

Pure sampling has a hidden cost. Each word in the long tail is very unlikely, but there are thousands of them, and together they hold real probability; every so often the die lands there and the text derails. Fan, Lewis and Dauphin (2018) proposed the blunt fix: keep only the k most likely words, renormalise, and sample among those. GPT-2’s published samples used k = 40. The trouble is that one k does not suit every context. Below are two: after “my dear Watson” the model is nearly certain; after “I saw a” almost anything could follow. Move k and watch how much probability each context keeps.

\[ P_k(w) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{P(w)}{\sum_{v \in V_k} P(v)} & w \in V_k \\[1.2ex] 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]
Read it as: Vk is the set of the k most probable words. Inside it, probabilities are scaled up so they add to 1; outside it, they are set to zero. k = 1 is greedy; k = |V| is pure sampling.
Say it“Top-k probability is P of w over the total probability of the top k words, if w is among them, and zero otherwise.”

Let the context decide

Top-p: sampling from the nucleus

Holtzman and colleagues’ answer was to cut by probability instead of by count. Sort the words from most to least likely and keep the shortest list whose probabilities add up to at least p: the nucleus. When the model is confident the nucleus is a handful of words; when it is unsure, it grows to hundreds or thousands. One setting, p = 0.9 or 0.95, adapts to every context. Slide p and compare the two nucleus sizes.

\[ \begin{gathered} V_p = \{w_{(1)}, \dots, w_{(m)}\}, \\ m = \min\Big\{ j : \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{j} P(w_{(i)}) \ge p \Big\} \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: w(1) is the most probable word, w(2) the next, and so on. Walk down that list adding probabilities and stop as soon as the total reaches p; those m words are the nucleus. Then renormalise and sample inside it, exactly as in top-k. p = 1 is pure sampling; a small p is close to greedy.
Say it“The nucleus is the smallest set of most-probable words whose probabilities add up to at least p.”

Repetition & creativity

Penalties against the stuck record

Low-temperature and greedy text falls into loops, and large neural models do it too: once a phrase has appeared, repeating it becomes more likely, not less. Two practical cures are common. The repetition penalty of Keskar and colleagues (2019) lowers the score of every word already used. A no-repeat n-gram rule simply forbids any three-word sequence from appearing twice, as Paulus and colleagues (2018) did for summaries. Both buy variety with probability: the text the model likes best is exactly what they forbid. Raise θ, or switch the ban on, and watch the red loops dissolve.

\[ z'_w = \begin{cases} \theta\, z_w & \text{if } w \text{ is already used} \\ z_w & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]
Read it as: zw = log P(w) is negative, so multiplying it by θ > 1 pushes it further down: in probability terms P(w) becomes P(w)θ before renormalising. θ = 1 is no penalty; Keskar and colleagues suggest about 1.2 with greedy decoding. (A network’s raw logits can be positive; implementations then divide those by θ instead, so the penalty depends on where the logits happen to sit.)
Say it“A word that has already appeared has its log-probability multiplied by theta; every other word is unchanged.”

Real-world project

Project: tuning a decoder to write like Doyle

The model is the one you have used all chapter: an interpolated Kneser–Ney trigram (chapter 4.1) trained on 20 of the 24 stories in The Adventures and The Memoirs of Sherlock Holmes (Project Gutenberg #1661 and #834). Four stories are held out: The Blue Carbuncle, The Copper Beeches, The Musgrave Ritual and The Final Problem. From them we take 40 openings, each 12 tokens of Doyle followed by his real next 40 tokens. Every decoder continues every opening, and we hold its text up against Doyle’s with three yardsticks:

  • perplexity under the model: how predictable the text is to the model itself (Doyle sets the natural level);
  • repeated 3-grams: the share of three-token sequences that already appeared earlier in the same 40 tokens (loops);
  • novel 4-grams: the share of four-token sequences that occur nowhere in the 20 training stories (copying vs composing).

What the table says. Greedy and beam text is 14 to 29 times more predictable to the model than Doyle’s own words, it loops, and beam search mostly stitches together phrases from the training stories (only 23% of its 4-grams are new). Truncation with k = 40 or p = 0.95 removes surprises and copies more. Pure sampling comes closest, yet even it is less surprising than Doyle (99 against 147): trained on only 20 stories, the trigram is over-confident, so its own samples are more predictable to it than real held-out text. A temperature slightly above 1 compensates, while T = 1.2 overshoots wildly because thousands of tail words share the extra probability. Large neural models usually err the other way: Holtzman and colleagues found pure sampling more surprising than human text, because their tails are unreliable. That is why top-p helps them, and why the right setting depends on the model.

The same experiment in Python

The Python version below builds the same model with NumPy, re-implements every decoder from scratch (sorting the full vocabulary with np.lexsort where the page uses a faster merge), and uses a bit-for-bit port of the page’s random-number generator, so even the sampled texts can be compared word for word.

import json, math, numpy as np
from collections import Counter, defaultdict

D = json.load(open("ds_dl/stories.json"))            # 24 stories, lower-cased word and punctuation tokens
vocab = D["vocab"]; ix = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}; V = len(vocab); BOS = V
train = [[ix[w] for w in s] for s, sp in zip(D["stories"], D["split"]) if sp == "train"]
test = [[ix[w] for w in s] for s, sp in zip(D["stories"], D["split"]) if sp == "test"]

# interpolated Kneser-Ney trigram (chapter 4.1): raw counts on top, continuation counts below
tri = set()
for s in train:
    h = [BOS, BOS] + s
    for i in range(2, len(h)): tri.add((h[i - 2], h[i - 1], h[i]))
L3, L2, L1 = defaultdict(Counter), defaultdict(Counter), Counter()
for s in train:
    h = [BOS, BOS] + s
    for i in range(2, len(h)): L3[(h[i - 2], h[i - 1])][h[i]] += 1
for u, v, w in tri: L2[v][w] += 1
for v, w in {(v, w) for u, v, w in tri}: L1[w] += 1
def ney(levels):
    k = Counter(c for C in levels for c in C.values()); return k[1] / (k[1] + 2 * k[2])
LEV = [{(): L1}, dict(L2), dict(L3)]; DISC = [ney([L1]), ney(L2.values()), ney(L3.values())]
TAB = [{h: (np.array(list(C)), np.array(list(C.values()), float), sum(C.values()), len(C)) for h, C in lev.items()} for lev in LEV]

def dist(u, v):                                      # P(. | u v), all V words at once
    q = np.full(V, 1 / V)
    for o, key in enumerate([(), v, (u, v)]):
        e = TAB[o].get(key)
        if e is None: continue
        ws, ks, c, t = e
        q *= DISC[o] * t / c; q[ws] += np.maximum(ks - DISC[o], 0) / c
    return q
RANK = np.empty(V, int); RANK[np.lexsort((np.arange(V), -dist(-1, -1)))] = np.arange(V)   # tie-break: unigram order

def mulberry32(a):                                   # the page's random numbers, bit for bit
    a &= 0xFFFFFFFF
    def r():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF; t = a
        t = ((t ^ (t >> 15)) * (1 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return r

def decode(prompt, n, T=1.0, k=0, p=1.0, theta=1.0, ban=False, greedy=False, seed=1):
    r, s = mulberry32(seed), list(prompt)
    for _ in range(n):
        val = dist(s[-2], s[-1])
        if theta != 1:                               # repetition penalty: logit z -> theta * z (z < 0)
            seen = np.array(sorted(set(s))); val[seen] = val[seen] ** theta
        if ban:                                      # never repeat a trigram
            val[[s[i] for i in range(2, len(s)) if s[i - 2] == s[-2] and s[i - 1] == s[-1]]] = 0
        order = np.lexsort((RANK, -val))             # most probable first
        if greedy: s.append(int(order[0])); continue
        w = val[order] ** (1 / T) if T != 1 else val[order]
        if k: w = w[:k]
        if p < 1: c = np.cumsum(w); w = w[: int(np.argmax(c >= p * c[-1])) + 1]
        c = np.cumsum(w); s.append(int(order[int(np.argmax(c > r() * c[-1]))]))
    return s[len(prompt):]

def beam(prompt, B, steps=18, ends=(".", "!", "?")):    # returns finished and live hypotheses
    E = {ix[x] for x in ends}; live, done = [(list(prompt), 0.0)], []
    for _ in range(steps):
        cand = []
        for s, sc in live:
            q = dist(s[-2], s[-1]); top = np.lexsort((RANK, -q))[: 2 * B]
            cand += [(s + [int(w)], sc + math.log(q[w])) for w in top]
        cand.sort(key=lambda c: -c[1]); live = []
        for s, sc in cand:
            if s[-1] in E: done.append((s[len(prompt):], sc))     # a finished sentence leaves the beam
            elif len(live) < B: live.append((s, sc))
    return done, live
logp = lambda ctx, ys: [math.log(dist(*(ctx + ys)[i - 2: i])[(ctx + ys)[i]]) for i in range(len(ctx), len(ctx) + len(ys))]
text = lambda ids: " ".join(vocab[i] for i in ids)

# ---- the experiment: 40 openings from the 4 held-out stories; every decoder writes 40 tokens after each
END = {ix[x] for x in ".!?"}
def openings():
    out = []
    for s in test:
        for j in range(10):
            st = int(len(s) * (j + 0.5) / 10)
            while s[st - 1] not in END: st += 1
            out.append((s[st - 8: st + 4], s[st + 4: st + 44]))   # 12-token prompt, Doyle's next 40
    return out
four = {tuple(s[i: i + 4]) for s in train for i in range(len(s) - 3)}
def scores(pairs):                                   # pooled over the 40 texts
    lp = n = rep = n3 = new = n4 = 0
    for ctx, ys in pairs:
        l = logp(ctx, ys); lp += sum(l); n += len(l)
        g3 = [tuple(ys[i: i + 3]) for i in range(len(ys) - 2)]; rep += len(g3) - len(set(g3)); n3 += len(g3)
        g4 = [tuple(ys[i: i + 4]) for i in range(len(ys) - 3)]; new += sum(g not in four for g in g4); n4 += len(g4)
    return math.exp(-lp / n), rep / n3, new / n4

lp = n = 0
for s in test:
    h = [BOS, BOS] + s; l = logp(h[:2], s); lp += sum(l); n += len(l)
print(f"|V| = {V}, training tokens {sum(map(len, train))}, discounts D1..D3 = {DISC[0]:.4f} {DISC[1]:.4f} {DISC[2]:.4f}")
print(f"perplexity on the 4 held-out stories ({n} tokens): {math.exp(-lp / n):.4f}")
O = openings()
runs = {"Doyle (held-out text)": lambda c, i: None,
        "greedy": lambda c, i: decode(c, 40, greedy=True),
        "beam search, B = 4": lambda c, i: max(beam(c, 4, 40, ends=())[1], key=lambda h: h[1])[0][len(c):],
        "greedy + penalty 1.2": lambda c, i: decode(c, 40, theta=1.2, greedy=True),
        "temperature T = 0.7": lambda c, i: decode(c, 40, T=0.7, seed=1000 + i),
        "top-k, k = 40": lambda c, i: decode(c, 40, k=40, seed=1000 + i),
        "top-p, p = 0.95": lambda c, i: decode(c, 40, p=0.95, seed=1000 + i),
        "pure sampling, T = 1": lambda c, i: decode(c, 40, seed=1000 + i),
        "temperature T = 1.2": lambda c, i: decode(c, 40, T=1.2, seed=1000 + i)}
print(f"{'decoder':24s} {'perplexity':>10s} {'repeated 3-grams':>17s} {'novel 4-grams':>14s}")
for name, f in runs.items():
    pp, rep, new = scores([(c, f(c, i) or ys) for i, (c, ys) in enumerate(O)])
    print(f"{name:24s} {pp:10.2f} {rep:17.1%} {new:14.1%}")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A language model only supplies probabilities; the decoding rule turns them into text, and it can change the result as much as the model can.
  • Greedy decoding and beam search chase the most probable text. They are deterministic, fine for short exact answers, and prone to loops, blandness and very short sentences (hence length penalties).
  • Real writing is not the most probable writing: human text keeps surprising a model, so maximising probability is the wrong goal for open-ended text.
  • Sampling keeps the surprises. Temperature reshapes the distribution (P1/T); top-k and top-p cut away the unreliable tail, top-p adapting to how confident the model is.
  • Repetition penalties and n-gram bans buy variety at the cost of probability. Judge a decoder by comparing its text with real text: predictability, loops, copying, and by reading it.

Check your understanding

Greedy decoding picks the most probable word at every step. Does it find the most probable sentence?

No. A likely first word can lead only to unlikely continuations, while a slightly less likely first word opens a much better path. The hero tree often shows it: the greedy path and the most probable of the 27 paths differ. Beam search narrows the gap but is not exact either.

Why does beam search without a length penalty favour short sentences, and what does α = 1 change?

Every word adds a negative log-probability, so a longer sentence almost always has a lower total. Dividing by |y|α compares sentences per word instead; at α = 1 the score is the average log-probability, so a long, fluent sentence can beat a short one.

You set T = 0.01. Which decoder have you effectively built, and why?

Greedy decoding (with ties aside). P1/T = P100 makes the top word’s share overwhelming unless the second word is almost exactly as likely.

Top-k with k = 40 and top-p with p = 0.9: which keeps more words after “my dear Watson”, and which after “I saw a”?

After “my dear Watson” the model is confident, so the 0.9 nucleus is only a handful of words and k = 40 keeps far more, most of them junk. After “I saw a” the probability is spread thinly, so the 0.9 nucleus holds about 1,600 words and k = 40 keeps far fewer. Top-p adapts; top-k does not.

A decoder’s text has much lower perplexity than human text under the same model. Is that good news?

Not for open-ended writing. It means the text is more predictable than people are: usually blander, more repetitive and more copied from the training data. The goal is to match human statistics, not to beat them; for tasks with one right answer (translation, a short factual reply) high probability matters more.
