SVM Theory: the Dual, Valid Kernels, Paths & Regression

Everything an SVM knows, it knows through one matrix

Chapter 1.9 showed what a support vector machine does. This chapter opens it up. Rewrite a model in its dual form and the training data appear only through dot products, so a kernel can stand in for them. We will ask which functions are allowed to be kernels, watch the solver find the support vectors two at a time, follow the whole family of solutions as C changes, handle many classes, and turn the machine into a regression tool. Dual representations

Predicting with the data itself

Start with a model you already know: ridge regression on features φ(x) (chapter 1.1 and its Bayesian cousin). Normally you solve for the M weights w. But the solution can always be written as a weighted sum of the N training feature vectors, w = Φ⊤a, and if you solve for the N numbers a instead, the features vanish from the problem: only the dot products k(xn, xm) = φ(xn)⊤φ(xm) are left. Those dot products fill the N×N kernel matrix (or Gram matrix) K.

Why bother? The primal solve costs an M×M inverse and the dual an N×N one, so for a handful of features the primal is cheaper. The dual wins when M is huge, or infinite, which is exactly what a Gaussian kernel gives you. Drag the points, or click to add one; switch between cubic features and a Gaussian kernel, and move the mouse over the plot to see a prediction assembled from the training points.

\[ J(\mathbf{w}) = \tfrac12\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}_n) - t_n\big)^2 + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\,\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{w} \]
Read it as: the usual sum of squared misses plus a penalty on the size of the weights. λ sets how hard the penalty pulls the weights towards zero. Set the gradient to zero and every weight vector that solves it is a combination of the training feature vectors.
Say it“J of w is a half the sum of squared errors, plus lambda over two times w-transpose-w.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{w} = \boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\mathbf{a}, \quad a_n = -\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\big(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}_n) - t_n\big) \\ &\mathbf{a} = (\mathbf{K} + \lambda\mathbf{I}_N)^{-1}\,\mathbf{t}, \quad K_{nm} = k(\mathbf{x}_n,\mathbf{x}_m) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each training point gets one number an, its residual divided by −λ. Substitute w = Φ⊤a back into J and solve: you need only the kernel matrix K, never the features themselves.
Say it“w is Phi-transpose a; a is K plus lambda I, inverted, times t.”
\[ \begin{aligned} y(\mathbf{x}) &= \mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}(\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_N)^{-1}\mathbf{t} \\ &= \sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n\, k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_n) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: to predict at a new x, compare it with every training point through k, weight each comparison by an, and add. The vector k(x) holds the N comparisons k(x, xn).
Say it“y of x is k of x transposed, times K plus lambda I inverse, times t: the sum of a-n times k of x and x-n.”

Constructing kernels

Which functions are kernels?

A kernel has to be a dot product in some feature space, even if we never write that space down. Sometimes you can expand the formula and read the features off. For (x⊤z)² in two dimensions the features are the three degree-2 products of the coordinates:

\[ \begin{aligned} k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}) &= (\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{z})^2 \\ &= x_1^2z_1^2 + 2x_1z_1x_2z_2 + x_2^2z_2^2 \\ &= \boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{z}) \\ \boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}) &= \big(x_1^2,\ \sqrt2\,x_1x_2,\ x_2^2\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: squaring a dot product gives a dot product of squared features. The √2 makes the cross term come out with its factor of 2. One multiplication and one square stand in for a three-dimensional lift.
Say it“x-transpose-z, squared, equals phi of x dot phi of z, with phi equal to x-one squared, root two x-one x-two, x-two squared.”

Expanding is not always possible, so we need a test that works on the kernel itself. Here it is: k is a valid kernel exactly when, for every finite set of points, the kernel matrix K is positive semidefinite (Mercer’s condition). If k is a dot product the quadratic form below is a squared length, so it can’t be negative; the converse is the deep part of the theorem.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\,\mathbf{c} &= \sum_{n,m} c_n c_m\, k(\mathbf{x}_n,\mathbf{x}_m) \\ &= \Big\lVert \sum_n c_n \boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}_n)\Big\rVert^2 \ \ge\ 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for every choice of weights c, and every set of points. Equivalently, every eigenvalue of every kernel matrix is ≥ 0. One set of points with a negative eigenvalue is enough to prove a function is not a kernel; passing on one set proves nothing.
Say it“c-transpose K c, the double sum of c-n c-m k of x-n and x-m, is the squared length of the sum of c-n phi of x-n, so never negative.”

In practice nobody checks Mercer’s condition from scratch. You build new kernels from old ones with rules that keep K positive semidefinite. Given valid kernels k1 and k2:

\[ \begin{aligned} k &= c\,k_1 \ (c > 0), \quad f(\mathbf{x})\,k_1(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\,f(\mathbf{z}) \\ k &= q\big(k_1\big), \quad \exp\big(k_1\big) \\ k &= k_1 + k_2, \quad k_1\,k_2 \\ k &= k_3\big(\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}),\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{z})\big), \quad \mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{z} \\ k &= k_a(\mathbf{x}_a,\mathbf{z}_a) + k_b(\mathbf{x}_b,\mathbf{z}_b) \\ k &= k_a(\mathbf{x}_a,\mathbf{z}_a)\,k_b(\mathbf{x}_b,\mathbf{z}_b) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: f is any function, q a polynomial with non-negative coefficients, k3 a valid kernel on some features φ(x), A a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix, and xa, xb two parts of x. A sum glues two feature vectors end to end; a product takes all pairwise products of their features. Subtraction is not on the list.
Say it“Positive multiples, sandwiches f k f, positive polynomials, exponentials, sums and products of kernels are all kernels.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\exp\!\Big(\!-\frac{\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\rVert^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big) \\ &\quad= \exp\!\Big(\!-\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}{2\sigma^2}\Big)\exp\!\Big(\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{z}}{\sigma^2}\Big) \\ &\qquad\times\exp\!\Big(\!-\frac{\mathbf{z}^{\top}\mathbf{z}}{2\sigma^2}\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the Gaussian kernel passes by the rules alone: the middle factor is the exponential of a (scaled) linear kernel, and the outer factors are the f(x)·k·f(z) sandwich. Swap x⊤z for any valid kernel and it still works, which is how you build Gaussian kernels on strings or graphs.
Say it“The Gaussian of the distance is e to the minus x-squared over two sigma squared, times e to the x-dot-z over sigma squared, times e to the minus z-squared over two sigma squared.”

Kernels on structures

Kernels for strings, sets and probability models

Kernels free you from vectors. Anything you can compare in a positive semidefinite way can be fed to an SVM: protein sequences, documents, sets, graphs. The simplest string kernel, the k-spectrum kernel, describes a string by how often each k-letter word appears in it. With 20 amino acids there are 20k possible words, but only the words that actually occur cost anything to compare. Edit either sequence, change k, or try the presets: shared words light up in both strings.

\[ \begin{aligned} k_k(s,t) &= \sum_{u \in \mathcal{A}^k} \phi_u(s)\,\phi_u(t) \\ \tilde k(s,t) &= \frac{k_k(s,t)}{\sqrt{k_k(s,s)\,k_k(t,t)}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: φu(s) counts the times word u appears in s, and 𝒜k is the set of every word of length k over the alphabet 𝒜. The kernel is a plain dot product of word-count vectors, so it is valid. Dividing by the lengths gives the cosine, valid by the f(x)·k·f(z) rule with f(s) = 1/√k(s,s): long proteins no longer look similar to everything.
Say it“k of s and t is the sum over words u of the count of u in s times the count of u in t; k-tilde divides by the root of k of s s times k of t t.”

Sets have a kernel too. Give a set A one feature for every possible subset U, equal to 1 when U ⊆ A. Two sets then share exactly the subsets of their intersection, and there are 2 to the power of its size of those. Here A is the set of distinct k-letter words in a sequence.

\[ k(A_1, A_2) = 2^{\lvert A_1 \cap A_2 \rvert} \]
Read it as: count the elements the two sets share, and raise 2 to that power. Valid, because it is the dot product of the “which subsets do I contain?” features. It grows explosively, so in practice it is normalised or logged.
Say it“k of A-one and A-two is two to the size of their intersection.”

A third route starts from a probability model. The crudest generative kernel, k(x, x′) = p(x)p(x′), calls two objects similar when both are likely; summing such products over the components of a mixture, or the hidden paths of a hidden Markov model, gives richer ones. The Fisher kernel (Jaakkola & Haussler, 1999) instead describes each object by the direction in which it would push the model’s parameters.

\[ \begin{aligned} k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}') &= \mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf{x})^{\top}\,\mathbf{F}^{-1}\,\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf{x}') \\ \mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf{x}) &= \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\ln p(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\theta) \\ \mathbf{F} &= \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\big[\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf{x})^{\top}\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: g is the Fisher score, the gradient of the log-likelihood: which way, and how hard, would this object tug the parameters? F, the Fisher information, rescales so the answer doesn’t depend on how you parametrise the model. Here the model is deliberately tiny: each of the first 20 residues is basic (K or R) with probability θ, fitted to all 861 yeast proteins in the project. Mitochondrial targeting signals are rich in basic residues, so two such N-termini tug θ the same way.
Say it“The Fisher kernel is g of x, transposed, times F inverse, times g of x-prime, where g is the gradient of the log-likelihood.”
Computing the support vector classifier

Solving the SVM: KKT conditions and SMO

Recall the soft-margin problem from chapter 1.9, now written with ESL’s letters β and β0. It is a quadratic programme: a quadratic objective with linear constraints. Lagrange’s method turns it into a problem in one multiplier αi per point, and the optimality (Karush–Kuhn–Tucker) conditions tell us, point by point, what the answer must look like.

\[ \begin{aligned} L_P &= \tfrac12\lVert\beta\rVert^2 + C\sum_i\xi_i - \sum_i\mu_i\xi_i \\ &\quad - \sum_i \alpha_i\big[y_i(x_i^{\top}\beta+\beta_0) - (1-\xi_i)\big] \\ \Rightarrow\ \beta &= \sum_i \alpha_i y_i x_i, \quad 0 = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i \\ \alpha_i &= C - \mu_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first line is the primal Lagrangian: the objective, minus a multiplier αi ≥ 0 for each margin constraint and μi ≥ 0 for each ξi ≥ 0. Setting its derivatives in β, β0 and ξi to zero gives the second line: β is built from the data, the αs balance between the classes, and since μi ≥ 0, every αi ≤ C.
Say it“Beta is the sum of alpha-i y-i x-i, the alpha-i y-i sum to zero, and alpha-i is C minus mu-i.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{\alpha}\ L_D = \sum_i \alpha_i - \tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j\, x_i^{\top}x_j \\ &\text{s.t. } 0 \le \alpha_i \le C, \quad \textstyle\sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: substitute the second line back in and β, β0, ξ all disappear. What is left is the dual: a concave quadratic in the αs inside a box, with one linear constraint. The data enter only through dot products xi⊤xj.
Say it“Maximise the sum of the alphas minus a half the double sum of alpha-i alpha-j y-i y-j x-i dot x-j.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\alpha_i = 0 \ \Rightarrow\ y_i f(x_i) \ge 1 \\ &0 < \alpha_i < C \ \Rightarrow\ y_i f(x_i) = 1 \\ &\alpha_i = C \ \Rightarrow\ y_i f(x_i) \le 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the KKT complementary-slackness conditions αi[yif(xi) − (1 − ξi)] = 0 and μiξi = 0, sorted into three cases: outside the street, exactly on its edge, or inside it (or beyond). β0 is found from the edge points, where yif(xi) = 1 exactly; averaging over all of them is the numerically stable choice.
Say it“Zero alpha means beyond the street; alpha between zero and C means on the edge; alpha at C means inside.”

A general quadratic-programming solver costs roughly N³. Sequential minimal optimisation (SMO; Platt, 1998) is far lighter: it changes just two αs at a time, the smallest number that can move while keeping ∑αiyi = 0, and a two-variable quadratic can be solved by hand. Each step picks the pair that breaks the KKT conditions worst. This is the algorithm inside libsvm and scikit-learn. Press “One SMO step” a few times and watch the line settle as the red rings, the KKT violators, disappear.

Computing the SVM for classification

Kernels inside the dual

To bend the boundary, map each input through basis functions h(x) and fit the support vector classifier there. Look at the dual: h(x) appears only inside dot products. So replace ⟨h(x), h(x′)⟩ by a kernel K(x, x′) and you never need h at all. Below, the same SVM is computed twice: once by building the polynomial features explicitly and fitting a linear SVM on them, and once with the kernel. Raise the degree d and compare the two boundaries, and the price of each route.

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x) &= h(x)^{\top}\beta + \beta_0 \\ &= \sum_i \alpha_i y_i \langle h(x), h(x_i)\rangle + \beta_0 \\ L_D &= \sum_i \alpha_i \\ &\quad - \tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j \langle h(x_i), h(x_j)\rangle \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: both training (LD) and prediction (f) touch the features only through ⟨h, h⟩. Write K(x, x′) for that dot product and the features drop out of the calculation entirely. β0 comes from any edge point, as before.
Say it“f of x is the sum of alpha-i y-i times the inner product of h of x with h of x-i, plus beta-nought.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{polynomial: } K = (1 + \langle x, x'\rangle)^d \\ &\text{radial basis: } K = \exp(-\gamma\lVert x - x'\rVert^2) \\ &\text{neural net: } K = \tanh(\kappa_1\langle x, x'\rangle + \kappa_2) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the three kernels ESL lists. The polynomial uses all products of the inputs up to degree d; the radial basis is the Gaussian; the “neural network” (sigmoid) kernel mimics a hidden layer of tanh units, but it is not positive semidefinite for all κs, as the valid-kernels lab showed.
Say it“Polynomial: one plus x dot x-prime, to the d. Radial: e to the minus gamma times the squared distance. Neural net: tanh of kappa-one x dot x-prime plus kappa-two.”
\[ \begin{aligned} K(x,x') &= (1 + \langle x, x'\rangle)^2 \\ &= 1 + 2x_1x_1' + 2x_2x_2' \\ &\quad + (x_1x_1')^2 + (x_2x_2')^2 + 2x_1x_1'x_2x_2' \\ h(x) &= \big(1,\ \sqrt2 x_1,\ \sqrt2 x_2, \\ &\qquad\ x_1^2,\ x_2^2,\ \sqrt2 x_1x_2\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with two inputs and degree 2, the kernel is the dot product of six features. With p inputs and degree d there are (p+d choose d) features; the kernel still costs p multiplications and one power.
Say it“One plus x dot x-prime, squared, expands into six terms, so h of x has six features.”

SVMs and the curse of dimensionality

When a kernel can’t tell signal from noise

A kernel fixes the geometry before the data arrive: the polynomial kernel treats every input coordinate the same way. If only a few inputs matter, it has no way to learn to ignore the rest. ESL makes the point with an orange: class 1 is a ball of standard normal points in four dimensions, and class 2 is the same distribution restricted to a shell around it, like the skin around the fruit. Then we pad both classes with useless noise features. The best possible error stays at 2.9% however many noise features we add; the SVM’s does not. Add noise features one at a time, or sweep them all.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{class 1: } X_1,\dots,X_4 \sim N(0,1) \\ &\text{class 2: the same, given } 9 \le \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^4 X_j^2 \le 16 \\ &\text{Bayes error} = \tfrac12\,P\big(9 \le \chi^2_4 \le 16\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: both classes have the same density inside the shell (class 2 more of it), and class 2 has none outside, so the best rule says “class 2” exactly inside the shell. It errs only on class-1 points that land there. Noise features X5, X6, … are N(0, 1) in both classes and carry no information.
Say it“The Bayes error is half the probability that a chi-squared with four degrees of freedom lands between nine and sixteen.”
\[ K(x,z) = \Big(1 + \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{4} x_j z_j}_{\text{signal}} + \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{j=5}^{4+q} x_j z_j}_{\text{noise}}\Big)^2 \]
Read it as: the degree-2 kernel adds up every coordinate’s product with equal weight. Each noise coordinate adds as much variance to the sum as each signal coordinate, so with q = 4 half of every similarity is noise. The SVM can only reweight training points (the αs), not coordinates.
Say it“K is one plus the signal part plus the noise part, all squared.”

A path algorithm for the SVM

The regularisation path

C is usually chosen by cross-validation, which means solving the SVM for many values of C. Hastie, Rosset, Tibshirani and Zhu (2004) noticed that you can get all of them for roughly the price of one. Write λ = 1/C and rescale the multipliers to lie in [0, 1]. As λ shrinks, points travel from the inside of the street (set L), across its edge (the elbow, set E), to the outside (set R), and between those events every αi moves along a straight line in λ. Slide λ and watch the street and the αs together; drag the points to reshape the whole path.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{\beta_0,\beta}\ \sum_i \big[1 - y_i f(x_i)\big]_+ + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\beta\rVert^2 \\ &f(x) = \tfrac{1}{\lambda}\Big(\textstyle\sum_i \alpha_i y_i K(x, x_i) + \alpha_0\Big) \\ &0 \le \alpha_i \le 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the hinge-loss form of the SVM with λ = 1/C. These αs are the usual ones divided by C, so they live in [0, 1]. At large λ every point is inside a wide street and every αi = 1 (with equal class sizes); as λ falls they peel off towards 0.
Say it“Minimise the total hinge loss plus lambda over two times beta squared; f is one over lambda times the alpha-weighted kernel sum plus alpha-nought.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L} &= \{i : y_i f(x_i) < 1,\ \alpha_i = 1\} \\ \mathcal{E} &= \{i : y_i f(x_i) = 1,\ 0 \le \alpha_i \le 1\} \\ \mathcal{R} &= \{i : y_i f(x_i) > 1,\ \alpha_i = 0\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: left of the elbow, on it, or right of it. Only elbow points have αs that move. An event is a point joining or leaving the elbow; between events the elbow conditions yif(xi) = 1 are linear equations in λ, which is why the paths are piecewise linear.
Say it“L, inside the street with alpha one; E, on the edge; R, outside with alpha zero.”

A wider view (ESL’s closing discussion). Why should a wide street generalise? Vapnik’s structural risk minimisation gives one answer: classifiers with a large margin form a less flexible family, whatever the dimension of the space. Sliding λ walks along a nested sequence of such families, and cross-validation picks the point on the path. The bound is loose in practice, but it explains why the SVM copes with huge, even infinite, feature spaces.

\[ h \ \le\ \min\big(\lceil R^2\lVert\beta\rVert^2\rceil,\ p\big) + 1 \]
Read it as: if the data sit inside a ball of radius R in p dimensions, linear classifiers whose margin is at least 1/‖β‖ have VC dimension h no bigger than this. A short β (wide street) means low capacity, even when p is enormous.
Say it“The VC dimension is at most the smaller of R squared beta squared and p, plus one.”
Multiclass SVMs

More than two classes

The SVM is a two-class machine, and there is no single agreed way to extend it. The two workhorses combine many two-class SVMs. One-versus-the-rest trains K classifiers, each separating one class from all others. Read their signs and some regions are claimed by several classes, or by none; reading the largest score instead fills the gaps, but the K scores come from separately trained problems, so nothing guarantees they are on comparable scales. One-versus-one trains a classifier for every pair of classes and lets them vote. Switch strategy, and click to add points of any class (a fourth class is allowed).

\[ y(\mathbf{x}) = \arg\max_{k}\ y_k(\mathbf{x}) \]
Read it as: one-versus-the-rest: yk is the SVM trained with class k as +1 and every other class as −1. Each problem uses all N points and is lopsided (one class against K − 1), and the argmax trusts that scores from different problems can be compared.
Say it“Pick the class k whose own classifier gives the largest score.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \#\text{classifiers} &= \tfrac{K(K-1)}{2} \\ y(\mathbf{x}) &= \arg\max_k\ \#\{\text{votes for } k\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: one-versus-one: every pair of classes gets its own SVM trained on just those two classes’ points, about 2N/K of them. More classifiers, but each is small, so training is often faster than one-versus-the-rest. Ties are possible; libsvm and scikit-learn give them to the lower-numbered class.
Say it“K times K minus one over two classifiers, and the class with the most votes wins.”

Other routes exist. A DAG-SVM trains the same K(K−1)/2 pairwise machines but evaluates only K − 1 of them, walking down a decision graph that eliminates one class per test. Weston and Watkins (1999) fold all K classes into one optimisation that keeps each training point a margin ahead of every wrong class: neater, but slower to train. Error-correcting output codes (Dietterich & Bakiri, 1995) train each SVM on a different split of the classes into two groups and decode the pattern of answers like a noisy codeword. And ESL notes the probabilistic alternative: swap the hinge for the multinomial log-likelihood and you get kernel multinomial logistic regression, with probabilities but without sparse support vectors. Despite its untidy theory, one-versus-the-rest remains the most widely used.

SVMs for regression

Support vector regression: a tube instead of a street

To regress with an SVM, keep the weight penalty and change the loss. The ε-insensitive loss charges nothing for a miss smaller than ε, then grows linearly. Picture a tube of half-width ε around the fitted curve: points inside it are free and drop out of the solution, just as points beyond the street did in classification. Only points on the tube’s wall or outside it become support vectors. Widen the tube with ε and watch the support vectors thin out; drag points, or click to add them.

\[ E_\epsilon(y - t) = \begin{cases} 0, & |y - t| < \epsilon \\ |y - t| - \epsilon, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]
Read it as: a flat valley of width 2ε with straight walls. Compare squared error, which charges every miss and lets one outlier dominate.
Say it“E-epsilon is zero inside the tube, and the size of the miss minus epsilon outside it.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\min\ C\sum_n(\xi_n + \hat\xi_n) + \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 \\ &\text{s.t. } t_n \le y(\mathbf{x}_n) + \epsilon + \xi_n \\ &\phantom{\text{s.t. }} t_n \ge y(\mathbf{x}_n) - \epsilon - \hat\xi_n \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: two slacks per point, ξn ≥ 0 for sticking out above the tube and ξ̂n ≥ 0 for below it. C is again the price of slack, against a smooth (short-w) curve.
Say it“Minimise C times the total slack plus a half w squared, with each target within epsilon of the curve, give or take its slack.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \max_{a,\hat a}\ &-\tfrac12\sum_{n,m}(a_n-\hat a_n)(a_m-\hat a_m)\,k(\mathbf{x}_n,\mathbf{x}_m) \\ &- \epsilon\sum_n(a_n+\hat a_n) + \sum_n(a_n-\hat a_n)\,t_n \\ \text{s.t. }\ &0 \le a_n, \hat a_n \le C, \quad \textstyle\sum_n (a_n - \hat a_n) = 0 \\ y(\mathbf{x}) &= \sum_n (a_n - \hat a_n)\,k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_n) + b \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: one pair of multipliers per point, one for each wall. At most one of them is non-zero, and both are zero for points strictly inside the tube, which is where the sparsity comes from. Once again the data appear only through the kernel. The solver on this page is the same SMO, run on 2N variables, exactly as libsvm does.
Say it“y of x is the sum of a-n minus a-hat-n times k of x and x-n, plus b.”

\[ H_c(r) = \begin{cases} r^2/2, & |r| \le c \\ c\,|r| - c^2/2, & |r| > c \end{cases} \]
Read it as: ESL sets the ε-insensitive loss beside Huber’s robust loss. Both grow only linearly in the tails, so outliers can’t dominate. They differ in the middle: Huber is quadratic there, so every point keeps some influence; the ε-insensitive loss is flat, which is what makes most points drop out.
Say it“Huber is r squared over two for small residuals and c times the size of r minus c squared over two for large ones.”

Choosing ε in the units of the target is awkward. The ν-SVR of Schölkopf and colleagues (2000) replaces ε by ν ∈ (0, 1] and lets the solver find the tube width; ν is then an upper bound on the fraction of points outside the tube and a lower bound on the fraction of support vectors. In the lab you can check the first half of that by eye: points strictly outside the tube are always support vectors, and every support vector is on the wall or beyond it.

Real-world project

Project: where proteins live, and how strong concrete gets

Two real datasets, two ideas from this chapter. First, building a kernel: in 1996 Horton and Nakai published 1,484 yeast proteins labelled with the compartment of the cell where each one works (UCI “Yeast”). Each protein came with eight numbers from hand-built expert programs: signal-peptide scores, a membrane score, a mitochondrial-targeting score and so on. We fetched the proteins’ amino-acid sequences from UniProt, matched by entry name: 861 of the names still resolve exactly (many have been renamed since 1996, and we did not guess). That gives three kernels: an RBF on the eight expert scores, an RBF on the k-mer profile of the raw sequence (the normalised spectrum kernel, made Gaussian by the rule in the valid-kernels section), and their sum, valid by the sum rule. We train on 600 proteins and test on the other 261, with one-versus-one SVMs over ten compartments. Pick k and C; the page rebuilds the 861×861 kernel matrices and trains 3 × 45 SVMs in your browser.

Part two: support vector regression on concrete

from collections import Counter
from sklearn.svm import SVC, SVR

from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.metrics.pairwise import rbf_kernel

# --- Part A: yeast protein localisation (UCI Yeast + UniProt sequences) ---
seq = {r[0]: r[3] for r in (l.split("\t") for l in open("uniprot.tsv").read().splitlines()[1:])}
rows = [l.split() for l in open("yeast.data") if l.split()[0] in seq]   # 861 of 1,484 names still match
classes = sorted({r[9] for r in rows})
p = np.random.RandomState(1).permutation(len(rows)); rows = [rows[i] for i in p]
X = np.array([[float(v) for v in r[1:9]] for r in rows]); y = np.array([classes.index(r[9]) for r in rows])
S = [seq[r[0]] for r in rows]; tr, te = slice(0, 600), slice(600, None)

def spectrum(S, k):                       # normalised k-spectrum kernel: cosine of k-mer count vectors
    V = [Counter(s[i:i + k] for i in range(len(s) - k + 1)) for s in S]
    K = np.array([[sum(c * b.get(u, 0) for u, c in a.items()) for b in V] for a in V], float)
    d = np.sqrt(np.diag(K)); return K / np.outer(d, d)

K_feat = rbf_kernel(X, gamma=3)                                   # RBF on Nakai's 8 scores
for k in (1, 2, 3):
    K_str = np.exp(-3 * (2 - 2 * spectrum(S, k)))                 # RBF on the k-mer profile
    for C in (1, 10):
        out = []
        for name, K in (("features", K_feat), ("string", K_str), ("sum", K_feat + K_str)):
            m = SVC(kernel="precomputed", C=C).fit(K[tr, tr], y[tr])   # one-vs-one inside
            out.append(f"{name} {np.mean(m.predict(K[te, tr]) == y[te]):.4f} ({m.n_support_.sum()} SV)")
        print(f"k={k} C={C:<2}  " + "   ".join(out))

# --- Part B: concrete compressive strength (UCI) ---

A = A[np.random.RandomState(0).permutation(len(A))]

print(f"linear regression       test RMSE {rmse(LinearRegression().fit(Z[:824], t[:824]).predict(Z[824:])):.3f} MPa")

Two honest caveats about the data: 623 of the 1,484 yeast proteins are missing because their 1996 names no longer match UniProt, and with only 261 test proteins one protein is worth 0.38 percentage points, so differences of a point or two between kernels are within noise; the gap between the sum and either part alone is not.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • In the dual, a model sees its training data only through the kernel matrix K, and predicts with y(x) = ∑ ank(x, xn): an N×N problem instead of an M×M one, worth it when M is huge or infinite.
  • A function is a valid kernel exactly when every kernel matrix it makes is positive semidefinite. Build new kernels from old with sums, products, positive scalings, exponentials and f(x)k f(z); never by subtraction.
  • Kernels work on anything you can compare: strings by shared k-mers, sets by shared subsets, objects by their Fisher scores under a probability model.
  • The SVM dual is a box-constrained quadratic programme; KKT conditions sort points into beyond, on and inside the street, SMO solves it two multipliers at a time, and the αs move piecewise linearly along the whole path in λ = 1/C.
  • Kernels cannot learn which inputs matter, many classes need several two-class SVMs (one-vs-rest or one-vs-one), and regression swaps the street for an ε-tube that keeps the solution sparse.

Check your understanding

You have 50 features and a million training points. Should you fit ridge regression in the primal or the dual?

The primal: it inverts a 50×50 matrix, while the dual needs a million-by-million one. The dual pays off when the feature space is larger than the number of points, or infinite.

k₁ and k₂ are valid kernels. Is k₁ − k₂ one too? Is exp(k₁)·k₂?

The difference need not be: take k2 = 2k1 and you get −k1, whose kernel matrices have negative eigenvalues. The second is valid: exp(k1) is a kernel by the exponential rule, and a product of kernels is a kernel.

In the SMO lab, why does each step change two αs and never one?

The constraint ∑ αiyi = 0 must hold after every step. Changing a single α would break it; changing two lets one compensate for the other.

Adding six pure-noise inputs makes a degree-2 polynomial SVM much worse on the “orange” data. Why can’t the SVM just ignore them?

The kernel weighs every coordinate equally in ⟨x, z⟩, and training only chooses weights αi on the training points, not on the coordinates. To ignore inputs you must select features or learn the kernel’s scaling, outside the SVM.

Raising ε in support vector regression usually reduces the number of support vectors. Why?

Points strictly inside the tube have an = ân = 0. A wider tube contains more points, so fewer sit on its walls or outside, and only those are support vectors. On the concrete data, ε from 0.5 to 4 MPa cuts them from 672 to 284.
