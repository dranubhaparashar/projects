Flexible, Penalised & Mixture Discriminant Analysis

Three ways to keep what is good about LDA and drop what is not

Linear discriminant analysis (chapter 1.4) is quick, easy to read and hard to beat. It also has three blind spots: its boundaries are straight, it gives each class a single centre, and with many correlated features its estimates get noisy. This chapter fixes all three with one idea. LDA can be rewritten as a regression, and once it is a regression we can swap in a curvier regression (FDA), a penalised one (PDA), or a richer model of each class (MDA). On the right, the teal class lives in two separate clumps with the orange class between them. Drag any of the four ringed handles and switch between the methods.

Generalising LDA

Where LDA falls short

LDA models each class as a Gaussian cloud, with its own centre μk but one covariance matrix Σ shared by all the classes. That buys a lot. The boundaries are straight lines (planes in higher dimensions), so the rule is easy to explain. A point is assigned to the nearest centre, in a distance that allows for the shape of the clouds, so LDA is a simple prototype classifier. And the K centres span at most K − 1 dimensions, which gives the discriminant coordinates: low-dimensional views of the data that show the classes as well as any view can. In large benchmark comparisons LDA is regularly among the best methods, often because a simple boundary is all the data can support.

But the same assumptions are its limits. Straight boundaries are too crude when the classes curve round each other. One centre per class is too few when a class has several modes, like a vowel spoken by men and by women. And with many correlated features (the 21 samples of a signal, the 256 pixels of an image), LDA has a great many coefficients to estimate. It fits them, noise and all, and the coefficient curve comes out jagged where the truth is smooth. Pick a problem and compare LDA with its fix; on the two flat problems you can click to move the star.

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta_k(x) &= x^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\mu_k \\ &\quad - \tfrac12\,\mu_k^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\mu_k + \log \pi_k \\ \hat G(x) & = \arg\max_k\ \delta_k(x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: πk is the share of class k in the training data, μk its mean and Σ the pooled within-class covariance. δk is linear in x, so two classes meet where two straight-line scores are equal. Maximising δk is the same as finding the centre closest to x in Mahalanobis distance, allowing for the prior.
Say it“Delta-k of x is x transpose Sigma-inverse mu-k, minus a half mu-k transpose Sigma-inverse mu-k, plus log pi-k. Predict the class with the largest delta.”

The key idea

LDA as a regression: optimal scoring

Here is the trick that makes everything else possible. Give each class a number, its score θ(k). Replace every training label gi by its class's score and fit an ordinary linear regression of those numbers on x. If the scores are well chosen, the fitted values xᵢ⊤β separate the classes: each class's points land near their own score. But which scores? Any numbers would do for a regression, so we let the data choose them too: pick the scores and the coefficients together to make the average squared residual as small as possible. To stop the trivial answer (every score zero), the scores must have mean square one.

Below are three classes. The score vector (θ(1), θ(2), θ(3)) has to be centred and of mean square one, so only its direction is free: one angle. Each coloured line is where the fitted regression equals that class's score. Turn the score angle and watch the residual error, then jump to the optimum and compare its direction with LDA's.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathrm{ASR} = \frac1N \sum_{l}\, \sum_{i} \big(\theta_l(g_i) - x_i^{\top}\beta_l\big)^2 \\ &\tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_i \theta_l(g_i)\,\theta_m(g_i) = \mathbb 1[l = m] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ASR is the average squared residual. Each of the L score functions θl turns a class label into a number, and βl (with an intercept, hidden in x) regresses those numbers on the features. The constraint says each score set has mean square one over the training data, and different score sets are uncorrelated. Minimise over the scores and the coefficients jointly.
Say it“ASR is one over N times the sum over l and over i of theta-l of g-i minus x-i transpose beta-l, squared; the scores are normalised and mutually orthogonal.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\hat\beta_l \ \propto\ v_l \\ &\min \mathrm{ASR}_l = r_l^2 = 1 - \mu_l \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: vl is LDA's lth discriminant direction. The best coefficient vectors point along these directions, the directions that best separate the class means relative to the within-class spread. So LDA is optimal scoring with linear regression. rl2 is the residual error of the lth score, and μl = 1 − rl2 is how much of the score the regression explains (a squared canonical correlation).
Say it“Beta-hat-l is proportional to LDA's l-th discriminant direction; its minimal residual, r-l squared, is one minus mu-l.”

Flexible discriminant analysis

FDA: swap in a flexible regression

Once LDA is a regression, the regression is a part we can replace. Hastie, Tibshirani and Buja (1994) called the result flexible discriminant analysis. Keep the optimal scores, but fit them with any regression method you like: polynomials, additive splines (their BRUTO), MARS, kernels, even a neural network. Each fitted score function ηl(x) is now a curved surface, so the boundaries curve too. A penalty J(η) keeps the fit from chasing noise.

The data below have three classes separated by two parabolas, plus some noise near the boundaries. With degree 1 FDA is exactly LDA. Raise the degree of the polynomial regression, then raise λ to tame it; click anywhere to classify a point.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathrm{ASR} = \frac1N \sum_{l=1}^{L} \Big[ \sum_{i=1}^{N} e_{il}^2 + \lambda\, J(\eta_l) \Big] \\ &e_{il} = \theta_l(g_i) - \eta_l(x_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same criterion, but the straight-line fit x⊤βl becomes a general function ηl(x), eil is the residual of point i on score l, and J(ηl) charges for roughness, weighted by λ. Here ηl is a polynomial of the chosen degree in the two features and J is the sum of its squared coefficients (the intercept is free): ridge regression on a polynomial basis.
Say it“ASR is one over N times the sum over l of: the squared residuals theta-l of g-i minus eta-l of x-i, plus lambda times the roughness J of eta-l.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\delta(x, k) = \sum_{l=1}^{L} w_l \big(\hat\eta_l(x) - \bar\eta_l^{\,k}\big)^2 \\ &w_l = \frac{1}{r_l^2\,(1 - r_l^2)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: to classify x, compute its fitted scores η̂l(x) and compare them with each class's average fitted scores η̄lk; pick the closest class. The weight wl rescales each score so its spread within a class is one: it uses rl2, the score's residual error. For linear regression this is exactly LDA's Mahalanobis distance (up to a term that is the same for every class).
Say it“The distance from x to class k is the sum over l of w-l times eta-hat-l of x minus eta-bar-l-k, squared; w-l is one over r-l squared times one minus r-l squared.”

Computing the FDA estimates

Three steps: regress, score, update

Minimising over scores and regressions together sounds hard, but when the regression is a linear smoother, so that the fitted values are a fixed matrix Sλ times the responses, it takes one regression and one small eigen-problem. Polynomials, splines and ridge regression are all linear smoothers. Code the classes as an N × K indicator matrix Y: row i has a 1 in the column of point i's class and zeros elsewhere. Then fit, once, a regression for every column of Y. The optimal scores are the eigenvectors of a K × K matrix built from the fits, and the score functions are combinations of the K fitted columns. Nothing is ever refitted. Step through the computation for the fit above.

\[ \begin{aligned} &1.\ \ \hat Y = S_\lambda Y \\ &\quad\ \hat\eta(x) = \big(\hat Y_1(x), \dots, \hat Y_K(x)\big) \\ &2.\ \ Y^{\top}\hat Y\,\Theta = N D_\pi\,\Theta\, \operatorname{diag}(\mu) \\ &\quad\ \Theta^{\top}D_\pi\Theta = I \\ &3.\ \ \eta(x) = \Theta^{\top}\hat\eta(x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: (1) Regress each indicator column on x with the chosen smoother; Ŷk(x) is a rough estimate of P(class k | x). (2) Solve a K × K eigen-problem; Dπ = Y⊤Y/N is the diagonal matrix of class proportions, the columns of Θ are the optimal scores, and the eigenvalues μ are 1 − r2. The first eigenvector is the constant score, with μ = 1: it is trivial and is dropped, and dropping it centres the rest. (3) The discriminant functions are the fitted columns combined with the scores as weights. If Sλ is plain linear regression, this is LDA.
Say it“One: Y-hat is S-lambda times Y. Two: Y transpose Y-hat times Theta equals N D-pi Theta times the diagonal of mu, with Theta normalised. Three: eta of x is Theta transpose eta-hat of x.”

Penalised discriminant analysis

PDA: smooth coefficients for signals and images

Now the third problem: too many correlated features. Each example below is a noisy signal of 21 samples (the waveform data, whose details come in a later section). LDA fits 21 coefficients per discriminant direction, and with neighbouring samples so alike, it can trade a large positive coefficient on one sample against a large negative one on the next. The coefficient curve comes out jagged, and part of what it encodes is noise. But the truth is smooth: neighbouring samples should get similar weights.

Penalised discriminant analysis (Hastie, Buja & Tibshirani, 1995) says so directly. It is optimal scoring with a quadratic penalty β⊤Ωβ on the coefficients, where Ω is chosen for the problem. Here Ω adds up the squared second differences of the coefficient curve, so it charges for wiggles. In images one would penalise differences between neighbouring pixels. Raise λ and watch the coefficient curve smooth out and the test error fall.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathrm{ASR} = \frac1N \sum_{l=1}^{L} \Big[ \sum_{i=1}^{N} e_{il}^2 + \lambda\,\beta_l^{\top}\Omega\,\beta_l \Big] \\ &e_{il} = \theta_l(g_i) - h(x_i)^{\top}\beta_l \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: h(x) may enlarge the features with a basis expansion (here it is just the 21 samples). The penalty β⊤Ωβ = Σj(βj+1 − 2βj + βj−1)2 is zero for a straight-line coefficient curve and grows with every bend. Larger λ means smoother curves and fewer effective parameters: the effective degrees of freedom df = tr[(X⊤X + λΩ)−1X⊤X], computed on centred X, falls from 21 towards 2.
Say it“The same residual sum, plus lambda times beta-l transpose Omega beta-l.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{u}\ u^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathrm{Bet}}\,u \\ &\text{s.t.}\ \ u^{\top}\big(\Sigma_W + \tfrac{\lambda}{N}\Omega\big)u = 1 \\ &D(x, \mu) = (x - \mu)^{\top} \\ &\qquad \big(\Sigma_W + \tfrac{\lambda}{N}\Omega\big)^{-1}(x - \mu) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same answer, seen as LDA with a penalised within-class covariance (ΣW and the between-class covariance ΣBet both divide by N). Find the direction u that spreads the class means most, measured against the within-class spread plus a roughness charge. The PDA coefficients from optimal scoring point exactly along these u. D is the penalised Mahalanobis distance that goes with it: rough directions count as noisy, so they matter less.
Say it“Maximise u transpose Sigma-between u, subject to u transpose, Sigma-W plus lambda over N Omega, u equal to one.”

Mixture discriminant analysis

MDA: several centres per class

LDA is a prototype method with one prototype per class. Chapter 1.6c showed that a few prototypes per class can follow far more irregular boundaries. Mixture discriminant analysis (Hastie & Tibshirani, 1996) brings that idea into the Gaussian model. Each class becomes a mixture of Rk Gaussian subclasses, with their own centres and mixing proportions, all sharing one covariance matrix as in LDA. Below, each class is really three clumps. Choose R, then step the EM algorithm and watch the subclass centres find the clumps. The lab starts each class's subclass centres at R of its points picked at random, so that EM has work to do; in practice one starts from k-means, which here lands almost on the answer. Click a training point to see how it is shared among its class's subclasses.

\[ \begin{aligned} &P(X \mid G = k) = \sum_{r=1}^{R_k} \pi_{kr}\, \phi_{kr}(X) \\ &P(G = k \mid X = x) \\ &\quad = \frac{\Pi_k\, P(x \mid G = k)}{\sum_{\ell} \Pi_\ell\, P(x \mid G = \ell)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: φkr(x) = φ(x; μkr, Σ) is the Gaussian density with subclass kr's centre and the shared covariance. Class k's density is a weighted sum of Rk bumps, with mixing proportions πkr that add up to 1 within the class. All bumps of all classes share one Σ. Πk is the class prior. Bayes' rule turns the densities into class probabilities; predict the most probable class. With Rk = 1 this is exactly LDA.
Say it“The density of class k is the sum over r of pi-k-r times the Gaussian with mean mu-k-r and covariance Sigma. The probability of class k at x is its prior times its density, over the same summed across all classes.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{E: } W(c_{kr} \mid x_i, g_i) = \frac{\pi_{kr}\,\phi_{kr}(x_i)}{\sum_{s} \pi_{ks}\,\phi_{ks}(x_i)} \\ &\text{M: } \mu_{kr} = \frac{\sum_{i \in k} W_{ir}\, x_i}{\sum_{i \in k} W_{ir}} \\ &\phantom{\text{M: }} \pi_{kr} = \frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i \in k} W_{ir} \\ &\phantom{\text{M: }} \Sigma = \frac1N \sum_{i}\sum_{r} W_{ir}\, d_{ir}\, d_{ir}^{\top} \\ &\phantom{\text{M: }} d_{ir} = x_i - \mu_{g_i r} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: we know each point's class but not its subclass, so EM guesses. Here i ∈ k means the points of class k. The E-step shares point i among its own class's subclasses in proportion to how well each explains it (other classes get nothing). The M-step re-estimates every centre, mixing proportion and the shared covariance as weighted averages. Each round raises the likelihood. Start from k-means run inside each class, as in chapter 1.6c.
Say it“E-step: the weight of subclass k-r for point i is pi-k-r times its Gaussian density at x-i, over the sum of the same for all subclasses of class k. M-step: weighted means, weighted proportions and a weighted pooled covariance.”

MDA meets optimal scoring

Blurred responses and reduced rank

Look again at the M-step. It is LDA, with one twist: there are R = ΣkRk subclasses instead of K classes, and each point belongs to its class's subclasses only partly, with weights W. LDA is optimal scoring, so the M-step can be done by optimal scoring too. Replace the indicator matrix Y by a blurred response matrix Z with one column per subclass. Row i holds point i's E-step weights in its own class's columns and zeros elsewhere, so each row still adds up to 1. Then run the three FDA steps on Z.

Why bother? Because it opens every door FDA opened. Keep only L discriminant dimensions and you get reduced-rank MDA: all the subclass centres are forced into an L-dimensional subspace, which saves parameters and gives low-dimensional views. Swap the linear regression for a flexible or penalised one and you get MDA with curved boundaries, or with smooth coefficients. The E-step then measures distances δ in the discriminant space. Try rank 1, then the quadratic regression, and click a point to read its row of Z.

\[ \begin{aligned} &Z_{i,\,(k,r)} = \begin{cases} W(c_{kr} \mid x_i, g_i) & g_i = k \\ 0 & g_i \ne k \end{cases} \\ &D_p = \operatorname{diag}\Big(\tfrac1N \textstyle\sum_i Z_{i,(k,r)}\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Z is N × R. A hard 0/1 indicator of the subclasses would be Y for R classes; Z blurs it with the E-step's uncertainty. Dp holds the subclasses' total weights, as fractions of N, and takes the place of Dπ in the normalisation Θ⊤DpΘ = I.
Say it“The entry of Z for point i and subclass k-r is point i's weight on that subclass if it is in class k, and zero otherwise.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{M: optimal scoring of } Z \text{ on } h(x), \\ &\qquad \text{keeping } L \text{ dimensions} \\ &\text{E: } W_{ir} \propto \pi_{kr}\, \exp\!\big(-\tfrac12\, \delta(x_i, c_{kr})\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the M-step is the three FDA steps with Z for Y; the subclass centres are the weighted means of the fitted scores, and πkr the subclass's share of its class's weight. The E-step uses the FDA distance δ from the section above, measured in the L kept dimensions. With linear regression and every dimension kept, exp(−δ/2) is proportional to the Gaussian density, so this is exactly the EM algorithm of the previous section.
Say it“M-step: optimal scoring of Z on h of x. E-step: the weight of subclass k-r is proportional to pi-k-r times e to the minus half delta.”

Example: waveform data

The waveform data: three classes on a triangle

Breiman, Friedman, Olshen and Stone (1984) built this simulated problem for their book on classification trees, and it has been a benchmark ever since. Take three triangular waves, h1, h2 and h3, each with a peak of height 6 at a different place along 21 samples. Each class blends two of them with a random weight U, then adds independent Gaussian noise to every sample. Pick a class and slide U to see how a signal is built.

\[ \begin{aligned} &X_j = U\,h_a(j) + (1 - U)\,h_b(j) + \epsilon_j \\ &(a, b) = (1, 2),\ (1, 3),\ (2, 3) \\ &h_1(j) = \max(6 - |j - 11|,\, 0) \\ &h_2(j) = h_1(j - 4) \\ &h_3(j) = h_1(j + 4) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each class blends its own pair of waves ha, hb: (1, 2) for class 1, (1, 3) for class 2, (2, 3) for class 3. j = 1, …, 21 indexes the samples; U is uniform on [0, 1], drawn once per signal; each εj is standard Gaussian noise. h2 peaks at j = 15 and h3 at j = 7. Without the noise, every signal lies on a straight segment between two of the three points h1, h2, h3 in 21-dimensional space: the three classes are the three edges of a triangle. The noise blurs the triangle in all 21 directions.
Say it“For class one, X-j is U times h-one of j plus one minus U times h-two of j, plus noise; h-one is a triangle of height six centred at eleven, and h-two and h-three are it shifted by four.”

As in the original study we draw 300 training signals (100 per class) and 500 test signals, ten times over, and report the mean error with its standard error. Because we know how the data were made, we can also compute the Bayes rule, the best any classifier can do: it averages the Gaussian noise density over U on a fine grid. The same simulation in Python

import math
import numpy as np
from sklearn.discriminant_analysis import QuadraticDiscriminantAnalysis
# optscore, dist, mulberry32, mda, mda_predict, lin and quad: as in the project's script at the end of the chapter

j = np.arange(1, 22)
h1, h2, h3 = (np.maximum(6 - abs(j - c), 0) for c in (11, 15, 7))   # h2, h3: h1 shifted by 4 either way
PAIRS = [(h1, h2), (h1, h3), (h2, h3)]

def gauss(r):
    u = 0
    while u == 0: u = r()
    return math.sqrt(-2 * math.log(u)) * math.cos(2 * math.pi * r())

def wave(seed, n, balanced):
    r, X, g = mulberry32(seed), [], []
    for i in range(n):
        k = i % 3 if balanced else min(2, int(r() * 3)); u = r(); a, b = PAIRS[k]
        X.append(u * a + (1 - u) * b + np.array([gauss(r) for _ in range(21)])); g.append(k)
    return np.array(X), np.array(g)

def bayes(X):                                     # p(x | class): integrate over U on a 400-point grid
    us, ll = (np.arange(400) + 0.5) / 400, []
    for a, b in PAIRS:
        d2 = ((X[:, None] - (us[:, None] * a + (1 - us[:, None]) * b)[None]) ** 2).sum(-1); m = d2.min(1)
        ll.append(-m / 2 + np.log(np.exp(-(d2 - m[:, None]) / 2).mean(1)))
    return np.argmax(ll, 0)

D2 = np.diff(np.eye(21), 2, axis=0); Om = np.zeros((22, 22)); Om[1:, 1:] = D2.T @ D2   # roughness penalty
res = {}
for s in range(10):
    Xtr, ytr = wave(1000 + s, 300, True); Xte, yte = wave(2000 + s, 500, False); Y = np.eye(3)[ytr]
    m, sd = Xtr.mean(0), Xtr.std(0); Q, Qt = quad((Xtr - m) / sd), quad((Xte - m) / sd)
    H, Ht = lin(Xtr), lin(Xte); qda = QuadraticDiscriminantAnalysis().fit(Xtr, ytr)
    out = {"LDA": (dist(f := optscore(H, Y), H).argmin(1), dist(f, Ht).argmin(1)),
           "QDA": (qda.predict(Xtr), qda.predict(Xte)),
           "FDA, quadratic, lambda=300": (dist(f := optscore(Q, Y, 300), Q).argmin(1), dist(f, Qt).argmin(1)),
           "PDA, smooth, lambda=1000": (dist(f := optscore(H, Y, 1000, None, Om), H).argmin(1), dist(f, Ht).argmin(1))}
    for nm, lam in (("MDA, 3 subclasses", 0), ("MDA, 3 subclasses, smooth", 1000)):
        f = mda(H, Xtr, ytr, 3, None, 50, lam, Om, K=3); out[nm] = (mda_predict(f, H, 3, K=3), mda_predict(f, Ht, 3, K=3))
    out["Bayes rule"] = (bayes(Xtr), bayes(Xte))
    for nm, (a, b) in out.items(): res.setdefault(nm, []).append(((a != ytr).mean(), (b != yte).mean()))
for nm, v in res.items():
    v = np.array(v); se = v.std(0, ddof=1) / math.sqrt(len(v))
    print(f"{nm:28s} train {v[:, 0].mean():.3f} ({se[0]:.3f})   test {v[:, 1].mean():.3f} ({se[1]:.3f})")

Real-world project

Project: recognising vowels from 15 speakers

David Deterding recorded 15 speakers of British English, 8 men and 7 women, each saying 11 vowels six times inside the words heed, hid, head, had, hud, hard, hod, hoard, hood, who’d and heard. Each utterance was analysed with linear prediction, which models the vocal tract as a tube made of short sections. The ten features are log area ratios: the logarithms of how much the tube widens or narrows from one section to the next, which is what shapes a vowel's sound. The data are on the UCI repository (Connectionist Bench, Vowel Recognition). The training set is 528 utterances from 8 speakers (4 men, 4 women), and the test set 462 utterances from 7 other speakers. That makes it hard: the test asks whether a classifier has learned the vowels or merely the voices.

The page fits every model in your browser. Features are standardised with the training means and standard deviations. The number of discriminant dimensions L, the ridge penalty λ and the number of subclasses R were chosen by leave-one-speaker-out cross-validation on the 8 training speakers (in the Python below), never by looking at the test set. Press Train.

Seeing the vowels: discriminant coordinates

The first two discriminant coordinates are the best two-dimensional view each method has of the 11 vowels. LDA's are straight-line projections; FDA's are curved functions of the ten features, built from a quadratic regression, so they can pull apart vowels that overlap in every flat view. Each vowel's label sits at its centre.

How many dimensions?

LDA and FDA can classify using only the first L discriminant coordinates. Fewer dimensions mean fewer things to estimate, and the later coordinates carry little signal (small μ) but plenty of noise. Compare the test error with the cross-validation choice.

The same project in Python

import numpy as np
from sklearn.discriminant_analysis import LinearDiscriminantAnalysis, QuadraticDiscriminantAnalysis

D = np.loadtxt("vowel-context.data")                      # UCI: Deterding vowels, 15 speakers
tr, te = D[D[:, 0] == 0], D[D[:, 0] == 1]                  # speakers 0-7 train, 8-14 test
Xtr, ytr, spk = tr[:, 3:13], tr[:, 13].astype(int), tr[:, 1].astype(int)
Xte, yte, K = te[:, 3:13], te[:, 13].astype(int), 11

def optscore(H, Y, lam=0.0, L=None, Om=None):             # ESL 12.5.1; default penalty: ridge, off the intercept
    N = len(H)
    if Om is None: Om = np.eye(H.shape[1]); Om[0, 0] = 0
    B = np.linalg.solve(H.T @ H + lam * Om, H.T @ Y)      # step 1: regress every column of Y
    M = Y.T @ H @ B / N; s = 1 / np.sqrt(Y.sum(0) / N)
    mu, V = np.linalg.eigh(s[:, None] * (M + M.T) / 2 * s[None])
    o = np.argsort(-mu); mu, Th = mu[o][1:], (s[:, None] * V[:, o])[:, 1:]   # step 2: drop the trivial score
    L = min(L or 99, int((mu > 1e-9).sum())); mu = np.minimum(mu[:L], 1 - 1e-12)
    Bt = B @ Th[:, :L]; eta = H @ Bt                        # step 3: eta(x) = Theta^T eta-hat(x)
    return dict(B=Bt, w=1 / (mu * (1 - mu)), cen=Y.T @ eta / Y.sum(0)[:, None])

def dist(f, H): return (((H @ f["B"])[:, None] - f["cen"][None]) ** 2 * f["w"]).sum(-1)

def mulberry32(a):                                          # the page's random generator
    s = [a & 0xFFFFFFFF]
    def r():
        s[0] = (s[0] + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF; t = s[0]
        t = (t ^ (t >> 15)) * (1 | t) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + ((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return (t ^ (t >> 14)) / 4294967296
    return r

def kmeans(X, R, seed):                                     # k-means++ start, then Lloyd
    r = mulberry32(seed); C = [X[int(r() * len(X))]]
    while len(C) < R:
        d2 = ((X[:, None] - np.array(C)[None]) ** 2).sum(-1).min(1)
        C.append(X[np.searchsorted(np.cumsum(d2), r() * d2.sum(), side="right")])
    C, a = np.array(C), None
    for _ in range(100):
        b = ((X[:, None] - C[None]) ** 2).sum(-1).argmin(1)
        if a is not None and (a == b).all(): break
        a = b; C = np.array([X[a == j].mean(0) if (a == j).any() else C[j] for j in range(R)])
    return a

def mda(H, X, g, R, L=None, iters=50, lam=0, Om=None, K=K):  # ESL 12.7: EM on the blurred response Z
    Z, nk = np.zeros((len(g), K * R)), np.bincount(g)
    for k in range(K): i = np.where(g == k)[0]; Z[i, k * R + kmeans(X[i], R, 100 + k)] = 1
    for _ in range(iters + 1):
        f = optscore(H, Z, lam, L, Om); f["pi"] = Z.sum(0) / np.repeat(nk, R)
        lg = (np.log(f["pi"]) - dist(f, H) / 2).reshape(len(g), K, R)[np.arange(len(g)), g]
        W = np.exp(lg - lg.max(1, keepdims=True)); W /= W.sum(1, keepdims=True)
        Z = np.zeros_like(Z)
        for r in range(R): Z[np.arange(len(g)), g * R + r] = W[:, r]
    return f                                                # the fit from the last M-step

def mda_predict(f, H, R, K=K):
    lg = np.log(f["pi"]) - dist(f, H) / 2
    return np.exp(lg - lg.max(1, keepdims=True)).reshape(len(H), K, R).sum(2).argmax(1)

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • LDA is optimal scoring: give the classes scores, regress them linearly on x, and choose scores and coefficients together to minimise the residual. The best coefficients are LDA's discriminant directions.
  • FDA swaps the linear regression for any flexible one (polynomials, splines, MARS), so the boundaries can curve; classify by the weighted distance to each class's average fitted scores.
  • With a linear smoother, FDA costs one multi-response regression of the indicator matrix and one K × K eigen-problem; nothing is refitted.
  • PDA adds a quadratic penalty β⊤Ωβ that matches the problem, such as smoothness along a signal: equivalently, LDA with within-class covariance ΣW + λΩ/N.
  • MDA models each class as a mixture of Gaussians with a shared covariance, fitted by EM; its M-step is optimal scoring on a blurred response matrix, which allows reduced rank and flexible versions.

Check your understanding

Why do the optimal scores need a normalisation such as mean square one?

Without it, the residual is minimised by setting every score to zero (or all to the same constant) and every coefficient to zero: a perfect fit to a meaningless target. Fixing the scores' mean square at one rules that out, and making different score sets uncorrelated stops them repeating each other.

You run FDA with plain linear regression and keep all K − 1 dimensions. How do its predictions compare with LDA's?

They are identical (with equal class priors, or after adding the prior term). The optimal-scoring coefficients point along LDA's discriminant directions, and the weights wl = 1/(rl2(1 − rl2)) turn the distance into LDA's Mahalanobis distance, up to a term shared by every class. On the vowels, both make 257 test errors.

In the FDA computation, why is the first eigenvector of the K × K problem thrown away?

It is the constant score, the same number for every class. A regression with an intercept fits a constant perfectly, so its eigenvalue is 1, but it says nothing about which class a point is in. Dropping it also forces the remaining scores to have mean zero.

PDA with a roughness penalty beat LDA clearly on the waveform data. When would you expect the penalty to help little?

When the features are not ordered in any meaningful way (so smoothness along the index means nothing), or when there are many training examples per coefficient, so LDA's estimates are already precise. A penalty helps when it encodes something true about the problem and the data are too few to learn it unaided.

Why does MDA use one covariance matrix for all subclasses rather than one each?

One shared Σ keeps the parameter count low (like LDA rather than QDA) and keeps the model inside the optimal-scoring framework, so the M-step is a weighted LDA and reduced-rank and flexible versions come for free. Several subclasses per class already give curved, irregular boundaries; separate covariances would add many parameters and make EM less stable.
