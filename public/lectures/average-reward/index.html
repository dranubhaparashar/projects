Continuing Tasks & Average Reward

When the task never ends, judge a policy by what it earns per step

A server farm, a thermostat or a trading desk has no final step and no reset button: it runs forever. Discounting was invented for exactly this case, but once values come from a function approximator it quietly stops helping. This chapter swaps it for a cleaner yardstick, the average reward per step, and learns it with differential Sarsa. In the figure, ten servers take customers of four priorities, paying 1, 2, 4 or 8 when a customer is let in. A gatekeeper decides who gets a server. Tap the picture to change the gatekeeper and watch the reward per step settle.

The problem

Control with approximation, and no last step

The previous chapters learned to predict values with a function approximator v̂(s, w). Control needs action values instead: q̂(s, a, w) ≈ q*(s, a), so the agent can compare its options and act greedily. In episodic tasks the step is small: replace v̂ by q̂ in the semi-gradient update, pick actions ε-greedily, and you have semi-gradient Sarsa. The interesting part of this chapter is what happens in continuing tasks, where the interaction never breaks into episodes. There, the return Gt is an infinite sum, and the usual fix is a discount γ < 1 that makes it finite.

Look at what discounting does to a real reward stream. Below, the access-control queue from the hero runs with a gatekeeper that accepts everyone; each bar is one step’s reward. The curve shows how much a reward k steps ahead counts. Push γ towards 1: the return grows without limit, while (1 − γ) times the return settles on one number, the reward per step.

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta_t &= R_{t+1} + \gamma\,\hat q(S_{t+1},A_{t+1},\mathbf w) \\ &\quad - \hat q(S_t,A_t,\mathbf w) \\ \mathbf w &\leftarrow \mathbf w + \alpha\,\delta_t\,\nabla \hat q(S_t,A_t,\mathbf w) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: semi-gradient Sarsa. δt is the TD error: what one step of experience says the value of (St, At) should be, minus what the approximator says now. The weights move a step α in the direction that raises q̂(St, At), scaled by that error. “Semi” because the target also depends on w and we do not differentiate through it.
Say it“w moves by alpha times the TD error times the gradient of q-hat at the state and action just taken.”
\[ \begin{aligned} G_t &= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^{k} R_{t+k+1} \\ (1-\gamma)\,G_t &\;\xrightarrow{\;\gamma\to1\;}\; r(\pi) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the discounted return weights a reward k steps away by γk, so the effective horizon is about 1/(1 − γ) steps. In a task that never ends, Gt grows like r(π)/(1 − γ) as γ approaches 1, where r(π) is the long-run reward per step. Multiply by (1 − γ) and the discount disappears, leaving the average.
Say it“G-t is the sum of gamma to the k times the reward k plus one steps ahead; one minus gamma times G-t tends to the average reward.”

Section 10.2

Semi-gradient n-step Sarsa

One-step Sarsa bootstraps after a single reward. The n-step version waits n steps, adds up the n rewards it saw, and only then leans on its own estimate q̂ of where it ended up. Large n is closer to Monte Carlo: less bias from a poor q̂, more variance from the rewards. The classic test is Mountain Car: an underpowered car in a valley must rock back and forth to climb the right-hand hill, at a cost of −1 per step until it gets there. q̂ is linear in tile-coded features: 8 offset grids of 9 × 9 tiles over position and velocity, so each state switches on exactly 8 of 648 features per action. Each point in the left plot is the average number of steps per episode over the first 50 episodes, for one n and one step size. Choose n and α, then run: the best results come from an intermediate n, and large n needs a small α.

\[ \begin{aligned} G_{t:t+n} &= R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \dots \\ &\quad + \gamma^{n-1} R_{t+n} \\ &\quad + \gamma^{n}\,\hat q(S_{t+n},A_{t+n},\mathbf w_{t+n-1}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: n real rewards, then the current estimate of the state–action pair reached after n steps. If the episode ends before t + n, the sum stops at the end and there is nothing to bootstrap. In Mountain Car γ = 1 and every reward is −1, so Gt:t+n = −n + q̂(St+n, At+n).
Say it“The n-step return is the next n discounted rewards plus gamma to the n times q-hat where you land.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \delta &= G_{t:t+n} - \hat q(S_t,A_t,\mathbf w_{t+n-1}) \\ \mathbf w_{t+n} &= \mathbf w_{t+n-1} \\ &\quad + \alpha\,\delta\,\nabla\hat q(S_t,A_t,\mathbf w_{t+n-1}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: δ is the n-step error; the update for time t happens n steps late, once its n rewards are known. With tile coding the gradient is 1 on the 8 active tiles of (St, At) and 0 elsewhere, so each of those 8 weights moves by α times the error. Step sizes are quoted as α × 8, the step for the whole estimate.
Say it“Move the weights by alpha times the n-step return minus the current estimate, times the gradient.”

Section 10.3

Average reward: a new yardstick for continuing tasks

Here is the alternative to discounting. Score a policy by the reward it collects per step in the long run, with every step counting equally, now or a million steps from now. This only makes sense if the long run does not depend on where you started. The condition is ergodicity: under π, the chance of being in each state settles to a fixed steady-state distribution μπ(s), whatever the start. Early choices can matter for a while, but their effect fades. The chain below has three states; each step it pays the reward of the state it is in, then moves on round the loop with the probability you set, or stays put. Three runs start in A, B and C. Slow down one of the moves, then cut the loop (B returns to A, and C keeps to itself) and see what goes wrong.

\[ \begin{aligned} r(\pi) &= \lim_{h\to\infty}\frac1h\sum_{t=1}^{h}\mathbb E_\pi\big[R_t\big] \\ &= \sum_s \mu_\pi(s)\sum_a \pi(a\mid s) \\ &\qquad\times \sum_{s',r} p(s',r\mid s,a)\,r \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first line is the definition: the average expected reward over the first h steps, as h grows (Eπ means the actions are chosen by π, from a start state S0). The second says what it equals for an ergodic chain: weight each state by how often you are there, then average the reward of what π does there. The start state S0 has disappeared. Policies that reach the highest r(π) are the optimal ones.
Say it“r of pi is the long-run average reward per step: the sum over states of mu of s times the expected reward from s.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\sum_s \mu_\pi(s)\sum_a \pi(a\mid s)\,p(s'\mid s,a) \\ &\quad = \mu_\pi(s') \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the steady state is a balance. If the states are filled in proportion to μπ and everyone takes one step under π, the proportions come out the same. A state you leave quickly gets a small share of μ; a state you linger in gets a large one.
Say it“Start distributed as mu, take one step under pi, and you are still distributed as mu.”

Section 10.3, continued

Differential returns and differential values

Values still matter: they are how an agent compares actions. In the average-reward setting the return is redefined as the reward above the average, added up forever: the differential return. Its expectation is the differential value: how much better off you are, in total, for starting here rather than in a typical state. Since the rewards above average sum to zero over the long run, these values are relative; adding the same constant to all of them changes nothing, so here they are pinned by ∑s μ(s) v(s) = 0. The Bellman equations keep their shape: drop γ and subtract r(π) from every reward. The ring below has five states; each step pays the reward of the current state, then the agent moves on to the next, or stays with the probability you choose. Click a state to change its reward, and watch the two ways of learning R̄ below it.

\[ \begin{aligned} G_t &= \textstyle\sum_{k\ge 0}\big(R_{t+k+1}-r(\pi)\big) \\ v_\pi(s) &= \textstyle\sum_a \pi(a\mid s) \\ &\quad\times\textstyle\sum_{s',r} p(s',r\mid s,a) \\ &\quad\times\big[r - r(\pi) + v_\pi(s')\big] \\ q_\pi(s,a) &= \textstyle\sum_{s',r} p(s',r\mid s,a) \\ &\quad\times\big[r - r(\pi) \\ &\quad + \textstyle\sum_{a'}\pi(a'\mid s')\,q_\pi(s',a')\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the differential return adds up how far each reward lands above or below the average. vπ and qπ are its expected values, and they obey the familiar one-step consistency: value here equals reward minus the average, plus value next. The optimality versions replace the average over π by a max over actions and r(π) by the best average r*, as in v*(s) = maxa ∑ p(s′, r | s, a)[r − r* + v*(s′)].
Say it“Value here is the expected reward minus the average reward, plus the value where you land; no gamma anywhere.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \delta_t &= R_{t+1} - \bar R_t \\ &\quad + \hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf w_t) - \hat v(S_t,\mathbf w_t) \\ \delta_t &= R_{t+1} - \bar R_t \\ &\quad + \hat q(S_{t+1},A_{t+1},\mathbf w_t) \\ &\quad - \hat q(S_t,A_t,\mathbf w_t) \\ \bar R_{t+1} &= \bar R_t + \beta\,\delta_t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the differential TD errors, for state values and for action values. R̄t is the agent’s running estimate of r(π), and it is learned from the same error with its own step size β. Why δ and not simply Rt+1 − R̄t? Once the values are right, δ strips out the predictable ups and downs of the rewards, so R̄ jitters far less.
Say it“Delta is reward minus R-bar, plus the next value, minus this value; R-bar moves by beta times delta.”

Example 10.2 · differential semi-gradient Sarsa

The access-control queuing task

Put the pieces together. Differential semi-gradient Sarsa is ordinary semi-gradient Sarsa with γ removed and the running average R̄ subtracted from every reward. Its test case is the queue from the hero: ten servers; a never-empty queue whose head customer has priority 1, 2, 4 or 8 with equal chance; accepting pays the priority and takes a server, rejecting pays 0; each busy server finishes with probability 0.06 per step. When no server is free the customer must be rejected. The state is (free servers, priority of the head customer): 44 states, so q̂ is a table, which is the special case of linear features with one feature per state–action pair. Everything starts at zero, and the agent learns from one unbroken stream of two million steps, with α = β = 0.01 and ε = 0.1 by default. Because the model is known, this page can also solve it exactly, by relative value iteration, and grade what Sarsa found. Train, then change β or ε and train again.

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta &= R - \bar R + \hat q(S',A',\mathbf w) \\ &\quad - \hat q(S,A,\mathbf w) \\ \bar R &\leftarrow \bar R + \beta\,\delta \\ \mathbf w &\leftarrow \mathbf w + \alpha\,\delta\,\nabla\hat q(S,A,\mathbf w) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each step: act, see R and S′, choose A′ ε-greedily, form the differential TD error δ, nudge the average-reward estimate by βδ and the weights by αδ times the gradient. With a table, the gradient is 1 for the entry (S, A) and 0 elsewhere, so only that entry moves. There is no discount to tune; β takes its place as the extra knob.
Say it“Delta is reward minus R-bar plus q-hat next minus q-hat now; R-bar gets beta delta, the weights get alpha delta times the gradient.”

Section 10.4

Why discounting stops helping

In a table, discounting is harmless: there is a policy that is best from every state at once, and γ just picks which one. With function approximation that guarantee is gone, because changing the weights to improve one state can make another worse, so we must rank policies by one number. The obvious choice is the discounted value averaged over the states the policy actually visits. Do the sum and the discount falls out: that number is r(π)/(1 − γ), so it ranks policies exactly as the average reward does, whatever γ is. Discounting a continuing task, judged this way, is futile. Deeper still, the policy improvement theorem, which says that acting greedily on qπ can only help, no longer holds once q is approximated: improving the estimate in one state can lower the true value elsewhere. In the little world below, a hub offers three round trips; on each trip, only the last step pays. Lower γ and watch the hub’s own discounted value change its mind, while the averaged objective never does.

\[ \begin{aligned} J(\pi) &= \sum_s \mu_\pi(s)\, v^{\gamma}_\pi(s) \\ &= \sum_s \mu_\pi(s) \sum_a \pi(a\mid s) \\ &\qquad\times \sum_{s',r} p(s',r\mid s,a) \\ &\qquad\times\big[r + \gamma v^{\gamma}_\pi(s')\big] \\ &= r(\pi) + \gamma \sum_{s'} \mu_\pi(s')\, v^{\gamma}_\pi(s') \\ &= r(\pi) + \gamma J(\pi) \\ &= \frac{r(\pi)}{1-\gamma} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first line is the averaged discounted objective. The next three expand vγ by its Bellman equation. Then the reward part averages to r(π), and the steady-state balance turns “μ, then one step” back into μ, so the second part is γ times J itself. Solve for J. Every policy’s score is its average reward times the same constant 1/(1 − γ), so γ cannot change the ranking.
Say it“J of pi is the mu-weighted discounted value, and it equals r of pi over one minus gamma.”

Section 10.5

Differential semi-gradient n-step Sarsa

The n-step idea carries over directly. The n-step differential return adds up n rewards, each minus the current average estimate, and then bootstraps from q̂ n steps later. The error it produces drives both R̄ and w, exactly as in the one-step method; the updates simply run n steps behind the action they judge. There is no γ and no episode end to handle, which makes the algorithm shorter than its episodic cousin. Below, each method learns the access-control queue for one million steps; every 50,000 steps its current greedy policy is frozen and graded exactly on the known model. Run the race, then change α: bigger n needs a smaller step.

\[ \begin{aligned} G_{t:t+n} = {} & R_{t+1}-\bar R + R_{t+2}-\bar R \\ & + \dots + R_{t+n}-\bar R \\ & + \hat q(S_{t+n},A_{t+n},\mathbf w) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: R̄ is the current estimate of r(π) when the update is made, at step t + n. Compared with the one-step error, n real rewards replace one, and the bootstrap waits n steps.
Say it“The n-step differential return is the next n rewards, each minus R-bar, plus q-hat n steps on.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \delta_t &= G_{t:t+n} - \hat q(S_t,A_t,\mathbf w) \\ \bar R &\leftarrow \bar R + \beta\,\delta_t \\ \mathbf w &\leftarrow \mathbf w + \alpha\,\delta_t\,\nabla\hat q(S_t,A_t,\mathbf w) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the n-step differential TD error is the n-step return minus the estimate for the pair being updated. It moves R̄ by β times itself and the weights by α times itself along the gradient. The agent keeps the last n + 1 states, actions and rewards in a small circular buffer.
Say it“Delta is the n-step return minus q-hat; R-bar moves by beta delta, w by alpha delta times the gradient.”

Real-world project

Project: an admission desk for a Google cluster

The queue so far was invented. Here the customers are real: 31,258 jobs submitted to a Google compute cluster of about 12,500 machines during 34 hours from about 20:20 US Eastern time on 1 May 2011, replayed in order from the Google cluster-usage trace (clusterdata-2011-2, task_events parts 1–30; Reiss, Wilkes & Hellerstein, 2011; licensed CC BY 4.0). For each job the trace gives its submission time, its priority (0 to 11; per the trace documentation, bigger means more important, 0–1 are “free” and 9–11 “production”), and when it was scheduled and when its tasks ended. The real cluster had far more capacity than this; the project asks a smaller, what-if question. Suppose these jobs arrive at a pool of 128 slots, each job needs one slot for as long as it actually ran (capped at 6 hours), and a desk must accept or turn away each job the moment it arrives, without knowing how long it will run. Accepted jobs pay by priority band: 1 for priority 0, 2 for priority 1, 4 for 2–8, 8 for 9–11. Those payments, the pool size and the cap are this chapter’s choices; the arrivals, priorities and run times are the trace’s.

Unlike the textbook queue, this world is not Markov in the state the desk sees: whether a slot frees soon depends on how long its job has been running, which the desk does not track. So the free-slot count is cut into buckets of 8 (state aggregation, the simplest linear features), and differential Sarsa must cope with an approximation that cannot be exact. It learns by replaying the first 60% of the trace (18,754 jobs, the first 21.0 hours) over and over; it is then tested, greedy and frozen, on the last 40% (12,504 jobs), which it never saw.

import json, heapq, math
import numpy as np

def mulberry32(seed):                       # the page's random-number generator, bit for bit
    s = [seed & 0xFFFFFFFF]
    def rnd():
        a = s[0] = (s[0] + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return rnd

def pick(Q, s, n, eps, rnd):                # epsilon-greedy; no free server means reject
    if n == 0: return 0
    if rnd() < eps: return 1 if rnd() >= 0.5 else 0
    q0, q1 = Q[2 * s], Q[2 * s + 1]
    return 1 if q1 > q0 else 0 if q0 > q1 else (1 if rnd() >= 0.5 else 0)

def diff_sarsa(env, nb, bucket, steps, alpha, beta, eps, n, seed):
    """Differential semi-gradient n-step Sarsa (Sutton & Barto 10.5), one-hot features."""
    rnd, Q, Rb, L = mulberry32(seed), [0.0] * (2 * nb * 4), 0.0, n + 1
    m, i = env.reset(); S, A, R = [0] * L, [0] * L, [0.0] * L
    S[0] = bucket(m) * 4 + i; A[0] = pick(Q, S[0], m, eps, rnd)
    for t in range(steps):
        r, m, i = env.step(m, i, A[t % L])
        j = (t + 1) % L; S[j] = bucket(m) * 4 + i; A[j] = pick(Q, S[j], m, eps, rnd); R[j] = r
        tau = t - n + 1
        if tau >= 0:
            G = 0.0
            for u in range(tau + 1, tau + n + 1): G += R[u % L] - Rb
            G += Q[2 * S[(tau + n) % L] + A[(tau + n) % L]]
            k0 = 2 * S[tau % L] + A[tau % L]; d = G - Q[k0]
            Rb += beta * d; Q[k0] += alpha * d
    return Q, Rb

def disc_sarsa(env, nb, bucket, steps, alpha, eps, gamma, seed):
    rnd, Q = mulberry32(seed), [0.0] * (2 * nb * 4)
    m, i = env.reset(); s = bucket(m) * 4 + i; a = pick(Q, s, m, eps, rnd)
    for t in range(steps):
        r, m, i = env.step(m, i, a); s2 = bucket(m) * 4 + i; a2 = pick(Q, s2, m, eps, rnd)
        Q[2 * s + a] += alpha * (r + gamma * Q[2 * s2 + a2] - Q[2 * s + a]); s, a = s2, a2
    return Q

greedy = lambda Q, nb: [1 if Q[2 * s + 1] >= Q[2 * s] else 0 for s in range(nb * 4)]

# ---------- Example 10.2: the access-control queue, 10 servers, priorities 1, 2, 4, 8, p = 0.06 ----------
class ACQ:
    k, pr, p = 10, (1, 2, 4, 8), 0.06
    def __init__(self, rnd): self.rnd = rnd
    def reset(self): return 10, int(self.rnd() * 4)
    def step(self, n, i, a):
        r = 0
        if a == 1 and n > 0: r = self.pr[i]; n -= 1
        n += sum(1 for _ in range(self.k - n) if self.rnd() < self.p)
        return r, n, int(self.rnd() * 4)

def acq_diff_sarsa(steps, alpha, beta, eps, n, seed):
    rnd = mulberry32(seed); env = ACQ(rnd)
    Q, Rb, L = [0.0] * 88, 0.0, n + 1
    m, i = env.reset(); S, A, R = [0] * L, [0] * L, [0.0] * L
    S[0] = m * 4 + i; A[0] = pick(Q, S[0], m, eps, rnd)
    for t in range(steps):
        r, m, i = env.step(m, i, A[t % L])
        j = (t + 1) % L; S[j] = m * 4 + i; A[j] = pick(Q, S[j], m, eps, rnd); R[j] = r
        tau = t - n + 1
        if tau >= 0:
            G = 0.0
            for u in range(tau + 1, tau + n + 1): G += R[u % L] - Rb
            G += Q[2 * S[(tau + n) % L] + A[(tau + n) % L]]
            k0 = 2 * S[tau % L] + A[tau % L]; d = G - Q[k0]
            Rb += beta * d; Q[k0] += alpha * d
    return Q, Rb

def acq_rvi(pol=None, iters=20000):         # relative value iteration on the exact model
    k, pr, p = ACQ.k, ACQ.pr, ACQ.p
    B = [[math.comb(b, f) * p ** f * (1 - p) ** (b - f) for f in range(b + 1)] for b in range(k + 1)]
    h, g = np.zeros((k + 1) * 4), 0.0
    for _ in range(iters):
        H = h.reshape(k + 1, 4).mean(1); T = np.empty_like(h); act = np.zeros(len(h), int)
        for n in range(k + 1):
            for i in range(4):
                s, best = n * 4 + i, -1e300
                for a in ((0, 1) if n > 0 else (0,)):
                    if pol is not None and n > 0 and a != pol[s]: continue
                    n1 = n - a; v = (pr[i] if a else 0) + sum(q * H[n1 + f] for f, q in enumerate(B[k - n1]))
                    if v > best + 1e-13: best, act[s] = v, a
                T[s] = best
        g = T[k * 4]; d = T - h; h = T - g
        if d.max() - d.min() < 1e-12: break
    return g, act

# ---------- the project: a server pool replaying the Google cluster trace ----------
class Trace:
    def __init__(self, D, k, rew, a, b, loop): self.D, self.k, self.rew, self.a, self.b, self.loop = D, k, rew, a, b, loop
    def reset(self): self.H, self.pos, self.clock = [], self.a, 0; return self.k, self.D["band"][self.a]
    def step(self, n, i, act):
        D, r = self.D, 0
        if act == 1 and n > 0: r = self.rew[i]; heapq.heappush(self.H, self.clock + 1000 * D["dur"][self.pos])
        self.clock += D["gap"][self.pos]; self.pos += 1
        if self.pos >= self.b: self.pos = self.a if self.loop else self.b - 1
        while self.H and self.H[0] <= self.clock: heapq.heappop(self.H)
        return r, self.k - len(self.H), D["band"][self.pos]

def run(D, k, rew, a, b, pol):              # replay a segment once with a fixed policy: reward per arrival
    env = Trace(D, k, rew, a, b, False); n, i = env.reset(); tot = 0
    for _ in range(a, b):
        r, n, i = env.step(n, i, pol(n, i) if n > 0 else 0); tot += r
    return tot / (b - a)

Wrap-up · section 10.6

Five lines to remember

  • Control with approximation learns q̂(s, a, w) and acts ε-greedily on it; semi-gradient Sarsa, one-step or n-step, is the workhorse, and an intermediate n usually learns fastest.
  • In a continuing task there are no episodes. Judge a policy by its average reward r(π) per step, which for an ergodic chain is ∑ μπ(s) × expected reward, whatever the start.
  • Differential values measure reward above the average, summed forever; their Bellman equations are the familiar ones with γ removed and r(π) subtracted from every reward.
  • Differential Sarsa learns R̄ with the same TD error that trains q̂: δ = R − R̄ + q̂(S′, A′) − q̂(S, A); R̄ += βδ, w += αδ∇q̂.
  • With approximation, the averaged discounted objective is r(π)/(1 − γ), so γ changes nothing about which policy is best; it survives only as a solution-method knob.

Check your understanding

Why do the differential values in this chapter need an extra condition such as ∑ μ(s) v(s) = 0?

Their Bellman equations only involve differences of values (value here versus value next), so adding the same constant to every v(s) gives another solution. One extra condition picks one member of that family. Differences between states, which are all that greedy action choice uses, are the same for every member.

In the hub world, γ = 0.5 picks the one-step trip. Is that wrong?

Not by its own measure: with γ = 0.5 the hub’s discounted value really is highest for the quick trip, because later rewards are worth little. But that policy earns less reward per step, and the visit-weighted discounted objective J(π) = r(π)/(1 − γ) prefers the trip with the highest average for every γ.

Why update R̄ with βδ rather than with β(R − R̄)?

Both average to the right answer, but R − R̄ carries all the predictable swings of the reward (a big payoff every few steps), so R̄ keeps jittering. Once the values are learned, δ has those swings explained away by the value difference, leaving only the genuinely unpredictable part, so R̄ settles much more tightly.

What breaks if a continuing task is not ergodic?

The long-run average can depend on where you start (as in the cut chain, where starting in C gives a different average than starting in A), so r(π) is no longer a single number for a policy, and the differential values built on it are not defined.

In the cluster project, why can the reservation rule beat the learned desk on training jobs, yet the methods still differ on test?

The reservation rule was chosen by searching 180 rules directly on the training replay, so it is tuned to exactly those jobs. Sarsa only sees the coarse state (bucketed free slots, band), learns from noisy ε-greedy experience in a world that is not Markov in that state, and must generalise. Test hours show which choices carry over to jobs never seen.
