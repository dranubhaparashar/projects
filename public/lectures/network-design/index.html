Designing a Network: Units, Outputs, Depth & Width

The idea

Why learn the basis at all?

The linear models of Part 1 bend by using basis functions: they replace x with a list of fixed features φ1(x), …, φM(x), such as polynomial terms or Gaussian bumps, and fit a weight for each. The features are fixed before any data arrive, and that is their weakness. To cover a D-dimensional input with bumps spaced m to an axis you need mD of them: 10 to an axis in 10 dimensions is ten billion bumps. That is the curse of dimensionality, applied to features.

Real data are kinder than the worst case. They usually lie close to a low-dimensional surface inside the big space, and the target often changes along only a few directions. A fixed grid cannot exploit either fact, because it was laid down before anyone looked. A neural network can: its hidden units are basis functions with parameters, h(wj⊤x + bj), and training moves them to where the data are. This is how Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville introduce deep feedforward networks: rather than choose a generic φ (as kernel methods do) or hand-engineer one, learn φ from the data. Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman reach the same model from statistics, as projection pursuit regression with a fixed-shape ridge function. Below, 400 points lie along a closed curve that winds through D dimensions. Raise the dimension and compare the fixed grid with the cells the data actually use.

\[ \begin{aligned} y_{\text{fixed}}(\mathbf x) &= \sum_{j=1}^{M} w_j\,\phi_j(\mathbf x) \\ M &= m^{D} \\ y_{\text{learned}}(\mathbf x) &= c + \sum_{j=1}^{H} v_j\, h(z_j) \\ z_j &= \mathbf w_j^{\top}\mathbf x + b_j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a fixed-basis model has one weight per feature and nothing else to learn, but tiling D dimensions at m bumps per axis takes mD features. The network’s features h(wj⊤x + bj) have their own direction wj and offset bj, learned with everything else, so H units can sit where the data are, and H does not have to grow with mD.
Say it“Fixed: y is a sum of M fixed features, with M equal to m to the D. Learned: y is a sum of v-j times h of w-j transpose x plus b-j.”
\[ \text{share} = \frac{\#\,\text{occupied cells}}{m^{D}} \]
Read it as: divide the unit cube into mD equal cells, the cells a fixed grid of bumps would have to cover. Count the cells that contain at least one of the 400 points. With N points, at most N cells can be used, so the share falls at least as fast as N/mD: almost every fixed feature would sit in empty space and never fire.
Say it“The share used is the number of occupied cells over m to the D.”

The last layer

Output units: match the target, then take the log

Almost every modern network is trained by maximum likelihood: the last layer outputs the parameters of a probability distribution over the target, and the loss is the negative log of the probability given to the true answer. Choose the distribution and the output unit follows. A real-valued target gets a linear unit giving the mean of a Gaussian, and the loss becomes squared error. A yes/no target gets a sigmoid unit giving the probability of a Bernoulli. One of K classes gets a softmax over K scores. The pairing matters for learning, not just for tidiness. The log in the loss undoes the exp inside the sigmoid and the softmax, so the gradient stays large exactly when the network is confidently wrong. Use squared error on a sigmoid instead, and a confidently wrong unit hardly learns. Below, z is the pre-activation of the output unit. Drag z far to the wrong side and compare the two gradients.

\[ \begin{aligned} -\log p_{\text{G}} &= \tfrac{(y-z)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \tfrac12\log 2\pi\sigma^2 \\ -\log p_{\text{B}} &= \zeta\big((1-2y)\,z\big) \\ -\log p_{\text{C}} &= -z_y + \log\textstyle\sum_j e^{z_j} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: G, B and C stand for Gaussian, Bernoulli and categorical. The linear unit’s output z is the Gaussian mean, so its loss is squared error divided by 2σ². For a 0/1 label y with p(y = 1) = σ(z), the loss is the softplus ζ(a) = log(1 + ea) of −z when y = 1 and of +z when y = 0: close to 0 when z already agrees, and growing like |z| when it does not. Softmax gives py = ezy / Σj ezj, and its log is the score of the true class minus the log-sum-exp of all scores.
Say it“Gaussian: y minus z squared over two sigma squared. Bernoulli: softplus of one minus two y, times z. Categorical: minus z-y plus log-sum-exp of the z's.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial(-\log p_{\text{B}})}{\partial z} &= \sigma - y \\ \frac{\partial (\sigma-y)^2}{\partial z} &= 2\,(\sigma-y)\,\sigma\,(1-\sigma) \\ \frac{\partial(-\log p_{\text{C}})}{\partial z_i} &= p_i - \mathbb 1[i=y] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: σ is short for σ(z). With the matched loss, the gradient is simply “prediction minus target”, so it can only be small when the prediction is right. Squared error on a sigmoid multiplies that same difference by σ(z)(1 − σ(z)), which is nearly 0 whenever |z| is large, whether the unit is right or badly wrong. The softmax gradient is the same story with K outputs.
Say it“The log-loss gradient is sigma of z minus y; the squared-error gradient is two times sigma minus y times sigma times one minus sigma; the softmax gradient is p-i minus the indicator that i is y.”

Other output types: predict the noise as well

Nothing forces the last layer to give only a mean. If a second output unit gives the spread, the network can say “here I am sure, there I am not”: this is heteroscedastic regression. The spread must be positive, so the unit’s output is exponentiated (or passed through softplus) before use. Goodfellow and colleagues list more options in the same spirit: one Bernoulli per output for multi-label targets, a full covariance, and mixtures of Gaussians for targets with several plausible values, which the next chapter builds. Below, 120 points have noise that grows with x. Make the noise grow faster and compare a model with one constant σ against one whose second output learns log σ(x).

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu(x) &= a + b\,x \\ \sigma(x) &= e^{\,c + d\,x} \\ -\log p(y\mid x) &= \log\sigma(x) + \tfrac12\log 2\pi \\ &\quad + \frac{\big(y-\mu(x)\big)^2}{2\,\sigma(x)^2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: two output units, one for the mean and one for log σ. Exponentiating keeps σ positive for any weights. The loss rewards a small σ only where the points really are close to the mean: shrinking σ lowers the first term but inflates the second wherever the residual is large. With d fixed at 0, σ is constant and the best a, b are ordinary least squares.
Say it“Minus log p is log sigma of x, plus y minus mu of x squared over two sigma of x squared, plus a constant.”

The bend

Hidden units: ReLU and its relatives

The rectified linear unit, g(z) = max(0, z), is the default hidden unit, and the reason is its gradient. When the unit is on, the gradient is exactly 1, so the signal passes back undamped, layer after layer; that is what a sigmoid, whose slope is at most ¼, cannot do. Its blind spot is the other half: when z < 0 the gradient is exactly 0, and a unit that is off for every example (a “dead” unit) never learns again. The generalisations give the negative side a slope α: absolute value rectification (α = −1), the leaky ReLU (α = 0.01), and the parametric ReLU, which learns α. Maxout goes further: each unit outputs the largest of k linear functions, so it can learn a convex activation of its own, at the price of k times the weights. Softplus is a smooth ReLU, yet it has not trained better in practice. Sigmoid and tanh saturate at both ends; tanh at least passes through 0 with slope 1, so a small tanh network starts out nearly linear. Rarer units include radial basis functions, which fire near a template; hard tanh, a clipped line; and plain linear units, which factor a big weight matrix into two thin ones. Below, z is drawn from a normal distribution, as it is at initialisation. Switch units and spread z out: watch how much gradient gets through on average and how many inputs give none.

\[ \begin{aligned} g(z) &= \max(0,z) + \alpha\min(0,z) \\ g_{\max}(\mathbf x) &= \max_{j\le k}\big(\mathbf w_j^{\top}\mathbf x + b_j\big) \\ \zeta(z) &= \log(1+e^{z}) \\ \zeta'(z) &= \sigma(z) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first line is the whole rectifier family: α = 0 is ReLU, α = −1 gives |z|, a small α is the leaky ReLU, and a learned α is PReLU. A maxout unit keeps k separate linear functions of the input and outputs the largest: with two pieces of slope 0 and 1 it is a ReLU; with more, it is any convex piecewise-linear shape. Softplus is the smooth version of the ReLU, and its slope is the sigmoid.
Say it“g of z is max of zero and z plus alpha times min of zero and z; maxout is the max over j of w-j transpose x plus b-j; softplus is log of one plus e to the z.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \bar g' &= \mathbb E_{z\sim\mathcal N(0,s^2)}\big[\,g'(z)\,\big] \\ \text{dead share} &= P\big(|g'(z)| < 0.01\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: during backpropagation every gradient that crosses this unit is multiplied by g′(z). Averaging g′ over the inputs the unit sees (here z ~ N(0, s²), computed by numerical integration) says how much signal passes on average. The dead share is the fraction of inputs for which almost nothing passes. (For the leaky ReLU, whose negative slope is exactly 0.01, that negative half is counted as passing.)
Say it“g-prime bar is the expected slope for z normal with spread s; the dead share is the probability that the slope is below one hundredth.”

Width

Universal approximation: one wide layer is enough, in principle

How expressive is a network with a single hidden layer? The universal approximation theorem says: as expressive as you like. Cybenko (1989) proved it for sigmoid units, Hornik, Stinchcombe and White (1989) for any squashing function, and Leshno and colleagues (1993) for every activation that is not a polynomial, the ReLU included. Any continuous function on a bounded region can be matched to within any ε by enough hidden units. For ReLUs in one dimension the proof is a picture: each unit adds one kink, so N units can join N + 1 points of the target with straight lines. Two warnings come with the theorem. It promises that the weights exist, not that training will find them. And “enough” can be astronomically many: in d dimensions the number of units needed can grow exponentially with d. Add units and watch the error fall; the kink target is worth trying with an even number of units.

\[ \begin{aligned} F(\mathbf x) &= \sum_{j=1}^{N} v_j\, g\big(\mathbf w_j^{\top}\mathbf x + b_j\big) \\ &\forall f \in C(K),\ \forall \varepsilon>0: \\ &\exists N, v_j, \mathbf w_j, b_j: \\ &\sup_{\mathbf x\in K} |f(\mathbf x) - F(\mathbf x)| < \varepsilon \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: F is a network with one hidden layer of N units. K is a closed, bounded region of input space and C(K) the continuous functions on it. For any of them, and any tolerance ε, some finite one-hidden-layer network stays within ε of f everywhere on K. The condition on g is only that it is not a polynomial (Leshno et al., 1993). Nothing is said about how big N must be.
Say it“For every continuous f and every epsilon there is an N and weights such that the largest gap between f and the network on K is below epsilon.”
\[ \begin{aligned} F(x) &= f(t_0) \\ &\quad + \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} c_j\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x - t_j) \\ c_j &= s_j - s_{j-1} \\ \max |f - F| &\le \tfrac18\, h^2 \max |f''| \\ h &= 4/N \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: place N + 1 evenly spaced knots t0, …, tN on [−2, 2], h apart, and let sj be the slope of the straight line from knot j to knot j + 1 (with s−1 = 0). Unit j switches on at tj and changes the slope by cj = sj − sj−1, so F joins the knots exactly. For a smooth target the gap shrinks like h²: double the units, quarter the error.
Say it“F is f at t-nought plus the sum over units of the change in slope times ReLU of x minus t-j; the error is at most an eighth of h squared times the largest second derivative.”

Depth

Depth: exponentially many pieces from a few units

If one layer is enough, why stack them? Because some functions that a deep network builds cheaply would need an enormous single layer. The cleanest example uses a tent: two ReLU units make a function h that climbs from 0 to 1 on [0, ½] and comes back down on [½, 1]. Feed the tent into another tent and the result goes up and down twice; L tents in a row give 2L−1 teeth and 2L straight pieces, from only 2L hidden units. A single hidden layer of N ReLUs in one dimension has at most N + 1 pieces, so it needs 2L − 1 units to match: an exponential gap. Telgarsky (2016) turned this into a proof that some deep networks cannot be approximated by shallow ones unless the shallow ones are exponentially wide, and Montúfar and colleagues (2014) counted the regions deep rectifier networks can carve in many dimensions. The catch is in the word can. With random weights, the count grows far more slowly: Hanin and Rolnick (2019) found it tracks the total number of units rather than exploding with depth. Add layers, then switch to random weights.

\[ \begin{aligned} h(x) &= 2\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x) \\ &\quad - 4\,\mathrm{ReLU}\big(x-\tfrac12\big) \\ g_L &= \underbrace{h\circ h\circ\cdots\circ h}_{L\ \text{layers}} \\ \#\text{pieces}(g_L) &= 2^{L} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: on [0, 1], h rises with slope 2 to 1 at x = ½, then the second unit switches on and turns the slope to −2. Since h maps [0, 1] back onto [0, 1], it can be applied again, and each application doubles the number of teeth. The deep network uses 2 units per layer and 6L + 1 parameters in all.
Say it“h is two ReLU of x minus four ReLU of x minus a half; g-L is h composed with itself L times, and it has two to the L pieces.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \#\text{regions} &\ge \prod_{l=1}^{L-1}\Big\lfloor \frac{n}{d}\Big\rfloor^{d} \\ &\quad\times \sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom{n}{j} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Montúfar et al. (2014) showed that a rectifier network with d inputs and L hidden layers of width n ≥ d can be given weights that carve its input space into at least this many linear regions. The product grows exponentially with depth; a single layer with the same total number of units has only polynomially many in n. With d = 1 and n = 2 the bound is 3 · 2L−1, more than the tent construction’s 2L.
Say it“The number of regions is at least the product over layers of n over d floored, to the d, times the sum over j up to d of n choose j.”

Weight space

Many weights, one function; many minima

Take a trained network with M hidden units and swap two of them, moving each unit’s incoming and outgoing weights together. The weight vector is different; the function is not. Bishop counts these weight-space symmetries: M! orderings of the units, and for tanh units, which are odd (tanh(−a) = −tanh(a)), a sign flip of each unit’s weights in and out, 2M more. Every function a tanh network computes is therefore reached by at least M!·2M different weight vectors. ReLU units have a continuous version: scale a unit’s incoming weights by any c > 0 and its outgoing weight by 1/c. Two consequences follow. The loss surface repeats itself, so it has many equally good minima. And you cannot average two trained networks weight by weight: the average of a network and its own relabelled copy is a different, usually worse, network. Swap and flip units, then average the result with the original.

\[ \begin{aligned} y(x) &= c + \textstyle\sum_{j} v_j \tanh(w_j x + b_j) \\ \theta_j &\to -\theta_j \\ \text{or}\ \ j &\leftrightarrow k: \ \ y\ \text{unchanged} \\ N_{\text{eq}} &\ge M!\,2^{M} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: θj = (wj, bj, vj) are unit j’s weights in and out. Because tanh is odd, flipping the sign of everything that enters and leaves unit j gives vj tanh(wjx + bj) back exactly. Because the sum does not care about order, any relabelling of the units gives the same y. Neq, the number of weight vectors giving the same y, is therefore at least M!·2M: each of the M! orders can be combined with any of the 2M sign patterns.
Say it“Flipping the signs of a unit, or swapping two units, leaves y unchanged, so there are at least M factorial times two to the M equivalent weight vectors.”

Multiple minima, and what to do about them

Symmetry explains copies of the same minimum. The loss of a network also has genuinely different minima, and which one training reaches depends on where it starts. Hastie and colleagues give the practical advice: start from small random weights (all zeros would leave every unit identical forever, because they would all receive the same gradient), try several starts, and then either keep the start with the lowest penalised training error or, usually better, average the predictions of all of them. Averaging predictions is safe because predictions do not care about labelling; averaging weights is not. Here a network with 6 tanh units is fitted ten times to 30 noisy points, by BFGS with a little weight decay. Shrink the starting scale to zero, then try each start.

\[ \begin{aligned} R(\theta) &= \sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(y_i - f(x_i;\theta)\big)^2 \\ &\quad + \lambda\,\Vert \mathbf w\Vert^2 \\ \bar f(x) &= \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} f\big(x;\hat\theta_s\big) \\ &\ne f\Big(x;\ \frac1S\sum_s \hat\theta_s\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each of the S = 10 starts θs(0), drawn uniformly from [−a, a], is run to its own minimum θ̂s of the penalised squared error (λ = 0.001 on the weights, not the biases). The average of the ten fitted functions is a sensible committee; the function of the averaged weights is not, because the starts may have put the same feature in different units, or with opposite signs.
Say it“Minimise the squared error plus lambda times the squared weights; the average of the predictions is not the prediction of the average weights.”

Width in practice

How many hidden units? A simulated study

Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman’s advice is short: it is better to have too many hidden units than too few. Too few and the model cannot bend enough; too many and the extra flexibility can be reined in with weight decay, which shrinks the unneeded weights towards zero. Typical numbers run from 5 to 100 units, more with more inputs and more data, and the inputs should be standardised so that one penalty suits every weight. The number of layers is chosen from background knowledge and experiment. They back the advice with a simulation, and this section re-runs one like it in your browser. The truth is a sum of two sigmoids of two inputs; 100 training points carry noise with a signal-to-noise ratio of 4; networks with 0 to 10 sigmoid units are each fitted from 10 random starts by BFGS, and scored on 10,000 fresh points. The score is test error divided by the noise variance, so 1 is perfect. Compare no weight decay with λ = 0.1, and step through the number of units.

\[ \begin{aligned} Y &= \sigma(\mathbf a_1^{\top}X) + \sigma(\mathbf a_2^{\top}X) + \varepsilon \\ \mathbf a_1 &= (3, 3),\ \ \mathbf a_2 = (3, -3) \\ X &\sim \mathcal N(0, I_2) \\ \frac{\operatorname{Var} f(X)}{\operatorname{Var}\varepsilon} &= 4 \\ \text{score} &= \frac{\text{test MSE}}{\sigma_\varepsilon^2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the target is itself a network with two sigmoid units, so 2 units is the right size. The noise variance is set to a quarter of the signal’s variance. Test MSE is the mean of (y − f̂(x))² over 10,000 fresh points. Each fit minimises the penalised squared error R(θ) + λJ(θ) of the previous section, with J the sum of squared weights; with 0 units the model is a linear regression. Even the true f would score about 1, since the noise cannot be predicted.
Say it“Y is sigma of a-one transpose X plus sigma of a-two transpose X plus noise, with signal-to-noise ratio four; the score is test mean squared error over the noise variance.”

Architecture

Choosing a shape: budget, compute and skip connections

A fully connected network is described by its depth L and width W, and every choice of the two costs parameters and arithmetic. At a fixed budget of parameters, depth and width trade against each other, and there is real evidence that the trade can favour depth. Goodfellow and colleagues report that on house-number transcription, deeper networks generalised better than shallower ones with the same number of weights, while simply adding weights to a shallow network did not help. Russell and Norvig make the same point when discussing generalisation: architecture choices act like prior beliefs, and convolutional and recurrent layers are architecture choices that build in what we know about images and sequences. Chains of layers are not the only topology: skip connections carry a layer’s input past it, which gives gradients a short route back to the early layers. Cost matters too. Hastie and colleagues note that fitting a network takes work proportional to the number of examples times the number of weights, for every pass over the data. Set a budget and choose a depth, and see what width it buys and what it costs.

\[ \begin{aligned} P(L,W) &= (d+1)\,W \\ &\quad + (L-1)\,(W+1)\,W \\ &\quad + (W+1)\,k \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first layer has d inputs plus a bias for each of W units; each of the L − 1 later hidden layers has W inputs plus a bias for each of W units; the output layer has W + 1 for each of k outputs. The width is the largest W that keeps P within the budget. Depth costs W² per layer, so deep networks must be narrower.
Say it“P is d plus one times W, plus L minus one times W plus one times W, plus W plus one times k.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{forward} &\approx 2P \ \ \text{flops per example} \\ \text{training} &\approx 6\,N\,P\,E \ \ \text{flops} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each weight takes part in one multiply and one add per example on the way forward. The backward pass costs about twice that: one product for the weight’s gradient and one to pass the gradient on. N examples and E epochs multiply it all. Backpropagation also has to keep every layer’s activations, so memory grows with the total width L·W per example.
Say it“A forward pass is about two P flops per example; training is about six N P E flops.”

History

What changed since the 1980s

The recipe in this chapter is younger than its ingredients. Backpropagation was popularised in 1986, and universal approximation proved in 1989. Most of what separates a network trained in 2015 from one trained in 1990 is more data and much faster computers. Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville single out two algorithmic changes on top of that. The first was replacing squared error with cross-entropy, so that sigmoid and softmax outputs stopped stalling when they were confidently wrong. The second was replacing sigmoid hidden units with piecewise-linear units such as the ReLU, which pass gradients through deep stacks. Rectifiers were not new; what was new was evidence that they trained deep networks better (Jarrett et al., 2009; Nair & Hinton, 2010; Glorot, Bordes & Bengio, 2011). Below, four recipes train the same small network on 180 points from three interleaved spirals, from the same starting weights, by plain gradient descent with momentum. Real-world project

Project: depth, width and activation on Covertype, at a fixed budget

The UCI Covertype data (Blackard & Dean; donated to the UCI repository in 1998, licensed CC BY 4.0, with the owners asking that the copyright notice of Jock A. Blackard and Colorado State University be kept) describe 30 × 30 m cells of four wilderness areas in the Roosevelt National Forest, Colorado. Each cell has ten measurements (elevation, aspect, slope, distances to water, roads and past fire ignition points, and three hillshade indices), its wilderness area and one of 40 soil types, and the task is to predict which of seven forest cover types grows there. The documentation names a training block (the first 11,340 records) and a validation block (the next 3,780), each with equal numbers of all seven types. This project trains on 4,200 records drawn from the training block, 600 per type, and tests on all 3,780 validation records. Inputs are 54 numbers: the ten measurements standardised with the training means and standard deviations, plus one-hot codes for area and soil.

The question is the one this chapter keeps asking: for a fixed budget of about 2,600 parameters, is it better to be wide or deep, and which hidden unit should you use? Every network below is the widest equal-width network with L hidden layers that fits the budget, trained with Adam (learning rate 0.003, batches of 100, 30 epochs, no weight decay) from three seeds. Python ran the whole sweep; your browser can re-train any cell of it, step for step.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Pick the output unit from the target’s distribution: a linear unit for a Gaussian mean, a sigmoid for a Bernoulli, a softmax for K classes. Train on the negative log-likelihood, so the gradient is “prediction minus target” and never stalls on a confident mistake.
  • Use ReLU hidden units by default. Leaky, parametric and maxout units fix the zero gradient on the negative side; sigmoid and tanh saturate and pass back a quarter or less of the gradient.
  • One hidden layer can approximate any continuous function, but it may need exponentially many units; depth can build some functions with exponentially fewer, as the tent sawtooth shows.
  • Weight space is full of symmetries (M!·2M copies for tanh) and genuinely different minima: start small and random, try several starts, and average predictions, never weights.
  • Prefer too many units plus weight decay to too few; choose depth and width by experiment at a fixed budget, keeping the cost of about 6NPE flops in mind.

Check your understanding

A binary classifier has a sigmoid output trained with squared error, and some training examples are stuck far on the wrong side. Why, and what is the fix?

The squared-error gradient with respect to z is 2(σ(z) − y)σ(z)(1 − σ(z)), and the last factor is almost 0 when |z| is large, so confidently wrong examples barely move the weights. Train on the log loss (binary cross-entropy) instead: its gradient is σ(z) − y, close to ±1 for those examples.

Why do the weights of a network usually start as small random numbers rather than zeros?

If every hidden unit starts with the same weights, they all compute the same thing and receive the same gradient, so they stay identical forever and the layer behaves like a single unit. Random starts break this symmetry; keeping them small starts the network in the nearly linear region of tanh or sigmoid units, and avoids saturating them.

A network with one hidden layer of 20 tanh units has been trained. How many other weight vectors give exactly the same function?

At least 20!·220 − 1, about 2.6 × 1024: any relabelling of the 20 units combined with any pattern of sign flips (each flip negates a unit’s incoming weights, bias and outgoing weight). That is why weight-by-weight averages of separately trained networks are meaningless.

The universal approximation theorem says one hidden layer is enough. Give two reasons to use more than one anyway.

The theorem promises only that suitable weights exist, not that training finds them, and the required width can grow exponentially with the input dimension or with the complexity of the function. Deep networks can represent some functions (such as the composed tent sawtooth, with 2L pieces from 2L units) far more compactly, and in practice deeper networks with the same number of weights often generalise better.

You can afford about 2,600 parameters for 54 inputs and 7 classes. How wide can a network with 4 hidden layers be, and roughly how many flops does training take on 4,200 examples for 30 epochs?

P = 55W + 3(W + 1)W + 7(W + 1); W = 20 gives 2,507 and W = 21 gives 2,695, so W = 20. Training costs about 6 × 4,200 × 2,507 × 30 ≈ 1.9 × 109 flops, around a second or two on a laptop.
