Supervised Principal Components & the False Discovery Rate

Score every feature on its own, then decide which scores to believe

With 10,000 genes and 50 patients, the first move is almost always the same: score each gene on its own against the outcome. This chapter is about what to do next. To predict, keep the high-scoring genes and summarise them with a principal component: that is supervised principal components. To discover, decide which high scores are real and which are luck, and promise a limit on the fraction of mistakes: that is the false discovery rate. On the right are 2,000 simulated genes, 200 of them truly changed. Drag across the figure to move the p-value cutoff and watch the false discoveries (red) pile up as you loosen it.

Predicting with p ≫ N

Supervised principal components

Principal components are a natural way to squeeze thousands of correlated genes into a few numbers. But the first principal component points wherever the data vary most, and the biggest source of variation in a set of arrays is often something the outcome doesn't care about: a batch, a cell type, the time of day. Supervised principal components (Bair & Tibshirani, 2004) add one step in front. Score each gene on its own against y, keep only the genes whose score clears a threshold θ, and take the principal components of those genes. Then regress y on the first one or two, and choose θ by cross-validation.

The data below are simulated so that we know the truth. There are N = 50 training samples and 1,000 genes. A hidden factor U drives y and 50 of the genes; a second, louder factor V drives another 100 genes and has nothing to do with y; the remaining 850 genes are noise. Keeping all 1,000 genes is ordinary principal components regression. Slide the number of genes kept and find where the test error bottoms out.

\[ s_j = \frac{\langle \mathbf x_j - \bar x_j,\ \mathbf y - \bar y\rangle}{\Vert \mathbf x_j - \bar x_j\Vert\,\Vert \mathbf y - \bar y\Vert} \]
Read it as: the score of gene j is its correlation with y, computed for that gene alone: the inner product of the centred gene column with the centred outcome, divided by both their lengths. A gene is kept when |sj| > θ. Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman use the standardised regression coefficient of y on xj; it differs from sj only by the constant ‖y − ȳ‖, so it ranks the genes in exactly the same order.
Say it“s-j is the inner product of centred x-j and centred y, over the product of their lengths: the correlation of gene j with y.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf X_\theta - \bar{\mathbf x}_\theta &= \mathbf U\mathbf D\mathbf V^{\top} \\ \hat y &= \bar y + \hat\gamma\,\mathbf u_1 \\ \hat\gamma &= \mathbf u_1^{\top}(\mathbf y-\bar y) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Xθ holds only the kept genes. Its singular value decomposition gives the principal components; u1 is the first one, scaled to length 1, with one entry per sample. Because u1 has length 1, the least-squares coefficient is just its inner product with y. A new sample x gets the score (xθ − x̄θ)·v1/d1.
Say it“Decompose the kept, centred genes as U D V-transpose. Predict y as its mean plus gamma-hat times the first component, where gamma-hat is u-one dotted with y.”

Why it works

The latent-variable view

Why should a principal component of the screened genes predict y? Write down the model the simulation uses. One unobserved quantity U, say the activity of a pathway, drives the outcome. A subset P of the genes also tracks U, each with its own loading and noise. Every other gene is unrelated to U. Under this model, the job of supervised principal components is to estimate U. Screening finds P, because only genes in P correlate with y. Inside P the genes share one common direction, U, so their first principal component is U plus averaged-out noise.

Ordinary principal components skip the screening, and two things pull them off course: a louder factor elsewhere in the genome, and the sheer number of noise genes, whose random variation adds up. Bair, Hastie, Paul & Tibshirani (2006) proved that the supervised version estimates U consistently as N grows, while the leading principal component of all the genes need not, when p grows with N. Add noise genes, turn up the loud factor V, and change N.

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 U + \varepsilon \\ x_j &= \alpha_{0j} + \alpha_{1j} U + \varepsilon_j, \quad j \in \mathcal P \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a two-level model. U is never measured. The outcome is a noisy linear function of U, and so is each gene in the set P. Genes outside P are independent of U (here, some follow another factor V and the rest are pure noise).
Say it“y equals beta-nought plus beta-one U plus noise; each gene in P equals alpha-nought-j plus alpha-one-j U plus its own noise.”
\[ \operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf x_{\mathcal P}) = \boldsymbol\alpha_1\boldsymbol\alpha_1^{\top} + \sigma^2 \mathbf I \]
Read it as: inside P the covariance is one rank-one signal plus equal noise in every direction. Its top eigenvector points along α1, with eigenvalue ‖α1‖² + σ²; every other eigenvalue is σ². Projecting onto that eigenvector gives a score proportional to U plus noise averaged over the genes in P.
Say it“The covariance of the genes in P is alpha-one alpha-one-transpose plus sigma-squared times the identity.”

A close cousin

Partial least squares: one step of the same idea

Partial least squares, the classic partner of principal components regression, also builds a direction from y. Its first direction weights every gene by its inner product with y and adds them up. Nothing is thrown away: the screening is soft. Restricting the sum to the kept genes gives thresholded PLS, and here the two methods meet. Write the PLS direction in matrix form, multiply it by XθXθ⊤ again and again, and you are running the power method, which converges to the top eigenvector of XθXθ⊤: that is u1, the supervised principal component. Thresholded PLS is supervised PCs stopped after one step. The step count is a dial between “stay close to y” and “follow the genes' shared structure”. Step the power method and watch the error; this uses the data set from the first lab.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf z^{(1)} &= \sum_{j\,\text{kept}} \langle \mathbf x_j,\, \mathbf y-\bar y\rangle\, \mathbf x_j \\ &= \mathbf X_\theta \mathbf X_\theta^{\top} (\mathbf y-\bar y) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first PLS direction is a weighted sum of gene columns (centred), each weighted by how much it covaries with y. With every gene kept it is ordinary PLS; with the screened genes only, it is thresholded PLS.
Say it“z-one is the sum over kept genes of x-j's inner product with y, times x-j: X-theta X-theta-transpose times y.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf z^{(s)} &\propto \big(\mathbf X_\theta \mathbf X_\theta^{\top}\big)^{s}(\mathbf y-\bar y) \\ &\longrightarrow\ \pm\,\mathbf u_1 \quad (s\to\infty) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each extra multiplication by XθXθ⊤ stretches the component along u1 by d1² and along uk by only dk². After s steps the leftover from the other directions shrinks like (d2²/d1²)s, so y's own influence fades and the supervised principal component remains.
Say it“z-s is proportional to X-theta X-theta-transpose to the power s, times y; it tends to plus or minus u-one.”

Feature selection

Pre-conditioning: denoise y, then select

A supervised principal component predicts well, but it is built from dozens of genes with small weights. Often we want a short list. The lasso gives one, but with p ≫ N it is easily fooled: the noise in y is large compared with each gene's signal, so the lasso picks genes that happen to fit the noise. Paul, Bair, Hastie & Tibshirani (2008) suggested pre-conditioning: first compute the supervised-PC predictions ŷ, which are much less noisy than y, and then run the lasso with ŷ as the response. The lasso is unchanged; only its target is. Slide the list length and compare the two lassos' test errors and how many of their genes are real.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}(\lambda) &= \underset{\beta_0,\,\boldsymbol\beta}{\arg\min}\ \Big[ \frac{\text{RSS}}{2N} + \lambda\sum_{j}|\beta_j| \Big] \\ \text{RSS} &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \big(\hat y_i - \beta_0 - \mathbf x_i^{\top}\boldsymbol\beta\big)^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the ordinary lasso (RSS is the residual sum of squares), with the supervised-PC fit ŷi where yi used to be. Columns are standardised first. Larger λ keeps fewer genes. Because ŷ is a smooth function of the shared factor, the genes that track that factor fit it far better than noise genes can.
Say it“Beta-hat of lambda minimises one over two N times the squared errors against y-hat, plus lambda times the sum of absolute coefficients.”

Feature assessment

Thousands of tests: why p-values mislead

Now turn the question round. Instead of predicting y, we ask which genes differ between two groups, say patients who reacted badly to radiotherapy and patients who didn't. For each gene compute a two-sample t-statistic and its p-value. A p-value below 0.05 is the classic signal, and it means: if this gene were unchanged, a result this extreme would turn up only 5% of the time. That promise is about one test. Run M tests on genes that are all unchanged and about 0.05 M of them will cross the line anyway.

The traditional cure controls the family-wise error rate (FWER), the chance of even one false positive. Bonferroni's rule tests each gene at α/M. It works, but with thousands of genes the bar is so high that real effects rarely clear it. Below is a world where nothing changes: two groups of 10 samples, every gene pure noise. Raise the number of genes and count the “discoveries”.

\[ \begin{aligned} t_j &= \frac{\bar x_{2j}-\bar x_{1j}}{\mathrm{se}_j} \\ \mathrm{se}_j &= \hat\sigma_j\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{N_1}+\tfrac{1}{N_2}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the difference between the two group means of gene j, measured in units of its standard error. σ̂j is the pooled within-group standard deviation. If gene j is unchanged and the data are roughly normal, tj follows a t distribution with N1 + N2 − 2 degrees of freedom, which turns it into a p-value.
Say it“t-j is x-bar-two-j minus x-bar-one-j over its standard error, sigma-hat-j times root of one over N-one plus one over N-two.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{FWER} &= \Pr(V \ge 1) \\ &= 1-(1-\alpha)^{M} \\ p_j \le \alpha/M \ &\Rightarrow\ \text{FWER} \le \alpha \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: V counts false positives. With M independent unchanged genes each tested at level α, the chance of at least one false positive is one minus the chance of none. Bonferroni divides α among the M tests; by the union bound the FWER is then at most α, whatever the dependence between genes.
Say it“The family-wise error rate is the probability that V is at least one: one minus one-minus-alpha to the M. Testing at alpha over M keeps it below alpha.”

A t-test p-value leans on normality. Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman use a permutation null instead: shuffle the group labels K times, recompute every gene's t-statistic each time, and pool all M×K of them into one reference distribution. That needs no distributional assumption and gives a fine-grained p-value even for the most extreme genes.

\[ p_j = \frac{1}{MK}\sum_{j'}\sum_{k} I\big(|t_{j'}^{k}| \ge |t_j|\big) \]
Read it as: tj'k is gene j' recomputed under the k-th random relabelling. The p-value of gene j is the fraction of all M×K permuted statistics at least as large as its own, in absolute value. Pooling over genes assumes the null genes share one distribution, which is reasonable for t-statistics.
Say it“p-j is one over M K times the number of permuted statistics, over all genes and all relabellings, whose size is at least that of t-j.”
A better error rate

The false discovery rate and Benjamini–Hochberg

A scientist screening genes doesn't need a list with no mistakes. She needs a list that is mostly right, so the follow-up experiments aren't wasted. Benjamini & Hochberg (1995) made that precise. Sort every test into the table below; the false discovery rate is the expected fraction of the called genes that are false. Their procedure is a single pass over the sorted p-values: compare the j-th smallest with α j/M, find the last one under the line, and call everything up to it. The line rises with j, so the more genes look real, the more lenient it becomes. Move α and the share of real effects, then repeat the experiment to see the promise kept.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{FDR} &= \mathbb E\!\left[\frac{V}{R}\right] \\ \frac{V}{R} &:= 0 \ \text{ when } R = 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: R genes are called significant and V of them are actually unchanged. V/R is the false fraction in this list; the FDR is its average over repeated experiments. FWER asks for V = 0; FDR only asks V to be a small share of R.
Say it“The false discovery rate is the expected value of V over R, counting zero when nothing is called.”
\[ \begin{aligned} L &= \max\big\{ j : p_{(j)} \le \alpha\, j / M \big\} \\ \text{call } j &\ \text{ if } p_j \le p_{(L)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: p(1) ≤ p(2) ≤ … are the sorted p-values. Walk down the list and remember the last rank j at which p(j) is still under the line αj/M. Every gene up to that rank is called, even ones that poked above the line earlier: it is a step-up rule.
Say it“L is the largest j for which the j-th smallest p-value is at most alpha j over M; reject every hypothesis whose p-value is at most p-sub-L.”

\[ \text{FDR}_{\text{BH}} \le \frac{M_0}{M}\,\alpha \le \alpha \]
Read it as: Benjamini & Hochberg proved that when the tests are independent, their procedure keeps the FDR at or below α times the fraction M0/M of unchanged genes, for any effect sizes. (Benjamini & Yekutieli later extended it to positively dependent tests.) The slack M0/M is why estimating it, below, can buy extra discoveries.
Say it“The BH false discovery rate is at most M-nought over M times alpha, which is at most alpha.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\text{FDR}}(C) &= \bar V(C) \,/\, R(C) \\ \bar V(C) &= \tfrac1K\textstyle\sum_{k}\#\{j : |t_j^{k}| \ge C\} \\ R(C) &= \#\{j : |t_j| \ge C\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the plug-in estimate for the rule “call |t| ≥ C”. The numerator is how many genes we would call if nothing were real, averaged over K relabellings; the denominator is how many we actually call. Choosing the loosest C whose estimate is at most α reproduces BH applied to the pooled permutation p-values.
Say it“FDR-hat at C is the average number of permuted statistics beyond C, over the number of observed statistics beyond C.”
Asymmetric cutpoints

SAM: different bars for up and down

The rule |tj| ≥ C treats up and down the same. But biology is rarely symmetric: a treatment may switch on a hundred genes and switch off five. Significance analysis of microarrays (SAM; Tusher, Tibshirani & Chu, 2001) lets the data choose separate cutpoints. Sort the observed statistics and plot them against their expected values, the averages of the sorted statistics over random relabellings. Unchanged genes hug the 45° line. Starting from the middle and moving outwards, the first gene that sits more than Δ above the line sets the upper cutpoint, and the first one more than Δ below sets the lower cutpoint. Everything beyond is called. Its FDR estimate counts calls among the relabelled data.

SAM also adds a constant s0 to every denominator. With few samples, some genes get a tiny standard error by chance and a huge t; s0 stops them dominating the list. The simulation has 1,000 genes in two groups of 8, unequal noise levels, 60 genes truly up and 10 truly down. Slide Δ, and compare SAM's list with a symmetric cutoff at the same estimated FDR.

\[ \begin{aligned} d_j &= \frac{\bar x_{2j}-\bar x_{1j}}{\mathrm{se}_j + s_0} \\ \bar d_{(j)} &= \frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K} d^{\,k}_{(j)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: dj is a t-statistic with s0 added to the standard error (s0 = 0 gives back t). d̄(j) is the expected j-th smallest statistic: sort the d's from each relabelling and average position by position.
Say it“d-j is the difference in means over its standard error plus s-nought. d-bar of j is the average over relabellings of the j-th smallest permuted d.”
\[ \begin{aligned} g_{(j)} &= d_{(j)} - \bar d_{(j)} \\ j^\ast &= \min\{ j \ge j_0 : g_{(j)} > \Delta \} \\ C_{\text{hi}} &= d_{(j^\ast)} \\ \widehat{\text{FDR}} &= \operatorname{median}_k V_k \,/\, R \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: g(j) is how far the j-th sorted statistic sits above its expected value, and j0 is where the expected statistics cross zero. Moving up from there, the first sorted statistic with a gap of more than Δ becomes Chi; Clo is found the same way going down. Vk counts the genes beyond the cutpoints (d ≥ Chi or d ≤ Clo) in the k-th relabelling and R counts them in the real data; the FDR estimate is the median Vk over R.
Say it“C-high is the first sorted d above the middle that beats its expected value by more than Delta. FDR-hat is the median false count over the number called.”

Another reading

A Bayesian view: the FDR as a posterior probability

Storey (2002, 2003) gave the FDR a clean probabilistic meaning. Suppose each gene is unchanged with probability π0 and changed with probability π1 = 1 − π0, independently, and its statistic Z comes from f0 or f1 accordingly. Use a slightly different rate, the positive FDR, which averages V/R only over experiments that call something. Then the pFDR of a rejection region Γ is exactly the posterior probability that a gene is unchanged given that its statistic fell in Γ, whatever the number of genes. Each gene gets a q-value, the smallest pFDR at which it would be called: the FDR analogue of a p-value. Move the cutoff c and compare the shaded areas, then run 2,000 simulated studies to check the formula.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{pFDR} &= \mathbb E\!\left[\frac{V}{R} \,\middle|\, R > 0\right] \\ \text{FDR} &= \text{pFDR}\cdot \Pr(R>0) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the FDR counts experiments with no calls as V/R = 0; the pFDR leaves them out. With many genes almost every experiment calls something, Pr(R > 0) ≈ 1, and the two agree.
Say it“The positive FDR is the expected V over R given that R is positive; the FDR is the pFDR times the probability that R is positive.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{pFDR}(\Gamma) &= \frac{\pi_0 P_0(\Gamma)}{\pi_0 P_0(\Gamma) + \pi_1 P_1(\Gamma)} \\ &= \Pr(H = 0 \mid Z \in \Gamma) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Bayes' rule, with P0(Γ) and P1(Γ) the chances that a null or a changed gene's Z lands in Γ. Of all the genes whose statistics land in Γ, the unchanged ones contribute area π0P0(Γ) and the changed ones π1P1(Γ). The pFDR is the null share of that total.
Say it“pFDR of Gamma is pi-nought P-nought of Gamma over the total probability of Gamma: the posterior probability of the null given Z is in Gamma.”
\[ \begin{aligned} q(z) &= \inf_{\Gamma \ni z} \text{pFDR}(\Gamma) \\ \text{fdr}(z) &= \frac{\pi_0 f_0(z)}{\pi_0 f_0(z) + \pi_1 f_1(z)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the q-value of an observed z is the smallest pFDR over all the nested rejection regions (here, all tails z ≥ c) that contain it. The local fdr is the posterior null probability at exactly z, not beyond it; it is always larger than the tail-area pFDR at the same cutoff when f1/f0 rises with z, because the tail includes stronger genes.
Say it“q of z is the infimum of the pFDR over regions containing z. The local fdr of z is pi-nought f-nought of z over the mixture density at z.”

Two practical consequences. First, π0 can be estimated: p-values from unchanged genes are uniform, so the flat right-hand part of a p-value histogram is mostly null. Storey's estimate counts the p-values above λ (often 0.5) and divides by M(1 − λ). Second, multiplying BH-adjusted p-values by that π̂0 gives q-values that use the M0/M slack: when many genes are changed, the list grows at the same nominal FDR. The project does both.

Real-world project

Project: genes that respond differently to radiation

Rieger and colleagues (2004) studied why a few radiotherapy patients suffer unusually severe side effects. They grew immortalised white-blood-cell lines from 57 people: 14 radiation-sensitive patients (RadS) and 43 controls (13 radiotherapy patients without unusual toxicity, 15 people who had skin cancer before 40, and 15 healthy people). Each cell line was mock-treated and hit with 5 Gy of ionising radiation, and every sample went on an Affymetrix HG-U95Av2 array. This is the study Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman use to introduce multiple testing; the data are public on NCBI GEO as GSE1725.

Following the paper's question, we test the response to radiation: for each patient and probe, log2(irradiated) − log2(mock), with signals floored at 20 because the old MAS 4 software reports negative values. We keep the 4,207 probes whose median signal over the 114 arrays is at least 200, a filter that never looks at the labels. Gene symbols come from GEO's annotation table for the platform, GPL8300. The page then computes 4,207 t-statistics and 200 random relabellings of the 57 patients, 841,400 null statistics in all. Press Run (it starts by itself when you scroll here).

Does the response predict sensitivity? Supervised PCs, honestly

A long list of significant genes doesn't guarantee a good predictor. Score each patient with a supervised principal component: on each leave-one-out training set of 56 patients, rank probes by |t|, keep the top k, take the first principal component, orient it so that RadS patients score higher, and classify the held-out patient by the midpoint of the two class means. The screening happens inside every fold, as chapter 1.13g insisted. Press Run, then pick k.

import gzip, json, sys, numpy as np

# ---- GEO GSE1725 series matrix (Rieger et al. 2004) and the GPL8300 annotation table ----
D = sys.argv[1]
lines = gzip.open(D + "/GSE1725_series_matrix.txt.gz", "rt", encoding="utf8", newline="").read().split("\n")
title = [s.strip('"') for s in next(l for l in lines if l.startswith("!Sample_title")).split("\t")[1:]]
rows = [l.split("\t") for l in lines if l and l[0] != "!" and not l.startswith('"ID_REF"')]
probe = [r[0].strip('"') for r in rows]
X = np.array([[float(v.strip('"')) for v in r[1:]] for r in rows])          # 12,625 probes x 171 arrays
symbol = {}
for l in gzip.open(D + "/GPL8300.annot.gz", "rt", encoding="utf8", newline="").read().split("\n"):
    f = l.split("\t")
    if len(f) > 2 and l[0] not in "!#^" and f[0] != "ID": symbol[f[0]] = f[2]

# ---- response to ionising radiation: log2(IR) - log2(mock) for each patient's cell line ----
col = {t: i for i, t in enumerate(title)}
kind = {"RadS": 0, "RadC": 1, "SkCa": 2, "NoCa": 3}
pats = sorted({t.rsplit("-", 1)[0] for t in title}, key=lambda s: (kind[s.rstrip("0123456789")], int(s.lstrip("RadSCkNoa"))))
L = np.log2(np.maximum(X, 20))                                              # MAS4 values can be negative: floor at 20
R = np.array([L[:, col[p + "-IR"]] - L[:, col[p + "-Mock"]] for p in pats]).T
used = [col[p + s] for p in pats for s in ("-IR", "-Mock")]
keep = np.median(X[:, used], axis=1) >= 200                                 # probes clearly expressed (label-blind filter)
R, probe = R[keep], [p for p, k in zip(probe, keep) if k]
lo = np.floor(R.min(1) * 1e4) / 1e4; st = np.ceil((R.max(1) - lo) / 65535 * 1e8) / 1e8
R = lo[:, None] + np.round((R - lo[:, None]) / st[:, None]) * st[:, None]   # the page's 16-bit storage grid
g = np.array([p.startswith("RadS") for p in pats])                          # 14 radiation-sensitive vs 43 others
M, N = R.shape
print(f"{M} probes, {N} patients ({g.sum()} RadS, {(~g).sum()} others)")
from scipy import stats                                                     # aside: the untreated arrays alone, all probes
mock = np.array([L[:, col[p + "-Mock"]] for p in pats]).T; pm = stats.ttest_ind(mock[:, g], mock[:, ~g], axis=1).pvalue
o_ = np.sort(pm); ok_ = np.flatnonzero(o_ <= .1 * np.arange(1, len(pm) + 1) / len(pm))
print(f"aside, mock arrays only: BH at 0.10 calls {ok_[-1] + 1} of {len(pm)} probes (parametric t-test)")

def tstats(R, g):
    a, b = R[:, g], R[:, ~g]; n2, n1 = g.sum(), (~g).sum()
    ss = ((a - a.mean(1, keepdims=True)) ** 2).sum(1) + ((b - b.mean(1, keepdims=True)) ** 2).sum(1)
    se = np.sqrt(ss / (n1 + n2 - 2)) * np.sqrt(1 / n1 + 1 / n2)
    return a.mean(1) - b.mean(1), se

def mulberry32(a):                                                          # the page's random numbers
    def r():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF; t = a
        t = ((t ^ (t >> 15)) * (1 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return (t ^ (t >> 14)) / 4294967296
    return r

dm, se = tstats(R, g); t = dm / se
K, rnd = 200, mulberry32(1725); null_t = []
for k in range(K):                                                          # K random relabellings
    ix = list(range(N))
    for i in range(N - 1, 0, -1):
        j = int(rnd() * (i + 1)); ix[i], ix[j] = ix[j], ix[i]

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Supervised principal components: score each feature against y on its own, keep those past a threshold θ, take their leading principal component, regress y on it; choose θ by cross-validation.
  • It works when a hidden factor drives both y and a subset of features: screening finds the subset, PCA averages away its noise, and louder unrelated factors no longer steer the component.
  • Thresholded PLS is the first step of the power method whose limit is the supervised PC; pre-conditioning runs the lasso on the denoised fit ŷ instead of y to get a shorter, truer gene list.
  • With M tests at level α, about αM nulls pass by luck. Bonferroni controls the chance of any mistake; Benjamini–Hochberg controls the expected false share, FDR ≤ αM0/M.
  • SAM picks separate up and down cutpoints from a plot of observed against expected statistics; under a two-group mixture the pFDR is the posterior probability of the null, and q-values follow.

Check your understanding

Why can ordinary principal components regression fail even when a single hidden factor drives y?

The first principal component follows the largest variance in all the genes. If another factor moves more genes, or more strongly, or the thousands of noise genes add up to more variance than the relevant subset, the first component tracks that instead and carries little information about y. Screening by correlation with y removes those genes first.

You choose the supervised-PC threshold by cross-validation. Where must the gene scoring happen?

Inside each fold, on the training part only. Scoring genes on all the data and then cross-validating leaks the held-out labels into the selection and makes the error look far too good.

In a study of 4,000 genes, BH at α = 0.1 calls 150 genes. Roughly how many of them do you expect to be false, and what does Bonferroni promise instead?

At most about 15 (10% of 150) in expectation, and fewer if many genes are changed, since the bound is αM0/M. Bonferroni would promise a 90% chance of no false positives at all, at the price of calling only the few genes with p ≤ 0.1/4000.

Why does the Benjamini–Hochberg line rise with the rank j?

If j genes are called at threshold t and all M were null, about Mt of them would pass by chance, so the false share is about Mt/j. Keeping Mt/j ≤ α means t ≤ αj/M: the more genes you call, the more chance passes you can absorb.

What is the difference between a q-value of 0.04 and a local fdr of 0.04?

The q-value says: call this gene and everything at least as extreme, and about 4% of that list is expected to be null. The local fdr says this particular gene, at exactly this statistic, has a 4% posterior chance of being null. The local fdr at the edge of a list is usually higher than the list's q-value.
