Multidimensional Scaling, Manifold Learning & PageRank

Keep the distances, lose the dimensions

Give someone a table of road distances between cities and nothing else, and they can draw you the map. That is multidimensional scaling: points placed so that their distances match a table. Turn it on data that lies on a curved sheet, measure distances along the sheet instead of through the air, and the same trick unrolls the sheet flat. That is manifold learning. The chapter ends with the most famous eigenvector on the web, Google’s PageRank. On the right are 400 points on a rolled-up sheet. Drag the roll to turn it, and tap it to unroll it with Isomap.

The idea

Distances in, a map out

Here are the road distances between seven European capitals, taken from OpenStreetMap. There are no coordinates, only the table. Can you place seven dots on a page so that their straight-line distances match it? Measure how badly a layout fits with its stress: add up, over every pair, the squared gap between the distance in the table and the distance on your page. Each pair is drawn as a line, orange where the dots sit too close and red where they sit too far apart. Drag the cities to bring the stress down, then let the algorithm finish the job.

\[ \begin{aligned} &S_M(\mathbf{z}_1,\dots,\mathbf{z}_N) \\ &\quad= \sum_{i<i'} \big(d_{ii'} - \lVert \mathbf{z}_i - \mathbf{z}_{i'} \rVert\big)^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: dii′ is the given dissimilarity between items i and i′ (here, kilometres by road). zi is the point we choose for item i on a k-dimensional page (here k = 2). The stress is the total squared mismatch. This is least-squares or Kruskal–Shephard scaling, and it needs only distances: the items themselves can be cities, words or proteins.
Say it“The stress is the sum over pairs of d-i-i-prime minus the distance between z-i and z-i-prime, squared.”
stress-1 (0 = perfect)
worst pair

The button uses SMACOF (de Leeuw, 1977), the standard way to minimise stress. Each step moves every point at once, pushing apart the pairs that sit too close and pulling together the pairs that sit too far apart. It can never increase the stress, so it slides downhill to a nearby minimum. Like every stress method it can stop in a local minimum, which is why a good starting layout matters; the next section supplies one.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{z}_i^{\text{new}} &= \frac1N \sum_{i' \ne i} d_{ii'}\,\frac{\mathbf{z}_i - \mathbf{z}_{i'}}{\lVert \mathbf{z}_i - \mathbf{z}_{i'}\rVert} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each partner i′ contributes an arrow of length dii′, the distance the table asks for, pointing from zi′ towards zi. Add the arrows and divide by N. (The layout stays centred on the origin, so this is the same as averaging where each partner would like i to be.) This is the Guttman transform; repeating it never raises the stress.
Say it“The new z-i is one over N times the sum, over the other points, of d-i-i-prime times the unit vector from z-i-prime towards z-i.”
Exact answer by eigenvectors

Classical scaling: distances to coordinates in one step

When the distances really are Euclidean, there is a closed-form answer with no iterations at all, due to Torgerson (1952) and Gower (1966). Squared distances hide inner products: ‖xi − xi′‖² = ‖xi‖² + ‖xi′‖² − 2⟨xi, xi′⟩. Double centring (subtracting row means and column means, then adding back the grand mean) cancels the squared lengths and leaves the matrix of centred inner products B. The top eigenvectors of B, stretched by the square roots of their eigenvalues, are the coordinates. Drag the points on the left: the right panel is rebuilt from their distances alone. Then switch to distances that are not Euclidean.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{B} &= -\tfrac12\,\mathbf{J}\,\mathbf{D}^{(2)}\,\mathbf{J} \\ \mathbf{J} &= \mathbf{I} - \tfrac1N \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top} \\ b_{ii'} &= -\tfrac12\big(d_{ii'}^2 - \bar d^{\,2}_{i\cdot} \\ &\qquad\quad - \bar d^{\,2}_{\cdot i'} + \bar d^{\,2}_{\cdot\cdot}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: D(2) holds the squared distances. J subtracts means. The bars are means of squared distances over a row, a column, and the whole table. If the distances came from points xi, then bii′ is exactly the inner product of the centred points, ⟨xi − x̄, xi′ − x̄⟩.
Say it“B is minus a half times J, D-squared, J: double-centred squared distances.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{B} &= \mathbf{V}\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf{V}^{\top}, \qquad \mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{V}_k\,\boldsymbol\Lambda_k^{1/2} \\ \min_{\mathbf{Z}} &\sum_{i,i'} \big(b_{ii'} - \langle \mathbf{z}_i, \mathbf{z}_{i'}\rangle\big)^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: keep the k largest eigenvalues λ1 ≥ λ2 ≥ … and their eigenvectors; scale each eigenvector by √λ. This Z is the best rank-k fit to the inner products (the second line, ESL’s classical scaling criterion). When B came from data X, B = XX⊤ after centring, so classical scaling gives exactly the PCA scores.
Say it“Z is V-k times Lambda-k to the half: top eigenvectors, scaled by root eigenvalues.”

Bending the rules

Sammon mapping and non-metric scaling

Least-squares stress treats an error of 50 km the same whether the pair is 100 km or 3,000 km apart, so big distances dominate. Sammon mapping (1969) divides each squared error by the distance itself, so small distances, the local structure, count for more. Sometimes the numbers are not distances at all, only judgements: “these two wines taste more alike than those two”. Non-metric scaling (Shepard 1962, Kruskal 1964) then uses only their order: it looks for points plus any increasing function θ that turns dissimilarities into distances. Below, the true layout is a 5×5 grid, but you are given its distances distorted by a power p. Raise or lower p and compare the three methods; the right panel is the Shepard diagram, given dissimilarity against fitted distance.

\[ \begin{aligned} S_{\text{Sm}}(\mathbf{z}) &= \frac{1}{\sum d_{ii'}} \\ &\times \sum_{i<i'} \frac{\big(d_{ii'} - \lVert \mathbf{z}_i - \mathbf{z}_{i'}\rVert\big)^2}{d_{ii'}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: least-squares stress with weight 1/dii′ on each pair. A 10% error costs a near pair as much, relative to its size, as a far pair: Sammon’s map keeps neighbourhoods honest and lets far distances give way.
Say it“Sammon stress: the sum of squared errors, each divided by its distance, over the total distance.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &S_{\text{NM}}(\mathbf{z},\theta) = \\ &\frac{\sum_{i<i'} \big[\theta(d_{ii'}) - \lVert \mathbf{z}_i - \mathbf{z}_{i'}\rVert\big]^2}{\sum_{i<i'} \lVert \mathbf{z}_i - \mathbf{z}_{i'}\rVert^2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: θ can be any increasing function. Alternate two moves: with z fixed, the best θ is an isotonic regression of the layout’s distances on the order of d (pool adjacent violators); with θ fixed, take a SMACOF step towards θ(d). The denominator stops the trivial answer of shrinking everything to a point. Its square root is Kruskal’s stress-1.
Say it“Squared gaps between theta of d and the layout distances, over the layout’s squared distances.”

Curved data

Modelling a curved manifold

PCA, and classical scaling with it, fits a flat sheet. Real data often lies near a manifold: a smooth low-dimensional surface curled up inside a high-dimensional space, such as images of one face turning, or one sound changing pitch. Bishop’s section 12.4.3 surveys several ways to model one; three stand out. Piece it together from flat bits: cluster the data, then fit a PCA line in each cluster (a mixture of probabilistic PCA models makes this a proper density). Bend a single sheet: the generative topographic mapping (GTM; Bishop, Svensén & Williams, 1998) places a grid of points in a latent space, maps them through a smooth function into data space, and fits a Gaussian bump at each. Or forget the coordinates and keep distances, the route Isomap and LLE take in the next sections. Switch between the two models and turn their dials.

\[ \begin{aligned} E &= \sum_{k=1}^{K}\ \sum_{n \in C_k} \lVert \mathbf{r}_n \rVert^2 \\ \mathbf{r}_n &= (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{u}_k\mathbf{u}_k^{\top})(\mathbf{x}_n - \boldsymbol\mu_k) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: cluster Ck has centre μk and principal direction uk. The residual rn is the part of xn − μk that the line cannot explain, so each point is charged its squared distance off its cluster’s line. More pieces lower E, but the pieces don’t join up, and nothing says which piece is next to which.
Say it“E sums, over clusters and their points, the squared distance from the point to its cluster’s principal line.”
\[ \begin{gathered} p(\mathbf{t}) = \frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathcal N\big(\mathbf{t}\,\big|\,\mathbf{y}_k,\ \beta^{-1}\mathbf{I}\big) \\ \mathbf{y}_k = \mathbf{W}\boldsymbol\phi(x_k) \\ \boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol\Phi\,\mathbf{W}^{\top} = \boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{T} \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: K latent points xk on a line are carried into data space as yk = Wφ(xk), a weighted sum of M smooth bumps φ. Each image gets a round Gaussian of precision β. EM alternates: R holds each centre’s responsibility for each point, G is diagonal with R’s row sums, and the last line, the M-step, solves for W, a weighted least-squares fit. Because Wφ(x) is smooth in x, neighbours in latent space stay neighbours in data space: the model is topographic, like Kohonen’s self-organising map but with a likelihood.
Say it“p of t is the average of K Gaussians centred at y-k, which is W phi of x-k; the M-step solves Phi-transpose G Phi W-transpose equals Phi-transpose R T.”

Geodesic distance

Isomap: measure along the sheet

On a rolled-up sheet, two points on neighbouring layers can be close through the air but far apart for an ant walking on the paper. Classical scaling sees the air distance and squashes the roll. Tenenbaum, de Silva & Langford (2000) fixed this in three steps: connect each point to its k nearest neighbours (short hops are nearly along the sheet), take the geodesic distance as the shortest path through that graph (Dijkstra), then run classical scaling on those path lengths. Change k, and tap any two points on the roll to see the path between them. Too small a k breaks the graph into pieces; too large a k lets edges jump between layers, a short circuit, and the unrolling fails.

\[ \begin{gathered} d_G(i,i') = \min \sum_{s=1}^{m} \lVert \mathbf{x}_{a_{s-1}} - \mathbf{x}_{a_s}\rVert \\ \text{over paths } i = a_0, \dots, a_m = i' \\ \text{of nearest-neighbour hops} \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: the geodesic distance is the length of the shortest chain of short hops from i to i′. Feed the table dG into classical scaling (the previous section) and the top two eigenvectors are the unrolled coordinates. With enough points, dG converges to the true distance along the manifold.
Say it“d-G of i and i-prime is the shortest total length over chains of neighbour hops from i to i-prime.”

Local geometry

Locally linear embedding: copy the recipe, not the distances

Roweis & Saul (2000) never compute a long-range distance. Each point is written as a weighted average of its k neighbours, with weights summing to one; on a smooth surface, small patches are nearly flat, so this recipe is almost exact. The weights do not change if the patch is rotated, shifted or scaled, so they describe the shape of the neighbourhood, not where it sits. LLE then looks for 2-D points that obey the same recipes. That is a single sparse eigenvalue problem, with no shortest paths. Tap a point on the roll to see its neighbours and weights, then change k and the regulariser.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{w}\ \Big\lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \sum_{j \in \mathcal N(i)} w_{ij}\,\mathbf{x}_j \Big\rVert^2 \\ &\text{s.t.} \textstyle\sum_j w_{ij} = 1, \quad \mathbf{w}_i = \frac{\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{1}} \\ &C_{jl} = (\mathbf{x}_j - \mathbf{x}_i)\cdot(\mathbf{x}_l - \mathbf{x}_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: find the best affine recipe for xi from its neighbours. C is the k×k matrix of inner products of the neighbours seen from xi; when k exceeds the dimension, C is singular, so a small multiple of its trace is added to the diagonal: the regulariser.
Say it“Choose weights summing to one that rebuild x-i from its neighbours; they are C-inverse one, normalised.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{\mathbf{Y}} \sum_i \Big\lVert \mathbf{y}_i - \sum_j w_{ij}\mathbf{y}_j\Big\rVert^2 \\ &\quad= \operatorname{tr}\big(\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{Y}\big), \quad \mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{Y} = N\mathbf{I} \\ &\mathbf{M} = (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W})^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: keep the weights, free the points. The answer is the eigenvectors of M with the smallest eigenvalues. The very smallest is the constant vector (eigenvalue 0, a useless shift), so skip it and keep the next two. The constraint stops the points collapsing.
Say it“Minimise the trace of Y-transpose M Y, with M equal to I minus W transposed times I minus W.”

Neighbours and a gentle push

Local multidimensional scaling

Chen & Buja (2009) asked: why not run stress-based MDS, but only trust the short distances? Keep the stress for pairs of neighbours, and for every other pair, which we don’t trust, just ask that they be far: a small reward for every unit of distance. Without that reward, nothing stops the unconstrained points from piling on top of each other. The dial τ sets how hard non-neighbours repel. The fit starts from classical scaling, which squashes the roll, and descends by gradient steps (with momentum). Press Run, then try a tiny and a large τ.

\[ \begin{aligned} S_L(\mathbf{z}) = &\sum_{(i,i') \in \mathcal N} \big(d_{ii'} - \lVert\mathbf{z}_i - \mathbf{z}_{i'}\rVert\big)^2 \\ &- \tau \sum_{(i,i') \notin \mathcal N} \lVert\mathbf{z}_i - \mathbf{z}_{i'}\rVert \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: 𝒩 is the set of symmetric k-nearest-neighbour pairs. The first sum is ordinary stress on those pairs only. The second rewards spreading out all the others. ESL derives it by giving non-neighbours a huge pretend distance D with a tiny weight w, and letting D grow while 2wD stays fixed at τ. Small τ lets the sheet keep its folds; large τ inflates the map into a round blob.
Say it“S-L is the stress over neighbour pairs minus tau times the summed distances between non-neighbours.”

The web as a matrix

PageRank: the eigenvector that ran a search engine

ESL closes its unsupervised chapter with Google’s PageRank (Brin & Page, 1998), and it belongs with MDS and Isomap: once again, a matrix built from pairwise relations, and an answer read off its leading eigenvector. Imagine a surfer who, at each click, follows a random link on the current page with probability d, or with probability 1−d gets bored and jumps to a page chosen uniformly at random. In the long run, the fraction of time spent on each page is its PageRank. A page ranks high when many pages link to it, and higher still when those pages rank high themselves. Tap one page, then another, to add or remove a link, and step the power method.

\[ p_i = \frac{1-d}{N} + d \sum_{j \to i} \frac{p_j}{c_j} \]
Read it as: page j splits its rank evenly among its cj out-links; page i collects shares from every page j linking to it; everyone also gets an equal slice of the bored-surfer jumps. d = 0.85 is the classic choice. A page with no out-links (dangling) is treated as linking to every page. ESL scales the ranks to average 1; we scale them to sum to 1. The order is the same.
Say it“p-i is one minus d over N, plus d times the sum over pages j linking to i of p-j over c-j.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{p} &= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{p} \\ \mathbf{A} &= \tfrac{1-d}{N}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top} + d\,\mathbf{L}\mathbf{D}_c^{-1} \\ \mathbf{p}^{(t+1)} &= \mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{p}^{(t)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Lij = 1 if j links to i; Dc holds the out-link counts. Every column of A is positive and sums to 1, so by the Perron–Frobenius theorem A has eigenvalue 1 with a positive eigenvector, unique up to scale, and every other eigenvalue has size at most d. PageRank is that eigenvector, and the power method, multiplying by A again and again, finds it, shrinking the error by a factor of about d per step.
Say it“p equals A p: PageRank is the eigenvector of A with eigenvalue one.”

Choosing

Choosing a nonlinear embedding

No method wins everywhere, so test them on data whose true layout you know. Two scores help. Rank agreement (Spearman’s ρ) between map distances and true distances along the sheet asks: is the global layout right? Neighbours kept (Q10) asks: of each point’s 10 true neighbours on the sheet, how many are still among its 10 nearest on the map? That checks the local layout. Pick a data set and race the four methods.

\[ Q_{10} = \frac{1}{10N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \big|\, \mathcal N_{i}^{\text{true}} \cap \mathcal N_{i}^{\text{map}} \,\big| \]
Read it as: for each point, count how many of its 10 nearest neighbours on the true sheet (the set 𝒩itrue) are also among its 10 nearest on the map (𝒩imap); average and divide by 10. 1 means every neighbourhood survived; a random map scores about 10/N.
Say it“Q-ten is the average share of each point’s ten true neighbours that stay among its ten map neighbours.”

Real-world project

Project: redraw Europe from road distances, then rank Wikipedia

Part A. I asked the OpenStreetMap routing engine OSRM for the fastest driving route between every pair of 26 European cities: 325 road distances and driving times. (OSRM’s two directions differ by up to 5%, from one-way streets, so each pair is averaged.) The page now forgets where the cities are and rebuilds the map from the table alone. To judge it, the rebuilt map is shifted, turned and scaled onto the real one (Procrustes alignment) and the leftover error is measured in kilometres. Switch the method and the distance; hover a city.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{s,\ \mathbf{R},\ \mathbf{c}}\ \sum_{i=1}^{26} \big\lVert s\,\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{z}_i + \mathbf{c} - \mathbf{x}_i \big\rVert^2 \\ &\text{with } \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: MDS knows distances only, so its map may come out rotated, mirrored, shifted or in different units. Procrustes finds the scale s, rotation or reflection R and shift c that lay it best over the real positions xi (from an SVD), and what remains is genuine error. The scale has a meaning of its own: real kilometres per road kilometre.

The same project in Python

import json, numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize
from sklearn.manifold import ClassicalMDS, smacof

data = json.load(open("x042-data.json"))      # the same JSON this page embeds
eu = data["eu"]; names = eu["names"]
D = np.array(eu["km"], float)                  # OSRM road distances (km), symmetrised
truth = np.array(eu["xy"])                     # real positions, azimuthal equidistant projection (km)

def stress1(D, Z):                             # Kruskal's stress-1
    iu = np.triu_indices(len(D), 1); d = np.linalg.norm(Z[:, None] - Z[None], axis=2)[iu]
    return np.sqrt(((D[iu] - d) ** 2).sum() / (d ** 2).sum())
def align(Z):                                  # Procrustes: shift, rotate/reflect and scale Z onto the real map
    Zc, Tc = Z - Z.mean(0), truth - truth.mean(0)
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Zc.T @ Tc); sc = s.sum() / (Zc ** 2).sum()
    err = np.linalg.norm(sc * Zc @ U @ Vt - Tc, axis=1)
    return f"scale {sc:.4f}  RMS error {np.sqrt((err ** 2).mean()):5.1f} km  worst: {names[err.argmax()]} ({err.max():.0f} km)"

# 1. classical scaling: eigenvalues of B = -1/2 J D^2 J
J = np.eye(len(D)) - 1 / len(D); lam = np.linalg.eigvalsh(-0.5 * J @ (D ** 2) @ J)[::-1]
Z0 = ClassicalMDS(n_components=2, metric="precomputed").fit_transform(D)
print("eigenvalues (top 3):", np.round(lam[:3]).astype(int), " most negative:", int(round(lam[-1])))
print(f"share of |eigenvalues| in 2 dims: {lam[:2].sum() / np.abs(lam).sum():.4f}")
print(f"classical   stress-1 = {stress1(D, Z0):.5f}   {align(Z0)}")

# 2. least-squares (metric) MDS by SMACOF, started from the classical solution
Z1, _ = smacof(D, metric=True, init=Z0, n_init=1, max_iter=5000, eps=1e-12)
print(f"metric MDS  stress-1 = {stress1(D, Z1):.5f}   {align(Z1)}")

# 3. Sammon mapping: minimise sum (d - |z_i - z_j|)^2 / d  /  sum d
iu = np.triu_indices(len(D), 1)
def sammon(z):
    Z = z.reshape(-1, 2); diff = Z[:, None] - Z[None]; dz = np.linalg.norm(diff, axis=2) + np.eye(len(Z))
    W = np.where(D > 0, 1 / np.where(D > 0, D, 1), 0); E = (W * (D - dz) ** 2)[iu].sum() / D[iu].sum()
    g = (-2 * W * (D - dz) / dz)[:, :, None] * diff; return E, g.sum(1).ravel() / D[iu].sum()
r = minimize(sammon, Z0.ravel(), jac=True, method="L-BFGS-B", options={"maxiter": 20000, "ftol": 1e-15, "gtol": 1e-12})
Z2 = r.x.reshape(-1, 2)
print(f"Sammon      stress E = {r.fun:.6f}  {align(Z2)}")

# 4. non-metric (Shepard-Kruskal) MDS: only the order of the distances is used
Z3, s3 = smacof(D, metric=False, init=Z0, n_init=1, max_iter=5000, eps=1e-12, normalized_stress=True)
print(f"non-metric  stress-1 = {s3:.5f}   {align(Z3)}")

# 5. driving time instead of distance
H = np.array(eu["hrs"]); ZH = ClassicalMDS(n_components=2, metric="precomputed").fit_transform(H)
Zh, _ = smacof(H, metric=True, init=ZH, n_init=1, max_iter=5000, eps=1e-12)
print(f"hours map   stress-1 = {stress1(H, Zh):.5f}   {align(Zh)}")
Zg = ClassicalMDS(n_components=2, metric="precomputed").fit_transform(np.array(eu["gc"], float))
print(f"great-circle classical: {align(Zg)}")

# 6. PageRank on the Wikipedia link graph: power method, then check against the eigenvector
wk = data["wiki"]; N = len(wk["names"]); d = 0.85
L = np.zeros((N, N))                           # L[i, j] = 1 if page j links to page i
for j, out in enumerate(wk["out"]): L[out, j] = 1
c = L.sum(0); M = np.where(c > 0, L / np.where(c > 0, c, 1), 1 / N)   # dangling pages vote for everyone
A = (1 - d) / N + d * M                         # the Google matrix: columns sum to one
p = np.full(N, 1 / N)
for it in range(1000):
    q = A @ p; ch = np.abs(q - p).sum(); p = q
    if ch < 1e-12: break
w, V = np.linalg.eig(A); v = np.real(V[:, np.argmax(np.real(w))]); v /= v.sum()
print(f"\nPageRank: {N} pages, {int(L.sum())} links, {int((c == 0).sum())} dangling; "
      f"power method converged in {it + 1} steps; max |power - eigenvector| = {np.abs(p - v).max():.1e}")
print("largest eigenvalue of A:", round(float(np.max(np.real(w))), 12))
for k, i in enumerate(np.argsort(-p)[:10]):
    print(f"{k + 1:2d}. {wk['names'][i]:32s} {p[i]:.5f}   in-links {int(L[i].sum())}")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Multidimensional scaling turns a table of dissimilarities into points: least-squares stress fits the distances, Sammon favours the small ones, and non-metric scaling keeps only their order.
  • Classical scaling double-centres the squared distances and keeps the top eigenvectors; on Euclidean distances it is exactly PCA, and negative eigenvalues warn that the distances are not Euclidean.
  • Curved data needs curved models: piecewise PCA and GTM fit the surface itself, while Isomap (geodesic distances), LLE (local linear recipes) and local MDS (neighbour stress plus repulsion) only keep what is true locally.
  • Neighbourhood size is the dial that matters: too few neighbours break the graph, too many short-circuit it across folds.
  • PageRank is the leading eigenvector of a column-stochastic link matrix, found by the power method; on 187 Wikipedia articles it puts Real number first although Machine learning has the most in-links.

Check your understanding

Classical scaling of a distance table gives eigenvalues 9, 4, 0, 0, −3. What does that tell you?

The −3 means no set of points in any Euclidean space has exactly these distances: they are not Euclidean (roads, survey judgements, geodesics). Two dimensions capture the positive part well (9 + 4), and the negative part is a measure of how far from Euclidean the table is.

Why does every MDS answer come with a rotation, a reflection and a shift left undecided?

Stress depends only on distances between the zi, and rotating, reflecting or shifting all points together leaves every distance unchanged. To compare a map with the truth you must first align it, which is what Procrustes analysis does.

Isomap with k = 20 on a Swiss roll returns a squashed spiral. What went wrong, and what do you change?

Some of the 20 neighbours lie on the next layer of the roll, so the graph has short-circuit edges and the geodesic distances cut across the gap. Lower k (or use a radius rule), and check that the graph stays connected.

Why do LLE’s weights have to sum to one?

So that the recipe ignores where the neighbourhood sits: shifting xi and its neighbours by the same vector leaves the reconstruction error unchanged only if Σwij = 1. Together with invariance to rotation and scaling, this lets the same weights describe the patch after it has been unrolled.

Without the damping term (d = 1), what can go wrong with PageRank?

The surfer can be trapped: a group of pages that link only to each other soaks up all the rank, and on a graph that is not strongly connected, or is periodic, the eigenvector for eigenvalue 1 may not be unique and the power method may not converge. The random jump (d < 1) makes A strictly positive, so the answer is unique and the error shrinks like dt.
