Dopamine & the Reward Prediction Error

A few hundred thousand neurons in the midbrain fire as if they were computing the TD error, and that one observation rewired the relationship between reinforcement learning and neuroscience

In the previous chapter the TD error δ migrated from the moment of the reward to the moment of the cue as the model learned. In 1997 Wolfram Schultz, Peter Dayan and Read Montague pointed out that the dopamine neurons of monkeys do exactly that. The figure is their three-panel story, generated live by a TD(λ) model and turned into spikes: a reward that nothing predicted makes the neuron fire; once a cue predicts the reward the burst moves to the cue and the reward is met with silence; and if the predicted reward fails to arrive the neuron falls quiet at the moment it was due. The hardware · 15.1

Neuroscience basics: neurons, spikes and a neuromodulator

This chapter needs only a little neuroscience, but it needs it precisely, so here is the vocabulary in one pass. A neuron is a cell that receives signals through a tree of thin branches, the dendrites, sums them in its cell body, and when the sum is large enough sends a signal of its own down a single long fibre, the axon, which can be a millimetre or a metre long and branches at its far end to contact other neurons. The signal is an action potential, or spike: a swing of about a tenth of a volt in the voltage across the cell membrane that lasts about a millisecond and travels down the axon without weakening. Spikes are all-or-none. A neuron does not send a small spike for a small input; it sends more or fewer spikes per second. The number of spikes a neuron emits per second, its firing rate, is the quantity most of this chapter’s experiments measure, and it is what the simulated neuron below turns a slow input current into.

Where an axon branch meets another neuron there is a synapse: a gap of a few tens of nanometres across which the arriving spike releases a chemical, a neurotransmitter, that binds to receptors on the receiving neuron and nudges its voltage up (an excitatory synapse) or down (an inhibitory one). The size of the nudge is the synaptic efficacy, or weight, and changing efficacies is what the brain’s learning is made of. Glutamate and GABA are the fast workhorse transmitters: a glutamate synapse says “more” to its target within a millisecond and is done. Dopamine is a different kind of substance, a neuromodulator. Its receptors are slow (they work through second messengers inside the cell rather than by opening a channel directly), its effect is less to excite or inhibit the target than to change how the target responds to its other inputs and, crucially, how its other synapses change, and it is released not only at tight synapses but diffusely into the surrounding tissue, where it reaches many cells at once. A learning rule that reads “strengthen the synapses that were just active if dopamine arrives” is a three-factor rule, and it is the most natural way to put a scalar reinforcement signal into a network of neurons. Keep that picture; the next chapter builds the actor–critic out of it.

The dopamine neurons themselves are few. In a human midbrain there are a few hundred thousand of them (on the order of 0.4 to 0.6 million; a rat has a few tens of thousands), clustered in two neighbouring groups at the top of the brainstem: the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) and, beside and below it, the ventral tegmental area (VTA). What makes them influential is their wiring. Each dopamine axon branches enormously as it ascends, and a single SNc neuron can form on the order of a hundred thousand release sites in the striatum, the large input structure of the basal ganglia. The SNc projects mainly to the dorsal striatum (the caudate and putamen, which handle movement and habits); the VTA projects to the ventral striatum (the nucleus accumbens, the part that responds to reward in almost every imaging study), to the prefrontal cortex, and to the amygdala and hippocampus. Neuroscientists call the first route nigrostriatal and the others mesolimbic and mesocortical. The striatum is also where the cortex sends its massive descending projection, so a corticostriatal synapse sees a cortical input, a striatal output, and a bath of dopamine: the three factors. Pathway

How does anyone know what a dopamine neuron is doing while a monkey works for juice? With a microelectrode: a fine insulated wire whose tip, lowered into the midbrain of an awake animal, picks up the voltage changes of the one or two neurons nearest it. Dopamine neurons were identified by their signature, a slow, regular background of broad spikes at one to eight per second (the tonic firing, typically three to five hertz in a monkey at rest) with occasional short bursts of two to six spikes at up to several tens of hertz, and occasional pauses in which the background goes silent for a few hundred milliseconds. Modern experiments in mice confirm the identity of the recorded cell by making dopamine neurons light-sensitive and checking that a flash drives it (optogenetic tagging), or measure dopamine release itself with chemical sensors. The bursts and pauses are the phasic response, and they are what the reward-prediction-error hypothesis is about. Every figure of a recording in this chapter has the same two parts: a raster, one row of tick marks per trial, each tick a spike, and below it a histogram of spikes per time bin summed over trials, which estimates the firing rate as a function of time.

Four things that are not the same · 15.2

Reward signals, reinforcement signals, values and prediction errors

Before asking what dopamine encodes, be clear about the candidates, because everyday language runs them together and the experiments only make sense once they are apart. In reinforcement learning the reward signal Rt is the number the environment hands the agent at each step: the juice, the food pellet, the point scored. The value V(s) is the agent’s prediction of the reward still to come from state s, learned and stored. A reinforcement signal is whatever quantity actually drives the change in the agent’s parameters: in supervised learning it is the error vector, in a bandit it is the reward itself, and in TD learning it is the TD error δt, since the update is w ← w + αδtzt and nothing else touches w. A prediction error is the difference between what was predicted and what happened; δt is a specific one, a reward prediction error, the error in the prediction of return, and it is both a prediction error and the reinforcement signal of a TD learner. There are other prediction errors in the brain (sensory ones, for instance, where a predicted sound fails to arrive) that have nothing to do with reward.

The distinction that matters most here is between the reward signal and the reinforcement signal. In a TD learner they come apart in both directions. A reward that was fully predicted produces no reinforcement at all: δ = R + γV′ − V = 0 when V had already risen to meet it. And a reinforcement can arrive with no reward in sight: when a cue that predicts reward appears, the prediction jumps from 0 to V and δ = γV − 0 is positive, so the learner is reinforced at the moment of the cue. If a neuron carried the reward signal it would fire whenever the juice came, trained or not. If it carried the reinforcement signal of a TD learner it would fire for surprising juice, fall silent for expected juice, fire for a cue that announces juice, and dip when announced juice fails to arrive. These are different predictions about a recording, and the data decide. Move the trial slider to see the four signals on one trial at successive stages of learning.

The claim · 15.3

The reward-prediction-error hypothesis of dopamine

Through the 1980s Wolfram Schultz and his colleagues in Fribourg recorded from dopamine neurons in monkeys (Macaca fascicularis) that were learning simple tasks for drops of fruit juice: reach into a box when it opens, touch a lever after a light, that sort of thing. Their first observation was the one everybody expected, that the neurons fired a burst when the monkey received juice. Their second was not expected. Once the monkey had learned that a light or a sound announced the juice, the burst at the juice faded away and a burst appeared at the light. The neurons had not stopped caring about reward; they had moved their response to the earliest event that predicted it. The third observation, made by withholding the juice on occasional trials, closed the case: at the moment the juice would have arrived, the neuron’s background firing paused. A reward signal cannot fall below zero when nothing happens. A prediction error can.

Montague, Dayan and Sejnowski (1996), and then Schultz, Dayan and Montague in a 1997 Science paper, made the correspondence exact: the phasic activity of dopamine neurons behaves like the TD error δt of a temporal-difference learner whose value function is the animal’s prediction of reward. Not the reward, not the value, but the error. This is the reward-prediction-error hypothesis of dopamine, and it is the most precise bridge between an algorithm and a cell type in all of neuroscience. It explains all three observations with one formula. An unpredicted reward: V was 0, R arrives, δ = R > 0, a burst. A predicted reward: V had risen to R/γ one step earlier, so at the reward δ = R + 0 − V ≈ 0, no response; but at the cue, where V jumps from 0, δ = γV > 0, a burst. An omitted reward: δ = 0 + 0 − V < 0, a pause. The hypothesis also fixes what a dopamine neuron is for: it is the reinforcement signal, broadcast to the striatum and cortex to tell every eligible synapse how much to change, exactly the role δ plays in w ← w + αδz.

One translation step sits between δ and the recording. A firing rate cannot be negative, and dopamine neurons idle at only a few spikes per second, so a large negative δ can at most silence the cell while a large positive δ can drive it far above baseline. The simplest reading is rate = baseline + gain × δ, cut off at zero. Bayer and Glimcher (2005) found that positive errors were well encoded by the burst size while negative errors were compressed, and later that the duration of the pause carries the size of a negative error (Bayer, Lau and Glimcher, 2007). \[ \text{rate}_t \;\approx\; \max\!\big(0,\; b + g\,\delta_t\big), \qquad \text{spikes in bin } t \sim \text{Poisson}\big(\text{rate}_t \cdot \Delta t\big) \]
Read it as: b is the tonic background (about 5 spikes/s here), g the gain in spikes per second per unit of prediction error, and Δt the width of a time bin (100 ms on this page).   • What is not claimedThat dopamine is pleasure, or reward itself: a fully predicted reward produces no phasic dopamine at all, and the cue that predicts it produces a lot. Nor that dopamine carries the value V: value-like activity lives downstream.
  • What it predictsResponses to cues scale with the reward they predict, and shrink when another cue already predicts it (blocking); a reward that comes at the wrong time produces an error at both times; a reward that is better than expected produces a burst even when a reward was expected.
  • What it leaves openWhat the value function is computed over (the animal’s state representation), how negative errors are read out from a floor of a few spikes, and whether one scalar is the whole story (section 7).
Dopamine and TD errors · 15.4, 15.6

Dopamine and TD errors: the response shifts backwards

The hero figure shows the beginning and the end of learning. The interesting part is the middle, and here the hypothesis is at its most specific, because TD learning says exactly how the response should travel from the reward to the cue. Run the model of the previous chapter, a TD(λ) learner with a complete serial compound (one feature for each time step since the cue appeared) and watch δ trial by trial. On trial 1 all the error is at the reward. On trial 2 the feature active just before the reward has learned a little, so the step into it is a small surprise and the reward a slightly smaller one. Trial after trial the surprise propagates backwards, one time step per trial when λ = 0, faster when the eligibility trace lets earlier features learn from the same error, until it reaches the cue, where it stops: the cue is the first thing in the trial, nothing predicts it, so the error there can never be learned away. The shift also tells you what the cue response means once learning is done. The value at the cue is the discounted reward, γISI × R, so the burst at the cue is γ times that, and it is smaller for a cue that is further from its reward. Dopamine neurons do fire less for cues that predict more distant rewards (Fiorillo, Newsome and Schultz, 2008, and Kobayashi and Schultz, 2008, measured the decline), which is the discount factor appearing in a recording. And now the awkward part, which Sutton and Barto raise and which is worth facing early. With a complete serial compound and a small λ, the model’s error passes through the intermediate times on its way back: on the middle trials there should be a bump of dopamine activity at moments between the cue and the reward. Schultz’s recordings show no such thing; the response seems to fade at the reward and grow at the cue with little in between. Two repairs are available within the theory. A trace parameter near 1 makes all the eligible features learn from the reward at once, so the intermediate bumps are tiny (set λ = 0.9 and read the “largest in between” figure); and a representation in which time since the cue is coded coarsely, like the microstimuli of the previous chapter, spreads the error so thinly over intermediate moments that it would not be seen above the noise. Pan, Schmidt, Wickens and Hyland (2005), recording in rats, found that the data fit TD with a long eligibility trace better than TD(0). Lower λ to zero and watch the bump appear, then try the presence representation, which has no clock at all.

Experimental support · 15.5

Experimental support: timing, blocking and what the monkeys showed

A hypothesis this specific earns its keep by surviving tests it was not built for. Here are the three families of results from the decade after 1997 that turned it from an elegant reading of three panels into the default account of phasic dopamine, each with the model’s prediction computed live so you can compare.

Timing. Hollerman and Schultz (1998) trained monkeys with a fixed interval between a cue and its juice, then on occasional trials delivered the juice half a second early or half a second late. If the dopamine neuron reported only whether juice arrived, the shifted trials would be unremarkable: the juice came either way. The neurons instead fired a burst at the new time of a late reward and showed a pause at the old time, when the expected juice failed to appear. That is what a TD error over a representation with a clock must do, because the features active at the usual reward time predict reward and are disappointed; and the reward, when it finally comes, is unpredicted by the features then active. An early reward produced a burst at its new time. The model with a complete serial compound also predicts a pause at the old time after an early reward, since the clock features are still ticking towards a reward that already came; in the recordings that second pause was weak or absent, a hint that for the animal the arrival of the juice ends the trial and resets the clock, and one of the small places where the representation, not the rule, needs work.

Blocking. The previous chapter’s Kamin blocking is the sharpest test of a prediction-error account of learning, so Waelti, Dickinson and Schultz (2001) ran it on dopamine neurons. Monkeys first learned that stimulus A predicted juice. Then A and a new stimulus B were presented together, still followed by the same juice. In a control condition stimulus C had predicted nothing, and C and a new stimulus D were then paired with juice. Behaviourally the monkeys learned about D and not about B, as Kamin’s rats did. The recordings matched: dopamine neurons responded to D when it was presented alone and barely responded to B. In Rescorla–Wagner terms, B was never associated with a prediction error because A already predicted the juice; in TD terms, δ during the AB trials was close to zero, so the weights of B’s features never moved, and B alone produces no δ at the test. The same study showed that if the juice was increased during the compound trials, so that a prediction error reappeared, the neurons did learn about the new stimulus: unblocking. Steinberg and colleagues (2013) later closed the causal loop in rats, driving dopamine neurons with light during the compound trials and producing learning about a stimulus that should have been blocked.

Magnitude, probability and the whole family of parametric results. Dopamine responses to a cue grow with the size of the reward it predicts and with the probability of the reward (Fiorillo, Tobler and Schultz, 2003, with cues predicting juice with probabilities from 0 to 1); the response to the juice itself shrinks as its probability rises and the pause when it is omitted deepens, exactly the pattern p·R at the cue, (1 − p)R at a delivered reward and −pR at an omission that a TD learner produces. Tobler, Fiorillo and Schultz (2005) found that the response to a given juice volume depends on what the cue led the animal to expect, a relative coding that a prediction error has and a reward signal lacks. Bayer and Glimcher (2005) fitted the burst size in a saccade task to a weighted history of past rewards and recovered a kernel that looks like the one TD with a constant step size implies: the most recent reward counts positively, the earlier ones negatively. By the time Cohen, Haesler, Vong, Lowell and Uchida (2012) recorded identified dopamine neurons in mice with optogenetic tagging, the finding that they report a reward prediction error had become something a new technique was validated against.

Three trials by hand: what the neuron should do on each

The smallest task that shows all three panels has three moments: the cue appears at step 0, a delay step follows at step 1, and the reward of size 1 arrives at step 2. The complete serial compound gives the cue two features, one for each step it has been on, with weights w0 and w1, so V0 = w0, V1 = w1, and V2 = 0 because the trial ends with the reward. Before the cue nothing is predicted. Take γ = 0.9 and a deliberately large α = 0.3 so that three trials get somewhere, and λ = 0 to start. The response attributed to each moment is the TD error of the transition into it: at the cue, γV0 − 0; at the delay step, γV1 − V0; at the reward, 1 + 0 − V1. The cue-onset error updates nothing (no feature was active before the cue), the other two update the feature that was active before the step. Then read every number as a firing rate with b = 5 spikes/s and g = 20.

    how exactly?
    With λ = 1 the trace decays by γλ = 0.9 per step, so when the reward error δ = 1 arrives the cue feature still has eligibility 0.9 and the delay feature 1. Both learn at once: w0 = 0.3×1×0.9 = 0.27, w1 = 0.3. On trial 2 the cue response is 0.9×0.27 = 0.243 straight away, and the delay-step error is 0.9×0.3 − 0.27 = 0: no intermediate bump at all. After trial 3 the cue response is 0.413 and the omission dip is still −0.657. Long traces move the response from the reward to the cue without it ever appearing in between, which is what the recordings look like.

Open questions I

Distributional dopamine: a population that encodes the whole reward distribution

The hypothesis treats dopamine neurons as copies of one scalar. They are not identical: in any recording some cells respond more to rewards and less to omissions than others, and for twenty years that was filed under noise. In 2020 Will Dabney, Zeb Kurth-Nelson, Naoshige Uchida and colleagues proposed that the variation is the point. Recall distributional reinforcement learning from the deep-RL chapters: instead of learning the expected return, learn the whole distribution of returns, which trains deep agents better and is richer information for a decision. One way to learn a distribution with a population of simple units is to give each unit the same TD error but a different pair of learning rates: α+ for positive errors and α− for negative ones. A unit that reacts more to good news than to bad settles above the mean; a pessimistic unit settles below it. Each converges to an expectile of the return distribution, a generalisation of the mean in the way a quantile generalises the median, and the set of expectiles pins down the distribution.

Three predictions follow, and the paper checked them in recordings from identified dopamine neurons in mice performing a task with seven possible juice sizes. First, each neuron should have a reversal point, the reward size at which its response flips from a pause to a burst, and the reversal points should differ systematically across neurons rather than scatter around one value. They did, spanning a range that was consistent across sessions. Second, a neuron’s reversal point should be predicted by its asymmetry, the ratio of its response slopes to positive and negative errors measured on separate trials: optimistic cells (steep for good news) should reverse high. They did, with a positive correlation across the population. Third, the reversal points and asymmetries together should decode to the actual distribution of rewards the animal was receiving. Running an imputation that finds a set of reward values whose expectiles match the recorded ones recovered a distribution that looked like the task’s, bimodal where the task was bimodal. The demonstration below runs the same logic on simulated neurons: a population learns from a reward distribution you choose, the reversal points fall on the expectile curve, and the decoder reads the distribution back. Switch the distribution and watch the cloud of reversal points change shape; the project lets you build your own.

\[ V_i \leftarrow V_i + \begin{cases} \alpha_i^{+}\,\delta_i & \delta_i > 0 \\ \alpha_i^{-}\,\delta_i & \delta_i \le 0 \end{cases}, \qquad \tau_i \doteq \frac{\alpha_i^{+}}{\alpha_i^{+} + \alpha_i^{-}}, \qquad \tau_i\,\mathbb E\big[(R - V_i)^{+}\big] = (1-\tau_i)\,\mathbb E\big[(V_i - R)^{+}\big] \]
Read it as: unit i sees its own error δi = R − Vi (one-step, for a cue followed directly by the reward) and steps by one rate for good news and another for bad. At the fixed point the expected upward step equals the expected downward step, which is the third expression: Vi is the τi-expectile of the reward distribution. τ = ½ gives the mean; τ → 1 gives the maximum, τ → 0 the minimum. The asymmetry τi is what the experiment measures from the two slopes, and Vi is the reversal point.

Why would a brain bother? The same reasons a deep agent does. A distribution tells the animal not just how good a cue is on average but how risky it is, which matters for an animal deciding whether to forage now or wait; and in the artificial systems the distributional targets act as auxiliary tasks that shape the representation and speed learning. The evidence is correlational and from one task in one species, the decoding is only as good as the number of neurons recorded, and alternatives exist (a population with a spread of reversal points could also arise from neurons tuned to different reward features). But it is the first serious proposal in two decades for what the scalar-TD account leaves out, and it came from reading the deep-RL literature back into the recordings, as the 1997 paper read TD into them.

Open questions II

Ramps, tonic dopamine and what one number cannot carry

Two more observations sit uneasily with a phasic prediction error, and both are live research. The first is the ramp. When rats run a maze towards a reward, dopamine concentration in the striatum, measured chemically, rises steadily over several seconds as the goal approaches and peaks at arrival (Howe, Tierney, Sandberg, Phillips and Graybiel, 2013); similar slow ramps appear in cue-to-reward intervals, largest when the reward is uncertain (Fiorillo, Tobler and Schultz, 2003). A ramp looks like a value signal, V rising as the reward nears, not an error, which should be flat and near zero on a well-learned approach: each step’s value is exactly γ times the next, so γVt+1 − Vt = 0. Gershman (2014) pointed out that the error is zero only if the animal knows exactly where it is. Suppose the animal’s belief about its position is a mixture of the true position and a vague prior over the whole track, with the prior’s weight shrinking as the goal comes into view. Then its value estimate starts too low and catches up faster than discounting predicts, and the TD error is positive and growing all the way to the goal: a ramp made of errors. Kim and colleagues (2020) tested the two readings in mice running in virtual reality, where the experimenter can teleport the animal or change the speed of the scenery: the dopamine signal moved with the rate of change of value, as an error account requires, not with the value itself. The second observation is about the background. The hypothesis says nothing about the tonic level of dopamine, the slow concentration in the striatum that depends on how many neurons are firing their few spikes per second and how fast released dopamine is cleared. Yet tonic dopamine clearly matters for behaviour: animals and people with less of it (Parkinson’s disease, dopamine antagonists) move and respond more slowly and are less willing to work for reward, and amphetamine, which raises it, speeds everything up, without changing which option is preferred. Niv, Daw, Joel and Dayan (2007) offered an account that fits the RL framework exactly. In a continuing task (the average-reward chapter) the quantity that measures how good life is overall is the average reward rate ρ̄, and every second spent doing something is a second not earning ρ̄: the opportunity cost of time. An animal choosing how fast to act should balance the cost of vigour (acting fast is tiring; take it to be c/τ for a response of latency τ) against ρ̄τ, the reward forgone while being slow. The optimum is τ* = √(c/ρ̄): richer environments call for faster responding. The proposal is that tonic dopamine reports ρ̄, which is exactly the quantity the average-reward TD error subtracts at every step, so the same system that signals the phasic error would carry its own baseline as the slow level. It makes a clean prediction, that tonic dopamine should scale with the reward rate of the environment and that raising it should speed responding without changing preference, and the broad pattern matches, though direct measurements of tonic dopamine against reward rate remain hard to make. Raise the reward rate and watch the optimal latency fall.

\[ \text{cost}(\tau) = \frac{c}{\tau} + \bar\rho\,\tau, \qquad \frac{d\,\text{cost}}{d\tau} = -\frac{c}{\tau^2} + \bar\rho = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \tau^{*} = \sqrt{c/\bar\rho}, \qquad \delta_t = R_{t+1} - \bar\rho + V(S_{t+1}) - V(S_t) \]
Read it as: τ is how long the animal takes to respond, c the price of hurrying (a cost per unit of speed, so c/τ per response), ρ̄ the average reward per unit time in this environment. Minimising gives the square-root rule. The last expression is the average-reward TD error from the continuing-tasks chapter: ρ̄ is subtracted from every reward, so the phasic error and the tonic level are two parts of one signal.

Add to these the finding that some dopamine neurons respond to aversive events and to novel or salient stimuli of no value, that the responses of VTA and SNc neurons differ in detail, that dopamine release in the striatum can be shaped locally by cholinergic interneurons independently of the midbrain spikes, and that the midbrain error itself is assembled by subtraction from inputs that carry reward and expectation separately (Eshel et al., 2015, showed the arithmetic is close to literal), and you have the state of the field: the reward-prediction-error hypothesis is the trunk, with a growing set of branches that it has to accommodate or that will eventually replace parts of it. For the algorithms in this book, none of that threatens the core correspondence. The TD error is what a learner needs to improve a prediction; something very like it is what the midbrain broadcasts.

What the booth leaves out

The booth is the hypothesis in its simplest form, and it is worth being explicit about what has been simplified so that a disagreement with a real recording is read correctly. The state is a perfect clock that starts at the cue and is never reset, which is why an early reward produces a pause at the old time here and did not clearly do so in the monkeys. The encoding is a straight line from δ to rate with a floor at zero and independent Poisson spikes; real dopamine neurons burst in short packets of spikes, their pauses carry information in their duration, and their trial-to-trial variability is not Poisson. The response is one component; Schultz (2016) distinguishes an early, unselective component of the burst, driven by how salient or surprising a stimulus is regardless of value, from the later component that carries the prediction error, and a novel or intense stimulus of no value produces the first without the second. The model also has no action: the monkeys had to reach or release a key to collect the juice, and in the real tasks the cue response partly reflects the value of what the animal is about to do. And the booth trains one weight vector with one step size, whereas the distributional half of the project assumes a population with a spread of step-size pairs; a real population presumably has both the spread and a common input. None of these changes the three panels; all of them matter once the question is quantitative.

import numpy as np

# ---------- the page's random-number generator, bit for bit ----------
def mulberry32(a):
    def nxt():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return nxt

# ---------- TD(lambda) with a complete serial compound, one cue ----------
T, CUE = 30, 5                      # steps per trial (0.1 s each), cue onset step

def run_trial(w, reward_at, size, g=0.95, lam=0.9, a=0.1, learn=True):
    """One trial. Features: x_k is on at step CUE+k. Returns the response e[s] = R_s + g V_s - V_{s-1}
    (the TD error attributed to the event at step s) and updates w in place when learn is True."""
    V = np.zeros(T + 1)
    for s in range(CUE, T): V[s] = w[s - CUE]
    R = np.zeros(T + 1)
    if reward_at is not None: R[reward_at] = size
    e = np.zeros(T + 1); z = np.zeros(T - CUE)
    for t in range(T):                                   # transition t -> t+1
        d = R[t + 1] + g * V[t + 1] - V[t]
        e[t + 1] = d
        if t >= CUE: z[t - CUE] += 1.0
        if learn: w += a * d * z
        z *= g * lam
    return e

def train(n, reward_at=15, size=1.0, **kw):
    w = np.zeros(T - CUE)
    for _ in range(n): run_trial(w, reward_at, size, **kw)
    return w

Wrap-up · 15.1–15.6

Five lines to remember

  • Dopamine is a neuromodulator released by a few hundred thousand midbrain neurons (VTA and SNc) onto the striatum and cortex; it acts slowly and diffusely, which makes it the natural carrier of a scalar reinforcement signal in a three-factor learning rule.
  • Reward, value, reinforcement and prediction error are four different things. In a TD learner the reinforcement signal is δt = Rt+1 + γV(St+1) − V(St): zero for a predicted reward, positive for a predictive cue, negative for an omission.
  • The reward-prediction-error hypothesis (Montague, Dayan and Sejnowski 1996; Schultz, Dayan and Montague 1997) says the phasic dopamine response is δt. It explains the burst to a surprise reward, its transfer to the earliest predictor, and the pause at an omission with one formula, and the response size at the cue is the discount factor made visible, γISIR.
  • The hypothesis survived its tests: late rewards produce a pause then a burst (Hollerman and Schultz 1998); the response to a cue is blocked when another cue already predicts the reward and unblocked when the reward grows (Waelti, Dickinson and Schultz 2001); responses scale with probability and magnitude; and driving the neurons causes learning. The remaining mismatches (no intermediate bump, no pause after an early reward, compressed negative errors) constrain the trace parameter, the state representation and the encoding, not the rule.
  • One scalar may not be the whole story: neurons with different asymmetric learning rates settle at different expectiles and jointly encode the reward distribution (Dabney et al. 2020); ramps can be errors under state uncertainty; and the tonic level may report the average reward rate that sets how vigorously to act (Niv et al. 2007).

Check your understanding

A dopamine neuron fires a burst when a well-trained monkey receives its usual juice on schedule. Is that consistent with the hypothesis?

No, not in the strict form: a fully predicted reward has δ ≈ 0 and should produce no phasic response. A burst would mean the reward was larger, earlier or more certain than the prediction, or that the prediction was not fully formed (the response fades over training rather than vanishing at once). A small residual response to predicted reward is in fact often seen and is usually attributed to incomplete prediction or to the model’s state not matching the animal’s.

Why does the response to the cue stop growing at γISIR rather than at R? What would a cue response of exactly R tell you?

The cue’s value is the discounted reward, γISI−1R, and the burst at the cue is γ times the value that appears there. A response equal to R would mean γ = 1 over that interval, no temporal discounting at all, or that the animal’s value includes something beyond the juice. Measuring cue responses at different delays is a way of measuring the animal’s discount function, and dopamine cue responses do fall with delay.

In the blocking experiment, B was paired with juice forty times and the neuron never came to respond to it. What single number explains that, and what would restore learning about B?

The TD error during the AB trials: A already predicted the juice, so δ at the reward was near zero and B’s weights were never moved. Anything that makes δ non-zero again during the compound trials restores learning: a larger reward (unblocking, which Waelti et al. observed), an earlier reward, or, as Steinberg et al. did, driving the dopamine neurons directly.

A TD(0) learner with a complete serial compound predicts a bump of dopamine at intermediate times during learning. Recordings do not show one. Name two changes to the model that remove the bump without abandoning the hypothesis.

A long eligibility trace (λ near 1), so that all the features active before the reward learn from it at once and the error moves to the cue without passing through the middle; and a coarse representation of elapsed time (microstimuli), which spreads the intermediate error over many weak features so that no single moment carries a visible bump. Pan et al. (2005) found evidence for the first.

Two simulated neurons have α+ = 0.03, α− = 0.01 and α+ = 0.01, α− = 0.03. Rewards are 0 or 1 with equal probability. Where do they settle?

At the τ-expectiles with τ = 0.75 and 0.25. For a fair coin on {0, 1} the τ-expectile e solves τ(1 − e)·½ = (1 − τ)e·½, so e = τ: 0.75 and 0.25. The optimistic neuron reverses at 0.75 (it bursts only for the reward of 1 and pauses hard for 0), the pessimistic one at 0.25. Their two reversal points already say the rewards are spread, which one neuron at the mean of 0.5 could not.

Why is a ramp of dopamine during an approach to reward a problem for the hypothesis, and what assumption dissolves it?

On a well-learned approach with a known position the value rises by exactly 1/γ per step, so the TD error is zero throughout and only the reward itself could produce a response; a steady rise looks like value, not error. If the animal is uncertain about its position and that uncertainty shrinks as the goal nears, its value estimate starts too low and rises faster than γ predicts, so the error is positive and growing: a ramp made of prediction errors, which is what the speed and teleport manipulations of Kim et al. (2020) support.
