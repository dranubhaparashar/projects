Words, Unicode & Regular Expressions

Before a model can learn language, somebody has to decide what a word is

Every language model begins with a stream of text and a set of decisions that look too small to matter: is “The” the same word as “the”? Is “don’t” one word or two? Is “é” one character or two? Which bytes does it occupy? This chapter is about those decisions and the tools that make them: counting words, describing a corpus honestly, reading Unicode, writing regular expressions, splitting text into tokens and sentences, and learning a vocabulary of sub-word pieces. The figure counts the distinct words in a 720-word essay as it is read. Switch the normalisation and watch the vocabulary curve bend: the same text, a different number of words.

The unit of analysis

Tokens, types and Heaps’ law

Ask how many words are in “The river rises in the hills, runs past the mill” and you have already made three decisions. Do the commas count? (Usually not, unless you are modelling punctuation, which language models do.) Are “The” and “the” the same word? (For a search engine yes; for a part-of-speech tagger the capital letter is a clue worth keeping.) Are “rises” and “rise” the same word? They share a lemma, the dictionary headword, but they are different wordforms, and a model that predicts the next word must predict the form, not the lemma. Spoken corpora add their own cases: “um” and “uh” are fillers, and a speaker who starts “the ri- the river” has produced a fragment. Whether to keep them depends on the job: a speech recogniser must predict them, a translator should drop them.

Two counts are worth separating from the start. A token is a running occurrence of a word: the sentence above has nine, and “the” accounts for three of them. A type is a distinct word: the sentence has seven. We write N for the number of tokens and |V| for the size of the vocabulary, the set of types. Their ratio |V|/N, the type–token ratio, is a crude measure of lexical variety, crude because it falls as the text grows: common words keep recurring while new ones arrive ever more slowly. How slowly is the subject of a very old empirical law.

\[ |V| \;=\; k\,N^{\beta}, \qquad 0 < \beta < 1 \]
Read it as: the number of distinct words grows as a power of the number of words read, with an exponent below 1, so the vocabulary keeps growing but ever more slowly. This is Heaps’ law (Heaps, 1978), also called Herdan’s law after the linguist who noted it first (Herdan, 1960). On large English corpora β is typically between 0.67 and 0.75 and k between 10 and 100; on a short text, before the common words have had time to repeat, β comes out close to 1. The law is a description, not a mechanism: it follows from Zipf’s law on word frequencies, which the project at the end of the chapter plots.

Wordforms, lemmas and morphemes

Below the word sits the morpheme, the smallest unit that carries meaning. “Unbelievable” is one wordform, one lemma and three morphemes: the prefix un-, the stem believe and the suffix -able. English is light on morphology, which is why word-level counting works tolerably for it; Turkish or Finnish can pack a whole English clause into one wordform, and a word-level vocabulary for such a language never stops growing. That is one reason the chapter ends with sub-word pieces: they let a model’s vocabulary sit between morphemes and words, wherever the data puts it.

Worked example by hand: a 40-word sentence

Here is a sentence written for this purpose. Count its tokens and types, then fit Heaps’ law to the first half and the whole, with nothing more than a logarithm.

  1. 1 · Tokens
    \( N = 40 \)
    how exactly?
    Split on spaces, strip the punctuation: “hills,” is the token hills, “water:” is water, “return.” is return. Six clauses of 6 + 8 + 10 + 4 + 4 + 8 words = 40.
  2. 2 · Types
    \( |V| = 27 \) case-folded, \( 28 \) if “The” ≠ “the”
    how exactly?
    Repeated words: the × 9 (one of them capitalised), in × 3, and × 3, high × 2. Everything else appears once. Repeats cost 8 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 13 tokens, so 40 − 13 = 27 types. Keeping case apart splits The from the: 28.
  3. 3 · Type–token ratio and the half-way count
    \( |V|/N = 27/40 = 0.675, \qquad |V|(20) = 15 \)
    how exactly?
    The first 20 tokens are “The river rises in the hills runs past the mill and the old bridge and the town still measures its”. Repeats so far: the × 5, and × 2, costing 4 + 1 = 5, so 20 − 5 = 15 types.
  4. 4 · Two-point Heaps fit
    \( \beta = \frac{\log(27/15)}{\log(40/20)} = \frac{\log 1.8}{\log 2} = 0.848, \qquad k = \frac{15}{20^{0.848}} = 1.18 \)
    how exactly?
    Take logs of |V| = kNβ at both points and subtract: log 27 − log 15 = β(log 40 − log 20), so β = log 1.8 / log 2 = 0.5878 / 0.6931 = 0.848 (any base works, the ratio is the same). Then 200.848 = e0.848 × 2.996 = e2.540 = 12.68 and k = 15 / 12.68 = 1.18. Check: 1.18 × 400.848 = 1.18 × 22.8 = 27.0.
  5. 5 · Reading the numbers
    least squares over all 40 prefixes: \( \beta = 0.870,\; k = 1.08 \)

    An exponent near 0.85 says the vocabulary is still fresh: almost every new word is new. A 100-million-word corpus has β near 0.7 and k in the tens, because by then “the”, “of” and “and” have been seen millions of times and the newcomers are rare words and names. The constants describe the text; they are not universal.

    how exactly?
    Least squares fits a line y = a + βx through the 40 points (x, y) = (log N, log |V|(N)) for N = 1 … 40, minimising the squared vertical errors; k = ea. The page computes it below; the two-point fit uses only the end points and is close.
The data

Corpora and their variety

A corpus (plural corpora) is a collection of text or speech gathered for study: a shelf of novels, a year of a newspaper, ten thousand transcribed phone calls, a crawl of the public web. Language models learn whatever their corpus contains, so the first fact about any model is which corpus it saw, and the corpus has dimensions that matter more than its size.

Language. The world has roughly 7,000 languages and the large corpora exist for a few dozen. Most text on the web is English, and much of the rest is Chinese, Spanish, Russian, German, Japanese and French; a model trained on a web crawl spends almost all of its capacity on them. Many speakers switch languages mid-sentence (code switching: “kal meeting hai, so please be on time”), which a monolingual corpus never shows. Genre. A recipe, a legal clause, a weather forecast and a text message are all English, but their vocabulary, sentence length, punctuation and spelling differ so much that a model fitted to one is lost in the others; the figure below measures four small samples. Time. Spelling, vocabulary and topics drift: a corpus of nineteenth-century letters writes “to-day”, a 2015 corpus has never heard of the words that fill a 2025 one. Speakers. Age, region, gender, class and dialect all shape text. African American English has grammar of its own (the habitual “be” in “she be working” means usually, not now), and a tagger or a toxicity filter trained only on the mainstream newspaper variety will misread it. Situation. People write differently to a friend, a court and a customer, and speak differently from how they write: spoken corpora are full of fillers, repairs and half-sentences.

The datasheet habit

Because the corpus decides what the model knows, the corpus deserves documentation. Gebru et al. (2021) proposed a datasheet for every dataset, modelled on the specification sheets that come with electronic components, and Bender & Friedman (2018) a data statement for language data in particular. The questions are plain: who made this, why, from what, when, in which language varieties, by which speakers, with what permission, cleaned how, and for which uses was it meant? The habit is to answer them before training, when the answers can still change the data. Characters

Unicode code points and normalisation

A computer stores text as numbers, and Unicode is the agreement about which number means which character. Each character is a code point, written U+ followed by its hexadecimal value: U+0041 is “A”, U+00E9 is “é”, U+0939 is the Devanagari letter “ह”, U+1F600 is a grinning face. The space runs from U+0000 to U+10FFFF, over a million slots, of which about 155,000 are assigned in Unicode 16 (2024). Every code point has a general category: letter, mark, number, punctuation, symbol, separator or other, each with subcategories (uppercase letter, decimal digit, combining mark, …). Regular expressions can test them: \p{L} matches any letter in any script, which is the right way to say “a letter” once you leave ASCII.

The complication that bites every text pipeline is that the same visible character can have more than one encoding. The letter “é” exists as a single precomposed code point U+00E9, and also as the plain “e” U+0065 followed by the combining acute accent U+0301, a mark that attaches to whatever precedes it. The two are canonically equivalent: they must look and behave identically, yet as strings they are unequal, have different lengths and sort differently. Unicode Standard Annex #15 defines four normalisation forms that make equivalent strings identical. NFD decomposes everything into base letters plus marks in a fixed order; NFC decomposes and then recomposes into the shortest precomposed form, the form almost all text on the web is in. NFKD and NFKC go further and apply compatibility mappings, which also unify characters that merely look alike or mean alike: the ligature “ﬁ” becomes “fi”, the circled “①” becomes “1”, fullwidth “Ａ” becomes “A”, the superscript in “m²” becomes a plain 2. Compatibility normalisation loses information (“m²” and “m2” are different things), which is why it belongs in search indexes and tokenisers and not in a document editor.

Two properties make the forms usable in a pipeline. Each is idempotent: applying it twice is the same as applying it once. And each is stable: a string normalised under one version of Unicode stays normalised under every later version, because newly added characters never acquire decompositions to older ones. Browsers and operating systems therefore agree, which is more than can be said for case folding, where the Turkish I still differs by locale.

Case folding is not lowercasing

  • Lowercasing maps each letter to its lowercase form; case folding maps every letter to a canonical form chosen so that case-insensitive matching works. For English they coincide.
  • German “ß” has the uppercase “SS” (and, since 2017, “ẞ”); folding maps both to “ss”. Greek final sigma “ς” folds to “σ”. Turkish dotless “ı” and dotted “İ” break the ASCII rule I ↔ i, which is why toLowerCase() has a locale argument.
  • Rule of thumb: normalise to NFC first, then fold, then compare. Folding the pieces of a decomposed string and recomposing can give a different answer.

Why a tokeniser cares

  • A vocabulary learned on mixed NFC and NFD text contains two copies of every accented word, each with half the counts.
  • Deduplicating a web corpus by exact match misses near-copies that differ only in normalisation or in curly versus straight quotes (U+2019 versus U+0027).
  • Zero-width characters (U+200B zero-width space, U+FEFF byte-order mark) are invisible, survive copy and paste, and split tokens where no human sees a boundary. Strip or normalise them deliberately.
Bytes

UTF-8 bytes, grapheme clusters and why bytes matter

A code point is a number; an encoding says how to write the number as bytes. ASCII used seven bits and stopped at 127. UTF-32 spends four bytes on every character, which is simple and wasteful. UTF-8 (Thompson and Pike, 1992) uses one byte for the 128 ASCII characters, so every old ASCII file is already valid UTF-8, and two, three or four bytes for everything else. The first byte announces the length by its leading bits (0, 110, 1110, 11110) and every continuation byte begins 10, so a reader dropped into the middle of a stream can find the next character boundary by skipping at most three bytes. The remaining bits, shown as x below, hold the code point itself, most significant bits first.

\[ \text{bytes}(c) = \begin{cases} 1 & c \le \text{U+007F} \\ 2 & c \le \text{U+07FF} \\ 3 & c \le \text{U+FFFF} \\ 4 & c \le \text{U+10FFFF} \end{cases} \qquad \begin{aligned} &\texttt{0xxxxxxx} \\ &\texttt{110xxxxx 10xxxxxx} \\ &\texttt{1110xxxx 10xxxxxx 10xxxxxx} \\ &\texttt{11110xxx 10xxxxxx 10xxxxxx 10xxxxxx} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the thresholds are exactly what 7, 11, 16 and 21 payload bits can hold. Latin letters with accents, Greek, Cyrillic, Hebrew and Arabic fit in two bytes; most of the rest of the world’s scripts, including Devanagari, Chinese, Japanese and Korean, take three; emoji and historic scripts take four. English text is therefore about one byte per character while Hindi text is about three, a fact that will matter when a tokeniser counts bytes.

Grapheme clusters: what the user calls a character

Neither bytes nor code points match what a person sees. The “e” plus combining acute is two code points and one visible letter. A Devanagari syllable such as “क्ष” is three code points drawn as one shape. Emoji go further: a family “👩‍👩‍👧” is three people glued by two invisible zero-width joiners (U+200D), five code points and eighteen bytes for one picture; a thumbs-up with a skin tone is the hand plus a modifier code point; a flag is two regional indicator letters, “G” “B” for the United Kingdom; and a variation selector (U+FE0F) can ask for the colourful version of a symbol that also exists as plain text. Unicode Standard Annex #29 defines the grapheme cluster, the unit a cursor should jump over, and the rules are intricate enough that you should never write them yourself: JavaScript exposes them through Intl.Segmenter, Python through the regex module’s \X. Reversing a string or truncating it to 20 “characters” by code point will cut a family in half.

Why byte-level tokenisers matter. A model needs a finite vocabulary. If the base symbols are characters, the vocabulary must start from every code point the data might contain: tens of thousands of them, most seen once or never, and any new character is unrepresentable. If the base symbols are bytes, there are exactly 256, every string that will ever exist is a sequence of them, and the sub-word learner of section 8 can build up whatever larger pieces the data justifies. That is the choice GPT-2 made (Radford et al., 2019) and most large models since have kept. The cost is in the figure above: a Hindi or Chinese character enters the model as three or four byte symbols before any merge, so a byte-level vocabulary learned mostly from English text spends more tokens, and more money, on the same sentence in another script (Petrov et al., 2023). The prefix structure of UTF-8 helps a little, since the learner sees the same lead byte before every character of a script and can merge it early.

A language for patterns

Regular expressions from scratch

A regular expression is a short string that describes a set of strings. “Every price in pounds”, “every date written day/month/year”, “every word that ends in -ing”: each is a pattern, and a regex engine finds every piece of a text that fits it, in time proportional to the length of the text. The notation goes back to Kleene (1956), who used it to describe what the simplest automata can recognise; it reached programmers through Thompson’s text editor in 1968 and lives today in every language and every grep. For language processing it is the workhorse for tokenisation, cleaning, information extraction and quick corpus searches, and it is the formal core of the chatbot at the end of the chapter. The operators are few; everything else is combination.

Idioms for corpus work

The same half-dozen patterns turn up in every text pipeline. Each is a one-liner; each has a trap that the second line names. Click any to load it into the tester above (the text is the notice board, so some will find nothing until you paste a text that contains the target).

s/\s+/ /g

Collapse whitespace

Tabs, newlines and runs of spaces become one space. Trap: it also destroys paragraph breaks and the indentation of code; keep a copy of the raw text.

<[^>]+>

Strip tags

Removes HTML and XML tags without matching across them. Trap: a > inside an attribute value breaks it; for real HTML use a parser and keep the regex for a quick look.

(?<!\w)[#@]\w+

Hashtags and mentions

A # or @ that does not follow a word character, then a run of word characters. Trap: \w is ASCII in many engines, so a hashtag in Greek or Hindi needs \p{L} and the u flag.

\d+(?:[.,]\d+)*\s?(?:km|kg|cm|m|%|°C)(?!\p{L})

Numbers with units

Integers and decimals with a thousands separator, then a unit from a fixed list, not followed by another letter. Trap: the list is never complete, longer units must come before their prefixes (cm before m), and \b would not work after % because both sides are non-word characters.

(?<=[.!?])\s+(?=\p{Lu})

Sentence boundaries

Split where whitespace follows end punctuation and precedes a capital: the two cues of section 7 in one lookbehind and one lookahead, consuming only the space. Trap: abbreviations; add the exception list.

\b(\p{L}+)\s+\1\b

Doubled words

A word followed by a space and itself, the classic proof-reading check. Trap: “had had” and “that that” are often correct English, so this flags candidates rather than errors.

Building a pattern: find every “the”

The way a pattern is actually written is by trial and error against examples, and the errors come in two kinds. A false positive is a match you did not want; a false negative is a wanted string the pattern missed. Shrinking the first raises precision, shrinking the second raises recall, and almost every edit trades one against the other. The classic exercise is to find every occurrence of the word the in a passage that also contains other, theatre, then and The. Step through the five attempts. Correct matches are amber, false positives red, and misses are underlined.

\[ P = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}}, \qquad R = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}}, \qquad F_1 = \frac{2PR}{P + R} \]
Read it as: precision is the share of what the pattern found that it should have found; recall is the share of what it should have found that it did find. F1 is their harmonic mean, which is low whenever either is low. The gold standard here is the set of 12 whole-word occurrences of the or The in the passage.
Say it“Precision is true positives over everything flagged; recall is true positives over everything that should have been.”

Capturing and rewriting

Groups, backreferences, lookahead and substitution

Parentheses do two jobs. They group, so that a counter or a disjunction applies to several characters at once: (ab)+ is ab, abab, …, where ab+ would be ab, abb, …. And they capture: whatever the group matched is stored in a numbered register, 1 for the first opening parenthesis, 2 for the second, and so on. The registers can be used in two places. Inside the pattern, a backreference \1 means “the same text group 1 just matched”, which is how you find a doubled word: \b(\w+)\s+\1\b finds “the the” and “had had” but not “the then”. In a substitution, the replacement string can quote them, written $1 in JavaScript and \1 in Python and sed: s/(\w+) (\w+)/$2, $1/ turns “Tolu Nkemelu” into “Nkemelu, Tolu”. A group you only need for grouping can be made non-capturing with (?:…), and a group can be named, (?<year>\d{4}), and quoted as $<year>.

Lookahead asserts without consuming. x(?=y) matches x only when y follows, but y is not part of the match and stays available for the next one; x(?!y) matches x only when y does not follow. Because the assertion is zero-width, it is the tool for conditions that would otherwise swallow text: “a word followed by a comma” without taking the comma, or “a line that does not begin with Lost” as ^(?!Lost). Most engines also offer lookbehind, (?<=£)\d+, for the mirror image. Try the substitutions. Captured groups are coloured in the text; the result box shows what the replacement produced.

Things that bite

  • Greed. ".*" on "a" and "b" matches the whole thing, from the first quote to the last. Use "[^"]*" or the lazy ".*?".
  • Backtracking. Nested counters such as (a+)+b on a long run of as with no b can take exponential time in backtracking engines; Thompson’s automaton construction never does, which is why RE2 and Rust’s regex refuse backreferences.
  • Escaping. A literal dot, plus or bracket needs a backslash, and in a Python or JavaScript string the backslash itself may need one: r"\." or /\./.

Where substitution earns its keep

  • Normalising a corpus: curly to straight quotes, several spaces to one, s/(\d),(\d{3})/$1$2/g to strip thousands separators.
  • Anonymising: replace every email with <EMAIL> and every 16-digit run with <CARD> before anything is logged.
  • The ELIZA transformation below: a pattern with a capture, a response that quotes it.
Segmentation

Word tokenisation and sentence segmentation

Tokenisation cuts running text into the units a model will count. Splitting on spaces is a start and a poor finish: it glues “hall.” and “hall” into different words, keeps “didn’t” whole when its two halves are did and not, and breaks “01632 960417” into two numbers that mean nothing apart. The Penn Treebank conventions, which most English tools inherit, make a set of specific choices: separate punctuation from words except where it belongs to them (the full stop in “Dr.”, the comma in “2,500”, the dot in “4.30”); split clitics, the contracted bits that lean on a neighbour, so “didn’t” becomes did + n’t, “we’re” we + ’re and the possessive “library’s” library + ’s; keep hyphenated words such as “hen-house” together; keep multiword names such as “Bridge Street” apart, as two tokens, and leave the grouping to later stages. Modern pipelines add rules for things the Treebank never saw: URLs, email addresses, hashtags and emoji stay whole.

The whole thing is one regular expression, a disjunction of alternatives tried in order, with the longest and most specific patterns first so that an email address is taken before its pieces can be. This is the tokeniser on this page:

https?://\S+|\S+@\S+\.[a-z]+                 # URLs and emails stay whole
|\d{1,2}:\d\d|\d{1,2}/\d{1,2}/\d{2,4}|\d{4}-\d\d-\d\d   # times and dates
|[£$€]?\d+(?:[.,]\d+)*(?:st|nd|rd|th|am|pm|p)?       # numbers, prices, 12th, 10am, 20p
|(?:Mr|Mrs|Ms|Dr|St|Prof|no|vs|etc|Jan|Feb|...|Dec)\.  # abbreviations keep their stop
|(?:[A-Z]\.)+                                 # initials: T. and U.S.
|\w+(?=n't\b)|n't\b|'(?:s|re|ve|ll|d|m)\b      # clitics, split off
|\p{L}+(?:[-']\p{L}+)*                        # words, with internal hyphens and apostrophes
|\.\.\.|--|[^\s\p{L}\p{N}]                    # ellipsis, dash, any other single mark

Hard cases, and what the rules above do with them

  • “Dr. Achebe”: the abbreviation alternative claims Dr. with its stop before the punctuation alternative can take the stop alone. Switch it off and Dr and . are two tokens, and the sentence splitter sees a boundary.
  • “4.30 p.m.”: the number pattern takes 4.30 whole because a dot between digits is part of the number; p.m. is matched by the rule for runs of single letters with stops.
  • “didn’t”: a lookahead lets the word pattern stop before n’t, and the clitic pattern then takes n’t. The Treebank writes did + n’t rather than didn + ’t, so that the second token is the same for don’t, won’t and can’t.
  • “library’s”: the possessive ’s is split, which is right for the possessive and wrong for the contraction of is in “the library’s closed”; the tokeniser cannot tell them apart, and a tagger later must.
  • “Tue-Sat”, “10am-5pm”: a hyphen between letters keeps a word together; between a number and a word it is split, so the range reads 10am - 5pm, which is what you want for a range and not for hen-house.

What the Treebank itself does differently

  • Quotation marks become `` and '', a LaTeX habit from 1993 that modern tools reverse.
  • Brackets become -LRB- and -RRB- so that parentheses can be used for parse trees.
  • Emails, URLs and emoji did not exist in its data; every modern tokeniser adds them in front of the Treebank rules, as the pattern on this page does.
  • Numbers with commas stay whole, as here; percentages become 5 and %, where this page keeps 5% only if you write it without a space.

Sentence segmentation

Sentences end with ., ! or ?, which would be easy if the full stop did not also end abbreviations, mark decimals and sit inside “4.30 p.m.”. Question marks and exclamation marks are nearly unambiguous; the full stop needs a decision. The simple rule that works surprisingly well: a stop ends a sentence when the next non-space character is a capital letter, a digit or an opening quote and the token before it is not a known abbreviation or a single initial. Machine-learned splitters (trained on text where the boundaries are marked) do better on hard cases, but they learn the same two cues. Switch the abbreviation list off to see what a naive splitter does to the notice board.

Languages without spaces. Chinese, Japanese and Thai write words with no spaces between them, so “tokenise on whitespace” is not even a starting point: 我们去了学校 (we went to school) is seven characters and three or four words depending on who you ask. Chinese processing has often used characters as tokens outright, because a character is already a meaningful unit and the word boundaries are genuinely disputed. That is the second argument, after unknown words, for letting the data decide the units.

Sub-words

Byte-pair encoding: let the data choose the vocabulary

A word vocabulary has a hole in it. Whatever words the training corpus contains, the test text will contain others: new names, new coinages, typos, fertiliser where the corpus had fertilizer, and every inflected form of a Turkish verb. Each is an unknown word that a word-level model can only map to one shared <UNK> symbol. Characters have the opposite problem: no unknowns, but sequences five times as long and units that carry almost no meaning on their own. Sub-word tokenisation sits between: frequent words stay whole, rare words are split into pieces that are themselves frequent (fertil + iser), and the pieces bottom out in single characters, so nothing is ever unknown.

Byte-pair encoding (BPE) is the simplest way to learn such pieces. It began as a compression trick (Gage, 1994) and was brought to translation by Sennrich, Haddow & Birch (2016). Start from a corpus split into words, each word written as its characters plus an end-of-word marker _ (so that er at the end of lower_ is a different symbol from er inside every). Count every adjacent pair of symbols across the corpus, weighting by word frequency. Merge the most frequent pair into one new symbol, everywhere it occurs, and write the merge down. Repeat k times. The vocabulary is the characters plus the k merged symbols, and the ordered list of merges is the tokeniser: to segment a new word, split it into characters and apply the merges in the order they were learned, whichever apply. The number k is the one knob, and it trades sequence length against vocabulary size.

\[ (a, b)^{*} = \arg\max_{(a,b)} \sum_{w \in \text{corpus}} \text{freq}(w)\cdot \#\{\,\text{adjacent } (a,b) \text{ in } w\,\}, \qquad |V_k| = |\text{chars}| + k \]
Read it as: at each step pick the pair of neighbouring symbols that occurs most often in the corpus, counting a word’s occurrences by its frequency, and make it one symbol. After k merges the vocabulary has grown by exactly k. Ties are broken by a fixed rule (here: the pair that was seen first), so the learner is deterministic.

Four merges by hand

An eight-word corpus: rain three times, rains twice, run twice, runs once. Written as symbols with the end marker: r a i n _ ×3, r a i n s _ ×2, r u n _ ×2, r u n s _ ×1. Paste “rain rain rain rains rains run run runs” into the box above to watch the same steps.

  1. 1 · Count pairs, merge the first maximum
    \( (r,a)=5,\;(a,i)=5,\;(i,n)=5,\;(n,\_)=5,\;(n,s)=3,\;(s,\_)=3,\;(r,u)=3,\;(u,n)=3 \;\Rightarrow\; \texttt{r}+\texttt{a}\to\texttt{ra} \)
    how exactly?
    (r, a) occurs in rain (3) and rains (2): 3 + 2 = 5. So do (a, i) and (i, n). (n, _) occurs at the end of rain (3) and run (2): 5. (n, s) is in rains (2) and runs (1): 3; (s, _) likewise 3; (r, u) and (u, n) are in run and runs: 2 + 1 = 3. Four pairs tie at 5; the rule takes the one seen first when scanning the words in order, which is (r, a) from the first word. The corpus becomes ra i n _, ra i n s _, r u n _, r u n s _.
  2. 2 · Recount, merge again
    \( (ra,i)=5,\;(i,n)=5,\;(n,\_)=5,\;\ldots \;\Rightarrow\; \texttt{ra}+\texttt{i}\to\texttt{rai} \)
    how exactly?
    The pair (r, a) is gone; (ra, i) inherits its 5 and is seen first. Only pairs touching the merged symbol change, which is why real implementations update counts incrementally rather than recounting. Corpus: rai n _, rai n s _, r u n _, r u n s _.
  3. 3 · The stem appears
    \( (rai,n)=5,\;(n,\_)=5 \;\Rightarrow\; \texttt{rai}+\texttt{n}\to\texttt{rain} \)
    how exactly?
    (rai, n) ties with (n, _) at 5 and is seen first. After the merge, rain is a single symbol inside both rain_ and rains_: the learner has found the stem without being told what a stem is. Corpus: rain _, rain s _, r u n _, r u n s _.
  4. 4 · The whole word
    \( (rain,\_)=3,\;(s,\_)=3,\;(r,u)=3,\;(u,n)=3,\;(rain,s)=2,\;(n,\_)=2 \;\Rightarrow\; \texttt{rain}+\texttt{\_}\to\texttt{rain\_} \)

    After four merges the vocabulary is the seven characters plus ra, rai, rain, rain_: 11 symbols, and the corpus takes 3·1 + 2·3 + 2·4 + 1·5 = 22 tokens for 8 words, 2.75 per word, down from 4.25. Merges 5 to 8, if you continue, are s_, ru, run and rains_; a ninth would make run_, and the vocabulary would be the word list plus its history.

    how exactly?
    (n, _) has fallen to 2 because the two runs are its only remaining occurrences. Four pairs tie at 3 and (rain, _) is seen first, in the first word. Token count: rain_ is 1 symbol × 3 words, rain s _ is 3 × 2, r u n _ is 4 × 2, r u n s _ is 5 × 1: 3 + 6 + 8 + 5 = 22; 22 / 8 = 2.75. Before any merge: 5·3 + 6·2 + 4·2 + 5·1 = 34, 34 / 8 = 4.25.

Three practical notes. First, real tokenisers pre-tokenise before learning: they split on whitespace and punctuation with a regular expression (GPT-2’s is a one-liner that separates contractions, letters, numbers and punctuation runs), and merges never cross those boundaries, so no symbol ever spans two words. Second, GPT-2 and its descendants run BPE over bytes, not characters, with the space attached to the front of a word rather than a marker at the end, which is why their tokens print with a leading space and why a word is a different token at the start of a line. Third, BPE is not the only learner: WordPiece (Schuster & Nakajima, 2012) merges the pair that most increases the likelihood of the corpus under a unigram model rather than the most frequent one, and the unigram method (Kudo, 2018) starts from a large vocabulary and prunes it. All of them produce a few tens of thousands of pieces, and the models of the following chapters never see a word again, only these.

Pattern and response

ELIZA from eight rules

In 1966 Joseph Weizenbaum published ELIZA, a program that held a conversation by doing nothing but pattern matching and substitution. Its most famous script imitated a non-directive psychotherapist, a persona chosen because such a therapist can reflect almost anything back as a question without appearing to know anything: “I am sad” becomes “Why are you sad?”. Each rule in the script was a pattern with a capture and a response that quoted the capture, with pronouns swapped so that the user’s I becomes the program’s you, plus a keyword ranking to decide which rule fires when several match and a fallback when none does. Weizenbaum was disturbed by how readily people confided in it, and the tendency to read understanding into fluent pattern-matching has been called the ELIZA effect ever since. The eight rules below were written for this page; they share the method and none of the original wording. Type a sentence about yourself and watch which rule fires and what it captured.

Everything in the chapter is in this toy. The input is normalised (lower-cased, punctuation stripped) so that one pattern covers many spellings; word boundaries keep I from matching inside it; capture groups lift out the part worth echoing; a substitution table swaps pronouns; a priority order resolves conflicts; and the fallback rule is the price of finite recall. What is absent is any representation of what the words mean, which is why the fourth or fifth exchange usually gives the game away. B · Frequencies, Zipf and Heaps

Sort the types by count and give each a rank: 1 for the most frequent. Zipf (1949) observed that the count falls roughly as the inverse of the rank, so that on log–log axes the frequency table is close to a straight line of slope about −1. Heaps’ law from section 1 is a consequence: if frequencies fall like a power of rank, the number of distinct words seen grows like a power of the text length.

\[ f(r) \;\approx\; \frac{C}{r^{s}} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \log f = \log C - s\,\log r, \qquad s \approx 1 \text{ for large corpora} \]
Read it as: the r-th most frequent word is about 1/rs as frequent as the most frequent one. The fit below is a least-squares line through (log r, log f) for the ranks whose count is at least 2; the hapax legomena form a flat shelf at f = 1 which no line can follow and which would otherwise dominate the fit. On a 900-word text the slope comes out below 1 because the vocabulary is far from saturated; on a billion words it is close to 1.

The same workbench in Python

Save the text box as corpus.txt (the page’s default is the essay followed by a blank line and the notices) and run the listing. It needs only the standard library and NumPy. The tokeniser is the same regular expression as the browser’s, written with [^\W_] because Python’s re has no \p{L}; the BPE tie-break (the pair seen first) is the same; the fits use np.polyfit where the page uses closed-form least squares.

import re, unicodedata, collections, math, numpy as np

text = open("corpus.txt", encoding="utf-8").read()      # the essay and the notices from this page

# 1. normalise and tokenise: NFKC, case-fold, runs of letters/digits with one internal apostrophe
norm = unicodedata.normalize("NFKC", text).casefold()
tokens = re.findall(r"[^\W_]+(?:['’][^\W_]+)?", norm)
counts = collections.Counter(tokens)
N, V = len(tokens), len(counts)
print(f"tokens {N}  types {V}  type/token {V/N:.3f}  hapax legomena {sum(1 for c in counts.values() if c == 1)}")

# 2. frequency table and Zipf fit: log f = a - s log r, over the ranks with f >= 2
ranked = counts.most_common()
print("rank  freq  word")
for r, (w, f) in enumerate(ranked[:10], 1): print(f"{r:4d} {f:5d}  {w}")
r = np.arange(1, V + 1); f = np.array([c for _, c in ranked])
m = f >= 2
s, a = np.polyfit(np.log(r[m]), np.log(f[m]), 1)
print(f"Zipf: log f = {a:.3f} - {-s:.3f} log r   (fit over {m.sum()} ranks with f >= 2)")

# 3. Heaps' law: |V| after every 10th token, least squares in log-log
seen, growth = set(), []
for t in tokens:
    seen.add(t); growth.append(len(seen))
Ns = np.arange(10, N + 1, 10)
beta, logk = np.polyfit(np.log(Ns), np.log([growth[n - 1] for n in Ns]), 1)
print(f"Heaps: |V| = {math.exp(logk):.3f} * N^{beta:.3f}")

# 4. regular-expression extraction on the raw text
patterns = {
  "email": r"[\w.+-]+@[\w-]+(?:\.[\w-]+)+",
  "url":   r"https?://[^\s)]+",
  "date":  r"\b\d{1,2}/\d{1,2}/\d{4}\b|\b\d{4}-\d{2}-\d{2}\b|\b\d{1,2}(?:st|nd|rd|th)? (?:Jan(?:uary)?|Feb(?:ruary)?|Mar(?:ch)?|Apr(?:il)?|May|June?|July?|Aug(?:ust)?|Sep(?:tember)?|Oct(?:ober)?|Nov(?:ember)?|Dec(?:ember)?)(?: \d{4})?\b",
  "price": r"[£$€] ?\d+(?:\.\d{2})?|\b\d+p\b",
  "time":  r"(?<![£$€\d.])\b\d{1,2}[:.]\d{2}(?: ?[ap]\.?m\.?)?\b|\b\d{1,2} ?[ap]m\b",
  "phone": r"\b0\d{4} ?\d{6}\b",
}
for name, p in patterns.items():
    found = re.findall(p, text)
    print(f"{name:6s} {len(found):2d}  {', '.join(found[:6])}{' ...' if len(found) > 6 else ''}")

# 5. byte-pair encoding: ten merges learned on the word frequencies
words = {tuple(w) + ("_",): c for w, c in counts.items()}
merges = []
for step in range(10):
    pairs = collections.Counter()
    for sym, c in words.items():
        for i in range(len(sym) - 1): pairs[sym[i], sym[i + 1]] += c
    best = max(pairs, key=pairs.get)                            # ties: the pair seen first
    merges.append(best)
    def merge(sym):
        out, i = [], 0
        while i < len(sym):
            if i < len(sym) - 1 and (sym[i], sym[i + 1]) == best: out.append(sym[i] + sym[i + 1]); i += 2
            else: out.append(sym[i]); i += 1
        return tuple(out)
    words = {merge(sym): c for sym, c in words.items()}
    print(f"merge {step + 1:2d}: {best[0]!r} + {best[1]!r} -> {(best[0] + best[1])!r}   ({pairs[best]} occurrences)")
tokens_per_word = sum(len(sym) * c for sym, c in words.items()) / N
print(f"after 10 merges: {len({x for sym in words for x in sym})} symbols, {tokens_per_word:.3f} tokens per word")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A token is an occurrence, a type is a distinct word, and what counts as either is a decision: case, punctuation, clitics, numbers. The vocabulary grows as k·Nβ (Heaps) because frequencies fall as C/rs (Zipf); both exponents describe your corpus, and your corpus deserves a datasheet.
  • Text is code points, code points are bytes, and neither is what a reader sees. Normalise to NFC (or NFKC for matching) before comparing anything; UTF-8 spends one byte on ASCII and three or four on most other scripts, and grapheme clusters, not code points, are the units a cursor or a truncation should respect.
  • A regular expression describes a set of strings with disjunction, counters, anchors and classes, matched in linear time. Write it against examples and measure it: false positives cost precision, false negatives cost recall, and \b[Tt]he\b is the shape most word patterns end up with.
  • Groups capture, backreferences and $1 reuse the capture, and lookahead asserts without consuming. Substitution with captures is how text is normalised, anonymised and, in ELIZA, answered.
  • Tokenisers are ordered regular expressions: Treebank-style for words and clitics, a two-cue rule for sentences. Byte-pair encoding replaces the word list with k merges learned from the data, so nothing is unknown and k trades sequence length for vocabulary size. The models of this part see only those pieces.

Check your understanding

A text has 10,000 tokens and 2,000 types. Heaps’ law with β = 0.7 is assumed. Roughly how many types would 100,000 tokens of the same kind of text have?

|V| scales as N0.7, so multiplying N by 10 multiplies |V| by 100.7 ≈ 5.0: about 10,000 types. The type–token ratio falls from 0.20 to 0.10, which is why the ratio is only comparable between texts of the same length.

Two strings print identically as “résumé” but a === b is false and a.length is 6 while b.length is 8. What happened, and what fixes it?

One is NFC (precomposed é, U+00E9) and the other NFD (e + U+0301), so the second has two extra code points. a.normalize('NFC') === b.normalize('NFC') is true. Normalise at the point where text enters the system, not at every comparison.

How many bytes does “我们” take in UTF-8, and how many UTF-16 code units in JavaScript? What about one “😀”?

Both characters are in the range U+0800–U+FFFF, so three bytes each: six bytes, and two UTF-16 units (one each). The emoji is U+1F600, above U+FFFF: four bytes in UTF-8 and two UTF-16 units, a surrogate pair, so '😀'.length === 2 while [...'😀'].length === 1.

The pattern \d+\.\d+ is meant to find prices but also matches “4.30 p.m.” and “version 2.1”. Which kind of error is that, and name two different ways to reduce it.

False positives: precision is suffering. Require the currency sign, £\d+\.\d\d, which also fixes the number of decimals; or use a negative lookahead for what follows a time, \d+\.\d\d(?! ?[ap]\.?m). Each repair risks a new false negative (prices written without a sign), so re-count both error types after every edit.

In the BPE lab, why does e_ get merged before th, even though the is the most frequent word?

Merges are chosen by the count of adjacent symbol pairs summed over all words, not by word frequency. The pair (e, _) occurs at the end of the, one, are, time and every other word ending in e, 186 times in the chapter corpus, while (t, h) occurs 137 times. Once th and e_ exist, their pair is the next most frequent and the_ appears at merge 4.

A sentiment classifier trained on film reviews is deployed on text messages and does badly. Which dimension of corpus variety is to blame, and what would a datasheet have said?

Genre (and probably time and speakers too): reviews are long, edited, punctuated and spelled conventionally; messages are short, unpunctuated and full of forms such as u, lol and :) that the review vocabulary never contained. A datasheet listing “genre: edited film reviews, 2005–2010; intended use: review scoring” would have flagged the mismatch before deployment.

The section-7 tokeniser turns “rock ’n’ roll” into five tokens: rock, ’, n, ’, roll. Which alternatives fired, and how would you fix it without breaking the clitic rule?

The clitic alternative wants ’s, ’re and the like, not ’n; the word alternative must begin with a letter; so the first apostrophe falls through to the final “any other mark” alternative, n is then a one-letter word, and the second apostrophe is another mark. The fix is a specific alternative placed earlier in the disjunction, ’n’ as a fixed string before the clitic rules, which is how every real tokeniser grows: by adding exceptions in front of the general cases.

ELIZA’s rule \bi am (.+) turns “I am tired of it” into “How long have you been tired of it?”. What goes wrong on “I am not sure what I am looking for”, and why?

The greedy .+ captures “not sure what I am looking for”, and after pronoun swapping the reply quotes “not sure what you are looking for”, which is grammatical but reflects the whole clause including the second I am. The rule has no notion of clause structure; the match is the longest string that fits. A pattern is a set of strings, never a parse.
