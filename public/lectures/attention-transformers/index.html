Attention & Transformers

Look back at everything, and choose what matters

A recurrent network has to carry everything it has read in one running state. Attention throws that constraint away: whenever the network needs something, it looks back over the whole input and takes a weighted blend of the parts that match what it is looking for. Stack that idea into blocks and you have the transformer, the architecture behind every large language model. The small transformer on the right was trained to rewrite dates in fourteen everyday styles as ISO 8601. Type a date, or pick a famous one, and hover an output digit to see which input characters it read.

The idea

Why attention: look back, don’t remember

The first neural translators (Sutskever, Vinyals & Le, 2014; Cho et al., 2014) read a sentence with one recurrent network, squeezed it into a single state vector, and wrote the translation from that vector alone. Long sentences broke them: whatever the first words said had to survive dozens of steps of a fading memory, the problem of chapter 2.5. Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio (2015) kept every encoder state and let the decoder, at each word it writes, take a weighted average of them: an attention over the input. The weights come from a score ej for each input position, turned into shares by a softmax. Below, the decoder wants the word at one position. Click a word to choose it, then make the sentence longer.

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha_j &= \frac{e^{\,s_j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{T} e^{\,s_k}} \\ \mathbf{c} &= \sum_{j=1}^{T} \alpha_j\, \mathbf{h}_j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: sj scores how relevant input position j is to what the decoder is about to write. The softmax turns the scores into weights αj that are positive and add up to 1. The context vector c is the weighted average of the encoder states hj: mostly the relevant ones, a little of the rest. Every input position is one step away from c, however long the sentence.
Say it“Alpha-j is e to the s-j over the sum of e to the s-k; the context c is the sum of alpha-j times h-j.”

The mechanism

Queries, keys and values: a soft dictionary

A Python dictionary finds the one key that equals your query and returns its value. Attention does the same, softly. Every item has a key kj (what it offers to be matched on) and a value vj (what it hands over). A query q is scored against every key with a dot product, the scores become weights by a softmax, and the answer is the weighted average of the values. Nothing is ever a hard “no”, so the whole look-up is differentiable and can be learned by gradient descent. Drag the query star (and the keys): point it towards a key to fetch that key’s value, and pull it away from the centre to make the look-up sharper.

\[ \operatorname{attend}(\mathbf{q}) = \sum_{j} \underbrace{\frac{e^{\,\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}_j}}{\sum_i e^{\,\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}_i}}}_{\alpha_j}\; v_j \]
Read it as: score every key by its dot product with the query: large when they point the same way and are long. Softmax the scores into weights αj that sum to 1. Return the weighted average of the values. Doubling the length of q doubles every score, which sharpens the softmax: the length of the query acts like an inverse temperature.
Say it“Attend of q is the sum over j of e to the q-dot-k-j, over the sum of the same for every key, times v-j.”

A detail that matters

Why divide by √dk

Real queries and keys have dozens or hundreds of dimensions, not two. A dot product adds up one product per dimension, so its typical size grows with the dimension dk. Feed huge scores to a softmax and it snaps to a single winner with weight 1, and its slope, the gradient that training needs, goes to zero everywhere. Vaswani et al. (2017) therefore divide every score by √dk. Below are one random query and ten random keys with independent standard-normal entries. Raise the dimension, then switch the scaling off.

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}) &= \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} \operatorname{Var}(q_i k_i) = d_k \\ \operatorname{Var}\!\Big(\frac{\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big) &= 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: if every qi and ki is independent with mean 0 and variance 1, each product qiki has variance 1, and the variances of dk independent terms add. So raw scores spread like √dk; dividing by √dk brings their spread back to 1 whatever the dimension.
Say it“The variance of q dot k is d-k; divide by root d-k and the variance is one.”
\[ \frac{\partial \alpha_i}{\partial s_i} = \alpha_i\,(1-\alpha_i) \;\le\; \tfrac14 \]
Read it as: how much a weight moves when its own score moves. It is largest, ¼, when αi = ½, and it vanishes when αi is 0 or 1. A saturated softmax barely passes any gradient back to the scores, so the queries and keys stop learning.
Say it“d-alpha-i by d-s-i is alpha-i times one minus alpha-i, at most a quarter.”

All positions at once

Self-attention: every token looks at every token

In self-attention the queries, keys and values all come from the same sequence. Each token’s vector xi is multiplied by three learned matrices to give its query, key and value, and every token attends over all tokens, itself included. Stack the vectors as the rows of a matrix X and the whole layer is three matrix products and a softmax, computed for all positions in parallel. A decoder that writes left to right adds a mask so that no position can peek at later ones. Below, six tokens live in a 2-D embedding space; WQ rotates and stretches each token’s query, while keys and values are the vectors themselves. Drag the words, turn WQ, and hover a row of the matrix.

\[ \begin{aligned} Q &= XW_Q,\quad K = XW_K \\ V &= XW_V \\ Z &= \operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\Big)\,V \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: row i of QK⊤ holds token i’s scores against every token; the softmax is taken along each row, so each row of weights sums to 1, and row i of Z is token i’s new vector: a blend of the values it attends to. M is 0 where attention is allowed and −∞ where it is not (above the diagonal, in a decoder); e−∞ = 0, so masked positions get weight exactly 0.
Say it“Z is the softmax of Q K-transpose over root d-k, plus the mask, times V.”

Order

Positions: putting order back

Shuffle the words above and every token’s output stays the same: self-attention sees a set, not a sequence. “Dog bites man” and “man bites dog” would look identical. The fix is to add a positional encoding to each token’s vector before the first layer. The original transformer uses sines and cosines of the position at geometrically spaced frequencies: the first pair of dimensions turns quickly, like the second hand of a clock, and later pairs ever more slowly, like the minute and hour hands. Together they give every position a unique fingerprint. The date model on this page uses exactly these encodings. Move the position slider, and change the offset k.

\[ \begin{aligned} PE_{p,\,2i} &= \sin\!\big(\omega_i\, p\big) \\ PE_{p,\,2i+1} &= \cos\!\big(\omega_i\, p\big) \\ \omega_i &= 10000^{-2i/d} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: dimensions come in pairs. Pair i turns at angular speed ωi: pair 0 by 1 radian per position (a full turn every 2π ≈ 6.3 positions), the last pair so slowly that a full turn takes tens of thousands of positions. Each pair is a point on a circle, so every value stays between −1 and 1.
Say it“P-E at position p, dimension two-i, is the sine of omega-i times p; dimension two-i-plus-one is the cosine.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{u}_i(p) &= \begin{pmatrix} \sin\omega_i p \\ \cos\omega_i p \end{pmatrix} \\ \mathbf{u}_i(p{+}k) &= R(\omega_i k)\,\mathbf{u}_i(p) \\ R(\theta) &= \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix} \\ PE_p \cdot PE_{p+k} &= \sum_{i} \cos(\omega_i k) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ui(p) is pair i of the encoding. Moving k positions on rotates it by the angle ωik: a fixed rotation of every pair, the same whatever p is. So a layer can learn “look k tokens back” with one matrix, and the similarity of two positions depends only on how far apart they are.
Say it“The encoding of p plus k is the encoding of p, rotated by omega-i k in every pair; their dot product is the sum of the cosines of omega-i k.”

Several questions at once

Multi-head attention

One softmax spends one budget of attention, so one head can only look in one direction at a time. A token often needs several things at once: the word just before it, the subject of its sentence, the matching bracket. Multi-head attention runs h smaller attentions side by side, each with its own WQ, WK, WV, and concatenates their outputs. Below, ten coloured tokens and two heads wired by hand: head 1 compares a query rotated one position back with every key’s positional encoding (the rotation trick from above), so it finds the previous token; head 2 compares colours, so it finds tokens of the same colour. Click tokens to recolour them, then merge the two heads into one and watch both jobs fail.

\[ \begin{gathered} \text{head}_i = \operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{Q_i K_i^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big)\, V_i \\ Q_i = XW^{Q}_{i},\ \ K_i = XW^{K}_{i} \\ V_i = XW^{V}_{i} \\ \operatorname{MultiHead}(X) = {} \\ \big[\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h\big]\, W^{O} \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: each head has its own three projections and projects the tokens down to dk = d/h dimensions and does its own attention, with its own softmax. The h outputs are laid side by side, back to d numbers per token, and WO mixes them. Because each head is h times narrower, h heads cost the same as one full-width head.
Say it“Head i is the softmax of Q-i K-i-transposed over root d-k, times V-i, where Q-i is X W-Q-i and so on; multi-head is the heads side by side, times W-O.”

How the two heads are wired

Each token’s vector is its colour as a one-hot code (3 numbers) next to its positional encoding (8 numbers, the formula above with d = 8). Head 1’s WQ keeps only the encoding and rotates every pair by −ωi, turning PEp into PEp−1; its WK keeps the encoding as it is. Its score for key j is then β1 PEp−1·PEj, largest at j = p − 1. Head 2’s WQ and WK keep only the colour, so its score is β2 for a same-coloured key and 0 otherwise. The sharpness values β play the part of 1/√dk and of the learned scale of the weights. “One head, both jobs” adds the two scores before a single softmax, which is what one head with both feature sets in its WQ and WK would compute.

Architecture

The transformer block

Attention moves information between tokens; it is a weighted average, so on its own it is nearly linear. Each block therefore follows it with a small feed-forward network (FFN) that works on every token separately: widen, ReLU, narrow. Two more parts make deep stacks trainable. A residual connection adds each sub-layer’s output to its input, so the block only has to learn a correction and gradients have a straight path down (chapter 2.4). Layer normalisation (Ba, Kiros & Hinton, 2016) rescales each token’s vector to mean 0 and variance 1 before a sub-layer reads it. The original transformer normalised after each residual addition; most models since, GPT-2 and the date model below included, normalise before (“pre-LN”, which trains more stably; Xiong et al., 2020). The running examples use the date model’s real numbers for one character of the hero’s input.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{x} &\leftarrow \mathbf{x} + \operatorname{MultiHead}\big(\operatorname{LN}(\mathbf{x})\big) \\ \mathbf{x} &\leftarrow \mathbf{x} + \operatorname{FFN}\big(\operatorname{LN}(\mathbf{x})\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: one pre-LN encoder block. Normalise, attend, add back; normalise, transform each token, add back. The vector x that flows through the additions is often called the residual stream: every sub-layer reads it and writes a correction into it. A decoder block has a third step in the middle: cross-attention, whose queries come from the decoder and whose keys and values come from the encoder’s output.
Say it“x becomes x plus multi-head attention of layer-norm of x; then x plus the feed-forward network of layer-norm of x.”
\[ \operatorname{LN}(\mathbf{x}) = \boldsymbol{\gamma} \odot \frac{\mathbf{x} - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}} + \boldsymbol{\beta} \]
Read it as: μ and σ² are the mean and variance of this token’s d numbers (not of a batch, as in batch norm). Subtract, divide, then let learned γ and β set each dimension’s scale and offset. ε = 10−5 avoids dividing by zero.
Say it“Layer norm of x is gamma times x minus mu over root sigma-squared plus epsilon, plus beta.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{FFN}(\mathbf{x}) &= \max\!\big(0,\ \mathbf{x}W_1 + \mathbf{b}_1\big)\,W_2 \\ &\quad + \mathbf{b}_2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a two-layer network applied to each token on its own, with the same weights for every position. W1 widens d to dff (usually 4d; 2d in the date model), the ReLU keeps the positive parts, W2 narrows back to d. About two thirds of a large model’s weights live here.
Say it“The FFN of x is the max of zero and x W-one plus b-one, times W-two, plus b-two.”

How big does this get? A block’s weights grow with d², while the attention scores grow with the square of the context length n: every token is compared with every token. Pick a real model, or set your own sizes, and see where the weights and the arithmetic go. (The counts are for GPT-style decoder-only models with learned positions and tied input and output embeddings.)

\[ \begin{gathered} N_{\text{layer}} = \underbrace{4d^2 + 4d}_{\text{attention}} + \underbrace{4d}_{\text{2 LNs}} \\ {} + \underbrace{2\,d\,d_{ff} + d_{ff} + d}_{\text{FFN}} \\ \text{FLOPs per token} \approx 2N + 2\,L\,n\,d \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: WQ, WK, WV, WO are each d × d with a bias; the FFN has two matrices d × dff; each layer norm has a γ and a β. A forward pass costs about two operations (a multiply and an add) per weight per token, plus the attention scores and weighted sums, which grow with the context length n (Kaplan et al., 2020).
Say it“Weights per layer: four d-squared plus four d for attention, two d d-ff plus d-ff plus d for the FFN, four d for the norms. Cost per token: about two N plus two L n d.”

Real-world project

Project: a transformer that reads dates

Every data pipeline meets dates written by people: “July 14th, 1789”, “14-JUL-1789”, “Tue 14 Jul 1789”, “the 14th of July 1789”. The international standard, ISO 8601, writes them all as 1789-07-14. Turning one into the other is a small translation problem with a crisp right answer, which makes it a favourite first task for attention models: you can see whether each output digit looks at the right part of the input. We generated dates uniformly from 1 January 1000 to 31 December 2999 and wrote each in one of fourteen real-world styles (long and short month names, weekday names, ordinals, Oracle’s DD-MON-YYYY, the German dotted form, year-first slashes and the compact ISO basic form), with random capitalisation and optional leading zeros. Weekdays are correct for each date in the proleptic Gregorian calendar. The model learned from 80,000 examples; the 840 test examples (60 per style) use dates it never saw in any style.

1 · Test it in your browser

An answer counts only if all ten characters are right. Because a correct prefix is fed back in, this exact match can be checked in one pass with the true answer as the decoder’s input (teacher forcing): if every position’s top choice is the true character, greedy decoding writes the whole answer correctly, and if any is wrong, it doesn’t.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{exact} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \mathbb{1}\big[\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{(n)} = \mathbf{y}^{(n)}\big] \\ \text{loss} &= -\frac{1}{10N}\sum_{n,t} \log p^{(n)}_t \\ p^{(n)}_t &= p_\theta\big(y^{(n)}_t \mid y^{(n)}_{\lt t}, \mathbf{x}^{(n)}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: exact match is the share of test dates whose ten output characters are all right (ŷ = y compares the whole answer; 1[…] is 1 when true, 0 when not). The loss is the training objective: the average surprise at each true character, given the input date and the true characters before it.
Say it“Exact match is the share of dates with every character right; the loss is minus the average log-probability of each true character.”

2 · Every head, every layer

The model has 24 attention heads: 4 per layer in the encoder’s self-attention (2 layers), the decoder’s masked self-attention (2 layers) and the decoder’s cross-attention to the input (2 layers). Nobody told any of them what to do. Pick a kind of attention and a layer, type or pick a date, and hover any cell. The note says, for each head, which part of the input its attention mostly lands on.

3 · Switch heads off

Are all 24 heads needed? Silence a head by zeroing its output (the other heads and every weight stay as trained) and rerun the test. Studies of real translation models found that a few specialised heads do most of the work and many can be removed with little loss (Voita et al., 2019; Michel, Levy & Neubig, 2019). The same model in numpy

The model was trained with our own numpy code (forward pass and hand-written backward pass, checked against finite differences to a relative error below 10−6), with Adam for 5,000 steps of 96 dates. The reference below loads the same float16 weights the page uses and runs them in float64, exactly as your browser does.

import base64, json, numpy as np
M = json.load(open("at-model.json"))                       # the weights embedded in this page
W = {k: np.frombuffer(base64.b64decode(v), "<f2").astype(float).reshape(M["shapes"][k]) for k, v in M["w"].items()}
d, H = 64, 4; dk = d // H
def ln(x, p):  return (x - x.mean(-1, keepdims=True)) / np.sqrt(x.var(-1, keepdims=True) + 1e-5) * W[p+"g"] + W[p+"b"]
def lin(x, p): return x @ W[p+"W"] + W[p+"b"]
def pe(T):
    a = np.arange(T)[:, None] / 10000 ** (np.arange(0, d, 2) / d)
    P = np.zeros((T, d)); P[:, 0::2], P[:, 1::2] = np.sin(a), np.cos(a); return P
def mha(xq, xkv, p, causal=False, off=()):
    q, k, v = (lin(x, p+n+".").reshape(len(x), H, dk).transpose(1, 0, 2) for x, n in ((xq, "q"), (xkv, "k"), (xkv, "v")))
    s = q @ k.transpose(0, 2, 1) / np.sqrt(dk)
    if causal: s = s + np.triu(np.full(s.shape[1:], -np.inf), 1)
    a = np.exp(s - s.max(-1, keepdims=True)); a /= a.sum(-1, keepdims=True)
    o = a @ v; o[list(off)] = 0                                 # switched-off heads write nothing
    return lin(o.transpose(1, 0, 2).reshape(len(xq), d), p+"o.")
def ffn(x, p): return lin(np.maximum(0, lin(x, p+"1.")), p+"2.")

def logits(src, tin, off={}):
    x = W["Ein"][src] + pe(len(src))
    for l in range(2):
        p = f"enc.{l}."; h = ln(x, p+"ln1."); x = x + mha(h, h, p+"att.", off=off.get(("enc", l), ()))
        x = x + ffn(ln(x, p+"ln2."), p+"ffn.")
    m = ln(x, "lne."); y = W["Eout"][tin] + pe(len(tin))
    for l in range(2):
        p = f"dec.{l}."; h = ln(y, p+"ln1."); y = y + mha(h, h, p+"self.", True, off.get(("self", l), ()))
        y = y + mha(ln(y, p+"ln2."), m, p+"cross.", off=off.get(("cross", l), ()))
        y = y + ffn(ln(y, p+"ln3."), p+"ffn.")
    return lin(ln(y, "lnd."), "out.")

def score(s, iso, off={}):                                   # teacher-forced: are all ten argmaxes right? and the loss
    src = [M["inV"].index(c) for c in s if c in M["inV"][1:]]
    tin = [11] + ["0123456789-".index(c) for c in iso[:9]]; y = ["0123456789-".index(c) for c in iso]
    L = logits(src, tin, off); lp = L - L.max(-1, keepdims=True); lp -= np.log(np.exp(lp).sum(-1, keepdims=True))
    return (L.argmax(-1) == y).all(), -lp[range(10), y].mean()

test = M["test"]; res = [score(s, iso) for s, iso, _ in test]
for f in range(14):
    ok = [r[0] for r, t in zip(res, test) if t[2] == f]
    print(f"{M['fnames'][f]:24s} {sum(ok)} / {len(ok)}")
print(f"all {len(test)}: {sum(r[0] for r in res)} right, mean loss {np.mean([r[1] for r in res]):.3e}")
for kind in ["enc", "self", "cross"]:
    off = {(kind, l): range(H) for l in range(2)}
    print(f"every {kind:5s} head off: {sum(score(s, iso, off)[0] for s, iso, _ in test)} right")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Attention is a soft look-up: score a query against every key, softmax the scores into weights, return the weighted average of the values. Every position is one step from every other.
  • Scores are divided by √dk so that their spread stays near 1 whatever the dimension; otherwise the softmax saturates and its gradient vanishes.
  • Self-attention on its own ignores order, so positions are added to the inputs; sinusoidal encodings make “k steps away” a fixed rotation. A causal mask stops a decoder from seeing the future.
  • Multi-head attention runs several narrow attentions with their own softmaxes, so a token can look in several places at once, for the cost of one wide head.
  • A transformer block is attention plus a per-token feed-forward network, each wrapped in a residual connection and a layer norm. A 173,003-weight one rewrites all 840 held-out test dates, in fourteen styles, correctly, and its heads learn readable jobs.

Check your understanding

A query has dot products 2, 0 and 0 with three keys whose values are 10, 20 and 30. What does attention return?

The weights are e²/(e² + 2) ≈ 0.787 and 1/(e² + 2) ≈ 0.107 each, so the answer is 0.787 × 10 + 0.107 × 20 + 0.107 × 30 ≈ 13.2: mostly the first value, pulled a little towards the others.

Queries and keys have 256 dimensions with unit-variance entries. What is the typical size of a raw score, and what goes wrong if you don’t scale?

The scores have standard deviation √256 = 16. Differences of tens between scores make the softmax put almost all its weight on one key, where its slope α(1 − α) is nearly 0, so the queries and keys get almost no gradient. Dividing by 16 restores a spread of about 1.

You shuffle the input tokens of a self-attention layer that has no positional encoding. What happens to its outputs?

They are shuffled in the same way and are otherwise unchanged: each token’s output depends only on the set of tokens, not their order. Self-attention is permutation-equivariant, which is why positions must be added.

A model has d = 512 and 8 heads. How wide is each head, and how many weights do WQ, WK, WV, WO have in total, ignoring biases?

dk = 512/8 = 64. Each head’s WQ is 512 × 64, so all 8 together form one 512 × 512 matrix; the same for keys and values, plus WO: 4 × 512² = 1,048,576 weights, the same as a single full-width head.

The date model gets “7/4/1776” wrong. Why can no model reliably get it right?

It is ambiguous: 4 July in the United States, 7 April in most of Europe. The string alone does not say which, and the model never saw slashed day-month dates in training. Attention can only choose among the information that is in the input.
