Automatic Differentiation & Computational Graphs

How a program works out its own derivatives: exactly, and for about the price of running it

Every gradient used to train a network is computed by the same trick. The program that computes the loss is broken into a graph of tiny operations, each of which knows its own derivative, and the chain rule is applied to that graph, not to a formula. Done in one direction it is forward mode; done backwards it is reverse mode, which deep learning calls back-propagation. In the figure, the function ln x₁ + x₁x₂ − sin x₂ runs as a graph: values flow forward, then derivatives flow back. Move x₁ and x₂ under the figure and watch both partial derivatives arrive from a single backward sweep.

The idea
Apply the chain rule to the operations a program actually ran
Two modes
Forward: one pass per input. Reverse: one pass per output
The payoff
A full gradient for a small constant times the cost of the function

The picture

Computational graphs: a program, drawn

However long a program is, every number it produces comes from a few earlier numbers through one simple operation: an addition, a product, a sine, a matrix multiply. Give each of those values a node, draw an arrow from each input of an operation to its result, and you have a computational graph. It is directed and has no cycles, because a value can only be made from values that already exist. Nodes may hold single numbers, vectors or whole tensors; the operations are whatever elementary functions the library knows how to differentiate.

Listing the nodes in the order they were computed gives the evaluation trace, also called a Wengert list after Wengert (1964), who used exactly this list to differentiate programs. Loops and if-statements are no obstacle: one run of the program leaves behind one straight-line trace. The first example, ln x₁ + x₁x₂ − sin x₂, is the running example of Baydin et al.’s survey of automatic differentiation; we will differentiate it three different ways. Pick a graph and move its inputs: every node shows the value it holds.

\[ \begin{aligned} v_i &= \phi_i\big(v_{\mathrm{Pa}(i)}\big) \\ i &= n+1, \dots, N \\ y &= v_N \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first n nodes v₁ … vn are the inputs. Every later node i is an elementary operation φi (add, multiply, sin, …) applied to its parents Pa(i), which all appear earlier in the list. The output y is the last node. Nothing else is needed to run the program, and, as the next sections show, nothing else is needed to differentiate it.
Say it“Each v-i is phi-i of its parents, for i from n plus one to N; the output y is v-N.”

Three ways to a derivative

Symbolic, numerical, automatic

There are three ways to get a derivative out of a computer. Symbolic differentiation manipulates formulas, the way you would by hand or a computer-algebra system would: exact, but it needs a closed-form expression and the formulas can grow enormously (section 6 shows how fast). Numerical differentiation nudges the input and watches the output: a finite difference [f(x + h) − f(x)]/h. It is two lines of code, but it is only an approximation, and a gradient with n inputs costs n + 1 runs of the program. Automatic differentiation is neither. It applies the exact derivative rule of each elementary operation, at the numbers the program actually computed, and combines them by the chain rule. The result is exact up to ordinary floating-point rounding.

The trouble with finite differences is the step h. Too big, and the secant line misses the tangent (truncation error). Too small, and f(x + h) and f(x) agree in so many digits that subtracting them leaves mostly rounding noise (cancellation). Here x₂ is held at 5, so f(x) = ln x + 5x − sin 5 and the true slope is 1/x + 5. Slide h across sixteen orders of magnitude, then switch to 32-bit floats.

\[ \begin{aligned} D^{+}_h &= \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \\ &= f'(x) + \tfrac{h}{2} f''(\xi) \\ D^{\pm}_h &= \frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h} \\ &= f'(x) + \tfrac{h^2}{6} f'''(\xi) \\ |\text{error}| &\approx \text{trunc.} + \frac{\varepsilon\,|f(x)|}{h} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: D+h is the forward difference and D±h the central one. Taylor’s theorem says the forward difference is off by a term proportional to h (ξ is some point between x and x + h), the central difference by one proportional to h². On top of that, each value of f carries a rounding error of about ε|f|, where ε is the machine’s relative precision (about 10−16 in float64, 6×10−8 in float32), and dividing by h magnifies it. The dashed lines in the plot are this rough model; the total is smallest where the two terms balance.
Say it“D-plus, the forward difference, equals f-prime plus h over two f-double-prime; the central difference equals f-prime plus h-squared over six f-triple-prime; and rounding adds about epsilon times f over h.”

Mode 1

Forward mode: carry a derivative along with every value

Pick a direction r in which to move the inputs, and attach to every value v a second number v̇, its tangent: how fast v changes as the inputs move along r. The inputs’ tangents are just r (the seed). Every operation knows its local derivatives (for a product ab they are b and a), so a node’s tangent is the sum of local derivative × tangent over its incoming edges, computed in the same sweep as its value. When the sweep reaches the output, ẏ is the derivative of y along r. In matrix terms, one sweep computes the Jacobian-vector product J r, without ever building J.

A tidy way to implement this is dual numbers: pairs a + bε with the rule ε² = 0. Teach arithmetic and sin, exp, … to work on such pairs, feed the program x + 1ε, and the ε-part of the answer is the derivative. The catch is the count: one sweep gives one directional derivative, so a full gradient over n inputs needs n sweeps. Choose a seed and watch the tangents fill in.

\[ \begin{aligned} \dot v_i &= \sum_{j \in \mathrm{Pa}(i)} \frac{\partial \phi_i}{\partial v_j}\,\dot v_j \\ \dot{\mathbf x} = \mathbf r \;&\Rightarrow\; \dot y = J\,\mathbf r \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a node’s tangent is each local derivative ∂φi/∂vj (evaluated at the current values) times that parent’s tangent, summed over parents. Seed the inputs with the direction r and the output’s tangent is J r, the directional derivative. With r = (1, 0) it is the first partial derivative.
Say it“v-i-dot is the sum over parents of the partial of phi-i with respect to v-j, times v-j-dot; seeding the inputs with r gives y-dot equals J r.”
\[ \begin{aligned} f(a + b\,\epsilon) &= f(a) + f'(a)\,b\,\epsilon \\ \epsilon^2 &= 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a dual number a + bε carries a value a and a tangent b. Expand f in a Taylor series: every term with ε² or higher vanishes, leaving the value and the derivative times b. For example ln(2 + 1ε) = ln 2 + ½ε, and (2 + 1ε)(5 + 0ε) = 10 + 5ε.
Say it“f of a plus b epsilon equals f of a plus f-prime of a times b epsilon, where epsilon squared is zero.”

Mode 2

Reverse mode: send the sensitivity backwards

Now turn the question round. Instead of asking how every node responds to one input, ask how the one output responds to every node. That sensitivity, v̄ = ∂y/∂v, is called the node’s adjoint. The output’s adjoint is 1. A node’s adjoint is complete once all of its consumers have reported, so the sweep runs through the trace backwards: each node multiplies its adjoint by each local derivative and adds the result into its parents’ adjoints. When the sweep reaches the inputs, their adjoints are the gradient, all of them, from one pass.

This is back-propagation: reverse-mode automatic differentiation applied to a loss. Linnainmaa described the general method in 1970; Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams made it the way neural networks are trained in 1986. There is a price: the backward sweep needs the values from the forward sweep (the local derivative of ab with respect to a is b), so they must all be kept in memory until it is done. Press Step and follow the adjoints from the output back to the inputs.

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar v_N &= 1 \\ \bar v_j &= \sum_{i \,:\, j \in \mathrm{Pa}(i)} \bar v_i \, \frac{\partial \phi_i}{\partial v_j} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: start at the output with adjoint 1. Every other node’s adjoint adds up, over the nodes i that use it, the user’s adjoint times the local derivative of that use. A node with two consumers gets two contributions: fan-out forward becomes a sum backward.
Say it“v-N-bar is one; v-j-bar is the sum over every node that uses v-j of v-i-bar times the partial of phi-i with respect to v-j.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \bar{\mathbf x}^{\top} &= \mathbf u^{\top} J \\ m = 1:\quad \bar{\mathbf x} &= \nabla f(\mathbf x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: one backward sweep computes a vector-Jacobian product: u is the seed on the outputs. For a function with one output (a loss), u = 1 and the result is the whole gradient, whatever the number of inputs. That is why training a network with a billion weights takes one backward pass, not a billion.
Say it“x-bar transpose equals u transpose J; with one output, x-bar is the gradient.”

Which mode?

Counting the work: a chain of Jacobians

Why does deep learning use reverse mode? Write a network as a chain of layers, f = fL ∘ … ∘ f₁. By the chain rule its Jacobian is a product of the layers’ Jacobians, J = JL … J₁. Matrix multiplication is associative, so every way of bracketing the product gives the same J, but the cost can differ by orders of magnitude. Forward accumulation starts at the input end, (J₃(J₂J₁)), and every partial product has as many columns as the function has inputs. Reverse accumulation starts at the output end, ((J₃J₂)J₁), and every partial product has as many rows as there are outputs. A training loss has millions of inputs (the weights) and one output, so reverse wins by roughly the number of weights.

Neither end is always right. When there are few inputs and many outputs, forward mode wins. And when both ends are wide but the middle is narrow, the best order is neither: multiply the thin middle first. For a chain, the cheapest bracketing is the classic matrix-chain problem, solved by dynamic programming. For a general graph, finding the cheapest way to accumulate the Jacobian is NP-complete (Naumann, 2008), which is one of the reasons the automatic-differentiation community has studied far more than the two pure modes. Try the presets, then build your own chain.

\[ \begin{aligned} J &= J_L J_{L-1} \cdots J_1 \\ J_k &\in \mathbb{R}^{d_k \times d_{k-1}} \\ C_{\text{fwd}} &= d_0 \sum_{k=2}^{L} d_k\, d_{k-1} \\ C_{\text{rev}} &= d_L \sum_{k=1}^{L-1} d_k\, d_{k-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: layer k maps dk−1 numbers to dk, so its Jacobian is dk × dk−1; d₀ = n inputs, dL = m outputs. Multiplying a (p × q) by a (q × s) matrix costs p·q·s multiply-adds. Forward accumulation keeps a dk × d₀ partial product, so every term carries a factor d₀; reverse accumulation keeps a dL × dk one, so every term carries dL.
Say it“J is J-L times down to J-1. Forward costs d-zero times the sum of d-k d-k-minus-one; reverse costs d-L times the sum of d-k d-k-minus-one.”

Derivatives as graphs

Symbol-to-symbol: the derivative is just more graph

A library can hand you derivatives in two ways. In the symbol-to-number style it takes the graph and some input numbers and returns the gradient as numbers; Torch and Caffe worked like this, and PyTorch’s eager mode still does. In the symbol-to-symbol style it takes the graph and returns new nodes that compute the derivative; Theano and the first version of TensorFlow worked like this. Both do the same arithmetic in the end. The symbolic style simply makes the backward computation visible as a graph, and a graph can be optimised, compiled, evaluated later for any inputs, and differentiated again: second derivatives come for free by running the same procedure on the derivative graph.

This is not the same as the symbolic differentiation of section 2. The derivative graph keeps every shared node shared: tanh′(x) = 1 − tanh²(x) points back at the tanh node instead of copying it. Written out as an ordinary formula, with every shared piece copied, a derivative can be far larger, a growth known as expression swell. Pick a function and raise the order; the list shows the nodes of the derivative graph that this page builds by running reverse mode on symbols.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{grad}(v_j) &= \sum_{i \in \mathrm{Ch}(j)} \mathrm{grad}(v_i) \otimes \frac{\partial \phi_i}{\partial v_j} \\ f^{(k)} &= \mathrm{grad}\big(f^{(k-1)}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same recurrence as reverse mode, but every term is a new node rather than a number: grad(vj) is built from the gradient nodes of vj’s children Ch(j), combined (⊗: a product here, a vector-Jacobian product for tensors) with nodes for the local derivatives. Applying grad to the result of grad gives the second derivative, and so on.
Say it“grad of v-j is the sum over its children of grad of v-i combined with the partial of phi-i with respect to v-j; the k-th derivative is grad of the k-minus-first.”

The general algorithm

    
General back-propagation

    

      
In a real library the nodes are tensors, and each operation comes with a bprop rule: given the gradient of the loss with respect to the operation’s output, return the gradient with respect to each input. That is a vector-Jacobian product, computed directly, without ever building the Jacobian. For a matrix product C = AB the rule is two matrix products (below); for an elementwise tanh it is an elementwise multiply. The algorithm itself never changes: sort the graph so every node comes after its inputs, set the gradient of the loss to 1, then walk the list backwards, and for each node add up what its consumers’ bprop rules send it.

      
Why is this efficient? The chain rule, written out in full, sums a product of local derivatives over every path from x to y, and the number of paths can grow exponentially with depth. Back-propagation is dynamic programming: the sum over all paths through a node factors into (paths into it) × (paths out of it), and the node’s gradient stores the second factor so it is computed once. Each edge is used once, so the backward pass costs about as much as the forward pass. The memory cost is that every forward value must wait for the backward pass; checkpointing keeps only some of them and recomputes the rest, Chen et al. (2016) trade one extra forward pass for memory that grows like the square root of the depth. Make the graph deeper and wider and compare the two ways of computing the same number.

    


def backprop(graph, loss, wanted):
    order = topological_sort(graph)                 # every node after its inputs; loss last
    grad = {loss: ones_like(loss)}                  # d loss / d loss = 1
    for v in reversed(order[:-1]):                  # all consumers of v are visited before v
        users = [c for c in consumers(v) if c in grad]
        if users:                                   # one vector-Jacobian product per edge
            grad[v] = sum(c.op.bprop(c.inputs, grad[c], wrt=v) for c in users)
    return [grad.get(w, zeros_like(w)) for w in wanted]

    

      
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial y}{\partial x} &= \sum_{\text{paths}} \;\prod_{t=1}^{T} \frac{\partial u_t}{\partial u_{t-1}} \\ \text{path: } & x = u_0 \to \cdots \to u_T = y \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: follow every route through the graph from x to y, multiply the local derivatives along it, and add up the routes. Correct, but a graph D layers deep and W wide has WD routes. Back-propagation computes the same sum with one multiplication per edge.
Say it“dy by dx is the sum over all paths from x to y of the product of the local derivatives along the path.”

      
\[ \begin{aligned} C &= AB \\ \bar A &= \bar C\, B^{\top}, \quad \bar B = A^{\top} \bar C \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the bprop of a matrix product. Given C̄, the gradient of the loss with respect to C (same shape as C), the gradients with respect to A and B are two more matrix products. The Jacobian of C with respect to A would be a four-dimensional array; it is never formed.
Say it“If C is A B, then A-bar is C-bar B-transpose and B-bar is A-transpose C-bar.”

Real-world wrinkles

    
Complications: kinks, branches, NaNs and in-place writes

    

      
The algorithm is simple; production systems are not, because programs are messier than formulas. Kinks: ReLU and |x| have no derivative at 0, so a library must choose a value there, normally one of the subgradients. Programs, not functions: AD differentiates the operations that ran, so two programs for the same function can disagree, and an if-statement is followed down the branch that was taken, cliff or no cliff. Floating point: overflow turns into ∞, and 0 × ∞ is NaN; a branch that was computed but masked out can still poison the gradient. In-place operations save memory but can overwrite a value the backward pass still needs; PyTorch catches this with a version counter on every tensor.

      
Goodfellow et al. list more: operations that return several tensors at once need a bprop that accepts several gradients; when a node has many consumers, adding their contributions into one buffer as they arrive saves memory over keeping them all; integer inputs (indices, counts) have no gradient; and every operation must say what it does where its derivative is undefined. Pick a case and move x to the trouble spot.

    

    
\[ \begin{aligned} \partial |x|\,\big|_{x=0} &= [-1,\,1] \\ \mathrm{relu}'(0) &:= 0 \\ \text{AD: } \big(\sqrt{x \cdot x}\big)'\big|_{0} &= \frac{1}{2\sqrt{0}}\,(0 + 0) \\ &= \infty \cdot 0 = \text{NaN} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: at a kink the set of slopes of lines touching the graph from below (the subdifferential) is an interval; a library picks one member, and PyTorch and JAX define relu′(0) as 0. The second line is what reverse mode does with √(x·x) at 0: the local derivative of √ is 1/(2√u) = ∞, the local derivatives of x·x are x and x, both 0, and ∞ × 0 has no value. Same function as |x|; different program; different answer.
Say it“The subdifferential of absolute x at zero is the interval minus one to one, and relu-prime of zero is defined as zero. Automatic differentiation of root x-times-x at zero gives infinity times zero, which is not a number.”

Beyond neural networks

    
Differentiation outside deep learning

    

      
Automatic differentiation is older than deep learning and much wider. Wengert (1964) described forward mode; Linnainmaa (1970, published 1976) the reverse mode; Speelpenning (1980) built a compiler that produced fast gradient code. Weather centres fit their forecast models to observations with adjoint models, reverse mode run over a whole atmospheric simulation (the method called 4D-Var); engineers optimise wing shapes with adjoints of flow solvers; banks compute price sensitivities with adjoint codes (Giles and Glasserman’s “smoking adjoints”, 2006). Tools come in two styles: source transformation, which reads a program and writes its derivative program (ADIFOR, Tapenade), and operator overloading, which records each operation on a tape as the program runs and sweeps the tape backwards (ADOL-C, and the autograd of PyTorch).

      
Goodfellow et al. point out that deep learning’s back-propagation is one special case of this field, which has also worked out checkpointing, cheaper Jacobian orderings, sparse Jacobians and higher derivatives. Below, a tape records a simulation: a shell fired at 30 m/s with air drag, stepped forward 100 times a second until it lands. Reverse mode through every step gives the derivative of the landing distance with respect to the launch angle, and Newton’s method uses it to aim. Set a target and press Solve.

    

    
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf s_{t+1} &= F(\mathbf s_t), \quad \mathbf s_0 = \mathbf s_0(\theta) \\ \boldsymbol\lambda_T &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf s_T} \\ \boldsymbol\lambda_t &= \Big(\frac{\partial F}{\partial \mathbf s_t}\Big)^{\!\top} \boldsymbol\lambda_{t+1} \\ \frac{dL}{d\theta} &= \boldsymbol\lambda_0^{\top}\, \frac{\partial \mathbf s_0}{\partial \theta} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the state st (position and velocity) is stepped forward by F, an Euler step with gravity and drag −k|v|v. The angle θ only sets the starting velocity. The adjoint λt = ∂L/∂st runs backwards in time, one transposed Jacobian per step, exactly reverse mode over the unrolled loop. (Here L depends on the last two states, because the landing point is interpolated between them, so the sweep starts from both.)
Say it“s-t-plus-one is F of s-t; lambda-t is the transpose of dF by ds-t times lambda-t-plus-one; dL by d-theta is lambda-zero dotted with ds-zero by d-theta.”

Real-world project

    
Project: a 100-line autodiff that learns concrete

    

      
Time to build the real thing and trust it with real data. The Concrete Compressive Strength dataset (Yeh, 1998; UCI Machine Learning Repository, dataset 165, CC BY 4.0) records 1,030 laboratory tests: how many kilograms of each of seven ingredients went into a cubic metre of concrete, the age of the sample in days, and the compressive strength it reached, in megapascals. Strength depends on the mix in a strongly nonlinear way, which is why Yeh used neural networks for it in the first place. Below, a reverse-mode engine of about 40 lines, written for this page, trains a network to predict strength: 8 inputs (standardised, with age taken as ln(age)), 16 tanh units, 1 output, 161 weights, full-batch Adam on 824 rows, with 206 rows held out by a fixed random split.

    

    
The nine columns, named as in the dataset

    
\[ \begin{aligned} H &= \tanh\!\big(X W_1 + \mathbf 1\mathbf b_1^{\top}\big) \\ \hat{\mathbf y} &= H\,\mathbf w_2 + b_2 \\ L &= \tfrac{1}{n}\,\Vert \hat{\mathbf y} - \mathbf y \Vert^2 \\ \text{rel. gap} &= \frac{|g_{\text{AD}} - g_{\text{FD}}|}{|g_{\text{AD}}| + |g_{\text{FD}}|} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: X is the n × 8 matrix of standardised inputs, W₁ is 8 × 16, the bias row b₁ is added to every row (1 is a column of ones), H holds the 16 hidden activations of every row, and w₂ has 16 entries. The loss is the mean squared error on standardised strength (so √L × the standard deviation of strength is the RMSE in MPa). A gradient check compares each autodiff partial derivative gAD with a central difference gFD; gaps of 10−5 or less mean the engine is right (a finite difference is itself only good to about 6 digits here), while a wrong backward rule shows up as gaps near 1.
Say it“H is tanh of X W-one plus b-one; y-hat is H w-two plus b-two; L is the mean squared error; the relative gap is the difference over the sum of the magnitudes.”

The same engine in Python

    
The reference is an independent Python version of the same design, about 25 lines of engine on top of numpy, plus a hand-derived backprop to check it against. It reads the same JSON the page embeds (the rows of Concrete_Data.xls, the split, the starting weights) and logs the training loss at step 1 and every 250 steps.


import json, numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

class T:                                         # a tensor that remembers how it was made
    def __init__(s, data, parents=(), back=None):
        s.data, s.parents, s.back = np.asarray(data, float), parents, back
        s.grad = np.zeros_like(s.data)
    def __matmul__(s, o): return T(s.data @ o.data, (s, o), lambda g: (g @ o.data.T, s.data.T @ g))
    def __add__(s, o):                           # o may be a bias row, broadcast down the batch
        return T(s.data + o.data, (s, o), lambda g: (g, g.sum(0) if o.data.ndim < g.ndim else g))
    def __sub__(s, o): return T(s.data - o.data, (s, o), lambda g: (g, -g))
    def tanh(s):
        t = np.tanh(s.data); return T(t, (s,), lambda g: (g * (1 - t * t),))
    def square(s): return T(s.data ** 2, (s,), lambda g: (2 * s.data * g,))
    def mean(s): return T(s.data.mean(), (s,), lambda g: (np.full(s.data.shape, g / s.data.size),))
    def backward(s):
        order, seen = [], set()
        def visit(v):                            # topological order: parents before children
            if id(v) not in seen:
                seen.add(id(v)); [visit(p) for p in v.parents]; order.append(v)
        visit(s); s.grad = np.ones_like(s.data)
        for v in reversed(order):                # each node hands its gradient to its parents
            if v.back:
                for p, g in zip(v.parents, v.back(v.grad)): p.grad = p.grad + g

d = json.load(open("x046-data.json"))
D = np.array(d["rows"]); X = D[:, :8].copy(); y = D[:, 8]; tr, te = d["train"], d["test"]
X[:, 7] = np.log(X[:, 7])                        # age in days -> log(age)
mu, sd = X[tr].mean(0), X[tr].std(0); Z = (X - mu) / sd
ym, ys = y[tr].mean(), y[tr].std(); yz = (y - ym) / ys

def init():
    return [T(np.array(d["W1"])), T(np.zeros(16)), T(np.array(d["W2"])[:, None]), T(np.zeros(1))]
def loss(P, idx):
    W1, b1, W2, b2 = P
    return ((T(Z[idx]) @ W1 + b1).tanh() @ W2 + b2 - T(yz[idx][:, None])).square().mean()

P = init(); L = loss(P, tr); L.backward()        # 1) gradient check at the starting weights
fd, ad, eps = [], [], 1e-6
for p in P:
    for i in np.ndindex(p.data.shape):
        old = p.data[i]; p.data[i] = old + eps; up = loss(P, tr).data
        p.data[i] = old - eps; dn = loss(P, tr).data; p.data[i] = old
        fd.append((up - dn) / (2 * eps)); ad.append(p.grad[i])
fd, ad = np.array(fd), np.array(ad)
print(f"parameters {len(ad)}; loss at start {float(L.data):.12f}")
print(f"autodiff vs central difference: max |diff| {np.abs(ad - fd).max():.2e}, max relative {np.max(np.abs(ad - fd) / np.maximum(np.abs(ad) + np.abs(fd), 1e-12)):.2e}")
W1, b1, W2, b2 = (p.data for p in P); A = Z[tr]; t = yz[tr][:, None]   # 2) hand-written backprop, for comparison
h = np.tanh(A @ W1 + b1); e = h @ W2 + b2 - t; go = 2 * e / len(e); gh = go @ W2.T * (1 - h * h)
hand = np.concatenate([(A.T @ gh).ravel(), gh.sum(0), (h.T @ go).ravel(), go.sum(0)])
print(f"autodiff vs hand-derived backprop: max |diff| {np.abs(ad - hand).max():.2e}")

def train(lr, steps, every=250):                 # 3) full-batch Adam on the 824 training rows
    P = init(); m = [np.zeros_like(p.data) for p in P]; v = [np.zeros_like(p.data) for p in P]; log = []
    for k in range(1, steps + 1):
        for p in P: p.grad = np.zeros_like(p.data)
        L = loss(P, tr); L.backward()
        if k == 1 or k % every == 0: log.append(float(L.data))
        for i, p in enumerate(P):
            m[i] = 0.9 * m[i] + 0.1 * p.grad; v[i] = 0.999 * v[i] + 0.001 * p.grad ** 2
            p.data = p.data - lr * (m[i] / (1 - 0.9 ** k)) / (np.sqrt(v[i] / (1 - 0.999 ** k)) + 1e-8)
    return P, log
def rmse(P, idx):
    W1, b1, W2, b2 = (p.data for p in P)
    pred = (np.tanh(Z[idx] @ W1 + b1) @ W2 + b2)[:, 0] * ys + ym
    return np.sqrt(np.mean((pred - y[idx]) ** 2)), 1 - np.sum((pred - y[idx]) ** 2) / np.sum((y[idx] - y[idx].mean()) ** 2)

ref = {}
for lr in (0.003, 0.01, 0.03):
    P, log = train(lr, 4000); ref[str(lr)] = log
    print(f"lr {lr}: loss at step 1, 1000, 2000, 4000 = {log[0]:.12f} {log[4]:.12f} {log[8]:.12f} {log[16]:.12f}")
P, log = train(0.01, 2000)
(r_tr, _), (r_te, r2) = rmse(P, tr), rmse(P, te)
print(f"8-16-1 tanh net, Adam lr 0.01, 2000 steps: train RMSE {r_tr:.4f} MPa, test RMSE {r_te:.4f} MPa, test R2 {r2:.4f}")
lin = LinearRegression().fit(Z[tr], y[tr]); p = lin.predict(Z[te])
print(f"linear regression (scikit-learn), same features: test RMSE {np.sqrt(np.mean((p - y[te]) ** 2)):.4f} MPa, test R2 {lin.score(Z[te], y[te]):.4f}")

Wrap-up

    
Five lines to remember

    
    
      
  • A computational graph has one node per value a program computes; differentiating the program means applying the chain rule to that graph, one elementary operation at a time.
    • 
      
  • Automatic differentiation is exact up to rounding, unlike finite differences, and keeps shared work shared, unlike naive symbolic differentiation.
    • 
      
  • Forward mode carries tangents with the values: one pass per input, a Jacobian-vector product. Reverse mode sends adjoints back: one pass per output, a vector-Jacobian product.
    • 
      
  • Back-propagation is reverse mode on a loss: the whole gradient for a small constant times the cost of the forward pass, paid for by storing the forward values.
    • 
      
  • AD differentiates the program, not the mathematics: kinks, branches, masked NaNs, overflow and in-place writes all need care, and the same tools power simulations far beyond deep learning.
    • 
    

    
Check your understanding

    

      
A function has 3 inputs and 2,000 outputs. Which mode would you use to get its full Jacobian, and how many passes does it take?
Forward mode: one pass per input, so 3 passes, each giving one column of the 2,000 × 3 Jacobian. Reverse mode would need one pass per output, 2,000 of them.

      
In the reverse sweep, why does a node that feeds two operations add the two contributions it receives?
Because the output depends on the node through both routes, and the multivariable chain rule sums the effects of all routes. Moving the node by a small amount changes y through the first consumer and through the second, and the two changes add.

      
Why does reverse mode need more memory than forward mode?
The local derivatives used in the backward sweep depend on the forward values (the derivative of ab with respect to a is b), and the backward sweep starts only after the forward sweep has finished. So the values must be stored until they are used. Forward mode uses each value and its tangent immediately and can discard them. Checkpointing reduces the storage by recomputing some values.

      
What does a symbol-to-symbol library give you that a symbol-to-number one does not?
A graph for the derivative, not just its value. That graph can be simplified and compiled, evaluated later for new inputs, and differentiated again to get higher derivatives (such as Hessian-vector products) with the same machinery.

      
Your training loss suddenly becomes NaN after you add torch.where(x > 0, torch.log(x), 0). The forward values look fine. What happened?
Both branches are computed for every element. Where x ≤ 0, log x is NaN or −∞ and its local derivative 1/x is ∞ (or worse); the where sends 0 to that branch, and 0 × ∞ = NaN in the backward pass. Make the unused branch safe, e.g. log(where(x > 0, x, 1)), before applying the outer where.
