Recommender Systems & Matrix Factorisation

Learning taste from who read what

A bookshop with 10,000 titles cannot show you all of them. A recommender picks the handful you are most likely to enjoy, and it does so without knowing anything about books: only which readers read and rated which titles. Every dot in the map is one of the 400 most-read books on Goodreads, placed so that books read by the same people sit together. Nobody told the model about genres; the colours (from readers’ shelf tags) are added afterwards, and the clusters still line up with them. Click a few books you have enjoyed: the model folds you in as a new reader and rings its top recommendations.

The setting

Users, items and a mostly empty matrix

Recommendation and its close cousin, choosing which advert to show, are among the largest commercial uses of machine learning: a better ranking of products is worth real money to a shop. The raw material is always the same. There are m users and n items, and a record of feedback: a user gave an item 4 stars, clicked it, bought it, listened to it for three minutes. Arrange it as a matrix R with one row per user and one column per item. Almost every cell is empty, because each person has met only a tiny share of the catalogue. The whole problem is to guess the empty cells, or at least to rank them for each row.

This chapter uses goodbooks-10k (Zajac, 2017): six million Goodreads ratings of the 10,000 most popular books. To run in your browser, we keep the 400 most-rated books and 2,000 readers drawn at random from those who rated at least 20 of them. Each reader’s ratings are split once, at random: 80% for training, 20% held out for testing. Switch the ordering and the view: even this heavily filtered corner of the data is mostly empty.

\[ \begin{aligned} \rho &= \frac{|\Omega|}{m\,n} \\ \Omega &= \{(u,i) : r_{ui} \text{ observed}\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Ω is the set of (user, item) pairs with a rating, m the number of users and n the number of items. The density ρ is the share of cells that are filled. Systems store only Ω, as a list of triples (u, i, r), never the full m × n table.
Say it“Rho, the density, is the number of observed ratings over m times n.”

Step 1 · no personal taste yet

Baselines: generous readers and beloved books

Before modelling taste, model the obvious. Some readers hand out fives freely; some books are loved by nearly everyone who opens them. A baseline predictor says: the average rating, plus how generous this reader is, plus how good this book is. Those offsets, bu and bi, are learned by least squares, with a penalty λ that pulls them towards 0 when there is little evidence. A book whose 70 ratings happen to average 4.8 should not be trusted as much as one with 700. The error measure is the root mean squared error on the held-out ratings, the yardstick of the Netflix Prize (2006–2009). Slide λ and watch the thinly rated books on the left shrink towards 0.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat r_{ui} &= \mu + b_u + b_i \\ b_i &= \frac{\sum_{u \in R(i)} (r_{ui} - \mu - b_u)}{\lambda + |R(i)|} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: μ is the mean of all training ratings; R(i) the readers who rated book i. The book’s offset is the average amount by which its ratings beat what μ and the readers’ offsets predict, except that λ is added to the count: like λ imaginary ratings of exactly the expected value. The reader offsets bu have the same formula with the roles swapped; ten rounds of alternating the two settle them.
Say it“Predict mu plus b-u plus b-i; b-i is the summed leftover over lambda plus the number of ratings.”
\[ \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal T|}\sum_{(u,i) \in \mathcal T} \big(r_{ui} - \hat r_{ui}\big)^2} \]
Read it as: 𝒯 is the test set, the 18,397 held-out ratings. Square each miss, average, take the root: the typical size of an error, in stars. Big misses count extra. A model that always says 3.95 scores about 1.0.
Say it“RMSE is the square root of the mean squared difference between the true and predicted ratings.”

Step 2 · collaborative filtering

Neighbourhoods: readers who liked this also liked

The first personal method is the oldest. To guess how you will rate a book, look at the books you have rated that are most like it, and see how you rated them. “Like it” is decided by the crowd, not by the text: two books are similar when the same readers rate both of them above (or below) their baselines. This is collaborative filtering: everyone’s ratings help to filter for everyone else. You could also compare readers instead of books (“find people with your taste”), but item-based neighbours (Sarwar et al., 2001; Linden, Smith & York, 2003, at Amazon) are usually better: there are fewer items than users, and an item’s audience changes more slowly than a person’s shelf. Similarities from only a few shared readers are noisy, so they are shrunk by n/(n + β). Pick a book and move β and K.

\[ \begin{aligned} s_{ij} &= \frac{\sum_{u} z_{ui}\, z_{uj}}{\sqrt{\sum_{u} z_{ui}^2\,\sum_{u} z_{uj}^2}} \\ &\quad\times \frac{n_{ij}}{n_{ij} + \beta} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: zui = rui − (μ + bu + bi) is how much reader u beat the baseline on book i. The sums run over the nij readers who rated both books. The first factor is a correlation between the two books’ leftovers; the second trusts it more when more readers back it up.
Say it“s-i-j is the correlation of the leftovers, times n-i-j over n-i-j plus beta.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat r_{ui} &= \mu + b_u + b_i \\ &\quad + \frac{\sum_{j \in N^K(i;u)} s_{ij}\, z_{uj}}{\sum_{j \in N^K(i;u)} s_{ij}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: NK(i; u) is the K books that u has rated and that are most similar to i (positive similarities only). Start from the baseline and add a similarity-weighted average of how far u beat the baseline on those neighbours.
Say it“Baseline plus the similarity-weighted average of the reader's leftovers on the K nearest books they rated.”

Step 3 · latent factors

Matrix factorisation: taste in a few numbers

Neighbourhoods compare books two at a time. Matrix factorisation looks for a few hidden dimensions of taste that explain all the ratings at once. Each reader gets a vector pu of k numbers (how much they like each kind of thing) and each book a vector qi (how much of each kind of thing it has). The predicted rating is the baseline plus their dot product. Stack the vectors into matrices P (m × k) and Q (n × k) and the model says R ≈ PQT: a full 2,000 × 400 table described by only (2,000 + 400) × k numbers. Nobody names the dimensions; the data chooses them. This idea won the Netflix Prize (Koren, Bell & Volinsky, 2009). In deep-learning language, P and Q are two embedding tables and the prediction is a bilinear layer; deep recommenders keep the embeddings and replace the dot product by a small network, and restricted Boltzmann machines (Salakhutdinov, Mnih & Hinton, 2007) were a strong Netflix entry too.

Unlike the SVD of a full matrix, only the observed cells enter the loss, so there is no closed-form answer. The simplest fit is stochastic gradient descent: visit the training ratings in a random order and nudge the four parameters involved in each one. Train, then raise k or lower λ until the test curve turns up.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat r_{ui} &= \mu + b_u + b_i + \mathbf p_u^{\top}\mathbf q_i \\ J &= \sum_{(u,i)\in\Omega} \big(r_{ui} - \hat r_{ui}\big)^2 \\ &\quad + \lambda \sum_{(u,i)\in\Omega} \big(\Vert\mathbf p_u\Vert^2 + \Vert\mathbf q_i\Vert^2 \\ &\qquad\qquad + b_u^2 + b_i^2\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the prediction adds a personal match pu·qi to the baseline. The loss J sums the squared misses over the observed ratings only, plus a penalty λ on the size of everything the rating touches, so a reader with many ratings is regularised more in total (but each rating sees the same λ). Without the penalty, k = 16 numbers per reader would happily memorise their 37 training ratings.
Say it“Predict the baseline plus p-u dot q-i; minimise the squared errors on the observed cells plus lambda times the squared sizes.”
\[ \begin{aligned} e &= r_{ui} - \hat r_{ui} \\ \mathbf p_u &\leftarrow \mathbf p_u + \eta\,(e\,\mathbf q_i - \lambda\,\mathbf p_u) \\ \mathbf q_i &\leftarrow \mathbf q_i + \eta\,(e\,\mathbf p_u - \lambda\,\mathbf q_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for one rating, measure the miss e. Move the reader’s vector towards the book’s (if the prediction was too low) by a step η, and shrink it slightly; do the same for the book with the old pu. The biases get b ← b + η(e − λb). One epoch is one pass over all 73,680 training ratings in a fresh random order.
Say it“p-u moves by eta times e q-i minus lambda p-u; q-i moves by eta times e p-u minus lambda q-i.”

Step 3, again · a second optimiser

Alternating least squares: fix one side, solve the other

The loss J is not convex in P and Q together (you can swap or rotate the hidden dimensions), but look at it with the books frozen: then each reader’s vector appears only in that reader’s own ratings, and finding it is an ordinary ridge regression with a closed-form answer. Freeze the readers and the same holds for each book. Alternating least squares (ALS) solves all readers exactly, then all books, and repeats. Each half-step can only lower J, there is no step size to tune, and every reader (or book) can be solved in parallel, which is why Spark’s recommender uses it. Here the model has just k = 2 factors, so every book is a point on a plane. Step through the half-steps and watch the books sort themselves out.

\[ \begin{aligned} A_u &= \sum_{i \in R(u)} \tilde{\mathbf q}_i \tilde{\mathbf q}_i^{\top} + \lambda\, n_u I \\ \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf p_u \\ b_u\end{bmatrix} &= A_u^{-1} \sum_{i \in R(u)} \tilde{\mathbf q}_i\,\big(r_{ui} - \mu - b_i\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Au is the reader’s (k + 1) × (k + 1) system matrix; q̃i is the book’s vector with a 1 appended, so the reader’s bias is fitted alongside pu. This is ridge regression of the reader’s nu ratings (minus μ and the books’ biases) on the books’ vectors, with penalty λnu, exactly the reader’s share of J. The book step swaps the roles. Each solve is a (k + 1) × (k + 1) linear system.
Say it“p-u and b-u equal the inverse of the sum of q-tilde q-tilde transpose plus lambda n-u I, times the sum of q-tilde times the leftover rating.”

Step 4 · what people do

Implicit feedback: what people do, not what they say

Most systems have no stars at all, only implicit feedback: plays, clicks, purchases, books shelved. And a shop does not need to know whether you would give a book 4.2 or 4.4 stars; it needs a short list that you will want. That changes the problem. Predicting stars only on rated cells ignores the loudest signal in the data: which books a person chose to read. Ratings are missing not at random; people mostly read what they expect to enjoy. Hu, Koren & Volinsky (2008) turned this into a model. Every cell becomes a 0/1 preference (read it or not), weighted by a confidence that is high where we saw an action and low, but not zero, everywhere else. ALS still works, with one trick that keeps the cost proportional to the observed cells. The score is recall@k on the held-out books each reader rated 4 or 5. Compare the four recommenders, then move α.

\[ \begin{aligned} J &= \sum_{u,i}\, c_{ui}\big(p_{ui} - \mathbf x_u^{\top}\mathbf y_i\big)^2 \\ &\quad + \lambda\Big(\sum_u \Vert\mathbf x_u\Vert^2 + \sum_i \Vert\mathbf y_i\Vert^2\Big) \\ p_{ui} &= [\,u \text{ read } i\,] \\ c_{ui} &= 1 + \alpha\,p_{ui} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the sum now runs over all 800,000 cells, not just the rated ones. pui is 1 if the reader read (rated) the book, else 0. An unread cell asks gently (weight 1) for a score near 0; a read cell asks firmly (weight 1 + α) for a score near 1. The stars themselves are not used. With α = 0, every cell counts equally and the model is a plain low-rank fit of the 0/1 matrix.
Say it“Minimise the confidence-weighted squared gap between preference and x-u dot y-i over every cell, plus lambda times the squared sizes.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf x_u &= \big(Y^{\top}Y + \alpha\, Y_u^{\top}Y_u + \lambda I\big)^{-1} \\ &\quad \times (1+\alpha)\, Y_u^{\top}\mathbf 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Y holds all 400 book vectors; Yu only the books u read. YTY is the same for every reader and is computed once per sweep, so each reader costs only their own books: that is the trick. The book step is symmetric.
Say it“x-u is the inverse of Y-transpose-Y plus alpha Y-u-transpose Y-u plus lambda I, times one plus alpha times the sum of the books u read.”
\[ \text{recall@}k = \frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{u \in E} \frac{\big|\,\text{top}_k(u) \cap L_u\big|}{|L_u|} \]
Read it as: Lu is the set of held-out books reader u rated 4 or 5; E the 1,989 readers who have at least one. topk(u) is the k highest-scoring books u has not read in training. Recall is the share of the liked books that the list recovers, averaged over readers.
Say it“Recall at k is the average, over readers, of the share of their liked held-out books that appear in their top k.”

Step 5 · the newcomers

Cold start: new readers and new books

Collaborative filtering knows a reader only through their history and a book only through its audience. A newcomer has neither: this is the cold-start problem. For a new reader, the factor model offers a neat fix. Keep the book vectors fixed and solve for the newcomer’s vector from the few books they have shown you: it is exactly one user step of ALS, called folding in, and needs no retraining. To test it honestly, the model below is trained on readers 1–1,500 only; readers 1,501–2,000 arrive cold, reveal their first n books, and we count how many of the 10 recommendations are books they went on to like. Two rivals: popularity, and a content-based recommender that matches the Goodreads shelf tags (fantasy, classics, romance…) of the books revealed. Slide n from 0 to 10.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf x_{\text{new}} &= \big(Y^{\top}Y + \alpha\,Y_S^{\top}Y_S + \lambda I\big)^{-1} \\ &\quad \times (1+\alpha)\,Y_S^{\top}\mathbf 1 \\ s_i^{\text{tags}} &= \mathbf t_i^{\top}\sum_{j \in S}\mathbf t_j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: S is the set of n books the newcomer revealed. Folding in (first line) is the implicit-ALS user update with Y frozen. The tag score sitags compares each book’s tag profile ti (22 genre weights from Goodreads shelves, scaled to length 1) with the sum of the revealed books’ profiles. With n = 0 both fall back to popularity.
Say it“The newcomer's vector is one ALS user step on the books they revealed; the content score is t-i dotted with the sum of their tag profiles.”

A new book is harder: no one has read it, so it has no vector, and folding in needs readers. Here content features earn their keep. Learn a map from what is known about any book (its tags, its text, its cover; for music, the audio itself, as van den Oord, Dieleman & Schrauwen did in 2013 with a deep network) to its factor vector, using the books that do have readers. A newcomer’s guessed vector then lets the system rank readers for it on day one. Below, a ridge regression maps the 22 shelf-tag weights to the 24 implicit-ALS factors, and each book is predicted leave-one-out, by a map fitted without it. Pick a book and pretend it has just been published.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf y}_i &= W^{\top}\mathbf t_i \\ W &= \big(T^{\top}T + \gamma I\big)^{-1}T^{\top}Y \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: T stacks the books’ tag profiles (400 × 22), Y their learned factors (400 × 24). W is the ridge solution that best predicts factors from tags; γ = 1. To judge the guess we ask “who should see this book?”: rank all 2,000 readers by xu·ŷi and measure the AUC, the chance that a reader who did read the book is ranked above one who did not (0.5 is guessing).
Say it“The guessed vector is W-transpose times the tag profile, with W the ridge regression of factors on tags.”

Step 6 · the feedback loop

Exploration: a recommender only learns about what it shows

Everything so far learned from a fixed log. A live system writes its own log: it only hears back about the items it chose to show. If it always shows what currently looks best (exploits), a good item that had an unlucky start is never shown again, so the mistake is never corrected. Collecting data about the rest means sometimes showing something uncertain (exploring). This is a bandit problem: each action (show book i) pays a reward (the reader loved it) drawn from an unknown distribution. When the choice also depends on who is asking, the reader’s features or factor vector are the context and the problem becomes a contextual bandit (Li et al., 2010); the same logic also warns that a model trained on its own past recommendations sees a biased sample.

Below, a front page features one book per visit, from ten real books. The chance that a visitor loves it is the share of that book’s Goodreads ratings that are 5 stars, across all 53,424 readers in goodbooks-10k; the policies do not know these numbers. Each policy is run 200 times for 3,000 visits. Compare the four policies, then tune ε and c.

\[ \text{Regret}(T) = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(\mu^{*} - \mu_{a_t}\big) \]
Read it as: μi is book i’s true chance of a 5-star reaction, μ* the best of them, at the book shown at visit t. Regret counts the loves lost by not always showing the best book. A policy that never stops exploring grows it linearly; a good one grows it like log T.
Say it“Regret at T is the sum over visits of mu-star minus the mu of the book shown.”
\[ \begin{aligned} a_t &= \arg\max_i\; \bar x_i + c\sqrt{\ln t \,/\, n_i} \\ \theta_i &\sim \text{Beta}(1 + s_i,\, 1 + f_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first line is UCB, the second Thompson sampling. x̄i is the observed love rate of book i after ni showings (si loves, fi not). UCB adds a bonus that is large for rarely shown books and shrinks as evidence grows. Thompson sampling draws a plausible love rate for each book from its posterior and shows the book with the highest draw. ε-greedy shows a random book with probability ε, otherwise the current best; greedy never explores.
Say it“UCB picks the book with the highest mean plus c root log t over n-i; Thompson samples each rate from a Beta and picks the largest.”

Real-world project

Project: recommending books to 2,000 Goodreads readers

The data is goodbooks-10k by Zygmunt Zając (2017), released under CC BY-SA 4.0: 5,976,479 ratings (1 to 5 stars) by 53,424 Goodreads users of the 10,000 most-rated books, with metadata and user shelf tags. This page uses the 400 most-rated books and a random sample of 2,000 users who rated at least 20 of them (seed 1043): 92,077 ratings, split per reader into 73,680 for training and 18,397 for testing. Every model on this page is trained here, in your browser, from those raw ratings. Its settings (λ for the baselines, β and K for the neighbours, k, λ, η and the number of epochs for SGD, k, α and λ for implicit ALS, 30 neighbours for the cosine recommender) were chosen by a coarse search on a validation split carved out of the training ratings; the test ratings were used only to report the numbers below.

The same pipeline in Python

import json, numpy as np
D = json.load(open("x043-data.json", encoding="utf8"))
A = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZabcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz0123456789-_"
def parse(s): return [((64 * A.index(s[k]) + A.index(s[k + 1])) // 5, (64 * A.index(s[k]) + A.index(s[k + 1])) % 5 + 1) for k in range(0, len(s), 2)]
tr, te = zip(*[[parse(p) for p in u.split(".")] for u in D["users"]]); U, I = len(tr), len(D["books"])
tu, ti, tr_r = (np.array(c) for c in zip(*[(u, i, r) for u in range(U) for i, r in tr[u]])); tr_r = tr_r.astype(float)
eu, ei, er = (np.array(c) for c in zip(*[(u, i, r) for u in range(U) for i, r in te[u]]))
Ru = [ti[tu == u] for u in range(U)]; Rr = [tr_r[tu == u] for u in range(U)]
Iu = [tu[ti == i] for i in range(I)]; Ir = [tr_r[ti == i] for i in range(I)]
mu = tr_r.mean(); rmse = lambda p: np.sqrt(np.mean((er - p) ** 2))
def mulberry32(a):                                    # the page's random numbers, bit for bit
    def r():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF; t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) ^ t) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        return (t ^ (t >> 14)) / 4294967296
    return r
init = lambda r, n, k: np.array([[0.2 * r() - 0.1 for _ in range(k)] for _ in range(n)])

def biases(lam, its=10):                              # r ~ mu + b_u + b_i, alternating closed form
    bu, bi = np.zeros(U), np.zeros(I)
    for _ in range(its):
        bi = np.bincount(ti, tr_r - mu - bu[tu], I) / (lam + np.bincount(ti, minlength=I))
        bu = np.bincount(tu, tr_r - mu - bi[ti], U) / (lam + np.bincount(tu, minlength=U))
    return bu, bi
bu, bi = biases(5)
Wm = np.zeros((U, I)); Wm[tu, ti] = 1; Z = np.zeros((U, I)); Z[tu, ti] = tr_r - mu - bu[tu] - bi[ti]
nij, sq = Wm.T @ Wm, (Z ** 2).T @ Wm                   # item-item Pearson on residuals, shrunk
with np.errstate(invalid="ignore", divide="ignore"): S = np.nan_to_num((Z.T @ Z) / np.sqrt(sq * sq.T)) * nij / (nij + 200)
np.fill_diagonal(S, 0)
def knn(u, i, K=40):
    s = S[i, Ru[u]]; o = np.argsort(-s, kind="stable")[:K]; o = o[s[o] > 0]
    return mu + bu[u] + bi[i] + (s[o] @ Z[u, Ru[u][o]] / s[o].sum() if len(o) else 0)

def mf_sgd(k=16, lam=0.1, eta=0.02, epochs=22, seed=7):
    r = mulberry32(seed); P, Q = init(r, U, k), init(r, I, k); bu, bi = np.zeros(U), np.zeros(I); n = len(tr_r)
    for _ in range(epochs):
        o = list(range(n))
        for a in range(n - 1, 0, -1): b = int(r() * (a + 1)); o[a], o[b] = o[b], o[a]
        for t in o:
            u, i = tu[t], ti[t]; e = tr_r[t] - mu - bu[u] - bi[i] - P[u] @ Q[i]
            bu[u] += eta * (e - lam * bu[u]); bi[i] += eta * (e - lam * bi[i])
            P[u], Q[i] = P[u] + eta * (e * Q[i] - lam * P[u]), Q[i] + eta * (e * P[u] - lam * Q[i])
    return P, Q, bu, bi
def mf_als(k=16, lam=0.1, its=10, seed=7):            # same objective, solved exactly one side at a time
    r = mulberry32(seed); P, Q = init(r, U, k), init(r, I, k); bu, bi = np.zeros(U), np.zeros(I)
    for _ in range(its):
        for u in range(U):
            F = np.c_[Q[Ru[u]], np.ones(len(Ru[u]))]; x = np.linalg.solve(F.T @ F + lam * len(F) * np.eye(k + 1), F.T @ (Rr[u] - mu - bi[Ru[u]])); P[u], bu[u] = x[:k], x[k]
        for i in range(I):
            F = np.c_[P[Iu[i]], np.ones(len(Iu[i]))]; x = np.linalg.solve(F.T @ F + lam * len(F) * np.eye(k + 1), F.T @ (Ir[i] - mu - bu[Iu[i]])); Q[i], bi[i] = x[:k], x[k]
    return P, Q, bu, bi
def ials(users, k=24, alpha=5, lam=50, its=10, seed=11):  # implicit feedback (Hu, Koren & Volinsky 2008)
    r = mulberry32(seed); X, Y = init(r, len(users), k), init(r, I, k); pos = {u: a for a, u in enumerate(users)}
    Iw = [np.array([pos[u] for u in Iu[i] if u in pos], int) for i in range(I)]
    for _ in range(its):
        YY = Y.T @ Y
        for a, u in enumerate(users): Yu = Y[Ru[u]]; X[a] = np.linalg.solve(YY + alpha * Yu.T @ Yu + lam * np.eye(k), (1 + alpha) * Yu.sum(0))
        XX = X.T @ X
        for i in range(I): Xi = X[Iw[i]]; Y[i] = np.linalg.solve(XX + alpha * Xi.T @ Xi + lam * np.eye(k), (1 + alpha) * Xi.sum(0))
    return X, Y
fold_in = lambda Y, items, alpha=5, lam=50: np.linalg.solve(Y.T @ Y + alpha * Y[items].T @ Y[items] + lam * np.eye(Y.shape[1]), (1 + alpha) * Y[items].sum(0))

print(f"{U} readers, {I} books, {len(tr_r)} training and {len(er)} test ratings, density {(len(tr_r) + len(er)) / U / I:.4f}")

The browser’s code is a separate JavaScript implementation of the same recipes, and both draw their starting vectors and SGD orders from the same seeded generator, so they train the same models. Every number in the scoreboard agrees with this listing to the 4 decimals shown; the remaining differences (around 10−15 in the RMSEs) come from summing in a different order and from Cholesky in the browser against LU in numpy. Three honest caveats. This is a small, dense corner of Goodreads: 400 very popular books, where a neighbourhood method holds its own against matrix factorisation; on the full long tail, factor models usually pull ahead. A random 80/20 split per reader ignores time; a stricter test would hold out each reader’s latest books. And recall@k only rewards books the reader happened to rate: a recommendation they would have loved but never met counts as a miss.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Recommendation is guessing the empty cells of a huge, sparse users × items matrix (here 88.5% empty after keeping only the most popular books), or ranking them for each user.
  • Start with a regularised baseline μ + bu + bi; neighbourhood methods then add “people who liked this also liked”, with similarities shrunk when few people back them.
  • Matrix factorisation gives every user and item a short vector and predicts with a dot product; fit it by SGD (one rating at a time) or ALS (exact ridge solves, alternating sides), and regularise.
  • For top-k lists, implicit feedback (what people chose) beats predicted stars: weighted ALS lifted recall@10 from 6.8% (explicit MF) to 32.1% on Goodreads.
  • Newcomers need help: fold new users in from a few actions, map content features to factors for new items, and explore (ε-greedy, UCB, Thompson), because a recommender only learns about what it shows.

Check your understanding

Why does a matrix factorisation model with k = 16 not simply memorise every reader’s ratings?

Because of the penalty λ on the vectors, and because each book’s vector is shared by all its readers. With λ = 0 and enough epochs the training error keeps falling while the test error rises, which is exactly overfitting.

What makes each ALS half-step easy, when the full problem is not convex?

With one side frozen, the loss is a sum of separate quadratic problems, one per user (or item): a ridge regression with a closed-form solution. Solving each exactly can only lower the loss, so J never increases.

A model with excellent RMSE on predicted stars produces poor top-10 lists. How can that be?

RMSE is measured only on books people chose to rate, and they mostly choose books they expect to like. Ranking every unread book by predicted stars surfaces niche books that would score well if read but that few people pick up. Top-k quality needs a model of what people choose, such as implicit feedback.

How do you recommend to a user who signed up a minute ago and has liked three books?

Fold them in: keep the item factors fixed and solve one user step of ALS from those three books. No retraining is needed. With zero actions, fall back to popularity or ask a few onboarding questions.

Why can a purely greedy recommender get stuck on a mediocre item for ever?

It only receives feedback on what it shows. If a better item had an unlucky first few showings, its estimate stays low and it is never shown again to correct that. Some exploration (ε-greedy, an uncertainty bonus, posterior sampling) keeps gathering evidence about the alternatives.
