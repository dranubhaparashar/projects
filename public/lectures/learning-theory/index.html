Learning Theory: PAC, VC Dimension & Sample Complexity

How many examples do you need before you can trust what you learned?

Every chapter so far judged a model on held-out data, after the fact. Learning theory asks the question before any data arrives: if a learner fits N examples, how sure can we be that it is nearly right on the next one, whatever the world looks like? The answers, probably approximately correct (PAC) learning and the Vapnik–Chervonenkis (VC) dimension, tie the amount of data you need to the richness of the hypotheses you allow. On the right, a hidden rectangle labels random points. The learner draws the tightest box around the positive ones, and the red strips are where it is still wrong. Click the plot to draw five more examples and watch the error shrink; drag the two dashed corners to move the hidden rectangle.

The question
How large must N be for the learner to be, with probability 1−δ, within ε of perfect?
The answer
N grows like (ln|H| + ln 1/δ)/ε, or with the VC dimension h for infinite classes
The catch
The bounds are worst cases: on real data they are often hundreds of times too cautious

The idea

Probably approximately correct

Why should a rule that fits the training examples work on new ones at all? Only because the future looks like the past. Learning theory makes that an explicit assumption: training examples and future examples are drawn independently from the same fixed distribution D. Without it, nothing can be promised. With it, we can compare two numbers: the training error, which we can see, and the true error, the chance that the hypothesis mislabels a fresh example, which we can't.

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{err}_{\mathcal D}(h) &= P_{\mathbf x\sim\mathcal D}\big[\,h(\mathbf x)\ne f(\mathbf x)\,\big] \\ \widehat{\operatorname{err}}(h) &= \tfrac1N \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb 1\big[h(\mathbf x_i)\ne y_i\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: f is the true labelling rule (the target) and h is the learner's guess. The true error is the probability mass of the region where they disagree, measured by the same distribution D that produced the data. The training error is just the fraction of the N training examples that h gets wrong. For the rectangle game with points spread evenly over the square, the true error is simply the area of the red strips.
Say it“The true error of h is the probability, over x drawn from D, that h of x differs from f of x. The training error is the average number of mistakes on the N examples.”

The tightest box never makes a training mistake, yet its true error is never exactly zero: it sits inside the hidden rectangle and misses thin strips along the edges. And with an unlucky sample (all the points bunched in one corner) it could be badly wrong. So the best promise we can make has two hedges in it. With probability at least 1−δ over the random sample (probably), the learned hypothesis has true error at most ε (approximately correct). This is Leslie Valiant's 1984 definition of PAC learning.

\[ P_{S\sim\mathcal D^N}\Big[\operatorname{err}_{\mathcal D}(h_S)\le\epsilon\Big]\ \ge\ 1-\delta \]
Read it as: S is a random training set of N examples and hS is what the learner returns from it. Across all the training sets you might have been dealt, at least a fraction 1−δ give a hypothesis whose true error is at most ε. A class is PAC-learnable when N only needs to grow polynomially in 1/ε and 1/δ, for every distribution D.
Say it“The probability, over training sets of size N, that the learned hypothesis has error at most epsilon, is at least one minus delta.”
\[ \begin{aligned} P\big[\operatorname{err}>\epsilon\big] &\le 4\big(1-\tfrac{\epsilon}{4}\big)^N \le 4e^{-\epsilon N/4} \\ \text{so } N &\ge \tfrac{4}{\epsilon}\ln\tfrac{4}{\delta} \text{ is enough} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: grow a strip inward from each side of the hidden rectangle until it holds probability ε/4. If every one of the four strips catches at least one training point, the tightest box reaches into all four, and its error is at most 4 × ε/4 = ε. One strip is missed by all N points with probability (1−ε/4)N; add up the four ways to fail. The argument never used the shape of D, so it holds for every distribution.
Say it“The chance of error above epsilon is at most four times one minus epsilon over four, to the N. So N of at least four over epsilon times log four over delta is enough.”

Sample complexity

A finite class: counting the ways to be fooled

The rectangle proof used geometry. A cleaner argument works for any finite hypothesis space H and any learner that returns a hypothesis consistent with the training data (zero training error). Call a hypothesis bad if its true error exceeds ε. A bad hypothesis agrees with a random example with probability less than 1−ε, so it survives N examples with probability less than (1−ε)N. The learner can only be fooled if some bad hypothesis survives, and there are at most |H| of them.

\[ \begin{aligned} &P\big[h_b \text{ survives } N \text{ examples}\big] \\ &\qquad \le (1-\epsilon)^N \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: hb is one fixed bad hypothesis, true error above ε. Each independent example exposes it with probability more than ε, so it slips past all N with probability at most (1−ε)N.
Say it“The probability that a bad hypothesis is consistent with N examples is at most one minus epsilon, to the N.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &P\big[\text{some bad } h \text{ survives}\big] \\ &\qquad \le |H_{\text{bad}}|\,(1-\epsilon)^N \\ &\qquad \le |H|\,e^{-\epsilon N} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the union bound: the chance that at least one of several events happens is at most the sum of their chances. Then use |Hbad| ≤ |H| and 1−ε ≤ e−ε. It doesn’t matter which consistent hypothesis the learner picks: if no bad one survives, any survivor is good.
Say it“The probability that any bad hypothesis survives is at most the number of bad hypotheses times one minus epsilon to the N, which is at most the size of H times e to the minus epsilon N.”

N the bound asks for

\[ N\ \ge\ \frac{1}{\epsilon}\Big(\ln|H| + \ln\frac{1}{\delta}\Big) \]
Read it as: set |H|e−εN ≤ δ and solve for N. This is the sample complexity of a finite class: enough examples that, with probability at least 1−δ, every consistent hypothesis has error at most ε. Two things to notice: the size of H enters only through its logarithm, and so does the confidence, so asking for 99.9% instead of 95% certainty is cheap. Halving ε doubles N.
Say it“N at least one over epsilon, times log of the size of H plus log of one over delta.”
The price of freedom

Why the hypothesis space must be restricted

Suppose the inputs are n yes/no attributes and we refuse to assume anything about the target: H is every Boolean function of n attributes. There are 2n possible inputs and each can be labelled either way, so |H| = 22n. Its logarithm is 2nln 2, and the sample complexity then grows like 2n: to learn, you must see a good fraction of every input there is. That is not learning but memorising, and on an input you haven’t seen, the unrestricted class has no reason to prefer either answer.

\[ \begin{aligned} |H_{\text{all}}| &= 2^{2^n} \\ \Rightarrow\ N &\ge \frac{1}{\epsilon}\Big(2^n\ln 2 + \ln\frac{1}{\delta}\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a Boolean function is a truth table with 2n rows, each a free yes/no, so there are 2 to the 2n of them. Put that |H| into the sample-complexity formula and the double exponential becomes a single one: N must grow like 2n/ε, the number of distinct inputs.
Say it“There are two to the two to the n Boolean functions, so N must be at least one over epsilon times two to the n log two plus log one over delta.”

The way out is to restrict H: bet that the target is simple, with a bias towards some kind of rule. The chart compares the sample size the bound asks for under different bets, on a log scale. Slide the number of attributes n and watch the unrestricted class run away while the restricted ones grow politely, like a polynomial in n.

A learnable class

Learning decision lists

A decision list is a chain of if–then–else rules: if test 1 then answer 1, else if test 2 then answer 2, …, else default. In a k-decision list (k-DL) every test is a conjunction of at most k literals (an attribute or its negation). These lists are surprisingly expressive (every decision tree of depth k is a k-DL), yet the class is small enough to be PAC-learnable, and there is a simple greedy learner (Rivest, 1987): find a test that the remaining examples pass only if they all share one label; make it the next rule; remove the examples it covers; repeat. Our learner picks, among such tests, the one covering the most examples.

\[ \begin{aligned} c = |\mathrm{Conj}(n,k)| &= \sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{2n}{i} \\ &= O(n^k) \\ |k\text{-DL}(n)| &\le 3^{c}\, c! \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a test chooses up to k of the 2n literals, so there are at most |Conj| = Σ C(2n, i) tests. A list is built by deciding, for each test, whether it is left out, used with answer yes, or used with answer no (3 options each), and then putting the used tests in some order (at most |Conj|! orders). The count overshoots, which is fine for an upper bound.
Say it“The number of conjunctions of up to k literals is the sum of 2n choose i, of order n to the k. The number of k-decision lists is at most three to that number, times its factorial.”
\[ N \ \ge\ \frac{1}{\epsilon}\Big(\ln\frac{1}{\delta} + O\big(n^k\log_2 n^k\big)\Big) \]
Read it as: take logs of the count: |Conj| ln 3 + ln(|Conj|!) is of order nk log nk, a polynomial in n for fixed k. Combined with a learner that finds a consistent list in polynomial time, this makes k-DL efficiently PAC-learnable.
Say it“N at least one over epsilon, times log one over delta plus order n to the k log n to the k.”

    Infinite classes

    Shattering and the VC dimension

    Most useful classes are infinite: a line in the plane has three real parameters, so ln|H| is infinite and the counting argument says nothing. Vapnik and Chervonenkis (1971) measured richness differently: not by how many hypotheses there are, but by how many different labellings they can produce. A class shatters a set of m points if, for each of the 2m ways to label them, some hypothesis in the class gets every label right. Its VC dimension h is the size of the largest set it can shatter.

    \[ \begin{aligned} &\mathrm{VC}(H) = \text{largest } m \text{ such that } H \\ &\qquad \text{shatters some } \mathbf x_1,\dots,\mathbf x_m \\ &\text{half-spaces in } \mathbb R^p\!:\ \ \mathrm{VC} = p+1 \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: to show VC = h you need two facts: some set of h points is shattered (you get to choose the points), and no set of h+1 points is. Linear classifiers sign(w·x + b) in p dimensions shatter p+1 points in general position and never p+2, so h equals the number of free parameters. That match is a coincidence of linear models, not a rule.
    Say it“The VC dimension of H is the largest m such that H shatters some m points. For half-spaces in p dimensions it is p plus one.”

    Choose a class, then drag the points (click empty space to add one, up to six). Every small tile is one labelling; a green frame means some hypothesis realises it. Click a tile to see the hypothesis that does it, or to see that none can.

    Counting labellings

    The growth function, and a one-parameter surprise

    Beyond its VC dimension a class cannot produce every labelling, and the number it can produce, its growth function ΠH(m), slows from 2m to a polynomial. That switch is the heart of VC theory: a polynomial number of effective hypotheses plays the role of |H| in the union bound, and ln ΠH grows only like h ln m.

    \[ \Pi_H(m) \ \le\ \sum_{i=0}^{h}\binom{m}{i} \ \le\ \Big(\frac{e\,m}{h}\Big)^{h} \]
    Read it as: Sauer’s lemma. ΠH(m) is the largest number of distinct labellings H can put on any m points. Once m passes h the count is held below a polynomial of degree h in m (the second inequality needs m ≥ h).
    Say it“Pi of m is at most the sum from i equals zero to h of m choose i, which is at most e m over h, to the h.”
    \[ \Pi_{\text{half-planes}}(m) = m^2 - m + 2 \]
    Read it as: Cover’s count (1965): for m points in the plane with no three on a line, exactly m² − m + 2 labellings can be cut off by a straight line. It equals 2m up to m = 3 and then falls ever further behind.
    Say it“Half-planes can produce m squared minus m plus two labellings of m points in general position.”

    The page scatters 12 random points and, for the first m of them, tries every one of the 2m labellings (4,096 at m = 12) and counts those the class can realise. Slide m and switch the class; press the button for a fresh scatter.

    labellings realised
    Sauer’s bound

    Is the VC dimension just the number of parameters in disguise? No. The classifier “positive where sin(αx) > 0” has a single parameter, α, yet it shatters arbitrarily many points. Put them at x = 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, … and choose α so that the wave’s frequency spells out the labels digit by digit. Click the points to flip their labels and watch one number fit them all.

    \[ \begin{aligned} x_i &= 10^{-i},\quad i = 1,\dots,m \\ \alpha &= \pi\Big(1 + \sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(1-y_i)\,10^{i}}{2}\Big) \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: with labels yi = ±1, the term (1−yi)/2 is 1 for a negative point and 0 for a positive one, so α/π is 1 plus a number whose decimal digits are 1 exactly at the negative points. At xi, the digits above position i give whole multiples of 2π, digit i decides whether sin starts its negative half, and the digits below add less than a half-turn.
    Say it“x-i is ten to the minus i; alpha is pi times one plus the sum of one minus y-i, over two, times ten to the i.”

    Bounds on test error

    VC bounds: training error plus a capacity penalty

    So far the learner had to be consistent. In real problems the best hypothesis still makes training mistakes, and we want a bound that holds for every member of H at once, whatever the learner picked. For a finite class, Hoeffding’s inequality says one hypothesis’s training error is unlikely to sit far below its true error, and the union bound spreads that over H. Vapnik’s bound replaces ln|H| with a term built from the VC dimension h.

    \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{err}_{\mathcal D}(h) &\le \widehat{\operatorname{err}}(h) \\ &\quad + \sqrt{\frac{\ln|H| + \ln(2/\eta)}{2N}} \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: with probability at least 1−η, every h in a finite class has true error at most its training error plus a penalty that shrinks like 1/√N, not 1/N: once mistakes are allowed, learning is slower. The penalty is the price of having chosen h from |H| candidates after looking at the data.
    Say it“True error is at most training error plus the square root of log H plus log two over eta, over two N.”
    \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Err}_{\mathcal T} &\le \overline{\operatorname{err}} + \frac{\epsilon}{2}\Big(1+\sqrt{1+\frac{4\,\overline{\operatorname{err}}}{\epsilon}}\Big) \\ \epsilon &= \frac{a_1}{N}\Big(h\big[\ln(a_2N/h)+1\big] \\ &\qquad\quad - \ln(\eta/4)\Big) \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: the bound in the form ESL gives it, for 0–1 loss: with probability at least 1−η, simultaneously for every member of a class with VC dimension h. The term h[ln(a2N/h) + 1] stands in for ln|H|: it is the log of the growth function, via Sauer. In the worst case a1 = 4 and a2 = 2. When the training error is zero the bound is just ε, which falls like (h ln N)/N; otherwise the gap falls like √ε.
    Say it“Test error is at most training error plus epsilon over two times one plus the square root of one plus four err over epsilon, where epsilon is a-one times h times log a-two N over h plus one, minus log eta over four, all over N.”

    Move N, h and the training error. The solid curve is Vapnik’s bound, the dotted one the finite-class bound for a class of 2h hypotheses (which can shatter at most h points). Anything above 0.5 tells you less than a coin flip would.

    Using the bound

    Structural risk minimisation

    A bound that is too loose to trust can still be useful to compare. Vapnik’s structural risk minimisation (SRM) arranges the models in nested classes of growing VC dimension (polynomials of degree 0, 1, 2, …), fits each, and picks the one whose bound on test error is smallest. It is a model-selection criterion, like AIC and BIC from chapter 1.10a. For regression with squared error the bound takes a multiplicative form; Cherkassky and Mulier suggest the constants c = a1 = a2 = 1 and, with η = 4/√N, it becomes the practical formula on the right.

    \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Err} &\le \frac{\overline{\operatorname{err}}}{\big(1-c\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\big)_+} \\ &= \frac{\overline{\operatorname{err}}}{\Big(1 - \sqrt{p - p\ln p + \tfrac{\ln N}{2N}}\Big)_+} \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: p = h/N, the VC dimension per example. The training error is inflated by a factor that grows with p; when the square root reaches 1 the factor is infinite (the (·)+ means “or zero if negative”), so models with too many parameters for the data are ruled out entirely.
    Say it“Test error is at most the training error divided by one minus the square root of p minus p log p plus log N over two N.”
    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{AIC} &= \overline{\operatorname{err}} + 2\,\tfrac{d}{N}\,\hat\sigma^2 \\ \mathrm{BIC} &= \overline{\operatorname{err}} + (\ln N)\,\tfrac{d}{N}\,\hat\sigma^2 \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: the rivals add a penalty instead of multiplying. d is the number of parameters and σ̂² the noise variance, estimated from the largest model. For N of 8 or more, ln N > 2, so BIC punishes complexity harder than AIC.
    Say it“AIC is training error plus two d over N sigma-hat squared; BIC uses log N in place of two.”

    Data: N points x uniform on [0, 1], y = sin(3πx) plus Gaussian noise. Each criterion picks a polynomial degree from 0 to 12 (h = d = degree + 1), and we know the true test error exactly. Change N and the noise, then run 100 fresh datasets to compare the criteria the way ESL does: by the relative error, 0 when the criterion picked the best model on that dataset and 1 when it picked the worst.

    Honest accounting

    What the bounds do and do not explain

    The bounds are true theorems, and they explain real things. They also leave out a great deal, because they promise the same thing for every distribution, every target and every learner that picks from H. Two classes with the same VC dimension get the same guarantee whether the data is easy or adversarial. A clean demonstration: take a learner whose class is enormous, 1-nearest neighbour (it can shatter any set of distinct points, so its VC dimension is infinite), and train it on 1,000 real mushrooms from the project below. Then replace a fraction of the training labels by coin flips.

    \[ \operatorname{Err}(q) \ \approx\ (1-q)\,\operatorname{Err}(0) + \tfrac{q}{2} \]
    Read it as: q is the fraction of training labels replaced by a fair coin. 1-NN copies the label of the nearest training point; that label is the true one with probability 1−q and a coin flip with probability q. The training error stays at zero throughout, because every training point is its own nearest neighbour, and so does every capacity-based bound. The test error does not.
    Say it“The error at noise level q is about one minus q times the clean error, plus q over two.”

    The bounds explain……but they do not explain
    Why some restriction (a bias) is needed: the unrestricted class needs as many examples as there are inputs.Why the same model learns real labels and memorises random ones: capacity is identical, only the data differs (Zhang et al., 2017, for deep networks).
    How N must scale: with ln|H| or h, linearly in 1/ε when consistent, like 1/ε² when not, and only logarithmically in 1/δ.The actual numbers: worst-case constants and union bounds make them tens to thousands of times too cautious on typical data (see the project).
    Why training error is optimistic, by an amount that grows with capacity per example, h/N.Models with far more parameters than examples (wide neural networks, boosting run long) that still generalise.
    That k-decision lists, conjunctions and linear separators in fixed dimension are efficiently PAC-learnable.Why k-nearest neighbours works at all: its VC dimension is infinite, so VC theory is silent.

    Tighter theories attach the guarantee to the data or the particular solution, not just the class. Margin bounds: a separating hyperplane with margin ρ around data inside a ball of radius R behaves as if its VC dimension were at most about R²/ρ², however many dimensions there are; that is the theory behind the support vector machine of chapter 1.9. PAC-Bayes bounds (McAllester, 1999) charge a posterior over hypotheses for how far it moved from a prior, a KL divergence instead of ln|H|. Others use compression (a hypothesis rebuilt from a few examples), algorithmic stability, or Rademacher complexity measured on the actual sample.

    \[ h_{\text{margin}} \ \le\ \min\!\Big(\Big\lceil \frac{R^2}{\rho^2}\Big\rceil,\ p\Big) + 1 \]
    Read it as: Vapnik’s bound for hyperplanes that must keep every point at distance at least ρ: in p dimensions, with all points in a ball of radius R, the effective VC dimension is limited by the geometry R/ρ as well as by p. A wide margin gives a small effective h even in a million dimensions.
    Say it“The VC dimension of margin-rho hyperplanes is at most the smaller of the ceiling of R squared over rho squared and p, plus one.”
    Real-world project

    Project: mushrooms, bounds versus reality

    The UCI Mushroom data (Schlimmer, 1987; CC BY 4.0) describes 8,124 mushrooms of 23 species from the Audubon field guide by 22 attributes such as odour, gill size and spore-print colour, each labelled edible (4,208) or poisonous or unknown (3,916). The 22 attributes take 117 values in all, giving n = 117 yes/no features (“odour = foul”) and 234 literals with their negations. We learn 1-decision lists with the greedy learner from the decision-list section and measure learning curves: shuffle the data (20 different seeds), keep 2,124 mushrooms aside for testing, and train on the first N of the other 6,000 for N from 5 to 6,000. Then we hold the measured errors up against the PAC bound for 1-DL and the VC bound. (A 1-decision list is a linear threshold function of its features, so h = n + 1 = 118 bounds its VC dimension.) This is a study of learning curves, not a foraging guide: never eat a wild mushroom on the strength of a model. Press Run (it also starts by itself when you scroll here).

    \[ \begin{aligned} \ln|1\text{-DL}(117)| &\le 235\ln 3 + \ln(235!) \\ \epsilon_{\text{PAC}}(N) &= \frac{\ln|H| + \ln(1/\delta)}{N} \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: with n = 117 there are |Conj(117, 1)| = 1 + 2×117 = 235 possible tests, so the class has at most 3235·235! lists. Turn the sample-complexity formula around: given N consistent examples, the error that the bound guarantees with probability 1−δ.
    Say it“The log of the number of 1-decision lists on 117 features is at most 235 log 3 plus log 235 factorial. The guaranteed error is log H plus log one over delta, over N.”

      import numpy as np
from math import lgamma, log

def mulberry32(a):                       # the page's random-number generator, bit for bit
    def r():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 2**32
    return r
def shuffle(n, seed):
    r, p = mulberry32(seed), list(range(n))
    for i in range(n - 1, 0, -1):
        j = int(r() * (i + 1)); p[i], p[j] = p[j], p[i]
    return np.array(p)

rows = [l.strip().split(",") for l in open("agaricus-lepiota.data") if l.strip()]
y = np.array([r[0] == "p" for r in rows])                  # True = poisonous
feats = [(j, v) for j in range(22) for v in sorted({r[j + 1] for r in rows})]
B = np.array([[r[j + 1] == v for j, v in feats] for r in rows])
L = np.hstack([B, ~B])                                     # 2n literals: "attr = v" and "attr != v"

def learn_dl(L, y):                      # greedy 1-decision list (Rivest 1987)
    rem, rules = np.ones(len(y), bool), []
    while True:
        yr = y[rem]
        if yr.all() or not yr.any(): return rules, bool(yr[0]) if len(yr) else rules[-1][1]
        cov, pos = L[rem].sum(0), (L[rem] & yr[:, None]).sum(0)
        score = np.where((cov > 0) & ((pos == 0) | (pos == cov)), cov, -1)
        k = int(score.argmax())
        if score[k] < 0: return rules, bool(yr.mean() >= 0.5)   # stuck (never happens here)
        rules.append((k, bool(pos[k] == cov[k]))); rem &= ~L[:, k]
def predict(L, rules, default):
    out = np.full(len(L), default)
    for k, lab in reversed(rules): out[L[:, k]] = lab
    return out

n = B.shape[1]; c = 1 + 2 * n                              # |Conj(n,1)| = 1 + 2n tests
lnH = c * log(3) + lgamma(c + 1)                           # ln |1-DL(n)| <= ln(3^c c!)
h, delta = n + 1, 0.05                                     # 1-DLs are linear threshold functions
pac = lambda N: (lnH + log(1 / delta)) / N
vc = lambda N: 4 * (h * (log(2 * N / h) + 1) - log(delta / 4)) / N   # ESL (7.44), err = 0
print(f"n = {n} boolean features, ln|H| = {lnH:.1f}, h = {h}")
print("    N  mean test err  worst of 20  rules   PAC eps   VC eps")
Ns = [5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 2000, 6000]
E = {N: [] for N in Ns}; R = {N: [] for N in Ns}
for rep in range(20):
    p = shuffle(len(y), 1000 + rep); pool, te = p[:6000], p[6000:]

      Wrap-up

      Five lines to remember

      • PAC learning promises, with probability at least 1−δ over the training sample, a hypothesis with true error at most ε, for every distribution, provided training and future data come from the same one.
      • For a finite class and a consistent learner, N ≥ (1/ε)(ln|H| + ln 1/δ) suffices: size counts only through its logarithm, but an unrestricted class still needs as many examples as there are inputs.
      • k-decision lists are a rich yet learnable class: ln|k-DL(n)| is polynomial in n, and a greedy learner finds a consistent list whenever one exists.
      • For infinite classes the VC dimension, the size of the largest shattered set, replaces ln|H|; it is not the number of parameters (sin(αx) has one parameter and infinite VC dimension).
      • VC bounds are true but worst-case: on mushrooms the PAC bound asked for over a hundred times more data than the learner needed; they are best used to compare models (SRM) and to understand scaling, not to predict test error.

      Check your understanding

      Why does δ appear inside a logarithm, while ε divides the whole bound?

      A bad hypothesis survives N examples with probability (1−ε)N ≈ e−εN: the failure probability falls exponentially in N. Making it smaller than δ needs N ≈ ln(1/δ)/ε, so a hundred times more confidence costs only ln 100 ≈ 4.6 extra units of 1/ε, while halving ε doubles everything.

      To show a class has VC dimension 3, is it enough to find three points it shatters?

      No. That shows VC ≥ 3. You must also show that no set of four points can be shattered. For half-planes: four points either have one inside the triangle of the other three (label it differently from them) or form a convex quadrilateral (label the diagonals alike, as in XOR).

      A class contains 220 hypotheses. What is the largest its VC dimension can be?

      20. Shattering m points needs 2m different labellings, and each hypothesis gives only one, so 2m ≤ |H| and m ≤ log2|H| = 20.

      Why can’t the greedy learner build a 1-decision list for x₁ XOR x₂, even from all 16 rows?

      Every single literal (x₁, ¬x₁, x₂, ¬x₂, …) is true on rows of both labels, so no first rule can be pure. XOR is not a linear threshold function, and every 1-DL is one. With k = 2 the test x₁ ∧ x₂ is pure and the learner succeeds.

      The VC bound for your model is 0.8 but its test error is 0.03. Is the theory wrong?

      No: 0.03 ≤ 0.8, so the bound holds; it is just loose. It must hold for the worst distribution and the worst target, uses the union bound over the whole class, and ignores what the particular learner does. A tight estimate of test error comes from held-out data or cross-validation.
