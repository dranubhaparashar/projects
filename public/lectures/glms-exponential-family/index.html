The Exponential Family & GLMs

One straight line, bent three ways

Linear regression predicts a number, logistic regression a probability, Poisson regression a count. They look like three different models, but they are one: a straight line η = w0 + w1x, passed through a function that suits the kind of answer, fitted by one algorithm with one gradient. The glue is a single family of distributions that contains the Gaussian, the Bernoulli, the Poisson and many more. On the right, pick a family and drag the two handles: you are moving the straight line underneath, and the curve is what it looks like after the bend.

The idea

One shape, many distributions

Write a distribution's log-probability as a function of the data x. For a surprising number of the distributions you already know, it splits into three tidy pieces: something that depends only on x, something that depends only on the parameters, and a product of the two, a parameter times a function of the data. Any distribution that can be written this way belongs to the exponential family. The parameter in the product is the natural parameter η, and the function of the data is the sufficient statistic u(x).

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\eta) &= h(\mathbf{x})\; g(\boldsymbol\eta)\; \exp\!\big(\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\big) \\ g(\boldsymbol\eta) &= 1 \Big/ \!\int h(\mathbf{x})\, \exp\!\big(\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\big)\, d\mathbf{x} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: h(x) is a base measure that ignores the parameters. exp(ηTu(x)) tilts it: each natural parameter ηi rewards data with a large ui(x). g(η) is whatever constant makes the total probability 1 (for discrete x the integral is a sum). Change η and you move through the family; h, u and g stay fixed.
Say it“p of x given eta is h of x, times g of eta, times e to the eta-transpose u of x.”

Three rewrites show how familiar distributions fit the mould. For each one the trick is the same: take the log, collect the terms that multiply the data, and read off η. Pick a family below and slide its natural parameter; the running examples recompute the probability both ways, the textbook way and the exponential-family way, and they always agree.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu^{x}(1-\mu)^{1-x} &= (1-\mu)\exp\!\Big(x \ln\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\Big) \\ \eta = \ln\frac{\mu}{1-\mu} \ &\Leftrightarrow\ \mu = \sigma(\eta) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the Bernoulli (x = 0 or 1) has u(x) = x, h(x) = 1 and g(η) = 1 − μ = σ(−η). Its natural parameter is the log-odds, and turning it back into a probability needs the logistic sigmoid. That is where logistic regression's S-curve comes from.
Say it“Eta is the log of mu over one minus mu; mu is sigma of eta.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\lambda^{x}e^{-\lambda}}{x!} &= \frac{1}{x!}\; e^{-e^{\eta}}\; \exp(\eta\, x) \\ \eta = \ln\lambda \ &\Leftrightarrow\ \lambda = e^{\eta} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the Poisson count has u(x) = x, h(x) = 1/x! and g(η) = exp(−eη). Its natural parameter is the log of the rate, so any real η gives a valid, positive rate. That is why Poisson regression models the log of the mean.
Say it“Eta is the log of lambda; lambda is e to the eta.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{u}(x) &= \begin{pmatrix} x \\ x^2 \end{pmatrix}\!, \quad \boldsymbol\eta = \begin{pmatrix} \mu/\sigma^2 \\ -1/(2\sigma^2) \end{pmatrix} \\ g(\boldsymbol\eta) &= \sqrt{-2\eta_2}\, \exp\!\Big(\frac{\eta_1^2}{4\eta_2}\Big) \\ h(x) &= 1/\sqrt{2\pi} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the Gaussian has two sufficient statistics, x and x², because expanding (x − μ)² gives a term in x and a term in x². η2 must be negative (it is minus a half over the variance), otherwise the bell would open upwards and could not be normalised.
Say it“u of x is x and x squared; eta is mu over sigma squared, and minus one over two sigma squared.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\eta) &= \exp\!\big(\boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}\big)\Big/\textstyle\sum_{j} e^{\eta_j} \\ \mu_k &= \frac{e^{\eta_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{M} e^{\eta_j}}, \quad \eta_M = 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a categorical variable with M outcomes, written as a one-hot vector x. The natural parameters are log-probabilities up to a shared constant, so we may pin one of them, ηM = 0, and the others are log-odds against outcome M. Turning them back into probabilities is the softmax.
Say it“mu-k is e to the eta-k over the sum of e to the eta-j.”

A useful trick

The normaliser knows the moments

The normaliser g(η) looks like bookkeeping, but it hides the whole distribution's summary. Differentiate the normalisation condition with respect to η and out falls the mean of u(x); differentiate again and out falls its covariance. It is easiest to see with A(η) = −ln g(η), the log-normaliser: its slope is the mean and its curvature is the variance. Slide η and compare the tangent's slope with the mean you get by summing over the whole distribution.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})] &= -\nabla \ln g(\boldsymbol\eta) = \nabla A(\boldsymbol\eta) \\ \operatorname{cov}[\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})] &= -\nabla\nabla \ln g(\boldsymbol\eta) = \nabla\nabla A(\boldsymbol\eta) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: no sums or integrals needed: the moments of u(x) are derivatives of one known function. Because a covariance can never be negative, A(η) always curves upwards (it is convex). That single fact will give every GLM with a canonical link a likelihood with one peak and no false summits.
Say it“The expected sufficient statistic is minus the gradient of log g; its covariance is minus the second derivative of log g.”

Maximum likelihood

Sufficient statistics: all the data you need

Now fit η to N independent observations. Take the log of the likelihood and the exponential family's shape pays off: the data touch η only through the sum Σ u(xn). Two data sets with the same sum have log-likelihoods that differ by a constant, so they lead to exactly the same estimate. That sum is a sufficient statistic: once you have it, you can throw the raw data away without losing anything about η. Setting the gradient to zero then gives a beautifully simple rule: choose η so that the model's expected u(x) equals the data's average u(x).

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln p(\mathbf{X}\mid\boldsymbol\eta) = {} & \textstyle\sum_n \ln h(\mathbf{x}_n) + N\ln g(\boldsymbol\eta) \\ & + \boldsymbol\eta^{\mathsf T}\textstyle\sum_n \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_n) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first term ignores η; the second ignores the data except for N; only the third mixes them, and it sees the data only through Σ u(xn).
Say it“The log-likelihood is the sum of log h, plus N log g of eta, plus eta times the sum of u of x-n.”
\[ -\nabla\ln g(\boldsymbol\eta_{\text{ML}}) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_n) \]
Read it as: the left side is the model's mean of u(x) (previous section); the right side is the data's mean. Maximum likelihood matches the two. For the Poisson, u(x) = x and the model's mean is eη, so eηML = x̄: the rate is estimated by the average count. For the Gaussian the sufficient statistics are Σx and Σx², which is why the sample mean and sample variance are all you need.
Say it“At the maximum-likelihood eta, minus the gradient of log g equals the average of u of x-n.”

Classes from the family

Why the sigmoid keeps turning up

Here is a deeper reason logistic regression is so common. Suppose each class produces its data from the same exponential family, with its own natural parameter but a shared h and a shared scale s. Then the log of the ratio of the two class densities has no room for anything but a straight line in x: the h's cancel, and what's left is linear. Bayes' theorem turns that log-ratio into the posterior through the logistic sigmoid. So whenever the classes look like Poissons, Bernoullis, or Gaussians with a shared variance, the exact posterior is a logistic regression. Set the two classes' rates and the prior, and watch the dots (Bayes' theorem, computed directly) sit on the sigmoid (the formula).

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\eta_k, s) &= \tfrac{1}{s}\, h\!\big(\tfrac{\mathbf{x}}{s}\big)\, g(\boldsymbol\eta_k)\, \exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{s}\boldsymbol\eta_k^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}\big) \\ a(\mathbf{x}) &= \tfrac{1}{s}(\boldsymbol\eta_1-\boldsymbol\eta_2)^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x} \\ &\quad + \ln\frac{g(\boldsymbol\eta_1)\,p(\mathcal C_1)}{g(\boldsymbol\eta_2)\,p(\mathcal C_2)} \\ p(\mathcal C_1\mid\mathbf{x}) &= \sigma\big(a(\mathbf{x})\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a(x) is the log of p(x|C1)p(C1) over p(x|C2)p(C2). Everything that is not linear in x, the h(x/s) factor, appears in both and cancels. The weights are the difference in natural parameters, the bias collects the normalisers and the prior. With K classes the same argument gives a softmax of K linear functions. For Poisson classes with rates λ1, λ2: a(x) = x ln(λ1/λ2) − (λ1 − λ2) + ln(π1/π2).
Say it“a of x is the difference of the natural parameters times x, over s, plus the log of the ratio of g times prior. The posterior is sigma of a.”

Generalised linear models

Canonical links: one recipe for three models

A generalised linear model (GLM) has three parts. A linear predictor a = wTφ(x), as in linear regression. A distribution for the target t from the exponential family. And an activation f that turns a into the mean, y = f(a) = E[t]; its inverse f−1 is called the link function. You may pick any link, but one choice is special: the link that makes the linear predictor equal the natural parameter, a = η. With this canonical link the gradient of the log-likelihood collapses to the same expression for every family: error times input, summed. Switch families, nudge the weights, and take gradient steps: the arrows are the same formula each time.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(t\mid\eta, s) &= \tfrac{1}{s}\,h\!\big(\tfrac{t}{s}\big)\,g(\eta)\exp\!\big(\tfrac{\eta\,t}{s}\big) \\ y \equiv \mathbb E[t\mid\eta] &= -s\,\tfrac{d}{d\eta}\ln g(\eta) \equiv \psi^{-1}(\eta) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the target's distribution, with natural parameter η and a known scale s (the variance σ² for the Gaussian; 1 for Bernoulli and Poisson). The mean y is a smooth, increasing function of η, so we can go back: η = ψ(y). The model sets y = f(wTφ).
Say it“y, the expected target, is minus s times the derivative of log g of eta; eta is psi of y.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_{\mathbf w}\ln p(\mathbf{t}\mid\mathbf w) &= \sum_{n} \tfrac{1}{s}\,(t_n - y_n) \\ &\qquad \times \psi'(y_n)\, f'(a_n)\,\boldsymbol\phi_n \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the chain rule from w to a to y to η. Each point pulls the weights by its error tn − yn, scaled by how the link bends at that point.
Say it“The gradient is the sum of one over s, times t-n minus y-n, times psi-prime of y-n, times f-prime of a-n, times phi-n.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &f^{-1} = \psi \ \Rightarrow \\ &\nabla E(\mathbf w) = \frac{1}{s}\sum_{n}(y_n - t_n)\,\boldsymbol\phi_n \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with the canonical link, ψ′(y)f′(a) = 1 and the bending factor vanishes. The error function E = −ln p has gradient “prediction minus target, times input”: identical for linear, logistic and Poisson regression. With an intercept, setting it to zero forces Σ yn = Σ tn: the fitted means add up to the observed total.
Say it“With the canonical link, the gradient of E is one over s times the sum of y-n minus t-n, times phi-n.”

Fitting

Newton's method is reweighted least squares

Gradient steps get there, slowly, and you must guess a step size. Newton's method uses the curvature too: it fits a bowl (a quadratic) to the error at the current weights and jumps straight to that bowl's bottom. For linear regression the error is a bowl, so one Newton step lands on the least-squares answer. For logistic regression the Hessian H = ΦTRΦ contains weights Rnn = yn(1 − yn) that change as the fit changes, so we repeat. Rearranged, every Newton step is a weighted least-squares fit to an adjusted target z, with weights recomputed each time: iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS). Drag the star to choose a starting point, then race Newton against gradient descent.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf w^{\text{new}} &= \mathbf w^{\text{old}} - \mathbf H^{-1}\nabla E(\mathbf w^{\text{old}}) \\ \nabla E &= \boldsymbol\Phi^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf y - \mathbf t), \quad \mathbf H = \boldsymbol\Phi^{\mathsf T}\mathbf R\,\boldsymbol\Phi \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: step against the gradient, but rescaled by the inverse curvature: long steps where the bowl is flat, short ones where it is steep. For the cross-entropy error, R is diagonal with Rnn = yn(1 − yn), the variance of each target; every entry is positive, so H is positive definite (given independent columns of Φ) and E has a single minimum.
Say it“w-new is w-old minus H inverse times the gradient; the gradient is Phi-transpose times y minus t, the Hessian is Phi-transpose R Phi.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf w^{\text{new}} &= (\boldsymbol\Phi^{\mathsf T}\mathbf R\boldsymbol\Phi)^{-1}\boldsymbol\Phi^{\mathsf T}\mathbf R\,\mathbf z \\ \mathbf z &= \boldsymbol\Phi\mathbf w^{\text{old}} - \mathbf R^{-1}(\mathbf y - \mathbf t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same step, rewritten as the normal equations of weighted least squares. zn = an + (tn − yn)/Rnn is a working response: the current linear predictor nudged by the error, measured on the η scale. Points whose prediction is near 0 or 1 get little weight; uncertain points get the most. For any canonical GLM, Rnn is the variance of tn (μn for Poisson, 1 for Gaussian, which is why one step is enough there).
Say it“w-new is Phi-transpose R Phi, inverted, times Phi-transpose R z; z is Phi w-old minus R inverse times y minus t.”

Another link

Probit: a threshold with noise

Here is another way to get an S-curve. Imagine each case has a hidden score a = wTφ, and the answer is yes when the score beats a random threshold θ. The probability of yes is then the chance that θ falls below a: the cumulative distribution of the threshold. If θ follows a logistic density, you get logistic regression back. If θ is Gaussian, you get probit regression, a GLM with the Bernoulli family but a non-canonical link. Slide the score and switch the threshold's distribution: the shaded area is the probability.

\[ \begin{aligned} f(a) &= \int_{-\infty}^{a} p(\theta)\,d\theta \\ \Phi(a) &= \int_{-\infty}^{a}\mathcal N(\theta\mid 0,1)\,d\theta \\ &= \tfrac12\Big[1+\operatorname{erf}\big(\tfrac{a}{\sqrt2}\big)\Big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: f(a) is the area under the threshold's density to the left of the score. With a standard Gaussian threshold the area is Φ(a), the inverse probit; erf is the error function, a close cousin that computers provide.
Say it“f of a is the integral of p of theta up to a; Phi of a is a half times one plus erf of a over root two.”
\[ \sigma(a) \approx \Phi(\lambda a), \qquad \lambda = \sqrt{\pi/8} \approx 0.627 \]
Read it as: squeeze the probit curve sideways by λ and it matches the sigmoid's slope at the centre; the two then differ by less than 0.02 anywhere. That is why fitted logistic coefficients are typically about 1.6 to 1.8 times the probit ones on the same data. The real difference is in the tails: the Gaussian's fall like e−a²/2, the logistic's like e−|a|.
Say it“Sigma of a is about Phi of lambda a, with lambda squared equal to pi over eight.”
P(θ < a), the shaded area
|σ(a) − Φ(λa)|

Those thin Gaussian tails have a cost. A mislabelled point far on the wrong side is, to a probit model, an almost impossible event, and its penalty grows like the square of its distance; to the logistic model the penalty grows only linearly. Drag the mislabelled point outwards and see which model gives in. Both are fitted by IRLS; the probit's weights are φ(a)²/(Φ(a)(1 − Φ(a))), because a non-canonical link keeps the bending factor from the gradient formula above.

Inference

Is the effect real? Wald, deviance and likelihood ratios

A fitted coefficient is an estimate, so we want its uncertainty. IRLS hands it over for free. Near the maximum, the log-likelihood is close to a parabola whose curvature is the Hessian XTWX from the last Newton step; that quadratic approximation says the estimates are roughly Gaussian with covariance (XTWX)−1. The square roots of its diagonal are the standard errors. Dividing a coefficient by its standard error gives the Wald z; dropping the variable, refitting and measuring how much the deviance rises gives the likelihood-ratio test. The two agree when the parabola is a good approximation. Shrink the sample and watch the parabola stop fitting.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} &\;\dot\sim\; \mathcal N\big(\boldsymbol\beta,\ (\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf W\mathbf X)^{-1}\big) \\ z_j &= \hat\beta_j \,/\, \widehat{\operatorname{se}}_j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for large samples the estimate scatters around the truth like a Gaussian whose covariance is the inverse of the curvature at the peak. W holds the final IRLS weights. |z| > 1.96 means the coefficient is more than about two standard errors from zero (p < 0.05), and β̂ ± 1.96 se is a Wald 95% interval.
Say it“Beta-hat is approximately normal around beta with covariance X-transpose W X inverse; z-j is beta-hat-j over its standard error.”
\[ \begin{aligned} D &= -2\textstyle\sum_n \big[t_n\ln\hat p_n \\ &\qquad\quad + (1-t_n)\ln(1-\hat p_n)\big] \\ \text{LR} &= D_{\text{without } j} - D_{\text{with } j} \\ &\;\dot\sim\; \chi^2_1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the deviance is minus twice the log-likelihood (for 0/1 targets the perfect, saturated model has likelihood 1). Removing a variable can only raise it; if the variable does nothing, the rise behaves like a chi-square with one degree of freedom, so a rise above 3.84 is significant at 5%. Drop several variables at once and the degrees of freedom count them.
Say it“D is minus two times the log-likelihood; the likelihood-ratio statistic is the rise in D when variable j is removed.”
\[ \chi^2_{\text{Pearson}} = \sum_n \frac{(t_n - \hat p_n)^2}{\hat p_n(1-\hat p_n)} \;\approx\; D \]
Read it as: the weighted residual sum of squares from the last IRLS step. It is the quadratic approximation to the deviance, just as the Wald test is the quadratic approximation to the likelihood-ratio test. A third member, the Rao score test, uses the gradient at the restricted fit, so it needs no refit with the variable in.
Say it“Pearson's chi-square is the sum of squared residuals, each divided by p-hat times one minus p-hat; it is roughly the deviance.”

The same project in Python

import json, numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm, chi2
from sklearn.linear_model import PoissonRegressor, LogisticRegression, LinearRegression
D = json.load(open("x016-data.json")); B, H = D["bike"], D["heart"]

# ---------- Part 1: bike-share demand as Poisson counts ----------
hr, wd, ws, se, yr = (np.array(B[k]) for k in ["hr", "wd", "ws", "se", "yr"])
temp = np.array(B["t"]) / 100 * 41                      # degrees C
t = (temp - 20) / 10; hum = np.array(B["h"]) / 100; wind = np.array(B["w"]) / 10000
y = np.array(B["c"], float); test = np.array([c == "1" for c in B["te"]])

def design(groups):
    cols = [np.ones_like(t)]
    if "hour" in groups:   cols += [(hr == h).astype(float) for h in range(1, 24)]
    if "work" in groups:   cols += [wd.astype(float)]
    if "hxw" in groups:    cols += [((hr == h) & (wd == 1)).astype(float) for h in range(1, 24)]
    if "year" in groups:   cols += [yr.astype(float)]
    if "season" in groups: cols += [(se == s).astype(float) for s in (2, 3, 4)]
    if "weather" in groups: cols += [(ws == w).astype(float) for w in (2, 3)]
    if "temp" in groups:   cols += [t, t * t]
    if "hum" in groups:    cols += [hum]
    if "wind" in groups:   cols += [wind]
    return np.column_stack(cols)

def pdev(y, mu):
    r = np.where(y > 0, y * np.log(np.where(y > 0, y, 1) / mu), 0)
    return 2 * np.sum(r - (y - mu))
def bdev(y, mu): return -2 * np.sum(y * np.log(mu) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - mu))

def irls(X, y, fam, tol=1e-10, it=50):
    """Newton = IRLS for a GLM with its canonical link. Returns beta, fitted means, deviance per iteration, covariance."""
    mu = (y + y.mean()) / 2 if fam == "poisson" else (y + 0.5) / 2
    eta = np.log(mu) if fam == "poisson" else np.log(mu / (1 - mu))
    hist = []; dev_old = np.inf
    for k in range(it):
        w = mu if fam == "poisson" else mu * (1 - mu)            # working weight = variance function
        z = eta + (y - mu) / w                                     # working response
        beta = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ (w[:, None] * X), X.T @ (w * z))
        eta = X @ beta; mu = np.exp(eta) if fam == "poisson" else 1 / (1 + np.exp(-eta))
        dev = pdev(y, mu) if fam == "poisson" else bdev(y, mu)
        hist.append(dev)
        if abs(dev - dev_old) < tol * (abs(dev) + 0.1): break
        dev_old = dev
    w = mu if fam == "poisson" else mu * (1 - mu)
    return beta, mu, hist, np.linalg.inv(X.T @ (w[:, None] * X))

print("=== Bike Sharing (UCI), hourly counts, 2011-2012 ===")
print(f"{len(y)} hours: {(~test).sum()} for training, {test.sum()} for testing (days 24-31 of each month)")
null_te = pdev(y[test], np.full(test.sum(), y[~test].mean()))
presets = {"hour of day": ["hour"],
           "+ working day x hour": ["hour", "work", "hxw"],
           "+ year, season, weather": ["hour", "work", "hxw", "year", "season", "weather"],
           "+ temperature, humidity, wind": ["hour", "work", "hxw", "year", "season", "weather", "temp", "hum", "wind"]}
print(f"{'Poisson GLM':30s} {'p':>3s} {'iters':>5s} {'train deviance':>15s} {'test D^2':>9s} {'dispersion':>10s}")
for name, g in presets.items():
    X = design(g); b, mu, hist, cov = irls(X[~test], y[~test], "poisson")
    mte = np.exp(X[test] @ b); d2 = 1 - pdev(y[test], mte) / null_te
    phi = np.sum((y[~test] - mu) ** 2 / mu) / ((~test).sum() - X.shape[1])
    print(f"{name:30s} {X.shape[1]:3d} {len(hist):5d} {hist[-1]:15.1f} {d2:9.4f} {phi:10.2f}")
Xf = design(presets["+ temperature, humidity, wind"]); bf, muf, histf, covf = irls(Xf[~test], y[~test], "poisson")
print("IRLS deviance by iteration (full model):", " ".join(f"{d:.1f}" for d in histf))

def heart(names, link="logit"):
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(yA))] + [XA[:, cols.index(c)] for c in names])
    if link == "logit":
        b, mu, hist, cov = irls(X, yA, "binomial")
    else:  # probit: Fisher scoring, the IRLS of a non-canonical link
        b = np.zeros(X.shape[1]); dold = np.inf; hist = []
        for k in range(50):
            eta = X @ b; mu = norm.cdf(eta); d = norm.pdf(eta); w = d * d / (mu * (1 - mu))
            z = eta + (yA - mu) / d; b = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ (w[:, None] * X), X.T @ (w * z))
            mu = norm.cdf(X @ b); dev = bdev(yA, mu); hist.append(dev)
            if abs(dev - dold) < 1e-12 * dev: break
            dold = dev
        eta = X @ b; d = norm.pdf(eta); mu = norm.cdf(eta)
        cov = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ ((d * d / (mu * (1 - mu)))[:, None] * X))
    return b, np.sqrt(np.diag(cov)), bdev(yA, mu), mu, len(hist)

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • The exponential family p(x|η) = h(x) g(η) exp(ηTu(x)) covers the Gaussian, Bernoulli, Poisson, categorical and more; η is the natural parameter (log-odds, log-rate, …).
  • The log-normaliser A = −ln g gives the moments by differentiation and is convex; maximum likelihood just matches the model's mean of u(x) to the data's, so Σ u(xn) is sufficient.
  • A GLM is a linear predictor, an exponential-family target and a link. With the canonical link the gradient is always Σ(yn − tn)φn, and the fitted means add up to the observed total.
  • Newton's method on a GLM is iteratively reweighted least squares: weights = the targets' variances, working response z = a + (t − y)/R. It converges in a handful of steps, unless the classes separate.
  • The final IRLS curvature gives standard errors and Wald tests; deviance differences give likelihood-ratio tests. Probit swaps in a Gaussian threshold; counts that vary more than Poisson allows need wider errors.

Check your understanding

Why is the natural parameter of a Bernoulli the log-odds, and what does that have to do with logistic regression?

Writing μx(1 − μ)1−x as (1 − μ) exp(x ln(μ/(1 − μ))) puts ln(μ/(1 − μ)) in front of x, so that is η. Logistic regression sets η = wTφ: a linear model of the natural parameter, i.e. the canonical link.

You have a million Poisson counts but your disk can store two numbers. Which two keep everything about the rate?

N and Σxn. The log-likelihood depends on the rate only through them, so the estimate (Σxn/N) and the whole likelihood curve can be rebuilt from them.

For a Poisson GLM, what are IRLS's weights and working response?

Rnn = μn (the Poisson variance) and zn = ηn + (tn − μn)/μn. Each step is a weighted least-squares fit of z on the features.

Your logistic regression's IRLS iterations never settle and one coefficient keeps doubling. Why?

The classes are linearly separable along that direction, so the likelihood keeps rising as the weight grows: the maximum is at infinity. Regularise (add a penalty or a prior), or drop the separating feature.

A Poisson model of bike counts reports a dispersion of 12. What is wrong with its standard errors, and roughly how do you fix them?

The counts vary about 12 times more than a Poisson allows, so the standard errors are too small by about √12 ≈ 3.5. A quasi-Poisson fit multiplies them by that factor; a negative-binomial model builds the extra variance in. The coefficients themselves are still sensible estimates of the mean.
