The EM Algorithm in General

Guess what you cannot see, then fit as if you could

Many models are easy to fit if only you knew one missing fact about each data point: which cluster it came from, what the blank entry was, what the weights were. The EM algorithm turns that wish into a method. It guesses the missing facts softly from the current model (E), fits the model as if those guesses were data (M), and repeats, and each round can only raise the likelihood. On the right, EM is learning three “template digits” from 603 real handwritten 2s, 3s and 4s, starting from pure noise and never told which digit is which. Press New start to watch it again from different noise.

The trick
Replace each hidden value by its expectation under the current model, then maximise as if the data were complete
The guarantee
Each round maximises a lower bound that touches the log-likelihood, so the likelihood never goes down
In practice
Mixture models, missing data, hidden Markov models, topic models, and tuning Bayesian hyperparameters

The idea

Hidden labels and the complete data

Sixteen numbers sit on a line below. We believe each one came from one of two bell curves (width 1, equally likely) and want the two centres μ1 and μ2. If someone told us which bell made each point, the job would be trivial: average each group. Nobody tells us. The bell that made point n is a latent (hidden) variable zn, and the data we wish we had, the pairs (xn, zn), is the complete data.

Hidden variables are not just a nuisance. A model with a well-chosen hidden cause can be far simpler than one that links every visible variable to every other: in Russell and Norvig’s heart-disease network, keeping the hidden “disease” node needs 78 parameters, while removing it and wiring the symptoms to each other directly needs 708. The price is that the hidden values must be dealt with during learning, and EM is the standard way to do that.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(x\mid\boldsymbol\theta) &= \sum_{k=1}^{K} p(x, z{=}k\mid\boldsymbol\theta) \\ &= \sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x\mid\mu_k,\sigma^2) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the model first picks a hidden label z = k with probability πk, then draws x from bell k. We never see z, so the probability of x adds up over every label it might have had. Here K = 2, π1 = π2 = ½ and σ = 1, so θ is just the pair of means.
Say it“p of x given theta is the sum over k of p of x and z equals k, which is the sum of pi-k times a normal density with mean mu-k.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\ln p(X\mid\boldsymbol\theta) \\ &\quad= \sum_{n=1}^{N}\ln\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x_n\mid\mu_k,\sigma^2) \\ &\ln p(X,Z\mid\boldsymbol\theta) \\ &\quad= \sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K} z_{nk}\big[\ln\pi_k \\ &\qquad\quad + \ln\mathcal{N}(x_n\mid\mu_k,\sigma^2)\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the top line is what we can actually compute: a log of a sum, which ties every mean to every other. The bottom line is the complete-data log-likelihood, where znk is 1 if point n came from bell k and 0 otherwise. The log now lands directly on each bell, the sum breaks into separate pieces, and each μk is maximised by the plain mean of its own points.
Say it“The log-likelihood is the sum over points of the log of a sum over bells. The complete-data log-likelihood is the sum over points and bells of z-n-k times log pi-k plus log of the normal density.”

Switch between the three surfaces. With labels hidden the surface has two equal peaks (swap the bells’ names and nothing changes) and a ridge between them. With labels known it is a single round bowl. The third surface is the clever one: we still don’t know the labels, but we replace each znk by its expected value under the dot’s parameters. The result is again a single bowl, easy to maximise, and its top is where EM moves next.

The engine

EM maximises a lower bound that touches

Why should maximising an expected log-likelihood help with the real one? Because of one exact identity. Take any distribution q(Z) over the hidden labels. The log-likelihood splits into a lower bound and a gap, and the gap is a KL divergence (chapter 0.8), which is never negative. A new, simpler model shows it best: fifteen points, one bell pinned at 1.6 and one free bell whose centre θ we must find. Press E-step, then M-step, and repeat; then move the start to the far right and do it again.

\[ \begin{gathered} \ln p(X\mid\theta) = \mathcal{L}(q,\theta) + \mathrm{KL}(q\,\Vert\,p) \\ \mathcal{L}(q,\theta) = \textstyle\sum_{Z} q(Z)\ln\dfrac{p(X,Z\mid\theta)}{q(Z)} \\ \mathrm{KL} = -\textstyle\sum_{Z} q(Z)\ln\dfrac{p(Z\mid X,\theta)}{q(Z)} \\ \mathrm{KL}(q\Vert p) \ge 0 \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: whatever q you choose, ℒ plus the KL gap adds up exactly to the log-likelihood. KL is zero only when q equals the true posterior over the labels, p(Z | X, θ). So ℒ is always a lower bound on ln p(X | θ), and it touches when q is the posterior.
Say it“Log p of X given theta equals script-L of q and theta plus the KL divergence from q to the posterior.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{E:}\quad & q(Z) \leftarrow p(Z\mid X,\boldsymbol\theta^{\text{old}}) \\ \text{M:}\quad & \boldsymbol\theta^{\text{new}} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol\theta}\mathcal{L}(q,\boldsymbol\theta) \\ & \phantom{\boldsymbol\theta^{\text{new}}} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol\theta}\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol\theta^{\text{old}}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the E-step makes the gap zero, so the bound touches the curve at θold. The M-step climbs to the top of the bound. Since ℒ = 𝒬(θ, θold) + (entropy of q), and the entropy does not involve θ, climbing ℒ means maximising the expected complete-data log-likelihood 𝒬. Then ln p(θnew) ≥ ℒ(q, θnew) ≥ ℒ(q, θold) = ln p(θold): the likelihood cannot fall.
Say it“E-step: set q to the posterior over Z at the old theta. M-step: the new theta maximises script-L, which is the same as maximising Q of theta and theta-old.”

Case 1

Gaussian mixtures revisited

Chapter 1.12 fitted Gaussian mixtures by EM as a recipe. Now we can see where the recipe comes from. With the labels known, the complete-data log-likelihood splits into one ordinary Gaussian fit per cluster. With the labels hidden, the E-step replaces each znk by its expectation, the responsibility γ(znk), and the M-step is the same closed-form fit with every point shared out by those weights. The data below are 150 synthetic points from three known Gaussians, so we can also peek at the truth. Step E and M one at a time and watch the two numbers in the trace.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol\theta^{\text{old}}) &= \sum_{n}\sum_{k}\gamma(z_{nk})\big[\ln\pi_k \\ &\quad + \ln\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the complete-data log-likelihood with each unknown 0/1 label replaced by its expected value, γ(znk) = p(znk = 1 | xn, θold). It has exactly the shape of the labels-known case, so it is just as easy to maximise.
Say it“Q is the sum over points and components of gamma-n-k times log pi-k plus the log Gaussian density.”
\[ \begin{aligned} N_k &= \textstyle\sum_n \gamma(z_{nk}), \qquad \pi_k = N_k/N \\ \boldsymbol\mu_k &= \tfrac{1}{N_k}\textstyle\sum_n\gamma(z_{nk})\,\mathbf{x}_n \\ \boldsymbol\Sigma_k &= \tfrac{1}{N_k}\textstyle\sum_n\gamma(z_{nk}) \\ &\quad\times(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^{\!\top} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with known labels (γ = 0 or 1) these are each group’s share, sample mean and sample covariance. With soft labels a point counts towards every component in proportion to its responsibility.
Say it“N-k is the total responsibility of component k; pi-k is N-k over N; mu-k and Sigma-k are the responsibility-weighted mean and covariance.”

A limit

k-means is EM in the cold

Chapter 1.12 called EM a “soft k-means”. Here is the exact connection. Give every component the same round covariance εI, fix the mixing weights, and let EM update only the means. As ε shrinks, the nearest centre’s term swamps all the others, responsibilities snap to 0 or 1, and EM becomes k-means. Lower the temperature ε and watch the soft colours harden; drag the three hollow starting rings to try other starts.

\[ \begin{aligned} \gamma(z_{nk}) &= \frac{\pi_k\, e^{-\lVert\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k\rVert^2/2\epsilon}}{\sum_j\pi_j\, e^{-\lVert\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_j\rVert^2/2\epsilon}} \\ &\xrightarrow{\ \epsilon\to0\ }\; r_{nk}\in\{0,1\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: divide top and bottom by the nearest centre’s term. Every other term carries a factor exp(−(gap in squared distance)/2ε), which vanishes as ε → 0. So the nearest centre takes responsibility 1 and the rest 0, whatever the πk (as long as none is zero): k-means’ hard assignment.
Say it“Gamma-n-k is pi-k times e to the minus the squared distance over two epsilon, normalised; as epsilon goes to zero it becomes the 0-or-1 indicator r-n-k.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_Z\big[\ln p(X,Z\mid\boldsymbol\mu)\big] \\ &\quad\to -\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\sum_{n}\sum_{k} r_{nk}\lVert\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k\rVert^2 \\ &\qquad + \text{const} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: in the same limit the expected complete-data log-likelihood is, up to a constant and a scale, minus k-means’ distortion J. Maximising it over the means gives each centre the plain average of its assigned points, which is k-means’ update step.
Say it“The expected complete-data log-likelihood tends to minus one over two epsilon times the sum of r-n-k times the squared distance, plus a constant.”

Case 2

Mixtures of Bernoulli distributions

Not all data are continuous. A black-and-white image is a vector of D bits, and the simplest model for it gives every pixel its own coin: pixel i is on with probability μi, independently of the rest. Independence is the problem: a single set of coins cannot say “if this pixel of a stroke is on, its neighbours are probably on too”. A mixture of Bernoullis can. Below, 200 noisy 5×5 images are made from three hidden templates (a bar across, a bar down, a diagonal) with each pixel flipped at random. Choose K and press Run EM; then try K = 1 and compare the covariance maps.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu) &= \prod_{i=1}^{D}\mu_i^{x_i}(1-\mu_i)^{1-x_i} \\ p(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\pi) &= \sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,p(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for one template, multiply μi for every pixel that is on and 1 − μi for every pixel that is off. The mixture lets K templates take turns, template k being used a fraction πk of the time.
Say it“p of x given mu is the product over pixels of mu-i to the x-i times one minus mu-i to the one minus x-i; the mixture sums pi-k times that for each template.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}] &= \textstyle\sum_k\pi_k\boldsymbol\mu_k \\ \operatorname{cov}[\mathbf{x}] &= \textstyle\sum_k\pi_k\big(\boldsymbol\Sigma_k+\boldsymbol\mu_k\boldsymbol\mu_k^{\!\top}\big) \\ &\quad - \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}]\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}]^{\!\top} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Σk = diag(μki(1 − μki)) is diagonal: within one template, pixels are independent. But the mixture’s covariance is not diagonal. Pixels that are on together in one template and off together in another become correlated, which is how a mixture captures strokes.
Say it“The mean is the pi-weighted average of the templates; the covariance is the pi-weighted sum of Sigma-k plus mu-k mu-k-transpose, minus the mean times its transpose.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \gamma(z_{nk}) &= \frac{\pi_k\,p(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k)}{\sum_j\pi_j\,p(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j)} \\ \boldsymbol\mu_k &= \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_n\gamma(z_{nk})\,\mathbf{x}_n \\ \pi_k &= N_k/N \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same two steps as for Gaussians. E: how much each template explains each image. M: each template becomes the responsibility-weighted average image. Because every p(x | μ) is at most 1, the likelihood is bounded: no component can collapse onto one point the way a Gaussian can.
Say it“Gamma-n-k is pi-k times the template's probability of image n, normalised over templates. The new template is the gamma-weighted mean image.”
\[ \boldsymbol\mu_k^{\text{MAP}} = \frac{\sum_n\gamma(z_{nk})\,\mathbf{x}_n + (a-1)}{N_k + a + b - 2} \]
Read it as: the maximum-likelihood template can put μki at exactly 0 or 1 when a pixel is always off (or on) in that cluster, and then any new image that disagrees at that pixel gets probability zero. A Beta(a, b) prior on each μki fixes this: with a = b = 2 it adds one imaginary “on” and one “off” to every pixel count. EM then climbs the log-posterior instead of the log-likelihood.
Say it“The MAP template is the weighted count of on-pixels plus a minus one, over N-k plus a plus b minus two.”

Case 3

EM for Bayesian linear regression

The hidden variable need not be a label. In Bayesian linear regression (chapter 1.2d) the weights w have a Gaussian prior with precision α, and the targets have noise precision β. Chapter 1.2e chose α and β by maximising the evidence p(t | α, β) with MacKay’s fixed-point updates. EM reaches the same answer by a different road: treat w as the hidden variable, whose posterior we can compute exactly. The data are 100 noisy samples of sin(2πx); the model has nine Gaussian bumps and a constant. Click the map to choose a start, then press Run.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{E:}\quad \mathbf{m}_N &= \beta\,\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol\Phi^{\!\top}\mathbf{t} \\ \mathbf{S}_N^{-1} &= \alpha\mathbf{I} + \beta\,\boldsymbol\Phi^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\Phi \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the E-step is the posterior over the weights at the current α and β: a Gaussian with mean mN and covariance SN, exactly as in chapter 1.2d. All the M-step needs from it are E[w] = mN and E[ww⊤] = mNmN⊤ + SN.
Say it“m-N is beta times S-N times Phi-transpose t; the inverse of S-N is alpha times the identity plus beta Phi-transpose Phi.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}}\big[\ln p(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{w}\mid\alpha,\beta)\big] \\ &\quad= \tfrac{M}{2}\ln\tfrac{\alpha}{2\pi} - \tfrac{\alpha}{2}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\mathbf{w}] \\ &\qquad + \tfrac{N}{2}\ln\tfrac{\beta}{2\pi} \\ &\qquad - \tfrac{\beta}{2}\textstyle\sum_n\mathbb{E}\big[(t_n-\mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\phi_n)^2\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the complete-data log-likelihood is the log prior of w plus the log-likelihood of t given w; take its expectation under the E-step’s posterior. α appears only in the first line and β only in the second, so each is maximised on its own.
Say it“The expected complete-data log-likelihood is M over two log alpha over two pi, minus alpha over two times the expected squared length of w, plus the same pair of terms for beta and the residuals.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{M:}\quad \alpha &= \frac{M}{\mathbf{m}_N^{\!\top}\mathbf{m}_N + \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{S}_N)} \\ \frac{1}{\beta} &= \frac{1}{N}\Big(\lVert\mathbf{t}-\boldsymbol\Phi\mathbf{m}_N\rVert^2 \\ &\qquad + \operatorname{Tr}\big(\boldsymbol\Phi^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\Phi\,\mathbf{S}_N\big)\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: 1/α becomes the average expected squared weight, counting both the mean weights and their uncertainty; 1/β becomes the average expected squared residual, again including the uncertainty in w. MacKay’s update is α = γ/mN⊤mN with γ = M − αTr(SN): a different formula with the same fixed point, because both climb the same evidence surface.
Say it“Alpha is M over m-N-squared plus the trace of S-N. One over beta is one over N times the residual sum of squares plus the trace of Phi-transpose Phi S-N.”

In general

The general recipe, and why it never goes downhill

Strip away the examples and EM is one line. Russell and Norvig write it with Z the hidden variables and x the observed values; Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman call the observed data Z, the missing part Zm, and the complete data T = (Z, Zm). The notation changes; the idea does not.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\boldsymbol\theta^{(i+1)} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol\theta} \\ &\quad\sum_{\mathbf{z}} P(\mathbf{Z}{=}\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\theta^{(i)}) \\ &\qquad\times\ln P(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Z}{=}\mathbf{z}\mid\boldsymbol\theta) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: average the complete-data log-likelihood over every possible value of the hidden variables, weighting each by how probable it is under the current parameters (E), then choose the parameters that maximise that average (M). Mixtures, hidden Markov models (Baum–Welch), Bayesian networks with hidden nodes and missing entries are all this line with a different model inside.
Say it“Theta i-plus-one is the argmax over theta of the sum over z of the probability of z given x and theta-i, times log P of x and z given theta.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\ell(\theta';\mathbf{Z}) = Q(\theta',\theta) - R(\theta',\theta) \\ &R(\theta',\theta) = \\ &\quad \mathbb{E}\big[\ln\Pr(\mathbf{Z}^m\mid\mathbf{Z},\theta')\,\big|\,\mathbf{Z},\theta\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ℓ is the observed-data log-likelihood. Write the complete-data likelihood as observed part times missing-given-observed, take logs and average over Zm at the current θ: ℓ equals Q (the expected complete-data log-likelihood) minus R (the expected log-probability of the missing data). By Jensen’s inequality, R(θ′, θ) is largest at θ′ = θ.
Say it“Ell of theta-prime equals Q of theta-prime and theta minus R of theta-prime and theta, where R is the expected log conditional density of the missing data.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\ell(\theta') - \ell(\theta) \\ &= \underbrace{\big[Q(\theta',\theta) - Q(\theta,\theta)\big]}_{\ge0 \text{ after the M-step}} \\ &+ \underbrace{\big[R(\theta,\theta) - R(\theta',\theta)\big]}_{\ge0 \text{ always}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the gain in log-likelihood is the gain in Q plus a term that is never negative. So any step that raises Q raises ℓ at least as much. Full maximisation is not required: generalised EM (GEM) only asks that Q increase, and ECM maximises over blocks of parameters in turn. With a prior, maximise Q(θ, θold) + ln p(θ) to climb the posterior (MAP-EM).
Say it“The change in ell is the change in Q plus R at theta minus R at theta-prime, and both brackets are non-negative.”

Here the recipe meets plain missing data. Sixty points come from a bivariate normal, but the y value has been lost for the points with the largest x (as if a sensor saturated). Throwing those points away biases the mean of y, because the lost ones were not a random sample. EM keeps them: the E-step fills in each lost y by its conditional expectation and its conditional variance; the M-step refits the mean and covariance. Raise the share of lost values, then Run; switch to ECM to see a partial M-step still climb.

Another view

EM as maximisation–maximisation

Neal and Hinton (1998) gave EM a symmetric reading. Define one function F of two arguments: the model parameters θ′, and a distribution P̃ over the missing data. EM is then coordinate ascent on F: the E-step maximises over P̃ with θ′ held fixed, the M-step maximises over θ′ with P̃ held fixed. Back to the fifteen points and one free bell. To draw F on a page, let P̃ be the posterior over labels computed at some value φ; then F(φ, θ) is a surface. Click anywhere to start, then step.

\[ \begin{aligned} F(\theta',\tilde P) &= \mathbb{E}_{\tilde P}\big[\ell_0(\theta';\mathbf{T})\big] \\ &\quad - \mathbb{E}_{\tilde P}\big[\ln\tilde P(\mathbf{Z}^m)\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ℓ0 is the complete-data log-likelihood. F is its average under P̃ plus the entropy of P̃: exactly Bishop’s bound ℒ(q, θ) with q = P̃. For fixed θ′, F is largest when P̃ is the true posterior Pr(Zm | Z, θ′), and there F equals the log-likelihood ℓ(θ′).
Say it“F of theta-prime and P-tilde is the expected complete-data log-likelihood under P-tilde, minus the expected log of P-tilde.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{E:}\quad \tilde P^{(j)} &= \arg\max_{\tilde P}F(\theta^{(j)},\tilde P) \\ \text{M:}\quad \theta^{(j+1)} &= \arg\max_{\theta'}F(\theta',\tilde P^{(j)}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: two players take turns raising the same score. Because each move can only raise F, and F = ℓ after every E-step, the log-likelihood never falls. The view also licenses shortcuts: any move that raises F is allowed, including updating P̃ for just one data point.
Say it“E: P-tilde j is the argmax of F over P-tilde at theta j. M: theta j-plus-one is the argmax of F over theta-prime at P-tilde j.”

Incremental EM: update one point at a time

The max–max view says a partial E-step is fine: refresh the responsibilities of a single point, and F still rises. For mixtures the M-step can then be patched cheaply, because the parameters depend on the data only through running sums. Bishop gives the update for a mean when point m’s responsibility changes. Race the two versions on the 150 points from the Gaussian-mixture section, from the same start.

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta_{mk} &= \gamma^{\text{new}}(z_{mk}) - \gamma^{\text{old}}(z_{mk}) \\ N_k^{\text{new}} &= N_k^{\text{old}} + \Delta_{mk} \\ \boldsymbol\mu_k^{\text{new}} &= \boldsymbol\mu_k^{\text{old}} + \frac{\Delta_{mk}}{N_k^{\text{new}}}\big(\mathbf{x}_m-\boldsymbol\mu_k^{\text{old}}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: when point m’s responsibility for component k changes, only its share of the running totals changes, so Nk and μk shift by that difference, Δmk, alone; covariances and mixing weights are patched the same way. One sweep through the data costs the same as one batch round, but the parameters improve after every point instead of once per sweep.
Say it“Delta-m-k is the change in point m's responsibility; N-k gains Delta-m-k, and mu-k moves towards x-m by Delta-m-k over the new N-k.”

Real-world project

Project: clustering handwritten digits with a Bernoulli mixture

MNIST (LeCun, Cortes & Burges) is the classic set of 28×28 handwritten digits, collected from American census staff and high-school students. We take the first 2,000 training images and the first 1,000 test images and make every pixel black or white (on if its grey level is 128 or more), so each image is a vector of 784 bits. The labels are never shown to EM; we use them only afterwards, to see what the clusters found. Everything below runs in your browser, and a NumPy version of the same algorithm, started from the same random numbers, gives the same results.

Fill in the missing half

A latent-variable model can answer questions about pixels it cannot see. Hide the bottom half of a test digit. The visible half gives each template a responsibility (an E-step that uses only the visible pixels), and the best guess for each hidden pixel is the responsibility-weighted average of the templates there. It is EM’s E-step used for prediction.

Dream up new digits

A mixture is a generative model: pick a template with probability πk, then toss each pixel’s coin. The samples show both what the model knows and what it does not. Strokes appear in the right places, but each pixel is tossed independently once the template is chosen, so the strokes come out speckled.

The same project in Python

import gzip, numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score

def load(img, lab, n):                       # MNIST (LeCun, Cortes & Burges), binarised at 128
    X = np.frombuffer(gzip.open(img).read(), np.uint8, offset=16).reshape(-1, 784)[:n]
    y = np.frombuffer(gzip.open(lab).read(), np.uint8, offset=8)[:n]
    return (X > 127).astype(float), y
Xtr, ytr = load("train-images-idx3-ubyte.gz", "train-labels-idx1-ubyte.gz", 2000)
Xte, yte = load("t10k-images-idx3-ubyte.gz", "t10k-labels-idx1-ubyte.gz", 1000)

def mulberry32(a):                           # the page's random numbers, bit for bit
    def r():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = (a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + ((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return r

def e_step(X, mu, pi):                       # responsibilities and ln p(x_n); ln 0 stands in as -1e30
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore"):
        lm, l1m, lpi = np.log(mu), np.log1p(-mu), np.log(pi)
    lm[np.isinf(lm)] = -1e30; l1m[np.isinf(l1m)] = -1e30; lpi[np.isinf(lpi)] = -1e30
    L = X @ lm.T + (1 - X) @ l1m.T + lpi
    m = L.max(1, keepdims=True); lse = m[:, 0] + np.log(np.exp(L - m).sum(1))
    return np.exp(L - lse[:, None]), np.where(lse < -1e29, -np.inf, lse)

def em(X, K, seed=1, init="digits", prior=True, tol=1e-5, max_iter=300):
    r = mulberry32(seed)
    if init == "noise": mu = 0.25 + 0.5 * np.array([r() for _ in range(K * 784)]).reshape(K, 784)
    else:                                    # K distinct random training digits, softened
        idx = []
        while len(idx) < K:
            i = int(r() * len(X)); idx += [i] if i not in idx else []
        mu = 0.25 + 0.5 * X[idx]
    pi, old = np.full(K, 1 / K), -np.inf
    for it in range(max_iter):
        g, lse = e_step(X, mu, pi)           # E-step
        if abs(lse.mean() - old) < tol: break
        old = lse.mean()
        Nk = g.sum(0); pi = Nk / len(X)      # M-step; a Beta(2,2) prior adds 1 and 2
        mu = (g.T @ X + prior) / (Nk[:, None] + 2 * prior)
    return mu, pi, it, lse.mean()

def report(K, **kw):
    mu, pi, it, ll = em(Xtr, K, **kw)
    g, _ = e_step(Xtr, mu, pi); gt, lt = e_step(Xte, mu, pi); hard = gt.argmax(1)
    names = np.array([np.argmax([g[ytr == c, k].sum() for c in range(10)]) for k in range(K)])
    bad = np.isinf(lt).sum()

          + f", accuracy {np.mean(names[hard] == yte):.3f}, ARI {adjusted_rand_score(yte, hard):.4f}")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • EM fits models with hidden variables: the E-step computes the posterior over the hidden values at the current parameters; the M-step maximises the expected complete-data log-likelihood 𝒬(θ, θold).
  • ln p(X | θ) = ℒ(q, θ) + KL(q ‖ p): the E-step makes the bound touch, the M-step climbs it, so the likelihood never falls, but EM only finds a local maximum and the start matters.
  • Gaussian mixtures, Bernoulli mixtures, missing data and Bayesian regression hyperparameters are all the same recipe; k-means is the limit of a Gaussian mixture as the shared variance goes to zero.
  • Any step that raises 𝒬 is enough (GEM, ECM), a prior turns it into MAP-EM, and the max–max view (F = expected complete-data log-likelihood + entropy) justifies incremental, one-point-at-a-time updates.
  • Bernoulli mixtures need a prior to avoid zero probabilities; on 2,000 MNIST digits, 10 templates found without labels match the true digit about half the time.

Check your understanding

Right after an E-step, what are ℒ(q, θ) and KL(q ‖ p) at the current θ?

q is the exact posterior p(Z | X, θ), so the KL divergence is 0 and ℒ(q, θ) equals ln p(X | θ): the bound touches the log-likelihood.

Why is maximising ℒ(q, θ) over θ the same as maximising 𝒬(θ, θold)?

ℒ(q, θ) = ΣZ q(Z) ln p(X, Z | θ) − ΣZ q(Z) ln q(Z). The first term is 𝒬; the second is the entropy of q, which does not depend on θ.

EM for a mixture has converged. Has it found the maximum-likelihood estimate?

Not necessarily. EM stops at a stationary point, usually a local maximum, and which one depends on the start (as the free-bell example and the MNIST starts show). Run it from several starts and keep the best likelihood.

A Bernoulli mixture fitted by maximum likelihood gives some test images probability exactly zero. Why, and what fixes it?

A pixel that is never on among a template’s training images gets μki = 0, so any image with that pixel on is impossible under that template; if that holds for every template, the image gets probability 0. A Beta prior (MAP-EM) keeps every μki strictly between 0 and 1.

In EM for Bayesian regression, why does the α update add Tr(SN) to the squared mean weights?

The M-step needs the expected squared length of w under its posterior, E[w⊤w] = mN⊤mN + Tr(SN): the squared mean plus the total posterior variance. Using only the mean would ignore how uncertain the weights are.
