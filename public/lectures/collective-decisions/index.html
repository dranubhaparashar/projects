Cooperation, Auctions, Voting & Bargaining

How self-interested agents reach one decision together

In 2009, 8,980 voters in Burlington, Vermont, ranked the candidates for mayor. Counted by instant runoff, the method the city used, those ballots elect Bob Kiss. Count the very same ballots by plurality and Kurt Wright wins; give Borda’s points, or ask who beats every rival head to head, and Andy Montroll wins. Nobody changed a vote: the rule decided. This chapter is about such rules, the mechanisms that turn many agents’ interests into one outcome: sharing what a coalition earns, handing out tasks, selling scarce goods, voting and haggling. Cooperative games

Coalitions: who teams up with whom

The previous chapter treated agents who each choose alone. Cooperative game theory assumes agents can make binding agreements, so the questions change: which groups will form, and how will each group share what it earns? Here is the standard model. A set N of n players, and a characteristic function v that gives, for every group C ⊆ N, the value v(C) that group can secure by itself. Value is assumed to be transferable, like money, so a group can split it any way it likes. A group is a coalition; N itself is the grand coalition.

An outcome of the game has two parts. A coalition structure CS splits the players into disjoint coalitions, each player in exactly one (a player alone is a coalition of one). A payoff vector x then says what each player receives, and it must be feasible: each coalition hands out exactly what it earned. The total value created, V(CS), is the social welfare of the structure.

Take three freelancers, A, B and C, who can bid for projects alone, in pairs or all together. The numbers are what each team could earn, in thousands of pounds. If merging two disjoint teams never loses value, the game is superadditive, and then the grand coalition always creates the most; but rivalry, overheads or clashing styles can break that, and then a split structure can be best. Change what each team can earn, and find the structure that creates the most value.

\[ \begin{aligned} &v : 2^N \to \mathbb{R}, \quad v(\varnothing) = 0 \\ &V(CS) = \textstyle\sum_{C \in CS} v(C) \\ &\textstyle\sum_{i \in C} x_i = v(C) \\ &\qquad \text{for each } C \in CS \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: v assigns a number to every one of the 2n possible groups (the empty group earns nothing). A structure’s welfare V(CS) adds up the values of its coalitions. A payoff vector is feasible when every coalition in the structure shares out exactly its own value, no more and no less.
Say it“v maps each subset of N to a real number, v of the empty set is zero; V of CS is the sum of v of C over the coalitions in CS; and in each coalition the payoffs add up to v of C.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &v(C \cup D) \ge v(C) + v(D) \\ &\qquad \text{for all disjoint } C, D \subseteq N \\ &\text{imputation: } \textstyle\sum_{i} x_i = v(N), \\ &\qquad x_i \ge v(\{i\}) \text{ for every } i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: superadditivity says two separate teams never earn less by merging. If it holds, the grand coalition reaches the highest possible welfare and the only question left is how to share v(N). A sharing of v(N) in which nobody gets less than they could earn alone is called an imputation.
Say it“v of C union D is at least v of C plus v of D whenever C and D don’t overlap; an imputation splits v of N so that each player gets at least v of i alone.”

Stability and fairness

Who gets what: the core and the Shapley value

Suppose the grand coalition forms. How should it share v(N)? There are two classic answers, one about stability and one about fairness. Stability first. If some coalition C is paid less in total than the v(C) it could earn alone, its members can walk out, share v(C) among themselves, and every one of them can be better off: C blocks the payoff. The core is the set of payoffs that no coalition blocks. A payoff in the core is self-enforcing; one outside it invites a breakaway.

The core can be empty. In the majority game any two of three players can claim the whole prize; whatever split you propose, the two worst-paid players together receive less than the prize and would rather go it alone. When the core is not empty it is often a whole region, so it says which payoffs are stable but not which one to choose.

Lloyd Shapley’s answer picks exactly one payoff. Imagine the players arriving one by one in a random order; each is credited with its marginal contribution, how much the value of the coalition grows when it joins. The Shapley value φi is that contribution averaged over all n! arrival orders. It is the only sharing rule that satisfies four axioms together: efficiency (the shares add up to v(N)), symmetry (two players who contribute the same to every coalition get the same), the null player rule (a player who adds nothing to any coalition gets nothing) and additivity (the value of two games played together is the sum of their values). It always exists, but it need not be stable: it can lie outside the core.

In the triangle below every point is one way to share a prize of 12 among A, B and C (the closer to a corner, the more that player gets). Players earn nothing alone here, so every point is an imputation. Drag the white point to propose a split, and see which coalitions would walk out; then try the glove market and the majority game.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{core} = \Big\{\, x : \ &\textstyle\sum_{i \in N} x_i = v(N), \\ &\textstyle\sum_{i \in C} x_i \ge v(C) \\ &\text{for all } C \subseteq N \,\Big\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the core holds every way of sharing out all of v(N) in which each coalition, including each single player, receives in total at least what it could earn on its own. The gap e(C, x) = v(C) − x(C) is the coalition’s excess: a positive excess means C blocks x.
Say it“The core is the set of x whose entries sum to v of N, such that for every coalition C the sum of x i over C is at least v of C.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \phi_i &= \frac{1}{n!} \sum_{\text{orders } o} \big[ v(P^o_i \cup \{i\}) \\ &\qquad\qquad - v(P^o_i) \big] \\ &= \sum_{C \subseteq N \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|C|!\,(n-|C|-1)!}{n!} \\ &\qquad \times \big[ v(C \cup \{i\}) - v(C) \big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Poi is the set of players who arrive before i in order o. Average i’s marginal contribution over all n! orders. The second line groups the orders by who came before i: a given set C precedes i in |C|!(n − |C| − 1)! of them.
Say it“Phi i is one over n factorial times the sum over orders of v of the predecessors plus i, minus v of the predecessors.”

Computation

When the numbers explode: computing with coalitions

Written out in full, a characteristic function needs 2n numbers: about a million for 20 agents, about 1015 for 50. Real systems therefore use compact representations, and the interesting question is which questions stay easy under which representation. Three are standard.

In an induced subgraph game (Deng and Papadimitriou, 1994) the players are the nodes of a weighted graph and a coalition earns the total weight of the edges inside it: think of pairs of agents who work well (positive weight) or badly (negative weight) together. The Shapley value is then trivial, half the weight of a player’s edges, yet deciding whether a given payoff is in the core is co-NP-complete. Marginal contribution nets (Ieong and Shoham, 2005) generalise this: the game is a list of rules “if the coalition contains these players (and perhaps lacks those), add this much”. An edge is a rule with two players. The Shapley value can be computed rule by rule, in time linear in the number of rules; a rule that only names players who must be present simply splits its value equally among them. In a weighted voting game each player has a weight wi and a coalition wins (value 1) when its weights reach a quota q. Only n + 1 numbers describe the game, but computing the Shapley value, here called the Shapley–Shubik power index, is #P-hard; in practice it is estimated by sampling random arrival orders.

Choosing the best coalition structure is hard as well. The number of structures is the Bell number Bn: 203 for 6 agents, 115,975 for 10, about 5.2 × 1013 for 20. Dynamic programming over subsets finds the optimum in O(3n) steps, still exponential. Sandholm and colleagues (1999) showed an anytime guarantee: once you have searched every structure with at most two coalitions (2n−1 of them), the best one found is worth at least 1/n of the optimum, provided no coalition has negative value, and no search of fewer structures can guarantee any bound at all. Click edges to change how well pairs work together (each click moves the weight one step; the seg buttons choose the direction), and compare the true optimum with the two-level search.

\[ \begin{aligned} v(C) &= \textstyle\sum_{\{i,j\} \subseteq C} w_{ij} \\ \phi_i &= \tfrac{1}{2} \textstyle\sum_{j \ne i} w_{ij} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a coalition earns the weights of all edges with both ends inside it. In any arrival order, an edge’s weight is credited to whichever of its two ends arrives second, and by symmetry each end is second half the time, so each player’s Shapley value is half the weight of its own edges.
Say it“v of C is the sum of w i j over pairs inside C; phi i is one half the sum over j of w i j.”
\[ \begin{aligned} B_{n+1} &= \textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} B_k \\ B_0 &= 1 \\ V(CS^*) &\le n \cdot \!\!\max_{|CS| \le 2} V(CS) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: to split n + 1 agents, choose which k of the other n share nothing with the newcomer’s coalition and split those k in Bk ways. The bound: the best structure CS* has at most n coalitions, each worth at most the best single coalition C, and C appears in the two-coalition structure {C, N − C}, which is worth at least v(C) when values are non-negative.
Say it“B n-plus-one is the sum over k of n choose k times B k; the optimum is at most n times the best structure with one or two coalitions.”

Power in a weighted vote, exact and sampled

A weighted voting game looks simple, yet weight is not power. A player whose weight never turns a losing coalition into a winning one is a null player with no power at all, however heavy it is; and two players with very different weights can be equally powerful. The exact Shapley–Shubik index needs all n! orders (120 here). The sampled estimate uses M random orders, and since each marginal contribution is 0 or 1, its standard error is at most 1/(2√M). Change the weights and the quota, then shrink the sample and watch the estimate wobble.

\[ \begin{aligned} v(C) &= \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \sum_{i \in C} w_i \ge q \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \\ \hat\phi_i &= \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \big[ v(P^{o_m}_i \cup \{i\}) \\ &\qquad\qquad - v(P^{o_m}_i) \big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a coalition wins when its weights reach the quota. Player i’s power is the fraction of arrival orders in which i is pivotal, turning the coalition from losing to winning; the estimate counts that over M random orders o1, …, oM.

Allocating tasks

The contract net: every task to the cheapest bidder

A team of agents has jobs to share out, and each agent knows its own costs better than anyone else does. The contract net protocol (Smith, 1980) borrows the way firms hire contractors. A manager announces a task with a description of what is needed. Each potential contractor works out what doing it would cost given everything it has already taken on, and sends a bid. The manager awards the task to the best bid, and the winner may itself become a manager and subcontract parts. Nobody needs a global view: knowledge stays where it lives, and the protocol keeps working as agents join or leave.

Here three delivery vans, each starting and ending at its own depot, share out parcels. A van’s cost is the length of the shortest round trip through its parcels, and its bid for a new parcel is the marginal cost: how much longer that round trip gets. Parcels are announced one at a time and never revisited, so the result is greedy and depends on the order. Re-contracting (Sandholm, 1993) lets agents put awarded tasks back on the market and keep any trade that lowers the total, a local search that often, though not always, closes part of the gap to the optimum. Finding the true optimum is a vehicle routing problem, NP-hard in general; with at most 8 parcels this page can afford to try all 38 assignments. Step through the announcements, then re-contract; drag a depot or click the map to add and remove parcels.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{bid}_i(t) &= c_i(T_i \cup \{t\}) - c_i(T_i) \\ \text{award}(t) &= \arg\min_i \ \text{bid}_i(t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: agent i already holds the tasks Ti, which cost it ci(Ti). Its honest bid for task t is the extra cost of adding t, and the manager awards t to the agent with the lowest bid. Each award is the cheapest choice at that moment, which is not the same as the cheapest plan overall.

Allocating scarce resources

Auctions: English, Dutch, sealed-bid and Vickrey

When something is scarce, an auction decides who gets it and at what price. Each bidder i has a private value vi, what the item is worth to them, and chooses a bidding strategy. The seller, or the designer of the rules, cares about four things: efficiency (the item goes to whoever values it most), revenue, whether bidders have a dominant strategy (a best way to bid whatever the others do), and above all whether the rules make bidding one’s true value the best strategy, so-called truth-revealing or strategy-proof rules.

In an English auction the price rises in steps of d and bidders drop out until one is left. The dominant strategy is simple: stay in while the price is below your value. So the highest-value bidder wins and pays about the second-highest value, plus at most one step: efficient, as long as d is small. Its costs are that everyone must take part in real time, and that rising bids make collusion easy to signal. A Dutch auction runs the clock downwards and the first bidder to call out wins at that price. That is strategically the same as a first-price sealed-bid auction: each bidder writes one bid, highest bid wins and pays it. Bidding your value there guarantees zero profit, so bidders shade; when the n values are independent and uniform on [0, 100], the symmetric equilibrium bid is (n − 1)/n of your value. In a Vickrey auction (sealed-bid second-price, Vickrey 1961) the highest bid wins but pays the second-highest bid. Your bid then only decides whether you win, never what you pay, and bidding exactly your value is a dominant strategy.

Which raises more money? The revenue equivalence theorem says that, for risk-neutral bidders with independent private values, any auction in which the highest value always wins and a bidder with the lowest possible value expects nothing gives the seller the same expected revenue. Drag the bars to set five bidders’ values, pick a format and press Run.

\[ \begin{aligned} b^{(2)} &= \textstyle\max_{j \ne i} b_j \\ u_i &= \begin{cases} v_i - b^{(2)} & \text{if } b_i > b^{(2)} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: in a Vickrey auction the winner’s profit is its value minus the best rival bid, which it cannot affect. Bidding above vi only adds wins at a price above your value (a loss); bidding below only gives up wins that would have been profitable. So bi = vi is best whatever the others do.
Say it“u i is v i minus the highest rival bid if you win, and zero otherwise.”
\[ \begin{aligned} b^{\text{1st}}(v) &= \tfrac{n-1}{n}\, v \\ \mathbb{E}[\text{revenue}] &= \tfrac{n-1}{n+1}\, \bar v \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with n bidders whose values are uniform on [0, v̄] (here v̄ = 100), the equilibrium first-price bid shades your value by the factor (n − 1)/n. The winner pays its own shaded bid in a first-price auction and the second-highest value in a Vickrey auction, and both have the same expected value: that of the second-highest of n uniform draws.
Say it“The first-price bid is n minus one over n times v; expected revenue is n minus one over n plus one times v-bar.”

Mechanism design

Designing the game: the commons and VCG

Game theory predicts what agents will do under given rules. Mechanism design runs the other way: choose the rules, a set of allowed strategies and an outcome rule, so that self-interested agents playing their best strategies produce an outcome the designer wants. Auctions are one example. Here is the warning case that shows why design matters.

The tragedy of the commons (Hardin, 1968): n herders share a pasture. Each cow earns 10 minus 0.1 for every cow on the pasture (crowding), and costs 2 to keep. Every herder adds cows while it pays for them, ignoring that each extra cow also lowers the earnings of everyone else’s cows. The equilibrium overgrazes, and the more herders, the worse it gets. The cure is to make each agent pay for the harm it does to others. Charging every cow the damage it causes at the efficient level fixes the commons exactly.

The Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanism turns that idea into a general recipe. Agents report their values for every possible outcome; the mechanism picks the outcome with the highest reported total, and charges each agent the loss its presence causes everyone else: what the others would have got had it stayed home, minus what they get now. Truthful reporting is then a dominant strategy, and the outcome is efficient. A single-item Vickrey auction is exactly VCG. The price: the payments leave the group (VCG is not budget-balanced), coalitions of agents can collude, and in combinatorial settings computing the welfare-maximising outcome can itself be NP-hard. Add herders and switch the per-cow charge on; then edit the four residents’ values and look for a profitable lie.

\[ \begin{aligned} W(C) &= C\,(a - k - bC) \\ C^{*} &= \frac{a-k}{2b} \\ C^{\text{Nash}} &= \frac{n}{n+1} \cdot \frac{a-k}{b} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with C cows in total, each earning a − bC and costing k, the pasture’s welfare is W(C). It peaks at C*. A herder with c of the cows maximises c(a − k − bC), which counts the crowding cost only on its own cows; in the symmetric equilibrium the total is CNash, which approaches twice C* as n grows. A charge of b·C*(n − 1)/n per cow, the harm one cow does to the others’ herds at the efficient level, moves the equilibrium to C*.
Say it“W of C is C times a minus k minus b C; the optimum is a minus k over two b, and the Nash total is n over n plus one times a minus k over b.”
\[ \begin{aligned} o^{*} &= \arg\max_{o} \textstyle\sum_{j} \hat v_j(o) \\ p_i &= \max_{o} \textstyle\sum_{j \ne i} \hat v_j(o) \\ &\quad - \textstyle\sum_{j \ne i} \hat v_j(o^{*}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: v̂j is what agent j reports. Choose the outcome with the largest reported total. Agent i pays the drop in everyone else’s welfare caused by taking i’s reports into account. Its utility, vi(o*) − pi, is the true total welfare at o* minus a term i cannot influence, so i’s best report is the one that maximises true total welfare: the truth.
Say it“o-star is the arg-max of the reported values summed over agents; i pays the others’ best total without i, minus the others’ total at o-star.”

Social choice

Voting: many rankings in, one decision out

When agents cannot pay each other, a group decision is usually a vote. Social choice theory studies the rules. A social welfare function takes every voter’s ranking of the candidates and returns a ranking for the group; a social choice function returns just a winner. The common rules are:

  • Plurality: count first preferences; most wins. Simple, but a candidate liked by many as second choice gets nothing.
  • Runoff (two rounds): if nobody has a majority, the top two by first preferences meet head to head.
  • Instant runoff (IRV; single transferable vote for one seat): repeatedly eliminate the candidate with the fewest first preferences and pass those ballots to their next surviving choice, until someone has a majority.
  • Borda count: with m candidates, a ballot gives m − 1 points to its first choice, m − 2 to its second, and so on down to 0.
  • Approval voting: each voter ticks every candidate they find acceptable; most ticks wins.
  • Condorcet methods: candidate a is a Condorcet winner if it beats every other candidate in a head-to-head majority. Copeland counts each candidate’s head-to-head wins (half a point for a tie) and elects the most.

Condorcet’s own discovery is that majorities can go round in a circle: A beats B, B beats C and C beats A, so there may be no Condorcet winner at all. To build intuition for how the rules differ, use the spatial model: voters and candidates are points on a map of opinion, and every voter ranks the candidates by distance, nearest first. A Yee diagram (after Ka-Ping Yee) colours every point of the map by the candidate who would win if a crowd of 400 voters were centred there. A perfectly well-behaved rule would give each candidate a region around itself; real rules do not. Drag the four candidates, switch rules, and look for regions that shift, split or vanish; then drag the white ring, the electorate’s actual centre.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Plur}(a) &= \#\{\, k : \text{rank}_k(a) = 1 \,\} \\ \text{Borda}(a) &= \textstyle\sum_{k} \big[ m - \text{rank}_k(a) \big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: rankk(a) is where voter k puts candidate a (1 = top). Plurality counts the voters who put a first. Borda gives m − rank points from every voter, so it rewards being ranked high by many, not just first by some. (Candidates a voter leaves unranked score 0 from that ballot.)
Say it“Plurality of a counts the voters who rank a first; Borda of a sums m minus a’s rank over all voters.”
\[ \begin{aligned} N(a \succ b) &= \#\{\, k : a \succ_k b \,\} \\ &\text{$a$ is a Condorcet winner if} \\ N(a \succ b) &> N(b \succ a) \ \text{ for all } b \ne a \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: N(a ≻ b) counts the voters who rank a above b. A Condorcet winner beats every rival in these head-to-head counts. Copeland’s score is the number of rivals a beats (plus half for ties), so a Condorcet winner always wins Copeland; when majorities cycle there is none.
Say it“N of a over b counts voters preferring a to b; a is a Condorcet winner exactly when it beats every b head to head.”

Impossibility

Arrow’s theorem and the manipulable vote

Every rule above misbehaves somewhere. Kenneth Arrow (1951) proved that this is not a failure of imagination. Ask a social welfare function, over three or more candidates, to satisfy four conditions:

  • Unrestricted domain: it accepts every possible combination of voters’ rankings, and returns a complete, transitive ranking.
  • Pareto efficiency: if every voter prefers a to b, so does the group.
  • Independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA): the group’s ranking of a against b depends only on how voters rank a against b, not on where they put some third candidate.
  • Non-dictatorship: no single voter whose ranking always becomes the group’s.

Arrow’s theorem: no such function exists. Something must give, and in practice it is usually IIA: that is the spoiler effect, when adding or removing a losing candidate changes who wins. A second result bites individual voters. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem (Gibbard 1973; Satterthwaite 1975): any social choice function that can elect any of three or more candidates and is not a dictatorship is manipulable: for some profile, some voter gains by voting insincerely. One defence is computational. Bartholdi, Tovey and Trick (1989) showed that finding a beneficial manipulation is NP-hard for some rules (second-order Copeland), and Bartholdi and Orlin (1991) for single transferable vote; for plurality and Borda a lone manipulator can find one quickly.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{if } \big(a \succ_k b \iff a \succ'_k b\big) \\ &\qquad \text{for every voter } k, \\ &\text{then } \big(a \succ b \iff a \succ' b\big) \\ &\qquad \text{for the group} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: take two profiles, ≻ and ≻′, in which every voter orders a and b the same way (they may move other candidates around). IIA demands that the group orders a and b the same way in both. Removing a candidate leaves every voter’s a-versus-b opinion intact, so under IIA it could never swap a winner a for b: a rule with a spoiler breaks IIA.

Negotiation

Bargaining: offers, deadlines and impatience

Two agents can share a surplus, but only if they agree how; if they never agree, both get nothing. In Rubinstein’s alternating offers model (1982) agent 1 proposes a split of a pie of size 1, agent 2 accepts or counter-proposes, and so on. Two forces decide the result. A deadline: if bargaining must end after T rounds, whoever proposes in the last round can offer the other nothing and expect acceptance, since the alternative is nothing too. Working backwards from that ultimatum, each proposer offers the other exactly what the other could get by waiting one round, and the first offer is accepted at once. And impatience: agent i values a share received one round later at δi times as much (0 < δi < 1). With no deadline at all, the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium gives the first proposer the share in the formula below: the more patient you are, the more you get, and with equal patience the first mover gets 1/(1 + δ), slightly more than half.

Agents bargaining over tasks live in a task-oriented domain (Rosenschein and Zlotkin, 1994): each starts with some tasks, a deal reallocates them, and an agent’s utility is how much cost it saves compared with the conflict deal of doing its own tasks. Sensible agents only consider the negotiation set: deals that are individually rational (no one loses) and Pareto optimal (no other deal is better for one and no worse for the other). In the monotonic concession protocol both propose a deal each round; if either finds the other’s offer at least as good as its own, they agree; otherwise at least one must concede, offering something better for the other. The Zeuthen strategy says who: the agent with less to lose from a breakdown, as measured by its willingness to risk conflict. Concede just enough to make the other the one who must concede next. Played by both, it ends at the deal that maximises the product of the gains: the Nash bargaining solution (Nash, 1950; Harsanyi, 1956).

\[ \begin{aligned} x_T &= 1, \quad x_t = 1 - \delta_{\,j(t)}\; x_{t+1} \\ x_1 &\xrightarrow{\,T \to \infty\,} \frac{1 - \delta_2}{1 - \delta_1 \delta_2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: xt is the share the proposer in round t keeps. In the last round the proposer takes everything. In round t the responder j(t), who will propose next, accepts any offer worth at least what it gets by waiting: δj times its own proposer share next round. Without a deadline, the first proposer’s share is Rubinstein’s formula.
Say it“The last proposer keeps everything; in round t the proposer keeps one minus delta of the responder times next round’s share; with no deadline the first share is one minus delta two over one minus delta one delta two.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{risk}_i &= \frac{u_i(\delta_i) - u_i(\delta_j)}{u_i(\delta_i)} \\ \delta^{\text{Nash}} &= \arg\max_{\delta} \; u_1(\delta)\, u_2(\delta) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: here δi is agent i’s current proposal (a deal, not a discount). riski is the share of its hoped-for gain that i would give up by accepting the other’s offer instead: the fraction it would lose by conceding, and so how much it can afford to risk conflict. The agent with the smaller risk concedes. The process ends near the deal that maximises the product of the two gains over the conflict deal.
Say it“Risk i is u i of my offer minus u i of yours, over u i of my offer; the Nash solution is the arg-max of u one times u two.”

Real-world project

Project: 42 real elections, six counting rules

Do the differences between voting rules matter on real ballots, or only in textbook puzzles? PrefLib (Mattei and Walsh, 2013) is a curated library of real preference data. From its PrefLib-Data repository this page takes every ranked-ballot election in seven collections, elections and ballots in all: the 2006 and 2009 Burlington, Vermont mayoral races; Aspen, Colorado’s 2009 mayor and city council; Berkeley and Oakland, California (2010–2012); a 2007 Takoma Park, Maryland special election; seven Debian project-leader elections and the Debian logo vote; and the 21 wards of the 2007 Glasgow City Council election. The candidate names are the ones in the files.

Two choices had to be made, and both are stated here. First, a few ballots rank two candidates equal (an overvote); as in many instant-runoff counts, such a ballot is cut at its first tie and keeps only the strict ranking before it ( ballots). Ballots may rank only some candidates. Second, the Glasgow wards actually elected three or four councillors each by single transferable vote; here they are counted as if electing one, a hypothetical that uses real ballots. Six rules are run on every election: plurality; a two-round runoff between the top two by first preferences; instant runoff; Borda (unranked candidates score 0 from that ballot); Copeland, with the Condorcet winner reported when there is one (a ranked candidate beats an unranked one; two unranked candidates are not compared); and k-approval, which treats each voter as approving their top k. Real voters never cast those approval ballots, so that column is a simulation. Score ties go to the candidate listed first in the file, and instant runoff eliminates the later-listed of two tied last-placed candidates.

Filter the board to the elections where the rules disagree, click one, then withdraw candidates to test for spoilers.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{IRV stops in round } r \text{ when } \\ &\quad \max_a \ \text{first}_r(a) > \tfrac{1}{2} \textstyle\sum_{b \in A_r} \text{first}_r(b) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Ar is the set of candidates still standing in round r, and firstr(a) counts the ballots whose highest-ranked surviving candidate is a. Ballots that rank no survivor are exhausted and drop out of the total, so “a majority” means a majority of the ballots still in play.
Say it“Instant runoff stops in round r when some candidate’s first preferences exceed half of all first preferences for the surviving candidates.”

import json, sys, time, numpy as np
E = json.load(open(sys.argv[1], encoding="utf-8"))      # x103-elections.json: 42 PrefLib elections
def ballots(e):                                           # [(ranking as candidate indices, count), ...]
    return [([ord(ch) - 97 for ch in k], int(v)) for k, v in (t.rsplit(":", 1) for t in e["b"].split())]
argmax = lambda s: int(np.argmax(s))                      # score ties go to the earlier-listed candidate
def firsts(B, m):
    s = np.zeros(m)
    for o, c in B:
        if o: s[o[0]] += c
    return s
def pairwise(B, m):                                       # P[i, j] = ballots ranking i above j
    P = np.zeros((m, m))                                  # (a ranked candidate beats an unranked one)
    for o, c in B:
        seen = set()
        for i in o:
            for j in range(m):
                if j != i and j not in seen: P[i, j] += c
            seen.add(i)
    return P
def runoff(B, m, P):
    s = firsts(B, m); a, b = sorted(np.argsort(-s, kind="stable")[:2])
    if P[a, b] != P[b, a]: return int(a if P[a, b] > P[b, a] else b)
    return int(a if s[a] >= s[b] else b)
def irv(B, m):
    alive, rounds = list(range(m)), []
    while True:
        s = np.zeros(m)
        for o, c in B:
            for i in o:
                if i in alive: s[i] += c; break
        rounds.append({i: int(s[i]) for i in alive})
        top = max(alive, key=lambda i: (s[i], -i))
        if 2 * s[top] > s[alive].sum() or len(alive) == 1: return top, rounds
        low = min(s[i] for i in alive)
        alive.remove(max(i for i in alive if s[i] == low))    # tie for last: drop the later-listed
def borda(B, m):                                          # m-1 points for 1st ... ; unranked score 0
    s = np.zeros(m)
    for o, c in B:
        for r, i in enumerate(o): s[i] += c * (m - 1 - r)
    return s
def copeland(P):                                          # wins + half a point per tie
    m = len(P); s = (P > P.T).sum(1) + 0.5 * ((P == P.T).sum(1) - 1)
    cw = [i for i in range(m) if all(P[i, j] > P[j, i] for j in range(m) if j != i)]
    return argmax(s), (cw[0] if cw else -1)
def approval(B, m, k):                                    # simulated: each voter approves their top k
    s = np.zeros(m)
    for o, c in B:
        for i in o[:k]: s[i] += c
    return s
def count(B, m, k=2):
    P = pairwise(B, m); cop, cw = copeland(P)
    return [argmax(firsts(B, m)), runoff(B, m, P), irv(B, m)[0], argmax(borda(B, m)), cop, argmax(approval(B, m, k)), cw]

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A cooperative game gives every coalition a value v(C); an outcome is a coalition structure plus a feasible payoff vector, and superadditive games make the grand coalition the best structure.
  • The core holds the payoffs no coalition can improve on by leaving, and it may be empty; the Shapley value, each player’s average marginal contribution over all arrival orders, is the unique split satisfying efficiency, symmetry, null player and additivity, and it may lie outside the core.
  • Compact representations decide what is computable: Shapley values are easy in induced subgraph games and MC-nets but #P-hard in weighted voting games; the best coalition structure is NP-hard to find, and searching all structures of at most two coalitions guarantees a factor n.
  • The contract net awards each task to the lowest marginal-cost bid; English and Vickrey auctions make truthful bidding dominant, first-price and Dutch auctions make bidders shade, and revenue equivalence says they all raise the same on average; VCG charges each agent the harm it does to others and so makes honesty a dominant strategy.
  • Voting rules can disagree on the same ballots, as they do in 20 of 42 real elections here; Arrow shows no ranking rule satisfies Pareto, IIA and non-dictatorship on every profile, Gibbard–Satterthwaite that every reasonable rule can be manipulated, and Rubinstein and Zeuthen show how patience and risk decide a bargain.

Check your understanding

In a superadditive game, is the grand coalition always the best coalition structure? Does that mean it will form?

It always creates the most total value, because any structure can be merged step by step into N without losing value. Whether it forms is a separate question: if the core is empty, every way of sharing v(N) leaves some coalition able to do better on its own, so the grand coalition is unstable even though it is efficient.

In the glove market, A holds a left glove and B and C hold right gloves; a pair is worth 12. Why is the core (12, 0, 0) while the Shapley value is (8, 2, 2)?

Any split that gives B or C something leaves the other right-glove holder with less than it could get by offering A a better deal, so competition between B and C drives their payoffs to zero in the core. The Shapley value instead credits B with the full 12 in the one order of six in which B arrives straight after A (A, B, C), and C likewise, so each gets 12/6 = 2; A completes the pair in the other four orders and gets 4 × 12/6 = 8. Stability and fairness disagree.

Why is bidding your true value a dominant strategy in a Vickrey auction but not in a first-price auction?

In a Vickrey auction your bid only decides whether you win; the price is the best rival bid either way. Raising your bid above your value can only add wins at a loss, lowering it can only lose profitable wins. In a first-price auction you pay your own bid, so bidding your value always earns zero profit and shading the bid trades a lower chance of winning for a profit when you do.

Instant runoff elected a different candidate from the Condorcet winner in Burlington in 2009. Which of Arrow’s conditions does that kind of outcome illustrate failing, and why?

Independence of irrelevant alternatives. Montroll beat Kiss head to head, yet Kiss won: Montroll was eliminated in round 4, before the final, because too few voters ranked him first. Removing Wright, a candidate who did not win, changes the IRV count to Montroll; the group’s verdict on Kiss versus Montroll depended on a third candidate.

In alternating-offers bargaining with no deadline, what happens to the first proposer’s share as both agents become very patient (δ → 1)?

With equal patience the first proposer gets 1/(1 + δ), which tends to 1/2: the advantage of moving first disappears because waiting one round costs almost nothing. With unequal patience the more patient agent gets more, and in the limit the shares depend on the ratio of how fast each agent’s impatience vanishes.
