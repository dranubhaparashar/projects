Choosing Inputs

Subset selection, principal components and partial least squares

Least squares uses every input you hand it, and every input brings its own noisy coefficient. With eight measurements on 67 men, that is eight guesses, some of them poor. This chapter is about using fewer, better inputs: keep only a subset of them, or squeeze all of them into a few derived directions. The idea

Why use fewer inputs?

Chapter 1.2 fitted a straight-line model by least squares. If the model is right, least squares is unbiased: on average its coefficients hit the truth. But “on average” hides a cost. Each coefficient is estimated from noisy data, and every extra input adds its own wobble to every prediction. There are two reasons to use fewer inputs. The first is prediction accuracy: dropping a weak input adds a little bias but can remove a lot of variance. The second is interpretation: a handful of strong effects is easier to understand and to trust than a long list of small ones.

Below is a made-up study where we know the truth. Ten candidate inputs are ordered from the strongest effect to the weakest; the last two have no effect at all. The model uses the first k of them. Slide k and the noise level, and watch the test error fall, bottom out and climb again.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{Err}(k) &= \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}} + \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{N}\big\lVert(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}_k)\,\mathbf{f}\big\rVert^2}_{\text{bias}^2} \\ &\quad + \underbrace{\sigma^2\,\tfrac{k+1}{N}}_{\text{variance}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the average squared error when the same N people are measured again with fresh noise. f holds their true mean responses and Hk is the least-squares “hat” matrix for an intercept plus the first k inputs. The bias term is the part of the truth that k inputs cannot reach; it shrinks as k grows. The variance term grows by σ²/N for every coefficient you estimate. The training error hides that cost: on average it is σ²(1 − (k+1)/N) + bias², and it only ever falls.
Say it“Error of k equals sigma squared, plus one over N times the squared length of I minus H-k times f, plus sigma squared times k plus one over N.”

Subset selection

Best subset: try every combination

In real data we don’t know which inputs matter or in what order. The most direct answer is brute force: for every size k, fit every subset of k inputs and keep the one with the smallest residual sum of squares. Those winners are the lower edge of the cloud in the hero. On the prostate data the winners happen to be nested (each adds one input to the last), but nothing forces that. Slide k to see the winner of each size and how it does on the 30 test men.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{RSS}(S) &= \min_{\beta}\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(y_i - \hat y_i\big)^2 \\ \hat y_i &= \beta_0 + \sum_{j\in S}\beta_j x_{ij} \\ \hat S_k &= \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{|S| = k}\ \mathrm{RSS}(S) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for a set S of inputs, RSS(S) is the error of the least-squares fit that uses only those inputs. The best subset of size k is the set with the smallest RSS among all sets of exactly k inputs. RSS can only fall as k grows, so it cannot tell you which k to use. That needs an estimate of test error, such as cross-validation (the project) or a penalty like AIC or BIC.
Say it“RSS of S is the smallest sum of squared residuals using only the inputs in S; S-hat-k is the set of size k with the smallest RSS.”

Why it explodes

Every input is either in or out, so p inputs give 2p models. Your browser just fitted all 256 prostate models; the stopwatch below says how long that took. Doubling for every extra input soon outruns any computer. The leaps and bounds algorithm (Furnival & Wilson, 1974) skips whole branches that cannot win, which stretches exhaustive search to about 30 or 40 inputs, and modern mixed-integer solvers push further on favourable problems. Slide p.

\[ \#\text{models} = \sum_{k=0}^{p}\binom{p}{k} = 2^{p} \]
Read it as: there are “p choose k” subsets of size k, and adding them up over every size gives two to the power p: one in-or-out choice per input.
Say it“The number of models is the sum over k of p choose k, which is two to the p.”

Greedy search

Forward and backward stepwise

Forward stepwise selection starts with the intercept alone and, at each step, adds the one input that lowers RSS the most. It fits only p(p+1)/2 + 1 models instead of 2p, and clever updating of the fit makes each step cheap. It works even when there are more inputs than people. Because it searches a narrow path, it has lower variance than best subset but may miss the best combination. Backward stepwise starts with every input and repeatedly drops the weakest one, the input with the smallest |z|-score. It needs more people than inputs. Hybrids consider both an add and a drop at every step.

On the prostate data all three searches agree at every size, so there is a second dataset: four inputs where x1 and x2 are almost copies of each other and the response is their difference. Step forward and backward on the trap.

\[ F = \frac{\mathrm{RSS}_0 - \mathrm{RSS}_1}{\mathrm{RSS}_1/(N-k_1-1)} \]
Read it as: how much one extra input lowers RSS (model 0 has k1 − 1 inputs, model 1 has k1), measured against the noise level that model 1 leaves. Forward stepwise adds the input with the largest F; with one input added, the largest F and the largest drop in RSS are the same choice.
Say it“F is RSS-nought minus RSS-one, over RSS-one divided by N minus k-one minus one.”
\[ z_j = \frac{\hat\beta_j}{\hat\sigma\sqrt{v_j}}, \qquad \hat\sigma^2 = \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{N-k-1} \]
Read it as: a coefficient divided by its standard error, where vj is the j-th diagonal entry of the inverse of the (centred) inputs’ Gram matrix. Backward stepwise drops the input with the smallest |z|. Dropping it raises RSS by exactly zj²σ̂², so it is also the cheapest input to lose.
Say it“z-j is beta-hat-j over sigma-hat times the root of v-j.”

Slower still

Forward stagewise: one nudge at a time

Forward stepwise refits every coefficient each time an input joins. Forward stagewise regression is more timid. It starts with the intercept at the mean of y and every other coefficient at zero. At each step it finds the input most correlated with the current residual, regresses the residual on that input alone, adds the result to that one coefficient, and leaves all the others untouched. It can revisit the same input many times and needs far more than p steps to reach least squares. That slowness looked wasteful for decades; in very high dimensions it turns out to be a virtue, as chapter 1.2b shows. Step it, then run it.

\[ \begin{aligned} j &= \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{k}\ \big|\mathrm{Corr}(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{r})\big| \\ \delta &= \frac{\langle \mathbf{x}_j, \mathbf{r}\rangle}{\langle \mathbf{x}_j, \mathbf{x}_j\rangle} \\ \beta_j &\leftarrow \beta_j + \delta \\ \mathbf{r} &\leftarrow \mathbf{r} - \delta\,\mathbf{x}_j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: pick the input most correlated with the current residual r (with standardised inputs this is the largest |⟨xk, r⟩|). δ is the slope of a one-input regression of r on that input. Add δ to its coefficient, subtract its contribution from the residual, and repeat. After the step, xj is uncorrelated with the new residual.
Say it“Pick the j whose input is most correlated with r; delta is x-j dot r over x-j dot x-j; add delta to beta-j and take delta x-j off the residual.”

Derived inputs

Principal components regression

Selection keeps some inputs whole and throws the rest away. The other family keeps a little of every input. When inputs are correlated, as cancer volume and capsular penetration are here, or the two Gleason measures, least squares struggles to separate their effects and the coefficients swing wildly. Principal components regression (PCR) first rotates the standardised inputs into uncorrelated directions, the principal components, ordered by how much the inputs vary along them. Then it regresses y on the first M components only. Because the components are uncorrelated, that regression is just M separate one-input regressions added together.

PCR is a cousin of ridge regression from chapter 1.2. Both work in the same rotated coordinates. Ridge shrinks every direction a little, low-variance ones the most; PCR keeps the first M directions untouched and deletes the rest. Slide M and compare their shrinkage factors.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{z}_m &= \mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}_m \\ \hat\theta_m &= \frac{\langle \mathbf{z}_m, \mathbf{y}\rangle}{\langle \mathbf{z}_m, \mathbf{z}_m\rangle} \\ \hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\text{pcr}}_{(M)} &= \bar y\,\mathbf{1} + \sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat\theta_m \mathbf{z}_m \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: vm is the m-th principal direction of the centred, standardised inputs (an eigenvector of XTX), and zm is every person’s score along it. Each θ̂m is a one-input regression slope; there is no need to fit them jointly because the z’s are uncorrelated.
Say it“z-m is X times v-m; theta-hat-m is z-m dot y over z-m dot z-m; the fit is y-bar plus the sum of theta-m z-m for the first M components.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}^{\text{pcr}}(M) &= \sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat\theta_m \mathbf{v}_m \\ \text{ridge keeps } &\tfrac{d_m^2}{d_m^2+\lambda} \\ \text{PCR keeps } &1 \text{ or } 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: turn the M slopes back into one coefficient per original input. dm² is the variance along direction m (times N). In the same coordinates, ridge multiplies the least-squares fit along each direction by dm²/(dm² + λ): smooth shrinkage. PCR multiplies by 1 for the first M directions and 0 for the rest: a hard cut. With M = p, PCR is plain least squares.
Say it“Beta-hat PCR of M is the sum of theta-hat-m v-m. Ridge keeps d-m squared over d-m squared plus lambda of each direction; PCR keeps all or nothing.”

Derived inputs

Partial least squares: directions that look at y

PCR picks its directions without ever looking at the response. If y happens to depend on a direction where the inputs barely vary, PCR throws that direction away first. Partial least squares (PLS), from Herman Wold’s work in econometrics and chemometrics, builds each direction from the inputs’ covariance with y. Below are two correlated inputs; colour shows the response. Turn the signal’s direction until it lies across the cloud’s short axis, and watch PCR’s first direction miss it while PLS’s does not.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{PCR: } &\max_{\alpha}\ \mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{X}\alpha) \\ \text{PLS: } &\max_{\alpha}\ \mathrm{Corr}^2(\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{X}\alpha) \\ &\qquad\times \mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{X}\alpha) \\ \text{s.t. } &\lVert\alpha\rVert = 1, \\ &\alpha^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{S}\,\hat\varphi_\ell = 0 \ \ (\ell < m) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each method chooses a unit-length combination α of the inputs, uncorrelated with the earlier ones (S is the inputs’ covariance matrix and φ̂ℓ the earlier directions). PCR asks only for spread. PLS asks for spread and correlation with y, and in practice the spread term tends to dominate, so PLS behaves much like ridge and PCR. The first PLS direction is simply proportional to XTy.
Say it“PCR maximises the variance of X alpha. PLS maximises the squared correlation of y with X alpha, times the variance of X alpha. Both with alpha of length one, uncorrelated with the earlier directions.”

The PLS algorithm, step by step

Each PLS step weights every input by its inner product with y, adds them up into one new direction zm, regresses y on it, and then removes zm from every input so that the next direction is uncorrelated with it. After p steps PLS reaches least squares; with uncorrelated inputs it gets there in a single step. Slide M on the prostate data.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\varphi_{mj} &= \langle \mathbf{x}^{(m-1)}_j, \mathbf{y}\rangle \\ \mathbf{z}_m &= \sum_{j}\hat\varphi_{mj}\,\mathbf{x}^{(m-1)}_j \\ \hat\theta_m &= \frac{\langle \mathbf{z}_m,\mathbf{y}\rangle}{\langle \mathbf{z}_m,\mathbf{z}_m\rangle} \\ \hat{\mathbf{y}}^{(m)} &= \hat{\mathbf{y}}^{(m-1)} + \hat\theta_m \mathbf{z}_m \\ \mathbf{x}^{(m)}_j &= \mathbf{x}^{(m-1)}_j - \tfrac{\langle \mathbf{z}_m, \mathbf{x}^{(m-1)}_j\rangle}{\langle \mathbf{z}_m,\mathbf{z}_m\rangle}\,\mathbf{z}_m \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: start from the standardised inputs and ŷ(0) = ȳ. The weight φ̂mj is how strongly input j (what is left of it) moves with y. zm is the weighted sum; θ̂m its one-input regression slope. The last line strips zm out of every input before the next round.
Say it“Phi-m-j is x-j dot y; z-m is the phi-weighted sum of the inputs; add theta-m z-m to the fit; then take z-m out of every input.”

Comparison

Selection and shrinkage, side by side

Every method in this chapter, and ridge and the lasso from chapter 1.2, draws a path from “no inputs” (all coefficients zero) to the least-squares answer as its dial turns. With two inputs we can draw all the paths on one map. Each is drawn for a very large sample, so the least-squares end point is the true β. Drag the true coefficients and change the correlation.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{y}} &= \sum_{j=1}^{p} \mathbf{u}_j\, f_j\, \mathbf{u}_j^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{y} \\ f_j^{\text{LS}} &= 1 \\ f_j^{\text{ridge}} &= \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda} \\ f_j^{\text{PCR}} &= \begin{cases} 1 & j \le M \\ 0 & j > M \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: write the centred inputs as X = UDVT (the singular value decomposition). Least squares projects y onto each direction uj and keeps all of it; the other methods keep a fraction fj. Ridge shrinks smoothly, PCR cuts hard. PLS and the lasso have no fixed factors (PLS’s depend on y, and the lasso works coordinate by coordinate), and best subset jumps between models.
Say it“y-hat is the sum over directions of u-j times f-j times u-j-transpose y, where f-j is one, d-j squared over d-j squared plus lambda, or one-then-zero.”

Which should you use? In a large simulation study, Frank and Friedman (1993) found that for minimising prediction error, ridge regression is generally preferable to subset selection, PCR and PLS, although its edge over PCR and PLS was small. The lasso sits between ridge and best subset: it shrinks like ridge and selects like best subset. Discrete methods (subset, PCR, PLS) can only stop at a few points along their paths, which makes them noisier to tune.

More than one response

Many outcomes at once

Suppose each person has K responses instead of one: say, several blood markers measured together. Least squares with K responses is just K separate regressions; the outcomes never share information. You can shrink or select each one separately, or with a single dial for all of them. But when the outcomes are correlated, there is a better idea: look for the few combinations of inputs that predict the outcomes well together, and pool them.

Canonical correlation analysis (CCA, Hotelling 1936) finds pairs of directions, one among the inputs and one among the outcomes, whose scores are as correlated as possible. Reduced-rank regression (Izenman, 1975) keeps only the first m of those pairs. Curds and whey (Breiman & Friedman, 1997) keeps all of them but shrinks each by an amount set by its canonical correlation. Below, 5 outcomes depend on 6 inputs through only 1 or 2 hidden combinations. Change the true rank and the noise, and slide the rank m.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{RSS}(\mathbf{B}) &= \operatorname{tr}\big[\mathbf{R}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{R}\big] \\ \mathbf{R} &= \mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{B} \\ \hat{\mathbf{B}} &= (\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{Y} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Y is N × K, one column per outcome, and B is p × K, one column of coefficients per outcome. The trace adds up the K separate residual sums of squares, so the minimiser is K separate least-squares fits side by side.
Say it“RSS of B is the trace of Y minus XB, transposed, times Y minus XB. B-hat is X-transpose-X inverse, X-transpose Y.”
\[ \max_{\mathbf{u}_m,\,\mathbf{v}_m}\ \mathrm{Corr}^2(\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{u}_m,\ \mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}_m) \]
Read it as: find a combination of outcomes Yum and a combination of inputs Xvm that are as correlated as possible, each pair uncorrelated with the earlier pairs. The m-th largest correlation is the canonical correlation cm. Pairs with cm near 0 are noise; pairs near 1 are real shared structure.
Say it“Maximise the squared correlation between Y u-m and X v-m.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{B}}^{\text{rr}}(m) &= \hat{\mathbf{B}}\,\mathbf{U}_m\mathbf{U}_m^{-} \\ \hat{\mathbf{B}}^{\text{c+w}} &= \hat{\mathbf{B}}\,\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf{U}^{-1} \\ \lambda_m &= \frac{c_m^2}{c_m^2 + \frac{p}{N}\,(1-c_m^2)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: U holds the outcome-side canonical vectors um as columns, Um the first m of them, and Um− a generalised inverse. Reduced rank: take the separate fits, express them in canonical coordinates, keep the first m, and map back. Curds and whey: keep every coordinate but multiply the m-th by λm, close to 1 when cm is strong and close to 0 when it is weak compared with p/N.
Say it“Reduced-rank B is B-hat, U-m, U-m inverse. Curds and whey is B-hat, U, Lambda, U inverse, with lambda-m equal to c-m squared over c-m squared plus p over N times one minus c-m squared.”

Real-world project

Project: predicting PSA, six ways

Stamey and colleagues (1989) studied 97 men about to have their prostate removed. The response is the log of prostate-specific antigen (lpsa), a blood marker. The eight inputs are log cancer volume (lcavol), log prostate weight (lweight), age, log benign prostatic hyperplasia (lbph), seminal vesicle invasion (svi, 0 or 1), log capsular penetration (lcp), Gleason score and the percentage of Gleason scores 4 or 5 (pgg45). The data comes from the website of The Elements of Statistical Learning, with its split of 67 training men and 30 test men. Like the book, we standardise each input using all 97 men (no PSA values are used; standardising with the 67 training men alone changes the test errors below by at most 0.02).

Each method’s dial is set by 10-fold cross-validation, repeated over 10 shuffles of the training men, with the one-standard-error rule: take the simplest model whose CV error is within one standard error of the lowest. The test men are touched only once, at the end. Everything below is computed by your browser. Pick a method to see its CV curve.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{CV}(c) &= \frac{1}{100}\sum_{r=1}^{10}\sum_{f=1}^{10} \mathrm{MSE}_{r,f}(c) \\ \hat c &= \text{the simplest } c \text{ with} \\ & \mathrm{CV}(c) \le \mathrm{CV}(c^{*}) + \mathrm{SE}(c^{*}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for a complexity c (a subset size, degrees of freedom, a lasso shrinkage s or a number of directions M), MSEr,f is the error on fold f of shuffle r of a model fitted to the other nine folds. c* has the lowest CV error and SE(c*) is the standard error of a single 10-fold estimate there. The rule trades a hair of estimated error for a simpler, steadier model.
Say it“CV of c is the average fold error over ten shuffles of ten folds; pick the simplest c whose CV is within one standard error of the best.”

The same project in scikit-learn

import itertools, numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression, Ridge, Lasso
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.cross_decomposition import PLSRegression
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

D = np.genfromtxt("prostate.data", skip_header=1, usecols=range(1, 10))      # ESL website
tr = np.genfromtxt("prostate.data", skip_header=1, usecols=10, dtype=str) == "T"
X, y = D[:, :8], D[:, 8]                                                     # y = log PSA
names = "lcavol lweight age lbph svi lcp gleason pgg45".split()
X = (X - X.mean(0)) / X.std(0, ddof=1)                                       # standardise, as the book does
Xtr, ytr, Xte, yte = X[tr], y[tr], X[~tr], y[~tr]
folds = [np.random.RandomState(r).permutation(len(ytr)) % 10 for r in range(10)]   # 10 shuffles of 10 folds

class Mean:                                       # the "no inputs" model
    def fit(self, X, y): self.m = y.mean(); return self
    def predict(self, X): return np.full(len(X), self.m)

class Subset:                                     # best subset of size k, by training RSS
    def __init__(self, k): self.k = k
    def fit(self, X, y):
        rss = lambda S: ((y - LinearRegression().fit(X[:, S], y).predict(X[:, S])) ** 2).sum()
        self.S = list(min(itertools.combinations(range(8), self.k), key=rss))
        self.m = LinearRegression().fit(X[:, self.S], y); return self
    def predict(self, X): return self.m.predict(X[:, self.S])

d = np.linalg.svd(Xtr - Xtr.mean(0), compute_uv=False)
lam_for_df = lambda df: brentq(lambda l: (d**2 / (d**2 + l)).sum() - df, 1e-9, 1e9, xtol=1e-12)
a_max = np.abs((Xtr - Xtr.mean(0)).T @ (ytr - ytr.mean())).max() / len(ytr)
lassos = [Lasso(alpha=a, tol=1e-12, max_iter=10**6) for a in a_max * np.logspace(0, -3, 31)[1:]]
L1 = np.abs(LinearRegression().fit(Xtr, ytr).coef_).sum()          # lasso complexity: s = |b|_1 / |b_LS|_1
grids = {   # each grid runs from the simplest model (the mean) to the most complex
    "Subset": ([Mean() if k == 0 else Subset(k) for k in range(9)], [f"k={k}" for k in range(9)]),
    "Ridge":  ([Mean()] + [Ridge(alpha=lam_for_df(df)) for df in np.arange(0.5, 7.6, 0.5)] + [LinearRegression()],
               [f"df={df:g}" for df in np.arange(0, 8.1, 0.5)]),
    "Lasso":  ([Mean()] + lassos, ["s=0"] + [f"s={np.abs(m.fit(Xtr, ytr).coef_).sum() / L1:.2f}" for m in lassos]),
    "PCR":    ([Mean()] + [make_pipeline(PCA(M), LinearRegression()) for M in range(1, 9)], [f"M={M}" for M in range(9)]),
    "PLS":    ([Mean()] + [PLSRegression(M, scale=False) for M in range(1, 9)], [f"M={M}" for M in range(9)])}

def mse(m, Xa, ya, Xb, yb): return np.mean((yb - m.fit(Xa, ya).predict(Xb).ravel()) ** 2)
def coefs(m):                                     # every model here is linear: read b0 and b off its predictions
    b0 = m.predict(np.zeros((1, 8))).ravel()[0]
    return [b0, *(m.predict(np.eye(8)).ravel() - b0)]

table = {}
for name, (ms, labels) in [("LS", ([LinearRegression()], ["all 8"]))] + list(grids.items()):
    cv = np.array([[mse(m, Xtr[F != f], ytr[F != f], Xtr[F == f], ytr[F == f]) for F in folds for f in range(10)] for m in ms])
    mean, se = cv.mean(1), cv.std(1, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(10)          # se of one 10-fold estimate
    pick = next(i for i in range(len(ms)) if mean[i] <= mean.min() + se[mean.argmin()])   # one-standard-error rule
    e = (yte - ms[pick].fit(Xtr, ytr).predict(Xte).ravel()) ** 2
    table[name] = coefs(ms[pick]) + [e.mean(), e.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(e))]
    print(f"{name:6s} picks {labels[pick]:8s} CV error {mean[pick]:.3f}   (lowest CV {mean.min():.3f} at {labels[mean.argmin()]})")
print("\n" + " " * 11 + "".join(f"{c:>8s}" for c in table))
for i, r in enumerate(["intercept"] + names + ["test error", "std error"]):
    print(f"{r:11s}" + "".join("       ." if abs(c[i]) < 5e-4 and i < 9 else f"{c[i]:8.3f}" for c in table.values()))

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Every input you add lowers bias and adds about σ²/N of variance; training error always falls, so it can never choose how many inputs to keep.
  • Best subset tries all 2p models, which is exact but explodes past about 40 inputs. Forward and backward stepwise are greedy shortcuts; forward stagewise moves one coefficient a little at a time.
  • PCR regresses on the first M principal components: a hard 1-or-0 version of ridge’s smooth shrinkage, blind to y. PLS builds its directions from the inputs’ covariance with y.
  • On two inputs, each method draws a different path from zero to least squares. Smooth shrinkage (ridge, then lasso) usually predicts best; discrete methods are noisier to tune.
  • With several correlated outcomes, pool them: reduced-rank regression keeps the strongest canonical directions and curds and whey shrinks each by its canonical correlation.

Check your understanding

Why can’t you pick the subset size k by its training RSS?

Adding an input can never raise the least-squares RSS, because the bigger model could always set the new coefficient to zero. So RSS always picks the largest model. You need an estimate of test error: a held-out set, cross-validation, or a penalty such as AIC or BIC.

Forward stepwise and best subset agree at every size on the prostate data. Name a situation where they disagree.

When two inputs are useless alone but strong together, like x1 and x2 in the trap, where y depends on their difference. Forward stepwise first grabs the input that looks best on its own and is then stuck with it; best subset can pick the pair directly.

You run PCR with M = p. What do you get?

Ordinary least squares. The p components span the same space as the p inputs, so nothing is thrown away. The same holds for PLS after p steps.

Why does PCR need standardised inputs, while best subset does not?

Principal components chase variance, so an input measured in large units would dominate the first component just because of its units. Best subset uses whole inputs with unpenalised least squares, which is unchanged by rescaling any input.

In curds and whey, what happens to a canonical direction with cm = 0.1 when p/N = 0.15?

λ = 0.01 / (0.01 + 0.15 × 0.99) ≈ 0.063, so its contribution is shrunk to about 6%: a weak correlation, small compared with p/N, is treated as mostly noise.
