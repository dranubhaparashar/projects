Dynamic Bayesian Networks & Particle Filters

Track anything with a cloud of weighted guesses

A dynamic Bayesian network describes a world that changes: one small Bayes net per moment, copied forward in time. Exact inference in it quickly becomes hopeless, so we cheat honestly: keep a few hundred random guesses of the hidden state, move each one the way the world moves, weight it by how well it explains the latest reading, and copy the good ones. That is a particle filter. In the figure, a hidden target only reports its noisy distance to the beacons, and 800 particles (purple) chase it. Move your pointer or finger over the figure to steer the target, then switch between one, two and three beacons: the cloud becomes a ring, two blobs, or one.

AIMA 14.5 & 14.5.1 · constructing DBNs

Building a dynamic Bayesian network

A dynamic Bayesian network (DBN) is a Bayes net for a process that unfolds in time. Time is cut into slices; each slice holds the same set of variables: hidden state variables Xt and observed evidence variables Et. You never draw the whole, endless network. You specify three things and the rest is copies: the prior P(X0), the transition model P(Xt+1 | Xt), and the sensor model P(Et | Xt). Two assumptions make the copying legal: the process is first-order Markov (the next slice depends only on the current one) and stationary (the same conditional tables apply at every step).

The hidden Markov model of the earlier chapters is a DBN with a single discrete state variable and a single evidence variable. The Kalman filter of the previous chapter is a DBN whose variables are all continuous, with linear-Gaussian tables. A DBN can be both at once and more: discrete modes next to continuous positions, several sensors, nonlinear links. Conversely, every DBN with discrete variables can be turned into an HMM, by gluing all its state variables into one “mega-variable” whose values are all combinations. The catch is size. If each of n Boolean state variables has k parents in the previous slice, the DBN needs n·2k numbers for its transition model; the equivalent HMM needs a 2n × 2n matrix. Pick a preset, then drag n and k and compare the two counts.

\[ \begin{aligned} &P(\mathbf X_{0:t}, \mathbf E_{1:t}) = P(\mathbf X_0) \\ &\quad \times \prod_{i=1}^{t} \Big( P(\mathbf X_i \mid \mathbf X_{i-1}) \\ &\qquad\qquad \cdot P(\mathbf E_i \mid \mathbf X_i) \Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the joint distribution of a whole history is the prior times, for each step, one transition factor and one sensor factor. Because the factors repeat, three small tables define a model of any length.
Say it“The joint over states and evidence is the prior times the product of transition and sensor terms.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &P(\mathbf X_{t+1} \mid \mathbf X_t) \\ &\quad = \prod_{j=1}^{n} P\big(X^{j}_{t+1} \mid \mathrm{Pa}(X^{j}_{t+1})\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: inside the transition model each state variable Xj has its own small table, conditioned only on its parents Pa(·) (a few variables in slice t, and possibly some in slice t+1). That factorisation is what a DBN has and an HMM throws away.
Say it“The transition model is a product of one table per variable, each given its parents.”
\[ \underbrace{n \cdot 2^{k}}_{\text{DBN}} \quad\text{versus}\quad \underbrace{2^{n} \times 2^{n}}_{\text{HMM}} \]
Read it as: for Boolean variables, each table P(Xjt+1 = true | parents) needs one number per parent combination, 2k of them, and there are n tables. The HMM’s single state has 2n values, so its transition matrix has 2n rows and 2n columns (each row summing to 1, so 2n(2n − 1) of them are free).
Say it“n times two to the k, against two to the n squared.”

AIMA 14.5.1 · modelling a sensor that fails

When the battery meter lies

Real sensors fail, and a DBN is the natural place to say how. Take a robot whose battery charge Bt runs from 0 to 5 and slowly drains (each step it loses one unit with probability 0.08), read by a meter Mt. With a plain Gaussian error model (the reading is the charge plus discretised noise of standard deviation 0.7) a single reading of 0 is a disaster: the charge cannot be 4 if the meter says 0, so the belief crashes towards empty, and the robot might abandon its job to recharge.

The first fix is a transient failure model: whatever the charge, the meter reads 0 with a small probability φ. Now one zero is shrugged off, because “a glitch” explains it better than “the battery just lost four units at once”. But a long run of zeros still drags the belief to empty: each new zero is another independent glitch, and a string of glitches becomes less likely than a flat battery. The second fix adds a hidden Boolean state variable, BMBroken, with a persistence arc from one slice to the next: once broken, the meter stays broken and reads 0. After a few zeros, “the meter broke” becomes the best explanation, and the battery belief simply follows its predicted slow drain. Lengthen the run of zeros and watch the three models part ways.

\[ \begin{aligned} &P(M_t = m \mid B_t = b) \\ &\quad = (1-\phi)\, G(m; b) + \phi\,[m = 0] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the transient failure model. G(m; b) is the discretised Gaussian error model (weights e−(m−b)²/(2·0.7²) over m = 0…5, normalised); with probability φ the meter glitches and reports 0 instead. φ = 0 gives the plain Gaussian model.
Say it“One minus phi times the Gaussian, plus phi on a reading of zero.”
\[ \begin{aligned} P(\mathit{Br}_{t+1} \mid \mathit{Br}_t) &= 1 \\ P(\mathit{Br}_{t+1} \mid \lnot\mathit{Br}_t) &= \beta = 0.002 \\ P(M_t = 0 \mid \mathit{Br}_t) &= 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the persistent failure model adds the state variable Br = BMBroken. It almost never breaks (β per step), never repairs itself, and a broken meter always says 0. A working meter follows the transient model above.
Say it“Broken stays broken; it breaks with probability beta; a broken meter reads zero.”

AIMA 14.5.2 · exact inference in DBNs

Exact inference: affordable in time, not in size

A DBN is a Bayes net, so in principle any exact algorithm works: unroll the network for as many slices as you have evidence and run variable elimination. Done naively, the cost of each new query grows with t. The fix is the one we used for HMMs: sum out slice t as soon as slice t+1 has been added, keeping just two slices in memory. This rollup filtering is exactly the forward recursion, and it costs the same at every step.

The trouble is what the forward message is about: the joint distribution of all n state variables, dn numbers for variables with d values. With good elimination ordering each update costs about dn+k operations rather than the d2n of a full transition matrix, but it is still exponential in n. And you cannot escape by storing one marginal per variable: even when each variable talks only to its neighbours, a few steps of shared history make them all correlated, so the belief state no longer factorises. Here n machines stand in a line; a fault in one makes its right-hand neighbour more likely to fail, and each machine may have a noisy warning light. The page filters exactly over all 2n joint states and, beside it, with a fully factored filter that keeps only n marginals. Switch to “light on the last machine only” and watch the correlation, and the factored filter’s error, grow.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf f_{1:t+1}(\mathbf x_{t+1}) \\ &\quad = \alpha\, P(\mathbf e_{t+1} \mid \mathbf x_{t+1}) \\ &\quad \times \sum_{\mathbf x_t} P(\mathbf x_{t+1} \mid \mathbf x_t)\, \mathbf f_{1:t}(\mathbf x_t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the forward message f1:t = P(Xt | e1:t) is pushed through the transition model (sum out the old slice), multiplied by the new evidence, and renormalised (α). The sum runs over every joint assignment of the n state variables.
Say it“New belief is alpha times the evidence likelihood times the old belief pushed through the transition model.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &I(X^1, \dots, X^n) \\ &\quad = \sum_{j=1}^{n} H(X^j) - H(X^1, \dots, X^n) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the multi-information, in bits, of the belief state: how much the sum of the separate uncertainties exceeds the joint uncertainty. It is 0 exactly when the belief is a product of its marginals, the only case in which n numbers would be enough.
Say it“Sum of the marginal entropies minus the joint entropy.”

PRML 11.1.5 · sampling-importance-resampling

Sampling-importance-resampling

Approximate inference in DBNs is built from one sampling move, so we take it apart first. You want samples from a density p(z) that you can only evaluate up to a constant, p̃(z). Importance sampling draws L samples from an easy proposal q(z) and gives each a weight p̃/q. Expectations then come out as weighted averages, but you are left with a weighted sample. Rejection sampling would give an unweighted one, but it needs a constant k with k·q(z) ≥ p̃(z) everywhere, which is often unknown or huge.

Sampling-importance-resampling (SIR) gets an unweighted sample without k. Stage one: draw z1, …, zL from q and compute normalised weights wl. Stage two: draw L new values from the set {z1, …, zL} itself, picking zl with probability wl. Good samples get copied, poor ones vanish. The result follows p only approximately, but more and more exactly as L grows. Move the proposal away from one of the modes, and notice that the effective sample size can look healthy while the answer is badly wrong.

\[ w_l = \frac{\tilde p(z_l) / q(z_l)}{\sum_{m=1}^{L} \tilde p(z_m) / q(z_m)} \]
Read it as: each sample’s weight is how much more the target likes it than the proposal did, divided by the total so the weights sum to 1. The unknown normalising constant of p cancels top and bottom.
Say it“w-l is p-tilde over q at z-l, normalised over all L samples.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(z \le a) &= \sum_{l:\, z_l \le a} w_l \\ &= \frac{\sum_l I(z_l \le a)\, \tilde p(z_l)/q(z_l)}{\sum_l \tilde p(z_l)/q(z_l)} \\ &\xrightarrow{L \to \infty} \frac{\int I(z \le a)\, \tilde p(z)\, dz}{\int \tilde p(z)\, dz} \\ &= \int I(z \le a)\, p(z)\, dz \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: why SIR works. After resampling, the chance that a value is at most a is the total weight of the samples at most a. Each sum over the samples is an average under q, so for large L it becomes an integral against q, the q cancels, and what is left is the true cumulative distribution of p.
Say it“The resampled CDF is a ratio of two importance-sampling averages, which tends to the CDF of p.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[f(z)] &\approx \sum_{l=1}^{L} w_l\, f(z_l) \\ \mathrm{ESS} &= \frac{1}{\sum_{l} w_l^{2}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for moments you do not need stage two at all: use the weights directly (resampling only adds noise). The effective sample size ESS says how many equally weighted samples the weighted set is worth: L when all weights are equal, 1 when one sample has all the weight. It measures weight balance, not coverage: a proposal that misses a mode entirely can still have a high ESS.
Say it“Expectations are weighted averages; ESS is one over the sum of squared weights.”

AIMA 14.5.3 · approximate inference, part 1

Why likelihood weighting dies in a DBN

The obvious approximate method for a DBN is likelihood weighting on the unrolled network. Each sample runs forward slice by slice: its state is drawn from the transition model, and its weight is multiplied by the probability of the evidence actually seen. Run all N samples together and this is even an online algorithm with constant work per step. It fails anyway, for a simple reason: the samples never look at the evidence. They wander wherever the transition model takes them, the evidence only reweights them, and after a few dozen steps almost every sample has drifted somewhere the evidence rules out. Nearly all the weight sits on one or two samples, and the number of samples needed for a fixed accuracy grows exponentially with t.

The cure has two parts. First, let the set of samples itself be the approximation to the forward message, so each step starts from where the samples are now, not from the prior. Second, keep the samples where the probability is: replace the weighted set by an unweighted one in which samples are copied in proportion to their weights. That is SIR applied at every step, and it is the particle filter. The test bed is a random walk read through Gaussian noise, where the Kalman filter gives the exact posterior to compare against. Raise N, and see how little it helps likelihood weighting.

\[ \begin{aligned} x^{(i)}_t &\sim P(X_t \mid x^{(i)}_{t-1}) \\ w^{(i)}_t &= w^{(i)}_{t-1}\, P(e_t \mid x^{(i)}_t) \\ &= \prod_{s=1}^{t} P(e_s \mid x^{(i)}_s) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: likelihood weighting in a DBN. Sample i’s state follows the transition model alone; its weight is the product of the evidence likelihoods along its whole path. Products of many factors spread over many orders of magnitude, which is why one path ends up with almost all the weight.
Say it“Sample the state from the transition model; multiply the weight by the evidence likelihood.”
\[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= x_{t-1} + v_t, & v_t &\sim \mathcal N(0, 1) \\ e_t &= x_t + w_t, & w_t &\sim \mathcal N(0, r^2) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the test model: a random walk with unit steps, starting from x0 ~ N(0, 1), observed with noise of standard deviation r. Because everything is linear and Gaussian, the Kalman filter computes the exact posterior mean and variance at every step.
Say it“x-t is x-t-minus-one plus unit noise; e-t is x-t plus noise of size r.”

AIMA 14.5.3 · particle filtering

The particle filter, one phase at a time

A particle filter keeps N samples, the particles, of the current state. It starts with N draws from the prior P(X0) and repeats three moves for every new piece of evidence. Propagate: move each particle by sampling its next state from the transition model. Weight: give each particle the likelihood of the new evidence in its state. Resample: draw N particles from the weighted set, each picked with probability proportional to its weight, and forget the weights. Every step costs the same, linear in N, however long the run.

Is the cloud really the posterior? Suppose the N particles are a fair sample of P(Xt | e1:t). Propagating them gives a fair sample of the one-step prediction; weighting by the evidence likelihood and resampling turns that into a sample of P(Xt+1 | e1:t+1), by exactly the forward equation of the exact-inference section. So the filter is consistent: as N → ∞ it gives the correct posterior. The test is a classic nonlinear benchmark: the sensor reports x²/20, so it cannot tell +x from −x and the posterior often has two peaks, which a Kalman filter cannot represent. The curve is the exact posterior, computed on a grid of 601 points. Step through one phase at a time, then press play.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\textbf{propagate:} \\ &\quad x^{(i)}_{t+1} \sim P(X_{t+1} \mid x^{(i)}_{t}) \\ &\textbf{weight:} \\ &\quad w^{(i)} = P(e_{t+1} \mid x^{(i)}_{t+1}) \\ &\textbf{resample:} \\ &\quad \text{draw } N \text{ times; } \Pr(i) \propto w^{(i)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the whole algorithm. With resampling at every step this is the bootstrap filter: the proposal is the transition model and the weight is just the evidence likelihood.
Say it“Move each particle with the transition model, weight it by the evidence, resample in proportion to weight.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\frac{N(x_{t+1} \mid e_{1:t+1})}{N} \\ &\quad \approx \alpha\, P(e_{t+1} \mid x_{t+1}) \\ &\quad \times \sum_{x_t} P(x_{t+1} \mid x_t)\, P(x_t \mid e_{1:t}) \\ &\quad = P(x_{t+1} \mid e_{1:t+1}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the consistency argument. N(x | e) counts particles in state x. After propagation the expected count at xt+1 is N times the one-step prediction; weighting multiplies it by the evidence likelihood; resampling normalises. The result is the forward update, so the share of particles in each state tracks the true posterior.
Say it“The share of particles in a state approximates alpha times evidence likelihood times predicted probability, which is the filtered posterior.”
\[ \begin{aligned} x_t &= \tfrac12 x_{t-1} + \frac{25\, x_{t-1}}{1 + x_{t-1}^2} \\ &\quad + 8\cos(1.2\,t) + v_t \\ e_t &= x_t^2 / 20 + w_t \\ v_t &\sim \mathcal N(0, 10), \quad w_t \sim \mathcal N(0, 1) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the benchmark (Gordon, Salmond & Smith, 1993), with x0 ~ N(0, 5). The state is pushed around by a strongly nonlinear map and a periodic forcing; the sensor squares it, so a reading of 5 says “x is near +10 or near −10”. The variances here are 10 and 1.
Say it“Half x plus twenty-five x over one plus x squared plus eight cos one-point-two t; the sensor sees x squared over twenty.”

PRML 13.3.4 · particle filters, in Bishop’s notation

The same filter as SIR, and how to resample

Bishop derives the particle filter for a general state-space model with hidden states zn and observations xn, writing Xn for all observations up to n. Suppose you hold L samples zn(l) drawn from the predictive distribution p(zn | Xn−1). Bayes’ rule says the posterior is the predictive times the likelihood p(xn | zn), renormalised, so this is exactly the SIR situation: proposal = predictive, unnormalised target = predictive × likelihood, and the importance weights reduce to the normalised likelihoods. The weighted samples then define the next predictive distribution as a mixture: one copy of the transition density per particle, mixed with the particle’s weight. Drawing from that mixture means choosing a component by weight (resampling) and then sampling the transition (propagation). Same algorithm, derived in two lines.

Two practical choices remain. When to resample: every resampling throws information away (copies are not new samples), so many filters resample only when the effective sample size falls below a threshold, such as N/2. How: drawing N independent picks (multinomial) is the noisiest option. Residual resampling first keeps ⌊N wi⌋ copies of each particle deterministically and draws only the remainder at random. Systematic resampling uses one random offset and N evenly spaced pointers, so each particle gets either ⌊N wi⌋ or ⌈N wi⌉ copies. Draw new weights to compare the offspring counts, then vary N and the threshold to see the effect on accuracy, measured against the exact grid filter.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[f(\mathbf z_n) \mid \mathbf X_n] &\approx \sum_{l=1}^{L} w_n^{(l)} f(\mathbf z_n^{(l)}) \\ w_n^{(l)} &= \frac{p(\mathbf x_n \mid \mathbf z_n^{(l)})}{\sum_{m=1}^{L} p(\mathbf x_n \mid \mathbf z_n^{(m)})} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with samples from the predictive distribution, posterior expectations are weighted averages, and the weights are the likelihoods of the new observation, normalised. This is the SIR weight with q = predictive and p̃ = predictive × likelihood: the predictive cancels.
Say it“Posterior expectations are sums of w-n-l times f; w-n-l is the normalised likelihood of x-n under sample l.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &p(\mathbf z_{n+1} \mid \mathbf X_n) \\ &\; = \!\int\! p(\mathbf z_{n+1} \mid \mathbf z_n)\, p(\mathbf z_n \mid \mathbf X_n)\, d\mathbf z_n \\ &\; \approx \sum_{l=1}^{L} w_n^{(l)}\, p(\mathbf z_{n+1} \mid \mathbf z_n^{(l)}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: replacing the posterior by its weighted samples turns the integral into a mixture of L transition densities. Sampling it (pick l with probability wn(l), then sample zn+1 from that component) gives the samples for the next step.
Say it“The next predictive is a mixture: weight w-n-l on the transition density from each sample.”

Real-world project

Project: tracking a waved albatross through a cheap, noisy tag

The data are real GPS fixes from the Movebank study “Galapagos Albatrosses” (Cruz, Proaño, Anderson, Huyvaert & Wikelski, 2013; Movebank Data Repository, doi:10.5441/001/1.3hp3s250, licensed CC0 1.0; described by Dodge et al., 2013). Tags recorded a fix every 90 minutes. We use one foraging trip of the bird with tag 147 (animal ID 4262-84830876, a waved albatross, Phoebastria irrorata, deployed at Punta Suarez according to the study’s reference data): 333 fixes from 12 June to 3 July 2008, out to 1,479 km from the colony and back. Positions are converted to kilometres east and north of the colony. This page embeds that extract of the CC0 data.

The GPS fixes are accurate to a few metres, so they serve as ground truth. To give the filter something to do, the page simulates a cheaper tag on top of them: each fix is transmitted only with probability p, and each transmitted fix gets a random error, usually small (σ km) but sometimes, with probability ε, a wild outlier (σ = 50 km). The filter sees only those degraded fixes and must reconstruct the whole track, every 90 minutes, including the gaps. Its model is the DBN drawn in the first section: a hidden behaviour mode (resting on the water or travelling), a velocity, a position, a hidden “outlier” switch, and the fix. A baseline Kalman filter with a constant-velocity model sees the same fixes; to be generous, its noise level q is chosen in hindsight, by trying eleven values and keeping the one with the smallest error against the truth.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf v_{t} &= \begin{cases} 0.2\,\mathbf v_{t-1} + 1.5\,\boldsymbol\xi & \text{rest} \\ 30\,\boldsymbol\xi & \text{take-off} \\ 0.9\,\mathbf v_{t-1} + 10\,\boldsymbol\xi & \text{travel} \end{cases} \\ \mathbf p_t &= \mathbf p_{t-1} + \mathbf v_t\, \Delta t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the transition model, velocities in km/h and Δt the real time between fixes (about 1.5 h). ξ is a pair of independent standard normal draws. The mode flips with probability 0.1 per step. Resting birds drift slowly; a bird that has just switched to travelling gets a fresh random heading and speed; a travelling bird keeps most of its velocity. These numbers were set by hand from the speeds in the track and a few trial runs, not fitted.
Say it“Velocity decays and jitters by mode; position adds velocity times delta-t.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &p(\mathbf y_t \mid \mathbf p_t) \\ &\; = (1 - \epsilon)\, \mathcal N(\mathbf y_t; \mathbf p_t, \sigma^2 I) \\ &\; + \epsilon\, \mathcal N(\mathbf y_t; \mathbf p_t, 50^2 I) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the sensor model, with the hidden outlier switch summed out. A fix far from a particle is not fatal to it, because the wide component can explain it. The filter is told the tag’s true σ and ε. The posterior chance that a fix is an outlier is the weighted share of the wide component.
Say it“One minus epsilon times a tight Gaussian, plus epsilon times a wide one.”

The same experiment in Python

This script reproduces the project at the default settings with the same random-number generator (mulberry32) and the same order of random draws as the page, in plain Python so that every floating-point operation happens in the same order. Its numbers should match the page’s defaults digit for digit.

import json, sys, math, time
S = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "./"
trip = json.load(open(S + "x120-trip.json"))           # 333 GPS fixes of one waved albatross trip (Movebank, CC0)
T = len(trip["t"]); TX, TY, TT = trip["x"], trip["y"], trip["t"]
M32 = 0xFFFFFFFF
def rng(a):                                             # mulberry32, the generator the page uses
    st = [a & M32]
    def r():
        st[0] = (st[0] + 0x6D2B79F5) & M32; a = st[0]
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & M32
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & M32)) & M32) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M32) / 4294967296
    return r
def gauss(r):
    u = 0.0
    while u == 0.0: u = r()
    return math.sqrt(-2 * math.log(u)) * math.cos(2 * math.pi * r())
def exp_(v): return math.inf if v > 709 else math.exp(v)
def log_(v): return -math.inf if v == 0 else math.log(v)

def tag_sim(seed, p_obs, sig, eps, sig_o):              # a cheaper, noisier tag: drop fixes, add errors
    r = rng(seed); seen, out, ox, oy = [0] * T, [0] * T, [0.0] * T, [0.0] * T
    for k in range(1, T):
        u1, u2 = r(), r(); g1 = gauss(r); g2 = gauss(r)
        seen[k] = 1 if u1 < p_obs else 0; out[k] = 1 if u2 < eps else 0
        s = sig_o if out[k] else sig; ox[k] = TX[k] + s * g1; oy[k] = TY[k] + s * g2
    return seen, out, ox, oy

B = dict(pSw=0.1, aT=0.9, sT=10, sJ=30, aR=0.2, sR=1.5, thr=0.5)
def systematic(w, r):
    N = len(w); u0 = r() / N; j = 0; c = w[0]; out = [0] * N
    for i in range(N):
        u = u0 + i / N
        while u > c and j < N - 1: j += 1; c += w[j]
        out[i] = j
    return out

def bird_pf(obs, N, seed, sig, eps, sig_o):
    seen, _, ox, oy = obs; r = rng(seed)
    x = [TX[0]] * N; y = [TY[0]] * N; vx = [0.0] * N; vy = [0.0] * N; m = [0] * N; w = [1 / N] * N
    la = math.log((1 - eps) / (2 * math.pi * sig * sig)); lb = math.log(eps / (2 * math.pi * sig_o * sig_o))
    ia = 1 / (2 * sig * sig); ib = 1 / (2 * sig_o * sig_o)
    ex, ey, pT, pO, nres = [TX[0]], [TY[0]], [0.0], [math.nan], 0
    for k in range(1, T):
        dt = TT[k] - TT[k - 1]
        for i in range(N):                              # 1. propagate through the transition model
            was = m[i]
            if r() < B["pSw"]: m[i] = 1 - m[i]
            g1 = gauss(r); g2 = gauss(r)
            if m[i] and not was: vx[i] = B["sJ"] * g1; vy[i] = B["sJ"] * g2
            elif m[i]: vx[i] = B["aT"] * vx[i] + B["sT"] * g1; vy[i] = B["aT"] * vy[i] + B["sT"] * g2
            else: vx[i] = B["aR"] * vx[i] + B["sR"] * g1; vy[i] = B["aR"] * vy[i] + B["sR"] * g2
            x[i] += vx[i] * dt; y[i] += vy[i] * dt
        if seen[k]:                                     # 2. weight by the mixture sensor model
            lw = [0.0] * N; rsp = [0.0] * N; po = 0.0
            for i in range(N):
                dx = x[i] - ox[k]; dy = y[i] - oy[k]; d2 = dx * dx + dy * dy; a = la - d2 * ia; b = lb - d2 * ib
                rsp[i] = 1 / (1 + exp_(a - b))
                lw[i] = log_(w[i]) + (a + math.log(1 + math.exp(b - a)) if a > b else b + math.log(1 + math.exp(a - b)))
            mx = max(lw); s = 0.0
            for i in range(N): w[i] = math.exp(lw[i] - mx); s += w[i]
            for i in range(N): w[i] /= s
            for i in range(N): po += w[i] * rsp[i]
            pO.append(po)
        else: pO.append(math.nan)
        sx = sy = sm = 0.0
        for i in range(N): sx += w[i] * x[i]; sy += w[i] * y[i]; sm += w[i] * m[i]
        ex.append(sx); ey.append(sy); pT.append(sm)
        e2 = 0.0
        for i in range(N): e2 += w[i] * w[i]
        if 1 / e2 < B["thr"] * N:                       # 3. resample when the effective sample size drops
            idx = systematic(w, r)
            x = [x[j] for j in idx]; y = [y[j] for j in idx]; vx = [vx[j] for j in idx]; vy = [vy[j] for j in idx]; m = [m[j] for j in idx]
            w = [1 / N] * N; nres += 1
    return ex, ey, pT, pO, nres

def bird_kf(obs, q, Rv):                                # constant-velocity Kalman filter, per axis
    seen, _, ox, oy = obs
    def run(z0, oz):
        p, v, P11, P12, P22, out = z0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 100.0, [z0]
        for k in range(1, T):
            dt = TT[k] - TT[k - 1]; p += v * dt
            a = P11 + 2 * dt * P12 + dt * dt * P22 + q * dt * dt * dt / 3; b = P12 + dt * P22 + q * dt * dt / 2; c = P22 + q * dt
            P11, P12, P22 = a, b, c
            if seen[k]:
                Sv = P11 + Rv; K1 = P11 / Sv; K2 = P12 / Sv; inn = oz[k] - p; p += K1 * inn; v += K2 * inn
                P11, P12, P22 = (1 - K1) * P11, (1 - K1) * P12, P22 - K2 * P12
            out.append(p)
        return out
    return run(TX[0], ox), run(TY[0], oy)

def q95(e): s = sorted(e); return s[min(len(s) - 1, int(0.95 * len(s)))]

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A DBN is a Bayes net copied through time: a prior, a transition model P(Xt+1 | Xt) and a sensor model P(Et | Xt). HMMs and Kalman filters are special cases; factoring the state into variables makes the model exponentially smaller than the equivalent HMM.
  • Hidden state variables with persistence arcs, such as BMBroken, let a DBN tell a passing glitch from a lasting fault, which no fixed error model can.
  • Exact filtering in a DBN costs the same at every step but is exponential in the number of state variables, because local links correlate all of them within a few steps.
  • SIR turns weighted samples into unweighted ones by drawing from them in proportion to their weights; likelihood weighting in a DBN fails because its samples ignore the evidence and its weights collapse.
  • The particle filter propagates, weights and resamples N particles; it is consistent, linear in N per step, and happy with nonlinear, multimodal and mixed discrete-continuous models, at the price of Monte Carlo noise.

Check your understanding

A DBN has 12 Boolean state variables, each with 2 parents in the previous slice. How many numbers do the DBN and the equivalent HMM need for the transition model?

The DBN needs 12 × 2² = 48 numbers. The HMM’s mega-variable has 2¹² = 4,096 values, so its transition matrix has 4,096 × 4,096 ≈ 16.8 million entries (4,096 × 4,095 of them free).

Why can’t a transient failure model cope with a meter that has broken for good?

It treats each zero reading as a fresh, independent glitch with probability φ. A run of k zeros then has probability about φk under “the battery is fine”, which soon falls below the probability of “the battery is flat”, so the belief slides to empty. Only a state variable that persists, like BMBroken, can make a long run of zeros cheap to explain.

The machines are connected only to their neighbours. Why does exact filtering still need all 2n joint states?

Each step couples every machine to its neighbour, and over several steps those couplings chain along the line, so evidence about one machine changes beliefs about all the others. The belief state becomes correlated (its multi-information is above zero), so it is not a product of n marginals, and any factored representation is an approximation.

In the SIR lab the effective sample size can be high while the weighted mean is far from −0.22. How?

ESS only measures how evenly the weight is spread over the samples you drew. If the proposal never puts samples near one of the modes, the weights among the samples it did draw can be quite even, so ESS is high, but that mode is simply missing, and the estimate is wrong. ESS cannot detect what was never sampled.

Why does resampling at every step not make the particle filter collapse the way likelihood weighting does?

Resampling resets the weights to equal and moves the particles to where the evidence says the state is. The next weights then depend only on one new observation instead of the product of all observations so far, so they never spread over many orders of magnitude. The price is some loss of diversity: copies are not independent samples, which is why some filters resample only when the ESS falls below a threshold.
