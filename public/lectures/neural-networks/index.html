Neural Networks & Backpropagation

Stack simple neurons, then pass the blame backwards

One neuron draws one straight line. Feed neurons into neurons and the network can bend its boundary into any shape, but now there are thousands of weights to set. Backpropagation finds, in a single backward sweep, how much each weight is to blame for the error, so gradient descent can fix them all at once. On the right is a network that learned to read handwriting this way. Draw a digit in the pad and watch it flow through 32 hidden neurons to ten answers.

The idea

Bending space with a hidden layer

In chapter 2.1 a single neuron hit the XOR wall: when orange points sit in two opposite corners and teal points in the other two, no straight line can separate them. Add two hidden neurons in between. Each draws its own line across the plane and squashes the distance from it through tanh into a number between −1 and 1. The output neuron no longer sees the points at (x₁, x₂); it sees them at (h₁, h₂), and there a single straight line is enough. Slide from input space to hidden space and watch the grid fold. You can drag the dots too.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{h} &= \tanh(W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}) \\ W &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.75 \\ 0.75 & 1 \end{pmatrix},\ \ \mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} \\ \hat y &= \sigma(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{h} + c) \\ \mathbf{v} &= (-4,\ 4),\ \ c = 3.3 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: row j of W and entry j of b define hidden neuron j: a weighted sum of the inputs, then tanh. Together they move each point to a new place, h. The output neuron is an ordinary logistic regression on h: σ turns its score into the probability of orange. These weights were set by hand for this picture; from section 6 on, the network finds its own.
Say it“h is tanh of W x plus b; y-hat is sigma of v dot h plus c.”

Layers

The forward pass, layer by layer

A multilayer perceptron is a chain of layers. Each layer takes the numbers coming in, forms one weighted sum per neuron (a matrix times a vector, plus a bias), and bends each sum with an activation function φ. Without the bend, two layers would collapse into one matrix and the network would be linear again. The last layer gives one score per class, and softmax turns the scores into probabilities. Below: 2 inputs, 3 hidden neurons, 2 classes, 17 numbers to learn. Move the inputs, click any connection to change its weight, and switch the activation.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{a} &= W^{(1)}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{(1)}, & \mathbf{h} &= \varphi(\mathbf{a}) \\ \mathbf{z} &= W^{(2)}\mathbf{h} + \mathbf{b}^{(2)}, & p_k &= \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: W(1) is 3×2 (one row per hidden neuron), W(2) is 2×3 (one row per class). a are the hidden neurons’ raw sums, h their outputs, z the class scores. Softmax exponentiates the scores, so they are positive, and divides by their total, so they add up to 1.
Say it“a is W-one x plus b-one, h is phi of a; z is W-two h plus b-two; p-k is e to the z-k over the sum of e to the z-j.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \tanh(a) &= \frac{e^{a}-e^{-a}}{e^{a}+e^{-a}} \\ \sigma(a) &= \frac{1}{1+e^{-a}} \\ \operatorname{ReLU}(a) &= \max(0,\ a) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: three common bends. tanh squashes into (−1, 1) and is centred on zero; the sigmoid squashes into (0, 1); ReLU (rectified linear unit) keeps positive sums and zeroes negative ones. ReLU is the default in modern deep networks because it is cheap and does not flatten out for large inputs.
Say it“tanh of a; sigma of a is one over one plus e to the minus a; ReLU of a is the max of zero and a.”

Width

Enough neurons make any curve

How much can one hidden layer do? Take one input x and one output. Each hidden neuron contributes one bent piece: a ReLU is a hinge that switches on at some x, a sigmoid is a soft step. The output adds the pieces up with its own weights. More neurons, more pieces, finer detail. Pick a target and add neurons; move the mouse over the plot to see the sum built term by term.

\[ f(x) = c + \sum_{j=1}^{N} v_j\,\varphi(w_j x + b_j) \]
Read it as: N hidden neurons, each with an input weight wj and bias bj that place and stretch its bend, and an output weight vj that sets how much of it to add. That is 3N + 1 numbers. To isolate the idea, this lab places the bends evenly by hand and solves only for the vj and c, by least squares; backpropagation would move the bends as well.
Say it“f of x is c plus the sum over j of v-j times phi of w-j x plus b-j.”
RMSE from the target
numbers in the network

This is the universal approximation theorem (Cybenko 1989; Hornik, Stinchcombe & White 1989): one hidden layer with enough neurons can approximate any continuous function on a bounded range as closely as you like. It is a promise of existence, not a recipe. It does not say how many neurons you need (for some functions, exponentially many) or that training will find the weights. In practice, deeper networks reach the same accuracy with far fewer neurons, because later layers reuse the pieces built by earlier ones.

Loss functions

Loss: one number for how wrong

To improve, the network needs a single number to push down: the loss. For regression, squared error is natural. For classification the standard choice is cross-entropy: minus the log of the probability the network gave to the right answer. It is the log-loss of logistic regression (chapter 1.3) again. Why not squared error on the probability? Look at the gradients, the signal that learning runs on. Push the score z deep into the wrong side and compare.

\[ \begin{aligned} L_{\text{CE}} &= -y\log p \\ &\quad - (1-y)\log(1-p) \\ \frac{\partial L_{\text{CE}}}{\partial z} &= p - y, \qquad p = \sigma(z) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: y is 1 or 0; p is the predicted probability of 1. The loss is −log of the probability given to the truth, so a confident wrong answer costs a lot. Its gradient with respect to the score z is just the error p − y: never small when the answer is badly wrong.
Say it“Cross-entropy is minus y log p minus one-minus-y log one-minus-p; its slope in z is p minus y.”
\[ \begin{aligned} L_{\text{SE}} &= \tfrac12\,(p - y)^2 \\ \frac{\partial L_{\text{SE}}}{\partial z} &= (p - y)\,p\,(1-p) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same error p − y, but multiplied by σ′(z) = p(1 − p), which is nearly zero whenever the sigmoid is saturated. A network that is confidently wrong gets almost no push to change: learning stalls exactly when it is needed most.
Say it“Squared error is half p minus y squared; its slope in z is p minus y, times p, times one minus p.”

\[ \begin{aligned} L &= -\log p_{y} \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_k} &= p_k - [k = y] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with many classes and softmax, cross-entropy is minus the log of the probability of the true class y. The gradient with respect to each score is again “predicted minus true”: pk for the wrong classes (push them down), py − 1 for the right one (push it up). [k = y] is 1 when k is the true class and 0 otherwise. This tidy error is where backpropagation starts.
Say it“L is minus log p-y; the slope in z-k is p-k minus one if k is the true class, else p-k.”
Calculus

The chain rule passes the blame backwards

Gradient descent needs ∂L/∂w for every weight. A weight deep inside the network affects the loss only through a chain of steps: it changes a sum, which changes a neuron’s output, which changes the score, which changes the loss. The chain rule says the overall rate of change is the product of the local rates along the chain. Here is the smallest chain worth studying: f = w₂ tanh(w₁x) with x = 1.5 and target y = 0.8. Drag the point on the loss landscape, then check the chain rule against a real nudge.

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} &= \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial f}}_{f-y}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\partial f}{\partial h}}_{w_2}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\partial h}{\partial a}}_{1-h^2}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\partial a}{\partial w_1}}_{x} \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} &= \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial f}}_{f-y}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\partial f}{\partial w_2}}_{h} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a = w₁x, h = tanh(a), f = w₂h, L = ½(f − y)². Each factor is easy on its own: the derivative of one step with respect to its input. Multiply them from the loss backwards and you have the gradient. Notice the reuse: (f − y) appears in both gradients, and in a big network every shared factor is computed once.
Say it“dL by dw-one is dL by df, times df by dh, times dh by da, times da by dw-one.”

The algorithm

Backpropagation, step by step

Backpropagation is the chain rule, organised. One forward pass stores every sum and every output. Then one backward pass carries an error signal δ from the loss towards the inputs, layer by layer; each layer’s δ is reused for all the weights feeding it, and then passed back once more. The whole backward pass costs about twice a forward pass however many weights there are, which is why it scales to billions of them. This is the network from the forward-pass section, with the input you set there. Press Next step and follow the numbers.

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol\delta^{(2)} &= \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y} \\ \boldsymbol\delta^{(1)} &= \big(W^{(2)\top}\boldsymbol\delta^{(2)}\big) \odot \varphi'(\mathbf{a}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: δ is ∂L with respect to a layer’s sums. At the output it is the softmax error. To move back a layer, send each output error back along the same weights (the transpose W⊤), add up what reaches each hidden neuron, and multiply (⊙, element by element) by the slope of its activation at its sum.
Say it“Delta-two is p minus y; delta-one is W-two transpose delta-two, times phi-prime of a, element by element.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(l)}} &= \boldsymbol\delta^{(l)}\,\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)\top} \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{b}^{(l)}} &= \boldsymbol\delta^{(l)} \\ W &\leftarrow W - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial W} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the gradient of the weight from neuron i into neuron j is (j’s error) × (i’s output): blame is shared in proportion to how loudly i was speaking. h(0) is the input x. Then every weight takes a small step of size η (the learning rate) against its gradient.
Say it“dL by dW-l is delta-l times h of l-minus-one transposed; dL by db-l is delta-l; W becomes W minus eta times its gradient.”

    Every gradient, checked by nudging

    The safest test of backpropagation code is a gradient check: nudge one weight up and down by ε = 10−5, rerun the forward pass, and compare (L+ − L−)/2ε with the backprop value. They should agree to about 10−10. (With ReLU, a nudge across the kink at zero can disagree; that is the kink, not a bug.)

    Playground

    Train a network yourself

    Put it together: forward pass on every training point, average the loss, backpropagate, step every weight, repeat. One full pass is an epoch. Pick a dataset that no straight line can split, choose the shape of the network, and press Train. The small squares show what each neuron of the first hidden layer responds to: watch them specialise.

    \[ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla_\theta \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} L\big(f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i),\ y_i\big) \]
    Read it as: θ is every weight and bias in the network. Average the cross-entropy over all n training points, get its gradient by backpropagation, and step against it with learning rate η. Too small an η crawls; too large an η overshoots and the loss jumps around. (Chapter 2.3 replaces this plain, full-batch step with faster variants.)
    Say it“Theta becomes theta minus eta times the gradient of the average loss.”

    Going deeper

    Deep chains and vanishing gradients

    Backpropagation multiplies: every layer the error passes through multiplies it by that layer’s weights and by the slopes of its activations. Many factors below 1 shrink the signal exponentially, so the first layers barely learn: the vanishing gradient. Many factors above 1 blow it up: the exploding gradient. This stopped deep networks for two decades. Below, a fresh network of 32-neuron layers sends one batch of random inputs forward and its error back. Add layers, and switch the activation.

    \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol\delta^{(l)} &= \varphi'(\mathbf{a}^{(l)}) \odot W^{(l+1)\top}\boldsymbol\delta^{(l+1)} \\ \boldsymbol\delta^{(1)} &\leftarrow \boldsymbol\delta^{(2)} \leftarrow \cdots \leftarrow \boldsymbol\delta^{(L)} \\ \max_a \sigma'(a) &= \tfrac14, \quad \max_a \tanh'(a) = 1 \\ \operatorname{ReLU}'(a) &\in \{0, 1\} \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: each step back multiplies the error by one layer’s transposed weights and one layer’s activation slopes, so the error reaching layer 1 has been multiplied L − 1 times. A sigmoid’s slope is at most ¼, so with ordinary weights each sigmoid layer cuts the signal to a fraction of itself. tanh’s slope is 1 at zero, and ReLU’s is exactly 1 for active neurons; with weights scaled to keep the signal’s size steady (the gain), they carry gradients much deeper. Too large a gain and the same product explodes.
    Say it“Delta-l is phi-prime of a-l times W-l-plus-one transpose delta-l-plus-one; delta-one is reached from delta-L one layer at a time.”

    Real-world project

    Project: reading handwritten digits

    In the 1990s Alpaydin and Kaynak, at Boğaziçi University in Istanbul, collected handwritten digits from 43 people on preprinted forms. Each digit was scanned to a 32×32 black-and-white bitmap and then counted in 4×4 blocks, giving an 8×8 image with values 0 to 16. scikit-learn ships 1,797 of them: the test part of the UCI “optdigits” data, written by 13 of those people. We split them once, stratified by digit: 1,347 to learn from, 450 to test on, and scale the pixels to 0–1. The network is 64 inputs → 32 tanh neurons → 10 softmax outputs: 2,410 weights and biases. It starts from exactly the weights in the numpy code at the bottom (Glorot-uniform random numbers rounded to 4 decimals, exported into this page), so your browser and numpy should walk the same path, step for step.

    The same project in numpy

    import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

d = load_digits()                                   # 1,797 handwritten digits, 8 x 8 pixels, values 0..16
X, y = d.data / 16, d.target
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=0)
n, Y = len(y_tr), np.eye(10)[y_tr]                  # 1,347 to learn from, 450 to test on

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)                      # the page's starting weights (Glorot uniform, 4 d.p.)
W1 = np.round(rng.uniform(-np.sqrt(6/96), np.sqrt(6/96), (64, 32)), 4); b1 = np.zeros(32)
W2 = np.round(rng.uniform(-np.sqrt(6/42), np.sqrt(6/42), (32, 10)), 4); b2 = np.zeros(10)

def forward(X):
    H = np.tanh(X @ W1 + b1)                        # hidden layer
    Z = H @ W2 + b2                                 # output scores
    P = np.exp(Z - Z.max(1, keepdims=True))
    return H, P / P.sum(1, keepdims=True)           # softmax

def backward(H, P):
    dZ = (P - Y) / n                                # softmax + cross-entropy
    dA = (dZ @ W2.T) * (1 - H**2)                   # back through tanh
    return X_tr.T @ dA, dA.sum(0), H.T @ dZ, dZ.sum(0)

loss = lambda: -np.mean(np.log(forward(X_tr)[1][np.arange(n), y_tr]))
gW1 = backward(*forward(X_tr))[0]                   # gradient check at the start
W1[20, 5] += 1e-5; up = loss(); W1[20, 5] -= 2e-5; down = loss(); W1[20, 5] += 1e-5
print(f"dL/dW1[20,5]: backprop {gW1[20, 5]:.12f}   finite difference {(up - down) / 2e-5:.12f}")

lr = 1.0
for epoch in range(401):
    H, P = forward(X_tr)
    if epoch in (0, 1, 10, 50, 100, 200, 400):
        acc = (forward(X_te)[1].argmax(1) == y_te).mean()
        print(f"epoch {epoch:3d}   loss {loss():.12f}   test accuracy {acc:.4f}")
    gW1, gb1, gW2, gb2 = backward(H, P)
    W1 -= lr * gW1; b1 -= lr * gb1; W2 -= lr * gW2; b2 -= lr * gb2

    The page and numpy compute the same sums in a different order (numpy hands matrix products to an optimised BLAS library), so their numbers differ in the last digit or two, around 10−15. At η = 0.1, 0.3 and 1 those differences stay that small for all 400 epochs. At η = 2 the loss overshoots and bounces for a while, and that instability magnifies the tiny rounding differences: the two runs drift apart by a few thousandths before both settle at the same test accuracy. At η = 3 training diverges and the two runs end in different places. That is a fact about unstable gradient descent, not a bug in either program. The hero at the top of the page uses numpy’s weights after 400 epochs at η = 1, rounded to 5 decimals; they give the same 97.56% test accuracy. One caveat about that number: the 450 test digits were written by the same 13 people as the training digits, so it flatters the network. Your own handwriting, through a mouse and a different scanner pipeline, is a harder test, as the pad will show.

    Wrap-up

    Five lines to remember

    • A neural network is layers of weighted sums, each followed by a non-linear activation; hidden layers move the data into new coordinates where a simple cut works.
    • With enough hidden neurons, one layer can approximate any continuous function, but depth builds the same shapes far more cheaply.
    • Classifiers train on cross-entropy: its gradient at the scores is simply p − y, and it never goes quiet when the network is confidently wrong.
    • Backpropagation is the chain rule, organised: one forward pass, then one backward pass that sends δ = (W⊤δ) ⊙ φ′(a) down the layers; each weight’s gradient is (its neuron’s δ) × (its input).
    • Always gradient-check new backprop code, and watch for gradients that vanish or explode with depth. On real handwriting, 2,410 weights trained this way read 97.6% of unseen digits correctly.

    Check your understanding

    Why does a network with no activation functions, however many layers, only draw straight boundaries?

    Each layer is a matrix times a vector plus a bias. A composition of such maps is again one matrix and one bias: W2(W1x + b1) + b2 = (W2W1)x + (W2b1 + b2). Without a bend between them, the extra layers add nothing.

    A layer has 64 inputs and 32 neurons. How many numbers does it learn, and what shape is ∂L/∂W?

    64 × 32 = 2,048 weights plus 32 biases, 2,080 numbers. ∂L/∂W has the same shape as W, because every weight gets exactly one gradient, δjhi.

    A sigmoid output is at p = 0.001 for an example whose label is 1. Compare the gradient at the score z under cross-entropy and under squared error.

    Cross-entropy: p − y = −0.999, a strong push. Squared error: (p − y)p(1 − p) ≈ −0.999 × 0.001 × 0.999 ≈ −0.001, about a thousand times weaker. The network would barely move.

    Your backprop gradient for one weight is 0.0123 and the finite-difference estimate is −0.0123. What is the most likely bug?

    A sign error, for example computing y − p instead of p − y, or adding the gradient in the update instead of subtracting it. Equal size with opposite sign almost never comes from anything else.

    A 20-layer sigmoid network learns in its last layers but its first layers hardly change. Why, and name two remedies.

    Vanishing gradients: the error is multiplied by σ′ ≤ ¼ and by the weights at every layer on its way down, so it is tiny by layer 1. Remedies: ReLU-family activations, initialisation scaled to the layer size (Glorot or He), normalisation layers, and residual connections (chapters 2.3 and 2.4).
