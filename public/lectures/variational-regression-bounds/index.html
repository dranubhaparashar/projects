Variational Regression & Local Bounds

Linear and logistic regression, solved by bounding what you cannot integrate

Bayesian logistic regression asks for integrals of the sigmoid against a Gaussian, and none of them has a formula. The local variational trick replaces the sigmoid by a curve that never rises above it and touches it at two chosen points, ±ξ. That curve is the exponential of a quadratic, so against a Gaussian it integrates in closed form, and the area it gives is a guaranteed underestimate that can be made tight by choosing ξ well. The lower panel shades both areas under a Gaussian p(a). Drag the touching point along the sigmoid, and drag the Gaussian’s peak or shoulder; then press Best ξ.

PRML 10.3, 10.3.1 · variational linear regression

A posterior for the weights and for α

Bayesian linear regression with a Gaussian prior w ~ N(0, α−1I) has a Gaussian posterior, as long as the prior precision α is a fixed number. The evidence framework of chapter 1.2e chose α by maximising the marginal likelihood. A fully Bayesian treatment gives α a prior of its own, a Gamma distribution Gam(α | a0, b0), and asks for the joint posterior p(w, α | t). That posterior has no standard form: integrating w out leaves a function of α that no named distribution matches. We keep the noise precision β fixed and known throughout, as the textbook does, so that the new ideas stand out.

Mean-field variational inference from chapter 6.10 handles it. Look for q(w, α) = q(w) q(α) and update each factor in turn: ln q(α) is the expected log joint over q(w), and ln q(w) is the expected log joint over q(α). Because the prior is conjugate, both come out in closed form: q(α) is a Gamma whose shape grows by M/2 and whose rate grows by half the expected squared length of w, and q(w) is a Gaussian that is exactly the fixed-α posterior with α replaced by its current mean E[α]. Each round can only improve the fit of q to the true posterior. The lab fits a polynomial of degree d (M = d + 1 basis functions, powers of u = 2x − 1) to ten noisy points from sin(2πx), starting from a deliberately weak prior, E[α] = 10−4. Press Step to make one round of updates, then drag the points or change the degree.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf t \mid \mathbf w) &= \prod_{n=1}^{N} \mathcal N\big(t_n \mid \mathbf w^{\top}\boldsymbol\phi_n,\ \beta^{-1}\big) \\ p(\mathbf w \mid \alpha) &= \mathcal N\big(\mathbf w \mid \mathbf 0,\ \alpha^{-1}\mathbf I\big) \\ p(\alpha) &= \operatorname{Gam}(\alpha \mid a_0, b_0) \\ q(\mathbf w, \alpha) &= q(\mathbf w)\, q(\alpha) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the targets are the weighted sum of basis functions plus Gaussian noise of precision β; the weights have an isotropic Gaussian prior of precision α; and α itself has a Gamma prior. We approximate the joint posterior by a product of one factor for w and one for α. Here a0 = b0 = 0.01, a broad prior with mean 1.
Say it“Gaussian likelihood, Gaussian prior on w with precision alpha, Gamma prior on alpha; q factorises into q of w times q of alpha.”
\[ \begin{aligned} q^\star(\alpha) &= \operatorname{Gam}(\alpha \mid a_N, b_N) \\ a_N &= a_0 + \tfrac{M}{2} \\ b_N &= b_0 + \tfrac12\, \mathbb E[\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf w] \\ \mathbb E[\alpha] &= a_N / b_N \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the Gamma’s shape counts the weights, half a unit of evidence per weight; its rate adds up how large the weights are expected to be. Big weights push E[α] down, small weights push it up.
Say it“a-N is a-nought plus M over two; b-N is b-nought plus half the expected squared length of w.”
\[ \begin{aligned} q^\star(\mathbf w) &= \mathcal N(\mathbf w \mid \mathbf m_N, \mathbf S_N) \\ \mathbf S_N^{-1} &= \mathbb E[\alpha]\,\mathbf I + \beta\, \boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\boldsymbol\Phi \\ \mathbf m_N &= \beta\, \mathbf S_N \boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\mathbf t \\ \mathbb E[\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf w] &= \mathbf m_N^{\top}\mathbf m_N + \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf S_N) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the usual Bayesian-regression posterior, with the unknown α replaced by its current expected value. The expected squared length of w is the squared length of the mean plus the total posterior variance, and it feeds straight back into bN.
Say it“S-N inverse is E-alpha times the identity plus beta Phi-transpose Phi; m-N is beta S-N Phi-transpose t.”

A comparison worth knowing: if the hyperprior is made infinitely broad, a0 = b0 = 0, the update becomes E[α] = M / E[w⊤w]. That is exactly the re-estimation step of the EM algorithm that maximises the evidence over α, so the evidence framework and this variational treatment then reach the same α. With a proper hyperprior the two differ slightly, and the variational version also hands back a whole distribution q(α) rather than a single value.

PRML 10.3.2 · predictive distribution

Error bars from q(w)

To predict a new target we average the noise model over the approximate posterior. Since q(w) is Gaussian and the noise model is Gaussian in w, the integral is the same one as in ordinary Bayesian regression: the predictive is a Gaussian centred on the posterior-mean curve. Its variance has two parts. One is the noise 1/β, which no amount of data removes. The other, φ(x)⊤SNφ(x), is the uncertainty about w: small where data pin the curve down, large in gaps and beyond the ends, and shrinking as N grows. The factor q(α) enters only through SN, which was built with E[α].

The lab draws points one by one from a fixed pool and refits q(w)q(α) to convergence each time. Drag the probe line across the plot, change N, and switch on the gap to see the weight term swell where there is no data.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(t \mid x, \mathbf t) &\simeq \int p(t \mid x, \mathbf w)\, q(\mathbf w)\, d\mathbf w \\ &= \mathcal N\big(t \mid \mathbf m_N^{\top}\boldsymbol\phi(x),\ \sigma^2(x)\big) \\ \sigma^2(x) &= \frac{1}{\beta} + \boldsymbol\phi(x)^{\top}\mathbf S_N\, \boldsymbol\phi(x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: replace the true posterior by q(w) and integrate the weights out. The answer is Gaussian with mean the posterior-mean curve and variance the noise plus the posterior variance of the curve’s height at x.
Say it“Sigma-squared of x is one over beta plus phi-transpose S-N phi.”

PRML 10.3.3 · the lower bound

The bound as a convergence check and a model score

The quantity mean-field updates climb is the lower bound L(q) = E[ln p(t, w, α)] − E[ln q(w, α)]. For this model every expectation in it has a closed form, so it splits into five readable terms: how well the curve fits the data, how plausible the weights are under the prior precision, how plausible that precision is under its hyperprior, and the entropies of q(w) and q(α). The Gamma terms need the expected precision, written ᾱ = E[α] = aN/bN, and the expected log precision E[ln α] = ψ(aN) − ln bN, written with an overline, where ψ is the digamma function.

The bound has two jobs. First, it is a bug detector: every update maximises it over one factor, so it must never go down, and a decrease means a mistake in the code. Second, since ln p(t) = L(q) + KL(q ‖ p), it is a lower estimate of the log evidence, which can be compared across models such as polynomial degrees. For this model the exact ln p(t) is available too: given α, the evidence is Gaussian, and the remaining one-dimensional integral over α can be done numerically. So the lab shows the exact evidence next to the bound, and the distance between them is the KL divergence that the factorised q cannot remove. Click a bar to see that degree’s bound climb, round by round, and drag points in the first lab to change the data.

\[ \begin{aligned} L(q) &= \mathbb E[\ln p] - \mathbb E[\ln q] \\ &= \text{the five terms below} \\ \bar\alpha &= a_N / b_N \\ \overline{\ln\alpha} &= \psi(a_N) - \ln b_N \\ \mathbb E[\ln p(\mathbf t\mid\mathbf w)] &= \tfrac N2 \ln\tfrac{\beta}{2\pi} - \tfrac\beta2 \mathbf t^{\top}\mathbf t \\ &\quad + \beta\, \mathbf m_N^{\top}\boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\mathbf t \\ &\quad - \tfrac\beta2 \operatorname{Tr}\!\big[\boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\boldsymbol\Phi\,\mathbb E[\mathbf w\mathbf w^{\top}]\big] \\ \mathbb E[\mathbf w\mathbf w^{\top}] &= \mathbf m_N\mathbf m_N^{\top} + \mathbf S_N \\ \mathbb E[\ln p(\mathbf w\mid\alpha)] &= -\tfrac M2 \ln 2\pi + \tfrac M2\, \overline{\ln\alpha} \\ &\quad - \tfrac{\bar\alpha}{2}\, \mathbb E[\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf w] \\ \mathbb E[\ln p(\alpha)] &= a_0 \ln b_0 - \ln\Gamma(a_0) \\ &\quad + (a_0 - 1)\,\overline{\ln\alpha} - b_0 \bar\alpha \\ -\mathbb E[\ln q(\mathbf w)] &= \tfrac12 \ln\lvert\mathbf S_N\rvert \\ &\quad + \tfrac M2 (1 + \ln 2\pi) \\ -\mathbb E[\ln q(\alpha)] &= \ln\Gamma(a_N) - \ln b_N + a_N \\ &\quad - (a_N - 1)\psi(a_N) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first term rewards fitting the targets and is penalised by the spread of the curve; the second rewards small weights when α is large; the third keeps α plausible under its hyperprior; the last two are the entropies of the two factors, which reward q for not being over-confident.
Say it“L is the expected log joint minus the expected log of q, term by term.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf t \mid \alpha) &= \mathcal N\big(\mathbf t \mid \mathbf 0,\ \mathbf C_\alpha\big) \\ \mathbf C_\alpha &= \beta^{-1}\mathbf I + \alpha^{-1}\boldsymbol\Phi\boldsymbol\Phi^{\top} \\ \ln p(\mathbf t) &= \ln \int_0^\infty p(\mathbf t \mid \alpha)\, p(\alpha)\, d\alpha \\ &= L(q) + \operatorname{KL}\big(q \,\Vert\, p(\mathbf w, \alpha \mid \mathbf t)\big) \\ &\ge L(q) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for a fixed α the weights integrate out to a Gaussian over all N targets at once; what remains is a single integral over α, computed here on a fine grid of ln α. The gap between the two numbers is exactly how far the factorised q is from the true posterior, measured by KL.
Say it“Log evidence equals the bound plus the KL divergence, so it is never below the bound.”

PRML 10.5 · local variational methods

Tangent lines and convex conjugates

Mean field is a global approximation: it replaces the whole posterior by a simpler family. A local variational method works on one awkward factor at a time, replacing it by a simpler function that bounds it, at the price of one new adjustable number per factor. The tool behind it is convex duality. A convex function lies above every one of its tangent lines. A tangent with slope λ is the line λx minus an intercept g(λ), and the intercept that makes it touch is the largest gap between the line λx and the curve: g(λ) = maxx{λx − f(x)}, the convex conjugate. Each λ gives a lower bound that is exact at one point, and taking the best λ for each x gives f back exactly.

Concave functions work the same way with every inequality reversed: they lie below their tangents, giving upper bounds and a minimum in place of the maximum. A function that is neither can often be made one or the other by an invertible change, such as taking a logarithm or rewriting it as a function of x², which is exactly how the sigmoid will be handled next. The classic example is f(x) = e−x: the tangent at ξ has slope λ = −e−ξ, and the conjugate works out as g(λ) = λ − λ ln(−λ). Drag the touching point along the curve, and switch functions.

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x) &\ge \lambda x - g(\lambda) \quad \text{for every } \lambda \\ g(\lambda) &= \max_{x}\ \{\lambda x - f(x)\} \\ f(x) &= \max_{\lambda}\ \{\lambda x - g(\lambda)\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for a convex f, any line with slope λ lowered by g(λ) stays under the curve; g(λ) is the biggest amount by which the line λx pokes above f. Running the same construction on g recovers f: the two functions are each other’s conjugates.
Say it“f of x is at least lambda x minus g of lambda; g is the max over x of lambda x minus f.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{concave } f\text{:} \\ f(x) &= \min_{\lambda}\ \{\lambda x - g(\lambda)\} \\ g(\lambda) &= \min_{x}\ \{\lambda x - f(x)\} \\ &\text{example } f(x) = e^{-x}\text{:} \\ g(\lambda) &= \lambda - \lambda \ln(-\lambda), \ \ \lambda < 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for a concave f the tangent lines lie above, so each one is an upper bound and the best is the smallest. For e−x, setting the derivative of λx − e−x to zero gives x = −ln(−λ), and substituting back gives the conjugate.
Say it“For concave functions, max becomes min and lower bounds become upper bounds.”

PRML 10.5 · bounds on the logistic sigmoid

Two bounds on the sigmoid

The logistic sigmoid σ(x) = 1/(1 + e−x) is neither convex nor concave, so neither tangent trick applies directly. Two changes of view each unlock one.

An upper bound. The log-sigmoid, ln σ(x), is concave. Its tangent lines therefore lie above it, and its conjugate turns out to be the binary entropy g(η) = −η ln η − (1 − η) ln(1 − η). Exponentiating gives σ(x) ≤ exp(ηx − g(η)), an exponential that touches the sigmoid where the tangent touches.

A lower bound, due to Jaakkola and Jordan, is the one that matters for regression. Pull a factor ex/2 out of the sigmoid: ln σ(x) = x/2 − ln(ex/2 + e−x/2). The second piece is even in x, and as a function of y = x² it is convex. A tangent in y is a lower bound, and in terms of x it is a quadratic. So σ(x) is bounded below by the exponential of a quadratic in x, touching at both ±ξ, with curvature set by λ(ξ) = (σ(ξ) − ½)/(2ξ). Slide ξ and flip to the log view: in log space the upper bound is a straight line and the lower bound is a downward parabola.

\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma(x) &\le \exp\big(\eta x - g(\eta)\big) \\ g(\eta) &= -\eta \ln \eta - (1 - \eta)\ln(1 - \eta) \\ \eta &= \sigma(-\xi) \ \text{ touches at } x = \xi \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the tangent to the concave curve ln σ at ξ has slope 1 − σ(ξ) = σ(−ξ); its intercept is minus the binary entropy of that slope. Raised to the exponent, it is an exponential curve lying on or above the sigmoid everywhere.
Say it“Sigma of x is at most e to the eta x minus the entropy of eta.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma(x) &\ge \sigma(\xi)\, \exp\!\Big\{\frac{x - \xi}{2} \\ &\qquad - \lambda(\xi)\big(x^2 - \xi^2\big)\Big\} \\ \lambda(\xi) &= \frac{1}{2\xi}\Big(\sigma(\xi) - \frac12\Big) \\ &= \frac{\tanh(\xi/2)}{4\xi} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the bound equals the sigmoid at x = ξ and at x = −ξ and lies below it everywhere else. In the exponent x appears only linearly and squared, so multiplied by a Gaussian in x it is still a Gaussian shape. λ(ξ) starts at 1/8 for ξ = 0 and falls towards 0 as ξ grows.
Say it“Sigma of x is at least sigma of xi times e to the x minus xi over two, minus lambda of xi times x-squared minus xi-squared.”

The payoff: an integral you can now do

Why accept a bound instead of the real thing? Because of integrals like I = ∫ σ(a) N(a | μ, s²) da, which is exactly the shape of a Bayesian logistic prediction. It has no closed form. Replace σ by its lower bound and the integrand becomes a Gaussian times the exponential of a quadratic, which integrates exactly, so F(ξ) ≤ I is a formula. The bound’s parameter ξ is still free; the best choice maximises F(ξ), and setting the derivative to zero gives a simple fixed point, ξ² = E[a²] under the tilted Gaussian. The price is that F(ξ) depends on a variable that is not in the original problem, and the bound is never better than its best ξ. Move μ and s and compare the best bound with the exact integral and with the probit approximation from chapter 1.3b, which is not a bound but is often closer.

\[ \begin{aligned} I &= \int \sigma(a)\, \mathcal N(a \mid \mu, s^2)\, da \;\ge\; F(\xi) \\ F(\xi) &= \sigma(\xi)\, e^{-\xi/2 + \lambda\xi^2} \\ &\quad \times \int e^{a/2 - \lambda a^2}\, \mathcal N(a \mid \mu, s^2)\, da \\ &= \frac{\sigma(\xi)\, e^{-\xi/2 + \lambda\xi^2}}{\sqrt{1 + 2\lambda s^2}}\; e^{h^2/2P \,-\, \mu^2/2s^2} \\ P &= \tfrac{1}{s^2} + 2\lambda, \quad h = \tfrac{\mu}{s^2} + \tfrac12 \\ \lambda &= \lambda(\xi) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: integrate the lower bound instead of the sigmoid. The Gaussian and the bound’s quadratic exponent combine into one Gaussian with precision P and linear coefficient h, whose integral is the familiar completing-the-square result. Maximising over ξ gives the tightest such bound.
Say it“F of xi, the integral of the bound, is a lower bound on I; pick xi to make it as large as possible.”

PRML 10.6, 10.6.1 · variational logistic regression

A Gaussian posterior for logistic weights

Bayesian logistic regression multiplies a Gaussian prior by N sigmoids, and the product is not Gaussian, so neither its normaliser (the evidence) nor its predictions can be computed exactly. The Laplace approximation (chapter 1.3b) fitted a Gaussian to the curvature at the peak. The variational approach does something different: it writes each likelihood factor as p(t|w) = eatσ(−a), with activation a = w⊤φ, and replaces σ(−a) by its Jaakkola–Jordan bound, giving each data point its own touching parameter ξn.

Now every factor is the exponential of a quadratic in w. Multiplied by the Gaussian prior, the whole product is a Gaussian shape in w, and normalising it gives q(w) = N(mN, SN) in closed form. Each data point adds 2λ(ξn)φnφn⊤ to the precision and (tn − ½)φn to the linear term, so the update looks like weighted least squares. Because the bound sits under the joint distribution everywhere, its integral is a lower bound on the evidence, which the Laplace method cannot promise. The lab uses two inputs and no bias, so the weight space is a plane and the true posterior can be computed on a grid for comparison. Drag the points, click empty space to add one of the chosen class, and compare the ellipses with the true posterior.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(t \mid \mathbf w) &= e^{at}\sigma(-a), \quad a = \mathbf w^{\top}\boldsymbol\phi \\ &\ge e^{at}\sigma(\xi)\exp\!\Big\{{-\frac{a + \xi}{2}} \\ &\qquad - \lambda(\xi)\big(a^2 - \xi^2\big)\Big\} \\ p(\mathbf t, \mathbf w) &\ge h(\mathbf w, \boldsymbol\xi)\, p(\mathbf w) \\ \ln h &= \sum_n \Big\{ \ln\sigma(\xi_n) + \big(t_n - \tfrac12\big) a_n \\ &\qquad - \tfrac{\xi_n}{2} - \lambda(\xi_n)\big(a_n^2 - \xi_n^2\big) \Big\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for t ∈ {0, 1}, σ(a)t(1 − σ(a))1−t equals eatσ(−a). Bounding σ(−a) below, one factor per data point, gives a function h of w that is quadratic in the exponent and lies under the likelihood everywhere.
Say it“The joint is at least h of w and xi times the prior, and log h is a quadratic in w.”
\[ \begin{aligned} q(\mathbf w) &= \mathcal N(\mathbf w \mid \mathbf m_N, \mathbf S_N) \\ \mathbf S_N^{-1} &= \mathbf S_0^{-1} + 2\sum_n \lambda(\xi_n)\, \boldsymbol\phi_n\boldsymbol\phi_n^{\top} \\ \mathbf m_N &= \mathbf S_N\Big(\mathbf S_0^{-1}\mathbf m_0 \\ &\qquad + \sum_n \big(t_n - \tfrac12\big)\boldsymbol\phi_n\Big) \\ p(t{=}1 \mid \boldsymbol\phi) &\simeq \sigma\big(\kappa(\sigma_a^2)\,\mu_a\big) \\ \mu_a &= \mathbf m_N^{\top}\boldsymbol\phi, \quad \sigma_a^2 = \boldsymbol\phi^{\top}\mathbf S_N\boldsymbol\phi \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: collect the quadratic and linear terms in w and complete the square. Here the prior is m0 = 0, S0 = α−1I. To predict, the activation under q is Gaussian with mean μa and variance σa², and the probit approximation, κ(σ²) = (1 + πσ²/8)−1/2, squashes it towards ½ when the weights are uncertain.
Say it“S-N inverse is the prior precision plus twice lambda of xi-n phi-n phi-n-transpose, summed over the data.”

PRML 10.6.2 · optimising the variational parameters

Tightening every ξ

The posterior above depended on N numbers ξn, one per data point, and they were already tuned. How? The bound on ln p(t) is a function of the ξs, so we maximise it. One route is EM, treating w as the hidden variable. The E-step computes q(w) with the current ξs. The M-step maximises the expected log of h(w, ξ)p(w) under that q, and setting its derivative to zero gives a beautifully simple answer: ξn² = E[an²], the expected squared activation of point n under q. Each bound is re-centred where its activation is likely to be. The other route integrates w out to get L(ξ) as an explicit formula and differentiates it directly; it leads to the same re-estimation equation.

The bound depends on ξ only through ξ², so the sign does not matter, and either route can only increase L(ξ) at each round. The lab uses a single input with a bias, so w = (w0, w1), the prior is N(0, I), and the exact log evidence is again computed on a grid as a ceiling. Press Step and watch each ξ jump to the root mean square of its activation; drag a point sideways, or up and down to flip its label.

\[ \begin{aligned} Q(\boldsymbol\xi) &= \sum_n \Big\{ \ln\sigma(\xi_n) - \tfrac{\xi_n}{2} \\ &\quad - \lambda(\xi_n)\big(\boldsymbol\phi_n^{\top}\mathbb E[\mathbf w\mathbf w^{\top}]\boldsymbol\phi_n - \xi_n^2\big) \Big\} \\ &\quad + \text{const} \\ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial \xi_n} &= \lambda'(\xi_n)\big(\xi_n^2 - \boldsymbol\phi_n^{\top}\mathbb E[\mathbf w\mathbf w^{\top}]\boldsymbol\phi_n\big) \\ (\xi_n^{\text{new}})^2 &= \boldsymbol\phi_n^{\top}\big(\mathbf S_N + \mathbf m_N\mathbf m_N^{\top}\big)\boldsymbol\phi_n \\ &= \mathbb E[a_n^2] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: keep the terms of the expected complete-data log likelihood that involve ξ. Since λ(ξ) strictly decreases for ξ > 0, λ′(ξ) is never zero there, so the only stationary point is where ξn² matches the second moment of an: the variance φ⊤SNφ plus the squared mean.
Say it“The new xi-n squared is phi-n-transpose times S-N plus m-N m-N-transpose times phi-n.”
\[ \begin{aligned} L(\boldsymbol\xi) &= \tfrac12 \ln\frac{\lvert\mathbf S_N\rvert}{\lvert\mathbf S_0\rvert} + \tfrac12 \mathbf m_N^{\top}\mathbf S_N^{-1}\mathbf m_N \\ &\quad - \tfrac12 \mathbf m_0^{\top}\mathbf S_0^{-1}\mathbf m_0 \\ &\quad + \sum_n \Big\{\ln\sigma(\xi_n) - \tfrac{\xi_n}{2} + \lambda(\xi_n)\,\xi_n^2\Big\} \\ &\le \ln p(\mathbf t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the integral of h(w, ξ)p(w) over w, done exactly because the integrand is a Gaussian shape. The first line is the familiar Gaussian normaliser; the sum is what each data point’s bound contributes. This is the quantity plotted against the rounds in the lab.
Say it“L of xi is half log the determinant ratio, plus half m-N-transpose S-N-inverse m-N, plus a sum over data points.”

PRML 10.6.3 · inference of hyperparameters

Learning the prior precision too

So far α was set by hand. To learn it, give it a Gamma hyperprior as in the linear case. Now the joint p(t, w, α) has two awkward features: the sigmoids, and the coupling between w and α. The recipe combines the two ideas of this chapter. A local bound handles the sigmoids, replacing p(t | w) by h(w, ξ). A global mean-field factorisation q(w) q(α) handles the coupling. The result is a single lower bound on ln p(t) that depends on q(w), q(α) and the ξs, and we maximise it over all three in turn.

Each update is one we have already met. q(w) is the Gaussian of the previous sections with S0−1 replaced by E[α]I. q(α) is the Gamma of the linear-regression section. Each ξn is re-set to √E[an²]. The interesting case is data that a line separates perfectly. Maximum likelihood then sends the weights to infinity, and a near-flat prior (dashed curve) allows almost that, giving a step-like predictive. The learned α settles at a finite value and keeps the predictive honest about the empty gap between the classes. Switch to separable data and run the updates, then make the hyperprior broader or narrower.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln p(\mathbf t) &\ge \widetilde L\big(q(\mathbf w), q(\alpha), \boldsymbol\xi\big) \\ \widetilde L &= \mathbb E\big[\ln h(\mathbf w, \boldsymbol\xi)\big] + \mathbb E[\ln p(\mathbf w \mid \alpha)] \\ &\quad + \mathbb E[\ln p(\alpha)] - \mathbb E[\ln q(\mathbf w)] \\ &\quad - \mathbb E[\ln q(\alpha)] \\ \mathbf S_N^{-1} &= \mathbb E[\alpha]\,\mathbf I + 2\sum_n \lambda(\xi_n)\boldsymbol\phi_n\boldsymbol\phi_n^{\top} \\ \mathbf m_N &= \mathbf S_N \sum_n \big(t_n - \tfrac12\big)\boldsymbol\phi_n \\ a_N &= a_0 + \tfrac M2 \\ b_N &= b_0 + \tfrac12\big(\mathbf m_N^{\top}\mathbf m_N + \operatorname{Tr}\mathbf S_N\big) \\ \xi_n^2 &= \boldsymbol\phi_n^{\top}\big(\mathbf S_N + \mathbf m_N\mathbf m_N^{\top}\big)\boldsymbol\phi_n \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a lower bound on a lower bound: the local bound h replaces the sigmoids, and the factorised q pays a KL price for ignoring the dependence between w and α. The Gamma terms are the same as in the linear case, and E[ln h] uses E[an] = mN⊤φn and E[an²] from q(w). Cycling through the three updates never lowers the bound.

Real-world project

Project: radar returns with error bars

In 1989 the Space Physics Group at the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory published 351 returns from a phased-array radar at Goose Bay, Labrador, aimed at free electrons in the ionosphere. Each return is described by 34 numbers: the real and imaginary parts of an autocorrelation function at 17 pulse numbers. A return is labelled good if it shows evidence of structure in the ionosphere and bad if the signal passed straight through. The data are the UCI Ionosphere data set (Sigillito, Wing, Hutton & Baker), licensed CC BY 4.0; the page embeds the complete file unchanged.

We follow the split described in the data set’s own notes: the first 200 returns for training, which are close to half good and half bad, and the remaining 151 for testing. Attribute 2 is zero in every return and is dropped, the other 33 are standardised with the training means and standard deviations, and a constant 1 is added for the bias, so w has M = 34 components, all under the same prior. We fit variational logistic regression with α learned under a Gam(0.01, 0.01) hyperprior (section 10.6.3), compare it with fixed values of α and with the MAP weights, and then ask the question the posterior is for: which predictions should we trust?

3 · Does the bound pick a sensible α?

For a fixed α, the converged L(ξ) is a lower bound on the evidence p(t | α), so plotting it against α is a variational version of the evidence curve. The hierarchical model of part 1 lands its E[α] near the top of that curve. Below it is the test log loss for the same values of α, which the bound never sees.

The same computation in Python

The script repeats the page’s algorithm in NumPy, checks the general bound against the closed form L(ξ), fits the MAP weights with scikit-learn at the same prior, and runs a long random-walk Metropolis chain as a reference posterior to see how good the Gaussian q(w) really is.

import json, sys, math
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import expit, digamma, gammaln
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
S = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "./"
D = json.load(open(S + "x115-iono.json"))
X = np.array(D["X"])[:, [j for j in range(34) if j != 1]]      # drop attribute 2: it is 0 in every row
y = np.array(D["y"], float)
Xtr, ytr, Xte, yte = X[:200], y[:200], X[200:], y[200:]          # the split in the UCI notes: first 200 train
mu, sd = Xtr.mean(0), Xtr.std(0)
Ptr = np.hstack([np.ones((200, 1)), (Xtr - mu) / sd]); Pte = np.hstack([np.ones((len(yte), 1)), (Xte - mu) / sd])
N, M = Ptr.shape
lam = lambda xi: np.where(xi < 1e-6, 0.125 - xi**2 / 96, (expit(xi) - 0.5) / (2 * np.maximum(xi, 1e-12)))

def vb(alpha=None, a0=1e-2, b0=1e-2, tol=1e-10, itmax=1000):
    """Variational logistic regression. alpha=None learns it (PRML 10.6.3), else fixed (10.6.1-10.6.2)."""
    xi = np.ones(N); Ea = 1.0 if alpha is None else alpha; hist = []
    for it in range(itmax):
        A = Ea * np.eye(M) + 2 * (Ptr.T * lam(xi)) @ Ptr
        SN = np.linalg.inv(A); mN = SN @ (Ptr.T @ (ytr - 0.5))
        Eww = mN @ mN + np.trace(SN)
        if alpha is None:
            aN = a0 + M / 2; bN = b0 + Eww / 2; Ea = aN / bN
        Ea2 = np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", Ptr, SN + np.outer(mN, mN), Ptr)
        xi = np.sqrt(Ea2)
        # the full bound with the current q(w), q(alpha), xi
        L = np.sum(np.log(expit(xi)) + (ytr - 0.5) * (Ptr @ mN) - xi / 2 - lam(xi) * (Ea2 - xi**2))
        sld = np.linalg.slogdet(SN)[1]
        if alpha is None:
            Elna = digamma(aN) - math.log(bN)
            L += -M / 2 * math.log(2 * math.pi) + M / 2 * Elna - Ea / 2 * Eww
            L += a0 * math.log(b0) + (a0 - 1) * Elna - b0 * Ea - gammaln(a0)
            L += 0.5 * sld + M / 2 * (1 + math.log(2 * math.pi))
            L += gammaln(aN) - (aN - 1) * digamma(aN) - math.log(bN) + aN
        else:
            L += -M / 2 * math.log(2 * math.pi) + M / 2 * math.log(alpha) - alpha / 2 * Eww + 0.5 * sld + M / 2 * (1 + math.log(2 * math.pi))
        hist.append(L)
        if it > 0 and abs(hist[-1] - hist[-2]) < tol: break
    return dict(mN=mN, SN=SN, xi=xi, Ea=Ea, L=L, hist=hist, it=it + 1)

def closed_bound(r, alpha):          # PRML-style closed form for fixed alpha at the q(w) optimum (after one more q(w) update)
    xi = r["xi"]; A = alpha * np.eye(M) + 2 * (Ptr.T * lam(xi)) @ Ptr; SN = np.linalg.inv(A); mN = SN @ (Ptr.T @ (ytr - 0.5))
    return 0.5 * np.linalg.slogdet(SN)[1] + M / 2 * math.log(alpha) + 0.5 * mN @ A @ mN + np.sum(np.log(expit(xi)) - xi / 2 + lam(xi) * xi**2)

def kap(s2): return 1 / np.sqrt(1 + np.pi * s2 / 8)
def predict(mN, SN, P):
    ma = P @ mN; s2 = np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", P, SN, P)
    return ma, s2, expit(kap(s2) * ma)
gh_x, gh_w = np.polynomial.hermite_e.hermegauss(80)
def exact_q(ma, s2): return np.array([np.sum(gh_w * expit(m + math.sqrt(v) * gh_x)) / math.sqrt(2 * math.pi) for m, v in zip(ma, s2)])
def scores(p, t): return np.mean((p > 0.5) == (t == 1)), -np.mean(t * np.log(p) + (1 - t) * np.log(1 - p)), np.mean((p - t) ** 2)

print(f"Ionosphere: {len(y)} returns, train {N} ({int(ytr.sum())} good), test {len(yte)} ({int(yte.sum())} good); {M - 1} features + bias")
H = vb()
print(f"\nVB with alpha learned (a0 = b0 = 0.01): {H['it']} iterations, bound {H['L']:.6f}, E[alpha] = {H['Ea']:.6f}")
print("  bound after iterations 1, 2, 3, 5, 10:", " ".join(f"{H['hist'][i]:.4f}" for i in (0, 1, 2, 4, 9)))
print("  bound never decreased:", bool(np.all(np.diff(H["hist"]) > -1e-9)))
ma, s2, pv = predict(H["mN"], H["SN"], Pte); pe = exact_q(ma, s2)
print(f"  test: accuracy {scores(pv, yte)[0]:.6f}, log loss {scores(pv, yte)[1]:.6f}, Brier {scores(pv, yte)[2]:.6f}")
print(f"  probit approximation vs 80-point Gauss-Hermite under q: max |diff| {np.max(np.abs(pv - pe)):.2e}")
print(f"  first test return: mu_a {ma[0]:.6f}, sigma_a {math.sqrt(s2[0]):.6f}, p(good) {pv[0]:.6f}")
F = vb(alpha=H["Ea"])
print(f"\nVB with alpha fixed at {H['Ea']:.4f}: bound {F['L']:.6f} (closed form {closed_bound(F, H['Ea']):.6f}), {F['it']} iterations")
for al in (0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0):
    r = vb(alpha=al); _, _, p = predict(r["mN"], r["SN"], Pte); a, ll, b = scores(p, yte)
    print(f"  alpha {al:6.2f}: bound {r['L']:10.4f}  test accuracy {a:.4f}  log loss {ll:.4f}  Brier {b:.4f}")
grid = np.logspace(-3, 2, 26); Ls = [vb(alpha=a)["L"] for a in grid]
print(f"  bound over 26 alphas 1e-3..1e2 peaks at alpha = {grid[int(np.argmax(Ls))]:.4g} (bound {max(Ls):.4f})")

clf = LogisticRegression(C=1 / H["Ea"], fit_intercept=False, tol=1e-12, max_iter=10000).fit(Ptr, ytr)
wm = clf.coef_[0]; pm = expit(Pte @ wm); a, ll, b = scores(pm, yte)
print(f"\nMAP (scikit-learn, same prior alpha = {H['Ea']:.4f}, bias penalised): test accuracy {a:.6f}, log loss {ll:.6f}, Brier {b:.6f}")
print(f"  |w_MAP| = {np.linalg.norm(wm):.4f}, |m_N| = {np.linalg.norm(H['mN']):.4f}, max |w_MAP - m_N| = {np.max(np.abs(wm - H['mN'])):.4f}")

# reference posterior by random-walk Metropolis, alpha fixed at E[alpha]
rng = np.random.default_rng(0); al = H["Ea"]; Lc = np.linalg.cholesky(H["SN"]) * (2.38 / math.sqrt(M) * 0.9)
def logpost(w): a = Ptr @ w; return np.sum(ytr * a - np.logaddexp(0, a)) - al / 2 * w @ w
w = H["mN"].copy(); lp = logpost(w); keep = []; acc = 0; T = 120000
for t in range(T):
    w2 = w + Lc @ rng.standard_normal(M); lp2 = logpost(w2)
    if math.log(rng.random()) < lp2 - lp: w, lp = w2, lp2; acc += 1
    if t >= 20000 and t % 10 == 0: keep.append(w.copy())
W = np.array(keep); pmc = expit(Pte @ W.T).mean(1)
print(f"\nMetropolis reference (alpha = {al:.4f}, {T} steps, acceptance {acc / T:.3f}, {len(W)} kept)")
print(f"  posterior sd of weights: MCMC mean {W.std(0).mean():.4f}, variational mean {np.sqrt(np.diag(H['SN'])).mean():.4f}")
print(f"  predictive p(good) on test: max |VB - MCMC| {np.max(np.abs(pv - pmc)):.4f}, mean {np.mean(np.abs(pv - pmc)):.4f}")
a, ll, b = scores(pmc, yte); print(f"  MCMC predictive: test accuracy {a:.6f}, log loss {ll:.6f}, Brier {b:.6f}")
json.dump({"Ea": H["Ea"], "L": H["L"], "it": H["it"], "pv": pv.tolist(), "pmc": pmc.tolist(), "Lfix": Ls, "mcsd": W.std(0).tolist()}, open(S + "x115-pyref.json", "w"))

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • With a Gamma hyperprior on α, Bayesian linear regression has no closed-form posterior, but the mean-field q(w)q(α) does: a Gaussian with α replaced by E[α] = aN/bN, and a Gamma whose rate grows with E[w⊤w]. Its predictive is Gaussian with variance 1/β + φ⊤SNφ.
  • The lower bound L(q) has closed-form terms; it must rise at every update, it approximates ln p(t) from below for model comparison, and its gap to ln p(t) is KL(q ‖ p).
  • Local variational methods bound one awkward factor at a time using convex duality: a convex f lies above its tangents, f(x) ≥ λx − g(λ) with the conjugate g(λ) = maxx{λx − f(x)}; concave functions give upper bounds.
  • The Jaakkola–Jordan bound σ(x) ≥ σ(ξ)exp{(x − ξ)/2 − λ(ξ)(x² − ξ²)} is the exponential of a quadratic, so with a Gaussian prior it gives a Gaussian q(w) for logistic regression and a lower bound on the evidence.
  • Each ξn is optimised by ξn² = E[an²], by EM or by direct maximisation; combining this local bound with a global q(w)q(α) also learns the prior precision.

Check your understanding

In variational linear regression, why is q(w) Gaussian and q(α) Gamma, when we only assumed that they factorise?

The optimal factor is the exponential of the expected log joint. Averaged over q(α), the log joint is quadratic in w, so q(w) is Gaussian; averaged over q(w), it is a linear combination of ln α and α, which is the log of a Gamma density. The forms come from the conjugate model, not from an extra assumption.

A colleague’s implementation reports a lower bound that drops slightly on round 12. What does that tell you?

That there is a bug, either in an update or in the bound itself. Each update maximises the bound over one factor with the others fixed, so in exact arithmetic it can never decrease. Tiny decreases at the level of rounding error after convergence are the only exception.

Why does the Jaakkola–Jordan bound use x² instead of x as the variable for its tangent line?

The term −ln(ex/2 + e−x/2) is not convex in x, but it is convex as a function of x². A tangent in x² is a lower bound that is quadratic in x, so the bounded likelihood is the exponential of a quadratic and combines with a Gaussian prior into a Gaussian.

The variational bound and the Laplace approximation both give a Gaussian over logistic-regression weights. Name one thing the variational method guarantees that Laplace does not.

Its normaliser is a strict lower bound on the log evidence, because the bound lies under every likelihood factor. Laplace’s evidence estimate can be too high or too low. The variational Gaussian is also fitted to the whole bound, not only the curvature at the mode, though in the project its weight standard deviations still come out smaller than a Metropolis reference.

After an update, ξn² = E[an²]. Why does that make sense as a place for the bound to touch?

Under q the activation an is spread around its mean, and the bound is exact at a = ±ξn. Choosing ξn as the root mean square of an puts the exact points where the activation is likely to be, so the bound is tight where it matters most for the integral.
