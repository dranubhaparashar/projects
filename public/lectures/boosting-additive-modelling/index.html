Boosting as Additive Modelling

Exponential loss, robust losses, and what AdaBoost is really doing

AdaBoost looks like a clever trick: fit a crude rule, give more weight to the points it gets wrong, fit another, and let them vote. Five years after it appeared, Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani showed that it is something quite ordinary. It builds an additive model one piece at a time, and every step it takes is the best step for one particular loss function, the exponential loss. Once you see that, you can ask what that loss is estimating, why it struggles with wrong labels, and which other losses to use instead. The algorithm

AdaBoost: many weak votes

A weak learner is any rule whose error is only a little better than a coin toss. The weakest useful one is a stump: a single cut on a single feature, “x3 < 0.7 means +1, otherwise −1”. AdaBoost.M1 (Freund & Schapire, 1997) fits a stump to the data, then raises the weight of every point that stump got wrong, so the next stump has to pay attention to them. After M rounds the stumps vote, each with a say αm that grows as its weighted error shrinks. Labels are y = +1 or −1 throughout this chapter.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{err}_m &= \frac{\sum_i w_i\, I\big(y_i \ne G_m(x_i)\big)}{\sum_i w_i} \\ \alpha_m &= \log\frac{1-\text{err}_m}{\text{err}_m} \\ w_i &\leftarrow w_i\, e^{\alpha_m I(y_i \ne G_m(x_i))} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: start with equal weights wi = 1/N. In round m, fit the stump Gm with the smallest weighted error. Its vote αm is large when errm is small and zero when errm = ½. Then multiply the weight of every misclassified point by eαm = (1 − errm)/errm; correct points keep theirs.
Say it“The error is the weighted share of mistakes; alpha is the log of one minus err over err; every mistake has its weight multiplied by e to the alpha.”
\[ G(x) = \operatorname{sign}\Big( \sum_{m=1}^{M} \alpha_m\, G_m(x) \Big) \]
Read it as: the final classifier is a weighted vote. Each stump says +1 or −1, and good stumps (large αm) count for more. The sum inside the sign is a real-valued score; keep an eye on it, because the rest of the chapter is about what that score means.
Say it“G of x is the sign of the sum over m of alpha-m times G-m of x.”

How good can a vote of stumps be? Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman test it on a deliberately awkward problem: ten independent standard normal features, and Y = +1 when the point lies outside the sphere Σj Xj² > 9.34 (the median of a χ² with 10 degrees of freedom, so the classes are balanced). No single cut on one feature can see a sphere. Below, 2,000 training and 10,000 test points generated in your browser. Drag the rounds slider, and grow a single big tree to compare.

The model

Boosting fits an additive model

Look again at the vote: Σ αmGm(x) is a sum of simple functions, each with its own coefficient. That shape is everywhere in statistics. It is a basis function expansion: choose a family of simple functions b(x; γ), each pinned down by a few numbers γ (where to cut, where to centre, how steep), and add up M of them. A one-hidden-layer neural network is one (b is a sigmoid of a linear combination), so are wavelets, MARS and, with trees as the basis, boosting. Pick a basis family and slide M to watch the sum grow towards the data.

\[ f(x) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \beta_m\, b(x;\gamma_m) \]
Read it as: f is built from M copies of one simple shape b. Copy m is placed by γm and scaled by βm. For AdaBoost, b is a stump that outputs ±1, γ is its feature and cut point, and β is half of α (section 4 explains the half).
Say it“f of x is the sum over m of beta-m times b of x with parameters gamma-m.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{\{\beta_m,\,\gamma_m\}_{1}^{M}} \ \sum_{i=1}^{N} L\big(y_i,\ f(x_i)\big) \\ &\text{with } f(x) = \textstyle\sum_{m=1}^{M} \beta_m\, b(x;\gamma_m) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the honest way to fit an additive model is to choose all the βs and γs together to minimise the total loss L. For most L and b this is a large, bumpy, non-convex problem. Fitting one basis function at a time is easy, and that is the opening boosting uses.
Say it“Minimise, over all the betas and gammas at once, the sum over the data of the loss between y-i and the expansion at x-i.”

The fitting rule

Forward stagewise: add one piece, never look back

Forward stagewise additive modelling replaces the big joint problem with M small ones. At step m, keep everything already in the model frozen, and choose the one new basis function, and its coefficient, that most reduces the loss. Then add it and move on. Earlier terms are never readjusted; that is the difference from stepwise methods, which refit all the coefficients each time a term enters. With squared-error loss, the step is easy to picture: the best new term is simply the best fit to the current residuals. Press Step and watch each step function chase what is left over; then switch to “refit all coefficients” to see what never looking back costs.

\[ \begin{aligned} (\beta_m,\gamma_m) &= \arg\min_{\beta,\gamma} \sum_{i=1}^{N} L\big(y_i, \\ &\qquad\ f_{m-1}(x_i) + \beta\, b(x_i;\gamma)\big) \\ f_m(x) &= f_{m-1}(x) + \beta_m\, b(x;\gamma_m) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: start from f0 (here, the mean of y). At each step, search over one new basis function and its scale, holding fm−1 fixed, and add the winner. This is Algorithm 10.2 of The Elements of Statistical Learning.
Say it“Beta-m and gamma-m minimise the loss of f-m-minus-one plus beta times b; then f-m is f-m-minus-one plus that term.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &L\big(y_i,\ f_{m-1}(x_i) + \beta\, b(x_i;\gamma)\big) \\ &\quad = \big(r_{im} - \beta\, b(x_i;\gamma)\big)^2 \\ &r_{im} = y_i - f_{m-1}(x_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with squared error, the loss of the new model is the squared misfit between the residual rim and the new term. So each step is a small least-squares fit to the residuals: for a given γ, the best β is Σ ribi / Σ bi².
Say it“The loss is r-i-m minus beta b, squared, where r-i-m is y-i minus the current fit.”

The derivation

AdaBoost is stagewise fitting of the exponential loss

Now run forward stagewise with a stump basis b(x; γ) = G(x) ∈ {−1, +1} and one particular loss, the exponential loss L(y, f) = e−y f. Four lines of algebra later, out comes AdaBoost.M1, weights and all. Follow the derivation down the page with the lab beside it: slide β to find the bottom of the loss curve, then take the step, and watch the weights change exactly as AdaBoost’s rule says.

\[ \begin{aligned} (\beta_m, G_m) &= \arg\min_{\beta,\,G}\ L_m(\beta, G) \\ L_m &= \sum_{i} e^{-y_i\,(f_{m-1}(x_i) + \beta G(x_i))} \\ &= \sum_{i} w_i^{(m)}\, e^{-\beta\, y_i G(x_i)} \\ w_i^{(m)} &= e^{-y_i f_{m-1}(x_i)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: step 1. The exponential splits into a product. The part that depends only on the old model, e−yifm−1(xi), does not involve β or G, so it acts as a fixed weight on point i. Points the current model gets badly wrong (yifm−1 very negative) carry huge weights. Bishop (PRML §14.3.1) writes the same thing with fm = ½Σ αlyl(x): his α is our 2β.
Say it“Beta-m and G-m minimise the sum of w-i times e to the minus beta y-i G of x-i, where w-i is e to the minus y-i times the old model.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\sum_i w_i\, e^{-\beta y_i G(x_i)} \\ &\quad = \big(e^{\beta} - e^{-\beta}\big) \sum_{y_i \ne G(x_i)} w_i \\ &\quad\ \ + e^{-\beta} \sum_i w_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: step 2. yiG(xi) is +1 for a correct point and −1 for a mistake, so each point contributes wie−β or wieβ. Regroup: for any β > 0 the first bracket is positive, so the best G is the one with the smallest weighted error, whatever β turns out to be. That is AdaBoost’s weak-learner step.
Say it“The weighted loss equals e to the beta minus e to the minus beta, times the weighted mistakes, plus e to the minus beta times the total weight.”
\[ \beta_m = \frac12 \log \frac{1-\text{err}_m}{\text{err}_m} \]
Read it as: step 3. Divide the loss by Σwi: it becomes (1 − err)e−β + err·eβ. Set its derivative in β to zero: err·eβ = (1 − err)e−β, so e2β = (1 − err)/err. This is exactly half of AdaBoost’s αm.
Say it“Beta-m is a half times the log of one minus err-m over err-m.”
\[ \begin{aligned} w_i^{(m+1)} &= w_i^{(m)}\, e^{-\beta_m y_i G_m(x_i)} \\ &= w_i^{(m)}\, e^{\alpha_m I(y_i \ne G_m(x_i))}\; e^{-\beta_m} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: step 4. The next weights are e−yifm(xi). Using −yiGm(xi) = 2I(yi ≠ Gm(xi)) − 1 and αm = 2βm: mistakes are multiplied by eαm, and the common factor e−βm vanishes when the weights are normalised. AdaBoost, recovered.
Say it“The new weight is the old weight times e to the alpha-m if the point was misclassified, times a constant.”

What it estimates

Why exponential loss? Its population minimiser

The exponential loss was not chosen for its meaning; it was chosen because it makes the stagewise step so cheap. So what does the score f(x) estimate? Imagine infinitely many training points at one x, a fraction p of them labelled +1. Which f minimises the expected loss there? The answer is the population minimiser, and for exponential loss it is half the log-odds. So AdaBoost’s score converts straight into a probability. Slide p and watch where each loss puts its minimum.

\[ \begin{aligned} f^*(x) &= \arg\min_{f}\ \mathrm{E}\big[e^{-Yf} \mid x\big] \\ &= \frac12 \log\frac{P(Y{=}1\mid x)}{P(Y{=}{-1}\mid x)} \\ P(Y{=}1 \mid x) &= \frac{1}{1 + e^{-2f^*(x)}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: at a point x with P(Y = 1 | x) = p, the expected loss is p·e−f + (1 − p)·ef. Its derivative is zero when e2f = p/(1 − p). So the boosted score estimates half the log-odds, and a logistic function of 2f turns it back into a probability.
Say it“f-star is a half times the log of the odds that Y is one; the probability is one over one plus e to the minus two f-star.”
\[ \begin{aligned} -\ell(Y, f) &= \log\big(1 + e^{-2Yf}\big) \\ p(x) &= \frac{1}{1+e^{-2f(x)}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the binomial deviance is the negative log-likelihood of logistic regression, written with the same half-log-odds score f (with Y′ = (Y + 1)/2 ∈ {0, 1}, ℓ = Y′ log p + (1 − Y′) log(1 − p)). Its population minimiser is the very same f*. At the population level the two losses agree; on a finite, noisy sample they behave very differently.
Say it“Minus the log-likelihood is the log of one plus e to the minus two Y f.”

Robustness

Losses for classification, and what wrong labels do to them

For a ±1 label, the product y·f(x) is the margin: positive when the point is classified correctly, large when confidently so. Every classification loss in this chapter is a function of the margin alone, and they all aim at the same goal: penalise negative margins. They differ in how hard. That matters most for a point whose label is simply wrong: the model will push it towards a large negative margin, and a loss that keeps growing fast there lets that one point dominate. Slide the margin and compare both the loss and the pull, the slope −dL/dz that sets how much the next fit cares about this point.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{misclassification:}\ \ & I(yf < 0) \\ \text{exponential:}\ \ & e^{-yf} \\ \text{deviance:}\ \ & \log\big(1+e^{-2yf}\big) \\ \text{squared:}\ \ & (1-yf)^2 \\ \text{hinge (SVM):}\ \ & (1 - yf)_+ \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: z = yf is the margin. Exponential loss grows like e|z| on the wrong side; deviance grows only linearly, like 2|z|, and so does the hinge. Squared error, (y − f)² = (1 − yf)² for y = ±1, is the odd one out: it is not monotone, so it also penalises points that are too right (z > 1). In the plot the deviance is divided by log 2 so that it passes through 1 at z = 0, like the others.
Say it“Each loss is a function of the margin y times f: an indicator, e to the minus y f, log of one plus e to the minus two y f, one minus y f squared, and the positive part of one minus y f.”

its share, deviance

Bishop (PRML §14.3.2) adds two more reasons to prefer deviance. It is the log-likelihood of a proper probability model, so its fitted scores are estimates you can check; the exponential loss is not the likelihood of any well-defined model. And it extends cleanly to K classes, through the softmax, where AdaBoost needs special variants. The K-class deviance uses one score per class, constrained to sum to zero:

\[ \begin{aligned} L &= -\sum_{k} I(y = \mathcal{G}_k)\, \log p_k(x) \\ p_k(x) &= \frac{e^{f_k(x)}}{\sum_{l=1}^{K} e^{f_l(x)}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: score each class, turn the scores into probabilities with the softmax, and pay minus the log of the probability given to the true class. With K = 2 and f1 = −f2 = f this is the binomial deviance above.
Say it“The loss is minus the log of p-k for the true class k, where p-k is e to the f-k over the sum of e to the f-l.”
Robust regression

Robust losses for regression: squared, absolute, Huber

The same story plays out for regression. Squared error pays (y − f)², so a residual ten times larger costs a hundred times more, and a few wild values can drag the whole fit. Absolute error pays |y − f|, so every point’s pull is capped. The Huber loss is squared for small residuals and absolute beyond a threshold δ, keeping the efficiency of least squares on clean data and the robustness of absolute error on outliers. Here a straight line is fitted to 30 points by each loss. Drag the three outliers (or add more) and move δ.

\[ \begin{aligned} &L(y,f) = \begin{cases} r^2 & |r| \le \delta \\ 2\delta\,|r| - \delta^2 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \\ &\text{where } r = y - f \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: inside ±δ the Huber loss is ordinary squared error. Outside, it continues as a straight line with the same slope, 2δ, so the two pieces join smoothly. Large δ gives least squares; small δ behaves like (a multiple of) absolute error.
Say it“L is y minus f squared when the residual is at most delta, and two delta times the absolute residual minus delta squared otherwise.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\arg\min_f \mathrm{E}\big[(Y-f)^2 \mid x\big] \\ &\qquad = \mathrm{E}(Y\mid x) \\ &\arg\min_f \mathrm{E}\big[\,|Y-f|\, \mid x\big] \\ &\qquad = \operatorname{median}(Y\mid x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the population minimisers. Squared error estimates the conditional mean, which a single extreme value can move as far as it likes; absolute error estimates the median, which ignores how extreme the extremes are. For symmetric noise they agree, so robustness costs nothing in what you estimate.
Say it“Squared error is minimised by the conditional mean; absolute error by the conditional median.”

Real-world project

Project: a spam filter, with some labels deliberately wrong

The Spambase data (Hopkins, Reeber, Forman & Suermondt, Hewlett-Packard Labs, 1999; UCI repository, CC BY 4.0) holds 4,601 emails, 1,813 of them spam. Each email is described by 57 numbers: how often 48 words (“free”, “remove”, “hp”, “george”…) and 6 characters (“!”, “$”…) appear, and three statistics of runs of capital letters. The Elements of Statistical Learning (§10.8) uses it to show boosting at work. As there, we train on 3,065 emails and test on the other 1,536, here chosen by a seeded shuffle. Each feature is cut into at most 32 bins at quantiles of the training data, so every stump is “feature j is in bin k or lower”.

Three boosted models of 400 stumps each, all plain forward stagewise (no shrinkage): AdaBoost.M1, exactly as derived above; exponential loss with real-valued stumps (each leaf gets a Newton step, as in scikit-learn’s gradient boosting); and binomial deviance, fitted the same way. Then we put the theory to the test. We flip a share of the training labels at random, keep the test labels clean, and see which loss copes. Move the label-noise slider, then the stopping round.

An additive model you can read

A sum of stumps is an additive model in the original features: f(x) = f0 + g1(x1) + … + g57(x57), because each stump touches one feature. Add up the stumps that use feature j and you get gj, a step function you can plot. That is the kind of picture ESL draws for the spam data. (Chapter 1.8b treats importance and partial dependence properly, for trees that mix features.) Features are ranked by how much their gj varies across the training emails. Click a feature.

The same project in Python

import numpy as np
from sklearn.base import BaseEstimator, ClassifierMixin
from sklearn.ensemble import AdaBoostClassifier, GradientBoostingClassifier

D = np.loadtxt("spambase.data", delimiter=",")                    # UCI Spambase, 4,601 emails
X, y = D[:, :57], D[:, 57].astype(int)                            # 1 = spam
p = np.random.RandomState(0).permutation(len(y)); tr, te = p[:3065], p[3065:]
edges = [np.unique(np.quantile(X[tr, j], np.linspace(0, 1, 33)[1:-1])) for j in range(57)]
B = np.column_stack([np.searchsorted(edges[j], X[:, j], side="right") for j in range(57)])  # <= 32 bins

class Stump(BaseEstimator, ClassifierMixin):       # the stump with the smallest weighted error
    def fit(self, X, y, sample_weight):
        self.classes_ = np.array([0, 1]); w = sample_weight; best = (np.inf,)
        for j in range(X.shape[1]):
            K = len(edges[j]) + 1
            cp = np.cumsum(np.bincount(X[:, j], w * (y == 1), K)); cn = np.cumsum(np.bincount(X[:, j], w * (y == 0), K))
            for s, e in ((1, cn + cp[-1] - cp), (-1, cp + cn[-1] - cn)):   # s=1: bins <= k say spam
                k = np.argmin(e[:-1])
                if e[k] < best[0]: best = (e[k], j, k, s)
        _, self.j, self.k, self.s = best; return self
    def predict(self, X): return np.where((X[:, self.j] <= self.k) == (self.s == 1), 1, 0)

flip = np.random.RandomState(1).permutation(len(tr))
print("noise  AdaBoost.M1         exponential (GB)    deviance (GB)       [test error after 400 stumps (best m)]")
for noise in [0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4]:
    yn = y[tr].copy(); f = flip[:int(noise * len(tr))]; yn[f] = 1 - yn[f]        # flip labels
    models = [AdaBoostClassifier(Stump(), n_estimators=400, learning_rate=1.0)] + [
        GradientBoostingClassifier(loss=l, max_depth=1, learning_rate=1.0, n_estimators=400, random_state=0)
        for l in ("exponential", "log_loss")]
    row = f"{noise:4.0%} "
    for m in models:
        e = [np.mean(q != y[te]) for q in m.fit(B[tr], yn).staged_predict(B[te])]
        row += f"  {e[-1]:.4f} ({min(e):.4f} @{np.argmin(e) + 1:3d})"
    print(row)

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Boosting fits an additive model f(x) = Σ βmb(x; γm), adding one basis function at a time and never readjusting the earlier ones: forward stagewise fitting.
  • Stagewise fitting of the exponential loss e−yf with ±1 stumps is AdaBoost.M1: the weights are e−y fm−1, the best stump minimises weighted error, and βm = ½ log((1 − err)/err) = αm/2.
  • The exponential loss and the binomial deviance share a population minimiser, half the log-odds, so a boosted score converts to a probability: p = 1/(1 + e−2f).
  • On the wrong side of the margin, exponential loss grows exponentially and deviance only linearly, so exponential loss hands far more weight to badly misclassified (often mislabelled) points. For regression, Huber and absolute losses cap each point’s pull in the same way.
  • On Spambase with 20% of training labels flipped, the flipped emails soak up about 40% of the weight under every loss, and deviance does not beat exponential loss on test error (discrete AdaBoost holds up best). Stopping early helps far more, and the best round arrives sooner as the noise grows.

Check your understanding

A stump has weighted error 0.5 in some round. What does AdaBoost do with it, and why does that make sense for the exponential loss?

αm = log(0.5/0.5) = 0, so it gets no vote and the weights do not change. On the loss side, (1 − err)e−β + err·eβ = cosh β is smallest at β = 0: adding a coin-toss rule cannot lower the loss.

What separates forward stagewise fitting from forward stepwise fitting?

Stagewise freezes every term already in the model and fits only the new one. Stepwise refits all the coefficients each time a term is added. Stagewise is cheaper and, with small steps, acts as a regulariser; its training loss at a given size is never lower than the refitted one.

A boosted model trained with exponential loss gives an email the score f = 1.1. What probability of spam does that suggest?

The score estimates half the log-odds, so p = 1/(1 + e−2×1.1) = 1/(1 + e−2.2) ≈ 0.90. (Treat it as a rough estimate: boosted scores are often overconfident and benefit from calibration.)

Why is squared error a poor loss for classification, even though it is fine for regression?

As a function of the margin, (1 − yf)² rises again for yf > 1, so it penalises points that are classified correctly with confidence and spends effort pulling them back towards the boundary. A classification loss should be monotone decreasing in the margin.

Two points have margins −3 and +2. How much more weight does the first get than the second under exponential loss, and under deviance?

Exponential: e3/e−2 = e5 ≈ 148 times. Deviance (weight = slope = 2/(1 + e2z)): (2/(1 + e−6)) / (2/(1 + e4)) ≈ 1.995/0.0360 ≈ 55 times. Deviance caps the weight of a hopeless point at 2.
