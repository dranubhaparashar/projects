Training Deep Networks

Backprop gives you the gradient. Everything else is the craft of using it

The last chapter ended with a gradient: the direction that makes the loss rise fastest. Step the other way and the network improves. But how big a step? Should the last few steps count too? Where should the weights start, and how do you stop a network from memorising? Those questions decide whether a deep network learns in minutes, crawls for days or blows up. The workhorse

Mini-batch SGD: a noisy compass

The true loss is an average over every training example, so its exact gradient needs a pass over the whole dataset: for ImageNet, more than a million images per step. Stochastic gradient descent estimates the gradient from a small random mini-batch instead. Each estimate points roughly downhill, never exactly, but it is cheap, and thousands of cheap, slightly wrong steps beat a handful of perfect ones. Below, a two-parameter model y = w·x + b fitted to 200 points; the map is its loss over (w, b). Change the batch size and watch the faint compass needles scatter or agree.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat g &= \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in \mathcal{B}} \nabla_\theta\, \ell_i(\theta) \\ \theta &\leftarrow \theta - \eta\, \hat g \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: pick B examples at random (the batch ℬ), average their gradients to get the estimate ĝ, and step a distance proportional to the learning rate η against it. One pass through all the data in batches is an epoch.
Say it“g-hat is the average gradient over the batch; theta moves by minus eta times g-hat.”
\[ \operatorname{Var}[\hat g] = \frac{\sigma_g^2}{B}\cdot\frac{N-B}{N-1} \]
Read it as: σg² is how much single-example gradients disagree. Averaging B of them divides the noise by B (the second factor is a small correction for drawing without replacement, and makes the noise vanish when B = N). Four times the batch buys half the noise, at four times the cost.
Say it“The variance of g-hat is sigma-g squared over B, times N minus B over N minus one.”

The first dial

The learning rate: timid, just right, explosive

Near a minimum, every loss looks like a bowl, and a bowl has a steepest and a flattest direction. Gradient descent shrinks the distance to the bottom along each direction by its own factor per step. In the flat direction you want big steps; in the steep one, a big step jumps clean over the bottom and lands higher up the far wall. The steep direction sets the speed limit, and the flat direction then takes forever. Below, a bowl that is 10× steeper one way than the other. Slide the learning rate past 0.1 and then past 0.2.

\[ \begin{aligned} L(\theta) &= \tfrac12\big(\lambda_1\theta_1^2 + \lambda_2\theta_2^2\big) \\ \theta_i^{(t+1)} &= (1-\eta\lambda_i)\,\theta_i^{(t)} \\ \text{stable only if}\ \ \eta &< \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the λs are the curvatures (the Hessian's eigenvalues). Each step multiplies coordinate i by (1 − ηλi). If that factor is between 0 and 1 the coordinate creeps toward 0; between −1 and 0 it zig-zags but still shrinks; beyond −1 it grows every step and training explodes. The best fixed rate, η = 2/(λmin + λmax), still leaves the flat direction crawling when the bowl is badly stretched.
Say it“Each step multiplies theta-i by one minus eta lambda-i; it converges only when eta is below two over the largest lambda.”

Remembering the way

Momentum: a heavy ball in a ravine

In a long, narrow ravine the gradient mostly points across the ravine, not along it. Plain gradient descent zig-zags from wall to wall and makes slow progress down the valley floor. Boris Polyak's fix (1964) is to let the parameters behave like a heavy ball: keep a running velocity and add each new gradient to it. The across-the-valley pushes alternate in sign and cancel; the along-the-valley pushes agree and add up. Stretch the ravine and compare the best plain gradient descent with momentum.

\[ \begin{aligned} v &\leftarrow \beta\, v + \nabla L(\theta) \\ \theta &\leftarrow \theta - \eta\, v \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: v is an exponentially fading sum of past gradients; β (usually 0.9) is how much of the old velocity survives each step. With β = 0 this is plain gradient descent. Roughly the last 1/(1−β) gradients are remembered.
Say it“v becomes beta v plus the gradient; theta moves by minus eta v.”
\[ v_\infty = \frac{g}{1-\beta}, \qquad \rho_{\text{best}} = \frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1} \]
Read it as: under a steady gradient g the velocity settles at g/(1−β): with β = 0.9 the ball moves 10× faster along a consistent slope. On a bowl with condition number κ = λmax/λmin, the best momentum shrinks the error by ρ per step, against (κ−1)/(κ+1) for the best plain descent: √κ instead of κ.
Say it“Terminal velocity is g over one minus beta; the best rate is root-kappa minus one over root-kappa plus one.”

Per-parameter step sizes

Adam: a step size for every weight

A network has millions of weights, and their gradients live on wildly different scales: a weight on a rare input feature may see gradients a thousand times smaller than one on a common feature. One learning rate cannot suit both. Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015) keeps two running averages per weight: of the gradient (momentum) and of the squared gradient (its typical size). Dividing one by the square root of the other gives every weight a step of roughly η, whatever its gradient’s scale. Below, a bowl 100× steeper up–down than left–right, aligned with the axes. Press One step and read the table: watch √v̂ rescale each coordinate.

\[ \begin{aligned} m &\leftarrow \beta_1 m + (1-\beta_1)\, g \\ v &\leftarrow \beta_2 v + (1-\beta_2)\, g^2 \\ \hat m &= \frac{m}{1-\beta_1^{\,t}}, \quad \hat v = \frac{v}{1-\beta_2^{\,t}} \\ \theta &\leftarrow \theta - \eta\, \frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat v}+\epsilon} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: everything is per weight. m is a smoothed gradient (β1 = 0.9), v a smoothed squared gradient (β2 = 0.999). Both start at 0, which biases them toward 0 in the first steps; dividing by 1 − βt undoes that. The step m̂/√v̂ is close to ±1 when the gradient is consistent, so each weight moves about η; ε = 10−8 only prevents division by zero.
Say it“m is the running mean of g, v the running mean of g squared; correct both for their zero start; step by eta times m-hat over root v-hat.”

Learning rates over time

Schedules: warm up, then cool down

With a constant learning rate, SGD never settles: near the minimum the true gradient is tiny but the mini-batch noise is not, so the parameters keep jittering in a cloud whose size grows with η. The cure is to start bold and finish gently. Classic recipes cut η by 10× at fixed epochs; modern ones glide down a cosine, often after a short warm-up from zero. Below, 400 SGD steps with batches of 5 on the regression from the first section; the plot shows how far the loss is above the best possible. Pick a schedule and compare it with the constant rate.

\[ \begin{aligned} \eta_t^{\cos} &= \tfrac12\,\eta_{\max}\Big(1+\cos\frac{\pi t}{T}\Big) \\ \eta_t^{\text{warm}} &= \eta_{\max}\cdot\frac{t}{T_w} \quad (t < T_w) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the cosine schedule starts at ηmax and slides smoothly to 0 at the final step T (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017). Warm-up ramps linearly from 0 over the first Tw steps (here 10% of the run), then hands over to the cosine; it protects large networks from huge, badly-aimed early steps. Step decay simply divides η by 10 at 50% and again at 75% of training.
Say it“Cosine: half eta-max times one plus cos of pi t over T. Warm-up: eta-max times t over T-w.”

Where to start

Initialisation: keep the signal alive

Pass an image through ten layers and, at every layer, the signal is multiplied by a fresh random matrix. If each multiplication shrinks it a little, after ten layers there is nothing left; if each grows it a little, it explodes. Gradients travel the same chain backwards and suffer the same fate, so the first layers never learn. The fix is to choose the scale of the random weights so that each layer passes on a signal of the same size. Below, 300 real Fashion-MNIST images (from the project) pass through a fresh 10-layer network, 100 units wide. Choose an activation, then slide the weight scale and watch the layers collapse or saturate.

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}[z_l] &= n_{l-1}\,\sigma_w^2\;\mathbb{E}\big[a_{l-1}^2\big] \\ \text{Xavier: } \sigma_w^2 = \frac{1}{n}, &\qquad \text{He: } \sigma_w^2 = \frac{2}{n} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: zl = W al−1 sums nl−1 random products, so its variance is n times the weight variance times the mean square of the inputs. For tanh near zero, a ≈ z, so keeping nσw² = 1 keeps the signal steady (Glorot & Bengio, 2010; LeCun et al., 1998). ReLU zeroes half its inputs, so E[a²] = ½Var[z], and you need nσw² = 2 (He et al., 2015). Below, the weight scale is written as σw = c/√n.
Say it“The variance of z-l is n times sigma-w squared times the mean square of the previous activations; Xavier sets sigma-w squared to one over n, He to two over n.”

Normalise as you go

Batch normalisation: re-centring every layer

Careful initialisation sets the scale at step zero, but training moves the weights, and the scales drift. Ioffe and Szegedy (2015) proposed a blunt fix: inside the network, after each linear layer, standardise every unit over the current mini-batch to mean 0 and variance 1, then let the network re-scale it with two learned numbers, γ and β. In practice it lets deep networks train with larger learning rates and makes them far less sensitive to the initial scale (why exactly it helps is still debated). Below, two 8-layer ReLU networks, 32 units wide, learn the project’s 3,000 Fashion-MNIST images with identical starting weights and batches; one has BatchNorm. Pick a learning rate and an initialisation, then train both.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_{\mathcal B} &= \frac1B\sum_{i} z_i \\ \sigma_{\mathcal B}^2 &= \frac1B\sum_i (z_i-\mu_{\mathcal B})^2 \\ \hat z_i &= \frac{z_i-\mu_{\mathcal B}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathcal B}^2+\epsilon}} \\ y_i &= \gamma\,\hat z_i + \beta \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for one unit, over the B examples of the batch: subtract their mean, divide by their standard deviation, then scale by γ and shift by β (both learned, starting at 1 and 0). Gradients flow through μ and σ too. At test time there is no batch, so the network uses running averages of μ and σ² collected during training.
Say it“Subtract the batch mean, divide by the batch standard deviation, then multiply by gamma and add beta.”

Against memorising

Dropout: training a crowd of thinned networks

A big network trained on few examples will memorise them, noise and all. Dropout (Srivastava, Hinton et al., 2014) switches off each hidden unit at random, with probability p, at every training step. No unit can rely on any particular partner being present, so the network has to spread what it knows across many units; in effect it trains a huge crowd of thinned networks that share weights, and at test time uses all units at once, a cheap approximation to averaging the crowd. Below, a 2–64–64–2 network learns two interleaved moons from just 60 noisy points; the background shows its decision on fresh points. Train with p = 0, then reset and train with p = 0.5.

\[ \begin{gathered} \tilde h_j = \frac{m_j\, h_j}{1-p}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\tilde h_j] = h_j \\ m_j \sim \text{Bernoulli}(1-p) \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: each unit’s output hj is kept (mj = 1) with probability 1 − p or zeroed. Survivors are scaled up by 1/(1 − p), so the expected output is unchanged and the test-time network, which keeps every unit and does no scaling, sees signals of the right size. This “inverted” form is what every modern library implements.
Say it“h-tilde is the mask times h over one minus p; on average it equals h.”

The same experiments in numpy

import numpy as np

def mulberry32(seed):                   # the page's seeded generator (state k = seed + k * 0x6D2B79F5)
    st = [seed]
    def draw(n):
        a = (st[0] + 0x6D2B79F5 * np.arange(1, n + 1, dtype=np.uint64)) & 0xFFFFFFFF; st[0] = int(a[-1])
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (a | 1)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (t | 61)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 2**32
    return draw

def train(X, y, Xte, yte, sizes, kind, lr, epochs, gain=2, p=0.0, B=50, beta=0.9, b1=0.9, b2=0.999):
    draw, shuf, drop = mulberry32(1), mulberry32(2), mulberry32(3)
    net = []
    for a, b in zip(sizes[:-1], sizes[1:]):    # Box-Muller normals, sd = sqrt(gain / fan-in): He; biases 0
        u = draw(2 * a * b); g = np.sqrt(-2 * np.log(u[0::2])) * np.cos(2 * np.pi * u[1::2])
        net += [np.sqrt(gain / a) * g.reshape(a, b), np.zeros(b)]
    M = [np.zeros_like(w) for w in net]; V = [np.zeros_like(w) for w in net]; t = 0
    def forward(Xb, masks=None):
        A, Z, H = [Xb], [], Xb
        for l in range(0, len(net), 2):
            Z.append(H @ net[l] + net[l + 1])
            if l == len(net) - 2: break
            H = np.maximum(Z[-1], 0)
            if masks is not None: masks.append((drop(H.size).reshape(H.shape) >= p) / (1 - p)); H = H * masks[-1]
            A.append(H)
        E = np.exp(Z[-1] - Z[-1].max(1, keepdims=True)); return A, Z, E / E.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    idx = list(range(len(y)))
    for ep in range(epochs):
        u = shuf(len(idx) - 1)                 # Fisher-Yates shuffle, carried over between epochs
        for i, k in enumerate(range(len(idx) - 1, 0, -1)):
            j = int(u[i] * (k + 1)); idx[k], idx[j] = idx[j], idx[k]
        for s in range(0, len(idx), B):
            b = idx[s:s + B]; masks = [] if p > 0 else None
            A, Z, P = forward(X[b], masks)
            dZ = P.copy(); dZ[np.arange(len(b)), y[b]] -= 1; dZ /= len(b); G = [None] * len(net)
            for l in range(len(net) // 2 - 1, -1, -1):         # backprop
                G[2 * l], G[2 * l + 1] = A[l].T @ dZ, dZ.sum(0)
                if l: dZ = (dZ @ net[2 * l].T) * (masks[l - 1] if masks else 1) * (Z[l - 1] > 0)
            t += 1
            for w, g, m, v in zip(net, G, M, V):
                if kind == "sgd": w -= lr * g
                elif kind == "mom": m *= beta; m += g; w -= lr * m
                else:
                    m *= b1; m += (1 - b1) * g; v *= b2; v += (1 - b2) * g * g
                    w -= lr * (m / (1 - b1 ** t)) / (np.sqrt(v / (1 - b2 ** t)) + 1e-8)
    P = forward(Xte)[2]
    return (forward(X)[2].argmax(1) == y).mean(), -np.log(P[np.arange(len(yte)), yte]).mean(), (P.argmax(1) == yte).mean()

D = np.load("fashion_small.npz")             # first 3,000 train / 1,000 test images, 14x14, 16 grey levels
X, y, Xte, yte = D["xtr"] / 15, D["ytr"], D["xte"] / 15, D["yte"]
for kind, lr in [("sgd", 0.05), ("mom", 0.02), ("adam", 0.001)]:
    print(f"{kind:5s} lr {lr:<6g} train acc %.4f  test loss %.6f  test acc %.4f" % train(X, y, Xte, yte, [196, 64, 32, 10], kind, lr, 10))
for p in [0, 0.5]:
    print(f"dropout p={p}: 500 images, train acc %.4f  test loss %.6f  test acc %.4f" % train(X[:500], y[:500], Xte, yte, [196, 128, 128, 10], "adam", 0.003, 30, p=p))

The browser and numpy start from bit-identical weights (the same generator and Box–Muller transform) and see the same mini-batches in the same order, so the two trajectories agree to about 12 significant digits; the only differences come from the order in which a matrix product adds up its terms. Two honest caveats. These are small networks on small, shrunken images, so the accuracies (about 80%) are well below what a convolutional network reaches on full Fashion-MNIST (above 90%); chapter 2.4 builds one. And each comparison here is one run from one seed: a different seed moves the accuracies by a point or so, enough to reorder close finishers.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Mini-batch SGD trades exact gradients for cheap, noisy ones; the noise shrinks like 1/B while the cost grows like B.
  • The learning rate matters most: above 2/λmax training explodes, far below it training crawls. A range test finds a good value in one sweep.
  • Momentum averages away the zig-zags and speeds up consistent directions; Adam also rescales every weight by its own gradient size.
  • Start weights at σw² = 1/n (tanh) or 2/n (ReLU) so signals and gradients keep their size through many layers; BatchNorm keeps them in range during training.
  • Decay the learning rate to settle into a minimum, and use dropout (or other regularisers) when a big network starts memorising a small dataset.

Check your understanding

Your loss suddenly becomes NaN after a few hundred steps. What is the first thing to try?

Lower the learning rate (by 3–10×): the steps have grown larger than the steepest curvature allows, so the loss is climbing the far wall each step until the numbers overflow. A warm-up, gradient clipping or BatchNorm also help, but the learning rate is the usual culprit.

You double the batch size. Roughly what happens to the gradient noise and to the cost of one step?

The variance of the gradient estimate halves (its standard deviation falls by √2), and each step costs twice as much. That is why, past a point, bigger batches stop paying off unless you also raise the learning rate.

With β = 0.9, how much faster can momentum travel along a constant slope than plain gradient descent with the same η?

About 1/(1−0.9) = 10×: the velocity accumulates to g/(1−β). That is also why momentum usually needs a smaller η than plain SGD.

A 20-layer ReLU network whose weights start with standard deviation 1/√n barely learns. Why, and what would you change?

With ReLU, nσw² = 1 halves the signal’s variance at every layer, so after 20 layers it has shrunk by 220 in variance and the gradients to the early layers are tiny. Use He initialisation, σw = √(2/n), or add normalisation layers.

Why does dropout scale the surviving units by 1/(1−p) during training?

So that each unit’s expected output is the same with and without dropout. At test time every unit is kept and nothing is rescaled, and the next layer still receives signals of the size it was trained on.
