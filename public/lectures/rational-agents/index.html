Rational Agents & Task Environments

Do the right thing, given what you can see, know and do

An agent is anything that perceives and acts. A rational agent picks, at every moment, the action it expects to score best on a stated performance measure, given everything it has perceived so far and whatever it was built knowing. That one definition tells you what to write down before you build anything (the measure, the environment, the actuators, the sensors) and which agent design the job needs. AIMA 1.1.4 · the rational-agent approach

Four ways to define AI, and why this book picks acting rationally

People have tried to pin down “artificial intelligence” along two axes. One asks whether we care about thinking (the reasoning inside) or acting (the behaviour outside). The other asks what we measure it against: human performance, or an ideal standard of doing the right thing, called rationality. That gives four camps.

human · acting

Acting humanly

Pass for a person in conversation (Turing’s test). It needs language, knowledge, reasoning and learning, but it rewards imitation, mistakes included.

human · thinking

Thinking humanly

Model how people actually think, and check the model against experiments on minds and brains. That is cognitive science.

ideal · thinking

Thinking rationally

Reason only by correct inference, the “laws of thought” tradition of logic. Elegant, but the world is uncertain and logic alone does not say what to do.

ideal · acting

Acting rationally

Build an agent that acts to get the best outcome, or the best expected outcome when it cannot be sure. This is the view the rest of the course takes.

Why the last one? It is more general than the laws of thought: correct inference is one way to act well, but not the only one. Snatching your hand back from a hot pan is a good action that involves no deliberation at all, and acting fast often beats acting after a long chain of reasoning. And it is easier to do science with: rationality has a precise mathematical meaning, so designs can be analysed and compared, whereas human behaviour is a moving target fitted to one species. This is the standard model of AI: build machines that do the right thing, where “right” is defined by the objective we give them.

One caveat matters straight away. Perfect rationality, always taking the exactly best action, is out of reach in any rich world, because computing it takes time and the world does not wait. The practical aim is limited rationality: act well when there is no time to do all the computation you would like. The lab makes this concrete. An agent can inspect options one by one; each option’s worth is a random number between 0 and 1, and each inspection costs c. Raise the cost of thinking and watch the best number of looks fall.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\text{best of } k] &= \frac{k}{k+1} \\ V(k) &= \frac{k}{k+1} - c\,k \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the largest of k independent uniform numbers has distribution function xk, so its average is k/(k+1). V(k) is what the agent nets after paying c for each of the k looks.
Say it“The value of k looks is k over k-plus-one, minus c times k.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &V(k+1) - V(k) \\ &\quad = \frac{1}{(k+1)(k+2)} - c \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each extra look adds less than the one before. Keep looking while the next look’s gain beats its cost; the best k is the first one where 1/((k+1)(k+2)) ≤ c, roughly 1/√c − 1.
Say it“The gain from one more look is one over k-plus-one times k-plus-two, minus c.”

AIMA 2 & 2.1 · agents and environments

Percepts in, actions out: the agent function

An agent perceives its environment through sensors and acts on it through actuators. A robot’s sensors are cameras and bump switches and its actuators are motors; a software agent senses file contents, network packets and typed text, and acts by writing files, sending packets and displaying output. The percept is what the sensors deliver at one instant; the percept sequence is everything the agent has ever perceived. Its choice can depend on its built-in knowledge and on the whole percept sequence, but on nothing it has not perceived.

Mathematically, an agent’s behaviour is its agent function: a map from every possible percept sequence to an action. You could, in principle, write it out as a table. The agent program is something else: the concrete code, running on some physical machine, that implements the function. Keep the two apart: the function is the abstract specification; the program is the implementation. (Calling something an agent is a choice of description, not a fact about it. A pocket calculator can be described as an agent, but nothing is gained by doing so.)

The classic small example is a vacuum world with two squares, A and B. The percept is [location, status], for example [A, Dirty]; the actions are Left, Right, Suck and NoOp. Below you write a rule for each of the four possible latest percepts. That is an agent function that only looks at the newest entry of the sequence. The page computes its exact expected score over a 1,000-step life, starting from each of the eight arrangements of robot and dirt with equal chance, scoring one point for each clean square at each step. Change the rules, then step the robot and watch the percept sequence grow.

\[ a_t = f(p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_t) \]
Read it as: the action at time t is a function f of the whole percept sequence up to t. A rule that only looks at pt is the special case where f ignores everything but the last argument.
Say it“a-t equals f of p-one through p-t.”
\[ \text{rows}(T) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} |\mathcal{P}|^{\,t} \]
Read it as: to tabulate f for every sequence of up to T percepts you need one row per sequence: |P| of length 1, |P|2 of length 2, and so on. The vacuum world has |P| = 4 possible percepts.
Say it“The table needs the sum, for t from one to T, of the number of percepts to the power t.”

AIMA 2.2, 2.2.1 & 2.2.2 · good behaviour

What counts as doing well: performance measures and rationality

An agent’s actions cause a sequence of environment states. If that sequence is desirable, the agent did well. This idea of judging behaviour by its consequences is captured by a performance measure, a score for a sequence of environment states. It must not be a score for the agent’s own internal states, or an agent could do well simply by convincing itself it had.

Designing the measure is harder than it looks. The rule of thumb: measure what you actually want in the environment, not how you think the agent should behave. Pay a vacuum robot for each load of dirt it sucks up and a rational robot can earn more by tipping the dirt back out and sucking it up again. Pay it for clean floor, one point per clean square per step, and that trick earns nothing. Even then there are real choices: is a floor that is reliably fairly clean better than one that alternates between spotless and filthy? When the objective we hand a machine is not quite what we want, it will pursue the wrong thing very effectively; getting the objective right is called the value alignment problem, and it returns in the chapter on agent safety.

What is rational at a given moment depends on four things: the performance measure that defines success, the agent’s prior knowledge of the environment, the actions it can take, and its percept sequence to date. A rational agent, for every possible percept sequence, chooses the action expected to maximise its performance measure, given that evidence and its built-in knowledge.

The book’s rule (suck if dirty, else go to the other square) is rational under specific assumptions: the clean-square measure over 1,000 steps, a known two-square layout, clean squares that stay clean, perfect sensing of location and dirt. Change an assumption and it can stop being rational. Below, dirt can come back with probability p per clean square per step, a move can cost c points, and the measure can be switched. The rivals are a lazy agent (suck if dirty, else stay), a dumper (suck if dirty, else tip dirt out), and a patrol-k agent that waits k steps in a clean square before checking the other (patrol-0 is the book’s rule). Every score is an exact expectation, computed by following the probability of every state through 1,000 steps. Switch the measure to “dirt sucked” and see who wins.

\[ \begin{aligned} a_t^{\ast} = \arg\max_{a}\; &\mathbb{E}\big[\,\text{performance} \\ &\quad \mid p_{1:t},\, K,\, a\,\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the rational action a* at time t is the one with the highest expected performance, given the percepts p1:t so far and the built-in knowledge K. Expected, because the agent cannot know what will actually happen.
Say it“a-star is the arg-max over actions of expected performance, given the percepts so far, the knowledge, and that action.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{score} &= \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \text{clean}_t \\ &\quad - \frac{c}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \text{moved}_t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the clean-floor measure counts the clean squares after each step (0, 1 or 2) and averages over the T = 1,000 steps; movedt is 1 when the action was Left or Right. With c = 0 it is the book’s measure.
Say it“The score is the average over steps of clean squares minus c times whether it moved.”

AIMA 2.2.3

Rational is not omniscient: information, learning and autonomy

An omniscient agent knows what its actions will actually lead to. A rational one only maximises expected performance on the evidence it has. You look both ways, the street is empty, you cross, and a branch falls on you: you were not irrational, just unlucky. Demanding the best actual outcome would require seeing the future, so no agent design could meet it.

Rationality does demand two things, though. First, information gathering: taking actions in order to change future percepts, such as looking before you cross, or exploring an unfamiliar place. Crossing without looking is irrational, not because of what happens but because a cheap action would have improved the evidence. Second, learning: the agent’s first configuration may encode its designer’s prior knowledge, but it should revise and extend that knowledge as experience comes in. An agent that relies on its designer’s knowledge instead of its own percepts lacks autonomy. A good design gives it some starting knowledge, much as evolution gives animals reflexes, plus the ability to learn enough to become independent of that starting point.

Here is a small test. A robot waits in a corridor of nine cells; after each dirt event it rolls to the dirt and comes straight back. Where should it wait? If it knew the true rates qi, the rational spot would be the median of q, which minimises the expected walk. Its designer assumed dirt was equally likely everywhere (a flat prior π), which puts it in the middle. The learning robot blends that prior with the counts it sees, and the prior strength m sets how much weight the designer’s guess keeps. Slide m from 0 to 1,000: from fully autonomous to completely bound by its designer.

\[ \hat q_i = \frac{n_i + m\,\pi_i}{N + m} \]
Read it as: after N events, ni of them in cell i, the estimated rate is a weighted blend: the prior πi carries weight m/(N+m) and the observed share ni/N carries N/(N+m). As N grows, experience takes over.
Say it“q-hat-i is n-i plus m pi-i, over N plus m.”
\[ \begin{aligned} s^{\ast} &= \arg\min_{s}\; \sum_{i} q_i\,|i - s| \\ &= \text{median}(q) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the expected walk from a waiting spot s is the q-weighted average distance to the dirt. It is smallest at a median: moving one cell further from the median adds more probability mass on the far side than it removes on the near side.
Say it“s-star minimises the sum of q-i times the distance from i to s; it is the median of q.”

AIMA 2.3 & 2.3.1 · the nature of environments

Specifying the task environment: PEAS

If rational agents are the solutions, task environments are the problems. Before designing an agent, write the task environment down as fully as you can, under four headings: Performance measure, Environment, Actuators, Sensors. Take a self-driving taxi. Performance: reach the right destination, safely, legally, quickly and comfortably, at low cost; these goals conflict, so trade-offs are needed. Environment: roads from country lanes to motorways, other traffic, pedestrians, cyclists, roadworks, weather, police, passengers. Actuators: steering, accelerator, brakes, signals and horn, plus a screen or voice to talk to passengers. Sensors: cameras, lidar, radar, GPS, speedometer, accelerometers, engine sensors, a microphone or touchscreen for the passenger.

Two points are easy to miss. The performance measure belongs to the task, not the agent: it is written by whoever wants the job done. And software agents have just as real an environment as robots do: a coding assistant’s environment is a repository, a shell and a developer, and its “actuators” are file edits and commands. What matters is how complex the relationship is between the agent’s behaviour, the percepts the environment produces and the performance measure, not whether the environment is physical. AIMA 2.3.2

Seven ways a task environment can be hard

Task environments vary enormously, but a few dimensions decide which agent design is appropriate. In each pair the second is the harder case.

  • Fully or partially observableFully observable if the sensors give the complete state that matters for choosing the action at each moment; then the agent needs no memory of the world. Partially observable through noise, inaccurate sensors or parts of the state the sensors simply miss (a vacuum that feels only its own square). With no sensors at all it is unobservable, and the agent can still sometimes reach its goals.
  • Single-agent or multiagentIs the other object an agent, or just part of the world? Treat it as an agent when its behaviour is best described as maximising a measure that depends on what you do. Chess is competitive; drivers avoiding collisions are cooperative (and competitive for parking spaces). Multiagent settings reward communication, and sometimes randomness, because predictability can be exploited.
  • Deterministic or nondeterministicDeterministic if the next state is fixed by the current state and the action. Otherwise nondeterministic; when the uncertainty comes with explicit probabilities (“25% chance the suck fails”) we call it stochastic. A partially observable world can look random even when it is not.
  • Episodic or sequentialEpisodic: experience divides into atomic episodes, and the right action in one does not depend on earlier ones (checking parts on an assembly line). Sequential: each decision can change all later ones (chess, driving). Episodic tasks are much simpler: no thinking ahead.
  • Static or dynamicDynamic if the world can change while the agent is deliberating, so dithering counts as deciding to do nothing. Static worlds wait. Semidynamic: the world waits but the score does not, as in chess with a clock.
  • Discrete or continuousApplies separately to the state, to time, to the percepts and to the actions. Chess has finitely many states and moves; driving has continuously varying positions, speeds and steering angles.
  • Known or unknownNot about the environment itself but about the agent’s (or designer’s) knowledge of its “laws”: in a known environment, the outcomes (or outcome probabilities) of every action are given. Known is not the same as observable: in solitaire you know the rules but cannot see the face-down cards.
\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{fully observable:} \\ &\quad s_t = g(p_t) \text{ for some } g \\ &\text{deterministic:} \\ &\quad P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t) \in \{0, 1\} \\ &\text{episodic:} \\ &\quad \text{best } a_t \text{ depends only on episode } t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: fully observable means some function g recovers the relevant state st from the current percept pt alone. Deterministic means every transition probability is 0 or 1. Episodic means the best action never depends on earlier episodes. The hardest case is partially observable, multiagent, nondeterministic, sequential, dynamic, continuous and unknown, which is roughly what a taxi driver faces.
Say it“Observable: s-t is g of p-t. Deterministic: every next-state probability is zero or one.”

AIMA 2.4, 2.4.1, 2.4.2 & 2.4.3 · the structure of agents

Agent programs: from lookup tables to reflexes with memory

The job of AI is to design the agent program that implements the agent function. It runs on an architecture, some computing device with sensors and actuators: agent = architecture + program. Every program in this chapter has the same skeleton: it takes the current percept and returns an action. Only the agent function sees the whole history; if the program needs history, it must remember it.

The bluntest program is the table-driven agent: append each percept to the sequence and look the sequence up in a table of actions. It works, and it is hopeless. A single greyscale camera at 640 × 480 pixels has 256307,200 possible frames, so one minute of video at 30 frames a second needs a table with more than 101,300,000,000 rows; the observable universe has about 1080 atoms. The real challenge of AI is to produce rational behaviour from a small program instead.

A simple reflex agent chooses on the current percept alone, using condition–action rules such as “if dirty then Suck”. It is as simple as can be and it is right only when the right decision can be read off the current percept, which in practice means a fully observable world. Here the robot feels only “is my square dirty?” and “did I just bump into something?”. With rules “clean and no bump: Right; bumped: Down”, it slides into a corner and bumps forever: an infinite loop. A randomised reflex agent (clean: pick a direction at random) escapes such loops (one of the rare cases where randomness helps a single agent), but it wanders.

A model-based reflex agent keeps internal state that tracks the parts of the world it cannot see now. Updating it needs two kinds of knowledge: a transition model (what my actions do and how the world changes by itself: “moving Right takes me one square right unless a wall is in the way”) and a sensor model (how the world shows up in percepts: “a bump means the square I tried to enter is a wall”). This robot was told its start square, so by combining the two it always knows where it is, learns where the walls are, and remembers when it last visited each square. Its rule is still a reflex, just applied to the state: clean here: move to the neighbour visited longest ago. The state is a best guess; it can be wrong when the models are. Step each program and compare the world with what the agent thinks.

\[ \begin{aligned} s_t &= \text{Update}(s_{t-1},\, a_{t-1},\, p_t) \\ a_t &= \text{Rule}(s_t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the agent folds its last action at−1 and the new percept pt into its previous state st−1, using the transition and sensor models, then fires the first rule whose condition matches the new state. A simple reflex agent is the case where the state is just the current percept.
Say it“s-t is the update of the old state, the last action and the new percept; a-t is the rule that matches s-t.”
\[ \text{rows} > |\mathcal{P}|^{T}, \qquad |\mathcal{P}| = 256^{\,640 \times 480} \]
Read it as: a table for T percepts needs at least |P|T rows (the longest sequences alone). With one camera frame per percept, |P| is 256 raised to the number of pixels: log10|P| = 307,200 × log10256 ≈ 739,811 digits per frame.
Say it“The table needs more rows than the number of percepts to the power T.”

AIMA 2.4.4 & 2.4.5

Goals and utilities: thinking ahead, and weighing outcomes

Knowing the current state is not always enough to decide. At a junction a taxi can turn left, turn right or go straight on; which is correct depends on where it is going. A goal-based agent keeps goal information, descriptions of desirable situations, and uses its model to look ahead: “what will happen if I do this, and does it bring me closer to the goal?”. Finding action sequences that reach goals is what search and planning do. The pay-off is flexibility: change the goal and the behaviour changes, with no rules rewritten. Our goal-based vacuum’s goal is “every square checked in this sweep”; it plans a shortest path to the nearest unchecked square, and starts a fresh sweep when it has checked them all.

Goals are blunt: a state either meets the goal or it does not. A utility function gives each state a degree of desirability, an internal version of the performance measure. Utilities are needed when goals conflict (speed against safety), and when no goal can be reached for certain, so the likelihood of success has to be weighed against how much success matters. A rational utility-based agent picks the action with the highest expected utility. Our utility-based vacuum believes each square has become dirty again with probability 1 − (1 − p)age, where age is the time since it last saw the square clean, and heads for the square with the most expected dirt per step of effort. Not every rational agent needs an explicit utility function (the book’s reflex vacuum was rational without one), and maximising expected utility is often hard to compute, which brings back limited rationality. Raise the dirt rate and compare the two planners.

\[ \mathrm{EU}(a \mid s) = \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\; U(s') \]
Read it as: the expected utility of action a in state s is the utility of each outcome s′, weighted by how likely a makes it. The rational utility-based agent takes the action with the largest EU.
Say it“E-U of a is the sum over outcomes of the probability of s-prime given s and a, times U of s-prime.”
\[ \text{value}(c) = \frac{1 - (1-p)^{\,\text{age}_c}}{d_c + 1} \]
Read it as: heading for square c pays off if c is dirty, which has probability 1 − (1 − p)age (a never-seen square starts at ½, the starting dirt rate), and costs dc moves plus one suck. The agent picks the square with the most expected dirt per step.
Say it“The value of a square is the chance it is dirty, over the distance plus one.”

AIMA 2.4.6

Learning agents: performer, critic, learner and explorer

Writing every rule by hand does not scale. Turing’s suggestion in 1950 was to build machines that learn and then teach them, and learning has a second advantage: it lets an agent work in initially unknown environments and become more competent than its starting knowledge allowed. A learning agent has four conceptual parts. The performance element is everything we have built so far: it takes percepts and chooses actions. The critic tells it how well it is doing, judged against a fixed performance standard; this is needed because percepts alone say nothing about success. The learning element uses the critic’s feedback to improve the performance element. The problem generator suggests exploratory actions that may be worse now but lead to new and informative experience.

The standard must stay outside the agent and fixed. If the agent could edit it, it could “succeed” by lowering the bar, the same trap as the self-deceiving performance measure. Below, the two-square robot sees both squares and does not know its rules. Its critic turns each step into a reward (clean squares minus c per move). Its learning element is Q-learning, from the reinforcement-learning part of the course: it nudges an estimate Q(s, a) of the long-run, discounted reward of each action in each of the eight states. Its problem generator takes a random action with probability ε. It starts optimistic (every Q at 20, the most any action could be worth) so that it tries everything. Set ε to 0 and then to 0.4 to see why the explorer matters.

\[ \begin{aligned} Q(s,a) &\leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\,\delta \\ \delta &= r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a') \\ &\quad - Q(s,a) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: after taking a in s, receiving reward r from the critic and landing in s′, the learning element moves Q(s, a) by α times the error δ, the gap between its old value and the target: r plus the discounted value γ of the best action from s′. The performance element then plays the action with the largest Q, except when the problem generator steps in.
Say it“Q of s a moves toward r plus gamma times the best Q of s-prime, by alpha times the difference.”

AIMA 2.4.7

How the parts see the world: atomic, factored and structured states

Every component above (the model of how the world changes, the rules, the utility function, the learner) has to represent the world somehow. Representations line up along an axis of growing expressiveness. In an atomic representation each state is an indivisible label with no internal structure: “state 37”. Search, game playing, hidden Markov models and Markov decision processes often work this way. A factored representation splits a state into a fixed set of variables or attributes, each with a value: Location = 3, Dirt1 = true, …. Two states can now share some values and differ in others, which lets you represent uncertainty about part of a state. Constraint satisfaction, propositional logic, planning, Bayesian networks and most machine learning use factored states. A structured representation describes objects and the relations between them: At(Robot, Sq3), Dirty(Sq1), Adjacent(Sq1, Sq2). First-order logic, knowledge-based learning and natural-language understanding live here.

A more expressive representation can say everything a less expressive one can, often far more concisely: the rule “suck whenever you are on a dirty square” is one structured sentence but one table row per atomic state. The price is that reasoning and learning get harder. A second distinction is how a representation maps onto memory: localist (one concept, one location, like a one-hot vector) or distributed (each concept spread over many locations, each location shared by many concepts), which is more robust to noise and puts similar concepts close together, as in the embeddings inside neural networks. Grow the corridor and watch how fast each representation grows.

\[ |\mathcal{S}| = n \cdot 2^{\,n} \]
Read it as: a vacuum corridor of n squares has n possible robot locations and 2n patterns of dirt, so an atomic representation needs n·2n distinct labels, and a localist one-hot code needs that many slots.
Say it“The number of states is n times two to the n.”
\[ \text{bits} = \lceil \log_2 n \rceil + n \]
Read it as: a factored state needs one location variable (⌈log2 n⌉ bits) and n true/false dirt variables. The same information, written as a distributed binary code, takes about log2|S| bits instead of |S| one-hot slots.
Say it“Bits equal the ceiling of log-two n, plus n.”

W, H = 8, 5
WALL = [0] * (W * H)

FREE = [i for i in range(W * H) if not WALL[i]]
DIRS = {"R": (1, 0), "D": (0, 1), "L": (-1, 0), "U": (0, -1)}; ORDER = ["R", "D", "L", "U"]
def step1(i, d):
    x, y = i % W + DIRS[d][0], i // W + DIRS[d][1]
    return -1 if x < 0 or y < 0 or x >= W or y >= H else y * W + x
def bfs(frm, wall):
    dist = [-1] * (W * H); first = [None] * (W * H); q = deque([frm]); dist[frm] = 0
    while q:
        u = q.popleft()
        for d in ORDER:
            v = step1(u, d)
            if v < 0 or wall[v] or dist[v] >= 0: continue
            dist[v] = dist[u] + 1; first[v] = d if u == frm else first[u]; q.append(v)
    return dist, first
def grid_run(kind, o, seed, T):
    R = rng(seed); dirt = [0] * (W * H)
    for i in range(W * H):
        r = R()
        if not WALL[i]: dirt[i] = 1 if r < 0.5 else 0
    pos, bump, clean_sum, nmoves = 0, False, 0.0, 0
    RA = rng(seed + 1000); gpos, gt, last = 0, 0, None
    gwall = WALL[:] if o["known"] else [0] * (W * H); visit = [-1.0] * (W * H); seen = [-1.0] * (W * H); sweep = [0] * (W * H)
    pdyn = o["p"] if o["dynamic"] else 0
    def pd(i): return 1 - 0.5 * (1 - pdyn) ** gt if seen[i] < 0 else 1 - (1 - pdyn) ** (gt - seen[i])
    for t in range(T):
        dirty = bool(dirt[pos])
        if kind not in ("reflex", "random"):
            if last in DIRS:
                v = step1(gpos, last)
                if bump:
                    if v >= 0: gwall[v] = 1
                elif v >= 0: gpos = v
            visit[gpos] = gt; sweep[gpos] = 1
            if not dirty: seen[gpos] = gt
        a = "N"
        if dirty: a = "S"
        elif kind == "random": a = ORDER[math.floor(RA() * 4)]
        elif o["full"] and kind != "utility":
            dist, first = bfs(pos, WALL); best = -1
            for i in FREE:
                if dirt[i] and dist[i] > 0 and (best < 0 or dist[i] < dist[best]): best = i
            if best >= 0: a = first[best]
        elif kind == "reflex": a = "D" if bump else "R"
        elif kind == "model":
            best = None
            for d in ORDER:
                v = step1(gpos, d)
                if v < 0 or gwall[v]: continue
                if best is None or visit[v] < visit[best[1]]: best = (d, v)
            if best: a = best[0]
        elif kind == "goal":
            dist, first = bfs(gpos, gwall)
            def pick():
                b = -1
                for i in FREE:
                    if not gwall[i] and not sweep[i] and dist[i] > 0 and (b < 0 or dist[i] < dist[b]): b = i
                return b
            b = pick()
            if b < 0:
                sweep = [0] * (W * H); sweep[gpos] = 1; b = pick()
            if b >= 0: a = first[b]
        elif kind == "utility":
            dist, first = bfs(pos if o["full"] else gpos, WALL if o["full"] else gwall); b, bv = -1, 0
            for i in FREE:
                if not o["full"] and gwall[i]: continue
                if dist[i] <= 0: continue
                pdi = (1 if dirt[i] else 0) if o["full"] else pd(i); v = pdi / (dist[i] + 1)
                if v > bv + 1e-12: bv, b = v, i
            if b >= 0: a = first[b]
        last = a; gt += 1
        # the world
        rS = R(); bump = False
        if a == "S":
            if dirt[pos] and not (o["stoch"] and rS < 0.25): dirt[pos] = 0
        elif a in DIRS:
            nmoves += 1; v = step1(pos, a)
            if v < 0 or WALL[v]: bump = True
            else: pos = v
        for i in FREE:
            r = R()
            if o["dynamic"] and not dirt[i] and r < o["p"]: dirt[i] = 1
        clean_sum += sum(1 for i in FREE if not dirt[i]) / len(FREE)

# ---------- learning agent: Q-learning on the two-square world ----------
def q2step(l, dA, dB, a, p, cost, ra, rb):
    if a == 0: l = 0
    elif a == 1: l = 1
    elif a == 2:
        if l: dB = 0
        else: dA = 0
    if not dA and ra < p: dA = 1
    if not dB and rb < p: dB = 1
    return l, dA, dB, (2 - dA - dB) - cost * (1 if a < 2 else 0)
def trans(s, a, p, cost):
    l, dA, dB = s >> 2, (s >> 1) & 1, s & 1
    for i in (0, 1):
        for j in (0, 1):
            pa = (p if i else 1 - p) if ((a == 2 and not l) or not dA) else (0 if i else 1)
            pb = (p if j else 1 - p) if ((a == 2 and l) or not dB) else (0 if j else 1)
            if pa * pb:
                l2, a2, b2, r = q2step(l, dA, dB, a, p, cost, 0 if i else 1, 0 if j else 1); yield pa * pb, l2 * 4 + a2 * 2 + b2, r
def vi(p, cost, g):
    Q = np.zeros((8, 4))
    for it in range(2000):
        V = Q.max(1); Qn = np.array([[sum(w * (r + g * V[s2]) for w, s2, r in trans(s, a, p, cost)) for a in range(4)] for s in range(8)])
        d = np.abs(Qn - Q).max(); Q = Qn
        if d < 1e-12: break
    return Q
def pol_avg(pol, p, cost, T=1000):
    P = np.full(8, 1 / 8); tot = 0
    for t in range(T):
        Qn = np.zeros(8)
        for s in range(8):
            for w, s2, r in trans(s, pol[s], p, cost): Qn[s2] += P[s] * w; tot += P[s] * w * r
        P = Qn
    return tot / T
def qlearn(p, cost, eps, alpha, g, N, seed):
    R = rng(seed); l = 1 if R() < 0.5 else 0; dA = 1 if R() < 0.5 else 0; dB = 1 if R() < 0.5 else 0
    q0 = 2 / (1 - g); Q = [[q0] * 4 for _ in range(8)]; rew = []
    am = lambda row: max(range(4), key=lambda i: (row[i], -i))
    for t in range(N):

        ra, rb = R(), R(); l, dA, dB, r = q2step(l, dA, dB, a, p, cost, ra, rb); s2 = l * 4 + dA * 2 + dB
        Q[s][a] += alpha * (r + g * max(Q[s2]) - Q[s][a]); rew.append(r)
    return Q, rew, [am(row) for row in Q]

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A rational agent chooses, for every percept sequence, the action that maximises its expected performance measure, given that evidence and its built-in knowledge. Rational is not omniscient, and with real time limits the aim is limited rationality.
  • The performance measure scores sequences of environment states, and should measure what you want in the world, not how you think the agent should behave; otherwise a rational agent will game it.
  • Specify the task with PEAS (performance, environment, actuators, sensors) and classify it: observable, agents, deterministic, episodic, static, discrete, known. The hard side of each pair demands more of the agent.
  • Agent = architecture + program. Simple reflex agents suffice only when the current percept tells all; model-based agents track what they cannot see with transition and sensor models; goal-based agents plan; utility-based agents weigh outcomes by expected utility.
  • Learning agents add a critic, a learning element and a problem generator to the performance element, so they can start with partial knowledge and become autonomous; every component works over atomic, factored or structured representations.

Check your understanding

A robot is paid for each piece of litter it puts in a bin. What could go wrong, and what would be a better measure?

A rational robot could maximise the payment by taking litter out of the bin, or creating litter, and binning it again: the measure rewards an activity rather than the state of the world. Score the state you want instead, for example the amount of litter left on the ground at each time step, with penalties for damage or energy.

A poker bot plays the move with the highest expected winnings and loses the hand. Was it irrational?

Not necessarily. Rationality is judged by expected performance given what it could perceive (its own cards and the betting), not by the outcome. Demanding the best actual result would require knowing the opponents’ cards and the deck, which is omniscience.

Why does the simple reflex vacuum get stuck in the grid world, and what are two ways out?

Its rules depend only on the current percept (dirty? bumped?), so in a corner the same percept recurs and it repeats the same failing action forever. Randomising the action when clean breaks the loop; keeping internal state (a model of where it has been) lets it choose sensibly instead of blindly.

Classify a crossword-solving program and a self-driving car on the seven properties.

Crossword: fully observable, single-agent, deterministic, sequential (one answer constrains others), static, discrete, known. Self-driving car: partially observable, multiagent (partly cooperative, partly competitive), nondeterministic, sequential, dynamic, continuous, and largely known in its own physics but not in how others behave.

When does an agent need a utility function rather than a goal?

When goals conflict and must be traded off (speed against safety), or when no goal can be reached with certainty, so the probability of success has to be weighed against how much each outcome matters. A goal only says happy or unhappy; a utility says how happy.
