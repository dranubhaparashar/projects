Inference for Linear Models

Standard errors, tests and the Gauss–Markov theorem

Least squares always hands you numbers: a slope of −7.2, a coefficient of 0.03. But the data was one sample out of many you could have collected, and a different sample would give different numbers. This chapter is about how much those numbers would wobble, which ones you can trust, and why least squares is the steadiest unbiased choice. On the right, the grey dots are 392 real cars. The set-up

Least squares as a projection

We have N examples. Each has p inputs and one output y. Stack the inputs as the rows of a matrix X with N rows and p + 1 columns, the first column all ones so the model gets an intercept. The linear model says y is roughly Xβ, and least squares picks the β that makes the squared misses as small as possible. Chapter 1.2 solved that problem. Here we look at the answer from a second angle, which is the key to everything that follows.

Think of y not as N dots on a chart but as one arrow in N-dimensional space, one coordinate per example. Each column of X is an arrow in the same space. Every choice of β gives a point Xβ in the flat plane those columns span. Least squares picks the point of that plane closest to y: the foot of the perpendicular. The fitted values ŷ are the shadow of y on the plane, and the residual y − ŷ sticks straight out of it.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{RSS}(\beta) &= \sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(y_i - f(x_i)\big)^2 \\ f(x_i) &= \beta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{p} x_{ij}\beta_j \\ \mathrm{RSS}(\beta) &= (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\beta)^{\top}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\beta) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for every example, take the gap between the real output and the model’s guess, square it, and add up. The last line is the same sum written as the squared length of one arrow, y − Xβ. Nothing in least squares itself needs probability: it is pure geometry.
Say it“R-S-S of beta is the sum of squared residuals, which is y minus X-beta, transposed, times y minus X-beta.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\beta &= (\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y} \\ \mathbf{H} &= \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top} \\ \hat{\mathbf{y}} &= \mathbf{H}\mathbf{y}, \quad \mathbf{X}^{\top}(\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{y}}) = \mathbf{0} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the best coefficients come from the normal equations. H is the hat matrix: it “puts the hat on y”, projecting it onto the column space. The last equation says the residual is perpendicular to every column of X: it sums to zero (the ones column) and is uncorrelated with each input. The diagonal entry hii is example i’s leverage: how strongly its own y pulls its own fitted value. The leverages add up to p + 1. If two columns of X are copies of each other, XᵀX cannot be inverted: ŷ is still the unique shadow, but many β give it.
Say it“Beta-hat is X-transpose-X inverse, X-transpose y. Y-hat is H y. X-transpose times the residual is zero.”

So far nothing is random. To ask “how sure are we?” we need a story about where y came from. The standard story, used for the rest of the chapter: the inputs are fixed, and each output is the true linear function plus independent noise with mean zero and the same variance σ² for every example. For the tests we also assume the noise is Gaussian.

Sampling distribution

How much a fit wobbles

Keep the inputs fixed and imagine re-running the experiment: same x values, fresh noise. Each run gives new y values and therefore new coefficients. The spread of β̂ across all those imagined runs is its sampling distribution, and its standard deviation is the standard error. Because β̂ is a fixed matrix times y, and y is Gaussian, β̂ is Gaussian too, centred on the truth. Below, the true line is y = 2 + 0.5x. Press “Draw 1,000 samples”, then change the noise, the number of points or their spread and draw again.

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta) &= (\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\sigma^2 \\ \hat\beta &\sim N\big(\beta,\ (\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\sigma^2\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the coefficients scatter around the true β (least squares is unbiased) with a covariance matrix that depends only on the inputs and the noise level. For a straight line the slope’s variance is σ²/Σ(xi − x̄)²: more noise widens it; more points, or points more spread out, narrow it.
Say it“The variance of beta-hat is X-transpose-X inverse times sigma squared; beta-hat is normal with mean beta and that covariance.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\sigma^2 &= \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{N-p-1} \\ \mathrm{RSS} &= \sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_i-\hat y_i)^2 \\ \frac{(N-p-1)\,\hat\sigma^2}{\sigma^2} &\sim \chi^2_{N-p-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: we never know σ², so we estimate it from the residuals. Divide by N − p − 1, not N: fitting p + 1 coefficients used up that many directions, so the residuals are a little smaller than the true noise. With this divisor the estimate is unbiased, its spread follows a scaled chi-squared, and it is independent of β̂.
Say it“Sigma-hat squared is the residual sum of squares over N minus p minus one; N minus p minus one times it, over sigma squared, is chi-squared with that many degrees of freedom.”

One coefficient

Standard errors, t-tests and confidence intervals

To test whether input j matters, ask: if its true coefficient were zero, how surprising would our β̂j be? Measure it in standard errors. If we knew σ, this z-score would be standard normal. We plug in σ̂ instead, and the extra uncertainty turns the normal into Student’s t distribution with N − p − 1 degrees of freedom: the same bell shape with heavier tails. Turn the same idea round and you get a confidence interval. Below, each horizontal bar is the 95% interval for the slope from one fresh sample. Draw intervals, then make N tiny and switch to the normal quantile.

\[ z_j = \frac{\hat\beta_j}{\hat\sigma\sqrt{v_j}}\ \sim\ t_{N-p-1} \ \text{ if } \beta_j = 0 \]
Read it as: vj is the j-th diagonal entry of (XᵀX)−1, so σ̂√vj is the standard error of β̂j. A z-score beyond about ±2 is unusual if the coefficient is really zero. The p-value is the chance of a score at least that extreme under that “no effect” hypothesis. Once N − p − 1 is above about 30, t and normal tail areas hardly differ.
Say it“z-j is beta-hat-j over sigma-hat times root v-j; under the null it has a t distribution with N minus p minus one degrees of freedom.”
\[ \hat\beta_j \pm t^{(1-\alpha/2)}_{N-p-1}\ \hat\sigma\sqrt{v_j} \]
Read it as: go a few standard errors either side of the estimate. With α = 0.05 the multiplier is the t quantile, a bit more than 1.96. Before the data arrive, this recipe has a 95% chance of producing an interval that covers the true βj. Once you have one interval, it either covers the truth or it doesn’t: the 95% describes the recipe, not that interval.
Say it“Beta-hat-j plus or minus the t quantile times its standard error.”

A group of coefficients

F-tests: dropping several inputs at once

Sometimes the question is about a group: can we drop all three engine variables? Or a categorical input coded as several dummy columns, which only make sense together? Fit the big model and the small one. The small one always fits worse; the question is whether it fits too much worse to blame on noise. The F statistic compares the extra residual error per dropped coefficient with the noise level. Below, four inputs x1–x4 with true coefficients 0.8, 0.4, a value you choose, and 0. Pick which inputs to drop, then simulate 2,000 datasets and watch how often the test says “keep them”.

\[ \begin{aligned} F &= \frac{(\mathrm{RSS}_0 - \mathrm{RSS}_1)/(p_1 - p_0)}{\mathrm{RSS}_1/(N - p_1 - 1)} \\ &\sim F_{p_1-p_0,\ N-p_1-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: model 1 is the big one with p1 + 1 coefficients; model 0 drops p1 − p0 of them. The top is how much the fit worsened, per dropped coefficient; the bottom is σ̂² from the big model. If the dropped coefficients are truly zero (and the noise is Gaussian), F follows the F distribution with those two degrees of freedom, so it hovers around 1. A large F means the dropped inputs were doing real work. Dropping a single input gives F = zj²: the t-test is the one-input F-test.
Say it“F is the drop in RSS per dropped parameter, over the big model’s RSS per residual degree of freedom.”

A joint question has a joint answer

Testing two coefficients one at a time is not the same as testing them together. When two inputs are strongly correlated, the data can be sure that at least one of them matters while being unsure which. The honest picture is a confidence region: an ellipse of coefficient pairs consistent with the data. Its tilt comes from the correlation. Raise the correlation between x1 and x2 and draw new samples until the origin sits inside the box of separate intervals but outside the ellipse.

\[ \begin{aligned} C_\beta = \big\{\beta :\ & (\hat\beta-\beta)^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}(\hat\beta-\beta) \\ & \le \hat\sigma^2\,\chi^{2\,(1-\alpha)}_{p+1}\big\} \\ S:\ & \mathbf{d}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_S^{-1}\mathbf{d} \le 2\,\hat\sigma^2 F^{(1-\alpha)}_{2,\,N-p-1} \\ & \mathbf{d} = \hat\beta_S-\beta_S \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first part is a region for the whole vector β: all the vectors whose squared distance from β̂, measured in the data’s own metric XᵀX, is within a chi-squared quantile of the noise. It treats σ̂ as if it were σ. The second part, used in the lab, is the exact version for a pair S of coefficients: VS is their 2 × 2 block of (XᵀX)−1, and the F quantile accounts for estimating σ. As N grows, 2F approaches the chi-squared with 2 degrees of freedom. The origin lies outside this ellipse exactly when the F-test of “both are zero” rejects.
Say it“The confidence set holds every beta whose distance from beta-hat, weighted by X-transpose-X, is at most sigma-hat squared times the chi-squared quantile.”

Why least squares

The Gauss–Markov theorem

Least squares is not the only way to estimate a slope. You could join the first and last points. You could split the data into a low third and a high third and join their averages (Wald’s grouping method). Both are linear in y and both are unbiased: on average they hit the true slope. The Gauss–Markov theorem says that among all such estimators, least squares has the smallest variance. It needs only uncorrelated noise with equal variance; it does not need Gaussian noise. But “best unbiased” is not “best”: a biased estimator such as ridge regression can trade a little bias for a big cut in variance. Simulate, then turn the ridge penalty λ and the true slope.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\tilde\theta = \mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{y},\ \ \mathrm{E}(\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{y}) = a^{\top}\beta \\ &\Rightarrow\ \operatorname{Var}(a^{\top}\hat\beta) \le \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{y}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: pick any combination θ = aᵀβ you want to estimate (a single slope, or a prediction). Any estimator that is a weighted sum of the y’s and is unbiased has at least the variance of the least-squares one. Least squares is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator, BLUE. The proof: write c = (least-squares weights) + d; unbiasedness forces d to be perpendicular to every column of X, so the two parts don’t interact and d can only add variance, σ²‖d‖².
Say it“If c-transpose-y is unbiased for a-transpose-beta, its variance is at least that of a-transpose-beta-hat.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{MSE}(\tilde\theta) &= \mathrm{E}(\tilde\theta-\theta)^2 \\ &= \operatorname{Var}(\tilde\theta) + \big(\mathrm{E}\tilde\theta - \theta\big)^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: an estimator’s average squared miss splits into its spread plus its squared bias. Gauss–Markov fixes the bias at zero and minimises the spread. Allow some bias and the total can be smaller. For a prediction at a new point x0, the expected squared error is σ² + MSE: the noise in the new y, which no estimator can remove, plus the estimator’s own MSE.
Say it“Mean squared error equals variance plus bias squared.”

Inside multiple regression

Multiple regression as successive orthogonalisation

With a single input and no intercept the least-squares slope is just ⟨x, y⟩/⟨x, x⟩, an inner product over a squared length. With an intercept, centre x first: the slope is ⟨x − x̄1, y⟩ / ⟨x − x̄1, x − x̄1⟩. Centring is a two-step move: regress x on the ones column, keep the residual, then regress y on that residual.

That move generalises. If the inputs were all perpendicular to each other, each coefficient would be its own simple regression, unaffected by the others. They usually aren’t, so we make them perpendicular one at a time, as Gram and Schmidt did. Each new input is regressed on the residual versions of the ones before it, and only what is left over, zj, is kept. The coefficient of the last input is then a simple regression of y on its leftover.

  1. Start with z0 = x0 = 1, the ones column.
  2. For j = 1, 2, …, p: regress xj on z0, …, zj−1, one at a time, with coefficients γℓj = ⟨zℓ, xj⟩/⟨zℓ, zℓ⟩, and keep the residual zj = xj − Σℓ<j γℓjzℓ.
  3. Regress y on the last residual zp: the slope is β̂p, exactly the multiple-regression coefficient.
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\beta_p &= \frac{\langle \mathbf{z}_p, \mathbf{y}\rangle}{\langle \mathbf{z}_p, \mathbf{z}_p\rangle} \\ \operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_p) &= \frac{\sigma^2}{\lVert \mathbf{z}_p\rVert^2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the multiple-regression coefficient of xp measures what xp adds after the other inputs have been allowed for. Its variance depends on how much of xp is left once the others are removed. If xp is nearly a combination of the others, zp is tiny, and the coefficient is unstable. Any input can be put last, so this holds for every coefficient.
Say it“Beta-hat-p is z-p dot y over z-p dot z-p; its variance is sigma squared over the squared length of z-p.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{X} &= \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\Gamma} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R} \\ \hat\beta &= \mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{y}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{y} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the algorithm rebuilds X from the perpendicular columns Z and an upper-triangular matrix Γ of the γ’s. Scale each zj to unit length and you have the QR decomposition: Q has orthonormal columns, R is upper triangular. Then the coefficients come from one easy back-substitution, and the fit is y projected onto Q’s columns.
Say it“X equals Q R; beta-hat is R inverse Q-transpose y; y-hat is Q Q-transpose y.”

Computational considerations

QR or Cholesky: how to actually solve it

No serious library computes (XᵀX)−1. There are two standard routes. The Cholesky route forms XᵀX, factors it as RᵀR with R upper triangular, and solves two triangular systems. The QR route factors X itself. Cholesky is cheaper when N is much larger than p. QR is more accurate, because forming XᵀX squares the condition number, the factor by which rounding errors can be magnified. Below, the inputs are the powers 1, x, x², …, xd of 50 evenly spaced x values in [0, 1], a famously ill-conditioned design, and y is built so that every true coefficient is exactly 1. Raise the degree and see which method keeps its digits.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{Cholesky: } \mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R} \\ &\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}, \quad \mathbf{R}\hat\beta = \mathbf{w} \\ &\text{QR: } \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}, \quad \mathbf{R}\hat\beta = \mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{y} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: both routes end with a triangular system, solved from the bottom row up. In exact arithmetic they give the same β̂ (the two R’s even agree, up to signs). With N examples and p inputs, Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman count roughly p³ + Np²/2 operations for Cholesky and Np² for QR: Cholesky wins when N ≫ p.
Say it“Cholesky factors X-transpose-X as R-transpose R; QR factors X as Q R; either way, back-substitute through R.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \kappa(\mathbf{X}) &= \frac{s_{\max}}{s_{\min}} \\ \kappa(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}) &= \kappa(\mathbf{X})^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: κ is the ratio of the largest to the smallest singular value. A double-precision number carries about 16 digits (machine epsilon ε ≈ 2.2 × 10−16). A careful solver loses roughly log10κ of them. Solving through XᵀX loses twice as many, and once κ(X) passes about 108 nothing may be left at all.
Say it“The condition number of X-transpose-X is the square of the condition number of X.”

Multiple outputs

Several outputs, one projection

Suppose we predict K outputs from the same inputs: here, a car’s fuel economy and its 0–60 mph time, from its weight, horsepower and model year. Stack the outputs as the K columns of a matrix Y. Each output gets its own coefficient vector, a column of B. A natural worry: if the outputs’ errors are correlated, shouldn’t a joint fit borrow strength across them? For least squares, no. With the same inputs for every output and the same error covariance for every example, the joint fit is exactly K separate regressions. Assume any error correlation you like and see that the coefficients don’t move.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{Y} &= \mathbf{X}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{E} \\ \mathrm{RSS}(\mathbf{B}) &= \operatorname{tr}\big[\mathbf{E}^{\top}\mathbf{E}\big] \\ \hat{\mathbf{B}} &= (\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Y} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the trace adds up the squared errors of every output for every example, which is just the K separate RSS’s added together. Each column of B̂ only appears in its own term, so each is fitted by itself. Bishop writes the same result for basis functions φ(x) with Gaussian noise of equal variance on every output: WML = (ΦᵀΦ)−1ΦᵀT. One pseudo-inverse Φ† = (ΦᵀΦ)−1Φᵀ is shared by every output: wk = Φ†tk.
Say it“Y is X B plus E; B-hat is X-transpose-X inverse, X-transpose Y: one regression per column.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{RSS}(\mathbf{B};\boldsymbol{\Sigma}) &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{e}_i^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{e}_i \\ \mathbf{e}_i &= \mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: if the K errors of one example are correlated with covariance Σ, the natural criterion (the Gaussian log-likelihood) weights them by Σ−1. Its minimiser is still B̂ above, for any Σ. It stops being that simple when Σ changes from example to example, or when the outputs use different inputs; then the outputs really are coupled.
Say it“Sum over examples of the residual vector, transposed, times Sigma inverse, times the residual vector.”

Real-world project

Project: which car features really matter for fuel economy?

The Auto MPG data (UCI repository, from the 1983 American Statistical Association data exposition, used by Quinlan in 1993) describes 398 cars from model years 1970–1982. Six have no horsepower recorded, leaving 392. For each we know the fuel economy in miles per gallon and eight inputs: cylinders, engine displacement (cubic inches), horsepower, weight (thousands of pounds), 0–60 mph acceleration time (seconds), model year, and where it was built (USA, Europe or Japan, coded as two dummy columns with the USA as the baseline). This is the same workflow Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman follow for their prostate-cancer example: fit, read the coefficient table, test a group, check on held-out data.

Weight, displacement, horsepower and cylinders are strongly correlated (pairwise correlations 0.84–0.95), so expect large standard errors among them. Read the table, then switch the output to log(mpg).

One car at a time: confidence versus prediction

The cars are shuffled once; 292 train the model and 100 are held back. For a held-back car the model gives two intervals. The confidence interval is for the average mpg of all cars with these exact specs: it only reflects the wobble of β̂, x0ᵀ(XᵀX)−1x0σ̂². The prediction interval is for this one car, so it adds the car’s own noise σ̂², the same σ² + MSE split as in Gauss–Markov. Step through the test cars.

The same analysis in Python

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rows = []
for line in open("auto-mpg.data"):                               # UCI Auto MPG
    f = line.split("\t")[0].split()
    if f[3] != "?": rows.append([float(v) for v in f])           # drop 6 cars with no horsepower
D = np.array(rows)
mpg, cyl, disp, hp, wt, acc, yr, org = D.T
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(mpg)), cyl, disp, hp, wt / 1000, acc, yr, org == 2, org == 3]).astype(float)
names = ["intercept", "cylinders", "displacement", "horsepower", "weight", "acceleration", "year", "Europe", "Japan"]

def ols(X, y):
    Q, R = np.linalg.qr(X)                                       # least squares by QR (ESL 3.2.3 / 3.9)
    b = np.linalg.solve(R, Q.T @ y)
    rss = np.sum((y - X @ b) ** 2); df = len(y) - X.shape[1]
    Rinv = np.linalg.inv(R); se = np.sqrt(np.sum(Rinv ** 2, axis=1) * rss / df)   # diag (X'X)^-1
    return b, se, rss, df

for label, y in [("mpg", mpg), ("log mpg", np.log(mpg))]:
    b, se, rss, df = ols(X, y); t = b / se; p = 2 * stats.t.sf(abs(t), df); q = stats.t.ppf(0.975, df)
    print(f"--- {label}: N = {len(y)}, df = {df}, sigma_hat = {np.sqrt(rss / df):.4f}, R^2 = {1 - rss / np.sum((y - y.mean())**2):.4f}")
    for n, bj, sj, tj, pj in zip(names, b, se, t, p):
        print(f"{n:13s} {bj:10.4f}  se {sj:8.4f}  t {tj:7.2f}  p {pj:.2g}  95% CI [{bj - q*sj:9.4f}, {bj + q*sj:9.4f}]")

keep = [0, 2, 4, 6, 7, 8]                                        # drop cylinders, horsepower, acceleration
for label, y in [("mpg", mpg), ("log mpg", np.log(mpg))]:
    b1, _, rss1, df1 = ols(X, y); b0, _, rss0, _ = ols(X[:, keep], y)
    F = ((rss0 - rss1) / 3) / (rss1 / df1)
    print(f"F-test ({label}), drop cylinders+horsepower+acceleration: F = {F:.4f} on (3, {df1}), p = {stats.f.sf(F, 3, df1):.4g}")

y = mpg; b1 = ols(X, y)[0]; others = [0, 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]
z = X[:, 3] - X[:, others] @ np.linalg.lstsq(X[:, others], X[:, 3], rcond=None)[0]
print(f"horsepower via its leftover z: <z,y>/<z,z> = {z @ y / (z @ z):.6f}  (multiple regression: {b1[3]:.6f})")

perm = np.random.RandomState(0).permutation(len(y)); tr, te = perm[:292], perm[292:]
for lab, cols in [("full model", list(range(9))), ("without cyl/hp/accel", keep), ("intercept only", [0])]:
    bb = ols(X[tr][:, cols], y[tr])[0]
    print(f"test MSE ({lab:21s}) = {np.mean((y[te] - X[te][:, cols] @ bb) ** 2):.4f}")
b, se, rss, df = ols(X[tr], y[tr]); s2 = rss / df; V = np.linalg.inv(X[tr].T @ X[tr])
h = np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", X[te], V, X[te]); q = stats.t.ppf(0.975, df); pred = X[te] @ b
print(f"test cars inside 95% prediction interval: {np.sum(abs(y[te] - pred) <= q * np.sqrt(s2 * (1 + h)))} of 100")

Xm = X[:, [0, 4, 3, 6]]; Y = np.column_stack([mpg, acc])         # two outputs from weight, horsepower, year
B = np.linalg.pinv(Xm) @ Y; E = Y - Xm @ B
print("multi-output B (rows: intercept, weight, horsepower, year; columns: mpg, acceleration)")
for r in B: print(f"   {r[0]:10.5f} {r[1]:10.5f}")
print(f"residual correlation between the two outputs = {np.corrcoef(E.T)[0, 1]:.4f}")

x = np.linspace(0, 1, 50)
for d in (5, 9, 13):                                             # conditioning: normal equations vs QR
    V = np.vander(x, d + 1, increasing=True); yy = V @ np.ones(d + 1)

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Least squares projects y onto the space spanned by X’s columns; the residual is perpendicular to every column, and the hat matrix H does the projecting.
  • With fixed inputs and independent noise of variance σ², β̂ is unbiased with covariance (XᵀX)−1σ²; estimate σ² by RSS/(N − p − 1).
  • One coefficient: t = β̂j/SE with N − p − 1 degrees of freedom. A group: the F-test on the rise in RSS. Correlated inputs can be jointly important yet individually “insignificant”.
  • Gauss–Markov: among linear unbiased estimators, least squares has the smallest variance. A biased one, such as ridge, can still have a smaller mean squared error.
  • Each coefficient is a simple regression of y on what is left of its input after the others (Gram–Schmidt, i.e. QR); solve with QR for accuracy, Cholesky for speed. Several outputs with the same inputs are just separate regressions.

Check your understanding

Why divide the residual sum of squares by N − p − 1 rather than N?

The residual lives in the N − p − 1 directions perpendicular to the column space; the fit has already absorbed the noise in the other p + 1 directions. So the expected RSS is (N − p − 1)σ², and dividing by N would underestimate σ², most badly when N is small.

Horsepower has p = 0.19 in the mpg model. Does that mean horsepower doesn’t affect fuel economy?

No. It means that once weight, displacement and cylinders are in the model, the data can’t pin down horsepower’s extra effect: those inputs are so correlated that horsepower has little leftover variation, so its standard error is large. On its own, horsepower is strongly related to mpg, and in the log(mpg) model it is significant.

You drop one input and the F statistic is 4.0. What is that input’s t statistic?

±2.0. For a single dropped coefficient, F is exactly the square of its t statistic, and the two tests give the same p-value.

Gauss–Markov says least squares is best. Why does anyone use ridge regression?

“Best” is only among unbiased linear estimators. Ridge is biased, but its variance can drop by more than its squared bias grows, so its mean squared error, and its prediction error, can be lower, especially with noisy data or correlated inputs.

Why do good libraries use QR instead of solving the normal equations?

Forming XᵀX squares the condition number, so rounding errors can grow by κ(X)² instead of κ(X). With ill-conditioned inputs the normal equations can lose every digit while QR still gives a usable answer. Cholesky is cheaper when N ≫ p and the inputs are well conditioned.
