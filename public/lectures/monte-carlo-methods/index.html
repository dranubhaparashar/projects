Monte Carlo Methods & Importance Sampling

No model of the world: play to the end, then average what happened

Dynamic programming needs the rulebook of the world: every transition probability, written down. Monte Carlo methods need only experience. Play an episode to the end, see what it was worth, and average over many episodes. Each cell of the figure is one blackjack situation, your total against the dealer’s face-up card, and its colour is what an agent that has only ever played would do there: stick or hit. Nobody told it the probabilities. Press Learn and watch basic strategy appear, then click any cell to see the two averages behind its colour.

The idea

Averaging what actually happened

In chapter 3.1b, dynamic programming computed values from a model, the probabilities p(s′, r | s, a). For blackjack, writing those down is genuinely awkward: the chance that the dealer finishes on 19 with a 6 showing needs a recursion over every card the dealer might draw. Dealing a hand, on the other hand, is trivial. Monte Carlo methods exploit that asymmetry. The value of a state is an expected return, vπ(s) = Eπ[Gt | St = s], and an expectation can be estimated by an average of samples. So: start in s, follow π to the end of the episode, note the return, repeat, average.

Two things follow. Monte Carlo works only for episodic tasks, because a return is known only when the episode ends; and it updates episode by episode, not step by step. The game here is the blackjack of Sutton and Barto (Example 5.1), also implemented as Gymnasium’s Blackjack-v1: cards come from an infinite deck, an ace counts 1 or 11, the dealer draws until reaching 17, and the hand ends +1 (win), 0 (draw) or −1 (loss), with γ = 1. A state is your sum (12–21), the dealer’s face-up card (ace–10) and whether you hold a usable ace (one counted as 11 without busting): 200 states. Below 12 you always hit, because no card can bust you. Pick a situation and deal hands: the average settles on the exact value, and its error shrinks like 1/√n.

\[ \begin{aligned} V_n(s) &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} G^{(i)} \;\longrightarrow\; v_\pi(s) \\ \operatorname{SE}\big(V_n(s)\big) &= \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: G(i) is the return of the i-th episode that started in s. By the law of large numbers the average Vn(s) converges to the true value vπ(s). σ is the standard deviation of a single return (at most 1 here, since every return is −1, 0 or +1), so the standard error falls as 1/√n: a hundred times more hands buys one more correct decimal place. The “exact” line comes from a dynamic-programming calculation over the dealer’s draws, used on this page only as a referee.
Say it“The estimate after n episodes is the average of their returns; it converges to v-pi of s, with standard error sigma over root n.”

Prediction · rl 5.1

Monte Carlo prediction: first visits and every visit

To estimate all 200 values at once, deal ordinary hands and credit every state a hand passes through with the return that followed it. The return is computed backwards from the end of the episode. If a state occurs more than once in an episode there is a choice: first-visit MC averages only the return after its first occurrence, every-visit MC averages them all. First-visit averages are averages of independent samples, so they are unbiased; every-visit averages are slightly biased for small n, but both converge to vπ(s) (Singh & Sutton, 1996). In blackjack the question never arises, because no state can repeat: your sum only rises, or your ace stops being usable. The second figure therefore uses a tiny task built to repeat.

Notice what the estimates do not do. The value of one cell is never computed from the values of its neighbours; each is its own average. Monte Carlo methods do not bootstrap. One consequence: the cost of estimating a single state does not depend on how many states there are, so you can estimate only the states you care about. Deal 10,000 hands, then a few hundred thousand more, and compare with the exact values; then step the looping task one episode at a time.

\[ \begin{aligned} G_t &= R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \dots \\ &\quad + \gamma^{T-t-1} R_T \\ &= R_{t+1} + \gamma\, G_{t+1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the return from time t is the discounted sum of the rewards still to come; T is the final step. Computed backwards, start with G = 0 at the end and, at each step back, multiply by γ and add the reward. In blackjack γ = 1 and only the last reward is nonzero, so every state in a hand gets the same return: the hand’s result.
Say it“G-t is R-t-plus-one plus gamma times G-t-plus-one.”
\[ V(s) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal T(s)|}\sum_{t\,\in\,\mathcal T(s)} G_t \]
Read it as: 𝒯(s) is the set of time steps, across all episodes so far, at which s was visited: only the first visit of each episode for first-visit MC, every visit for every-visit MC. V(s) is the average return that followed those visits.
Say it“V of s is the average of the returns G-t over the visits to s.”

Without a model · rl 5.2

Estimating action values

With a model, state values are enough to act: look one step ahead, weigh each action’s outcomes by their probabilities, pick the best. Without a model there is nothing to look ahead with, so we estimate the value of each action directly: qπ(s, a), the expected return if you take a in s and follow π afterwards. Monte Carlo handles this exactly as before, averaging over visits to the pair (s, a) instead of the state. The trouble is that a deterministic policy takes only one action in each state, so the other action’s average never receives a single return, and you cannot compare what you never try. Estimating action values therefore needs continual exploration. The simplest fix is exploring starts: begin each episode with a randomly chosen state and action, so that every pair has a chance of being tried. Switch exploring starts off and watch one estimate starve.

\[ \begin{aligned} q_\pi(s,a) &= \mathbb E_\pi\big[\,G_t \mid S_t = s,\; A_t = a\,\big] \\ \pi'(s) &= \arg\max_a\, q_\pi(s,a) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the action value is the expected return when you are in s, take a now, and follow π from the next step on. A policy that is greedy with respect to q, π′, picks the action with the larger value; no model is needed to make that choice, which is the whole point of estimating q rather than v.
Say it“q-pi of s, a is the expected return given state s and action a; the greedy policy takes the arg-max over a.”

Control · rl 5.3

Monte Carlo control with exploring starts

Control means finding a good policy, and the recipe is the generalised policy iteration of chapter 3.1b: evaluate the current policy, make it greedy with respect to the evaluation, repeat. Done literally, each evaluation would need infinitely many episodes. Instead, as value iteration truncates evaluation to a single sweep, Monte Carlo truncates it to a single episode: after every episode, update the averages Q(s, a) for the pairs it visited and make the policy greedy in those states. With exploring starts this is Monte Carlo ES. The algorithm cannot settle on a suboptimal policy (that policy’s values would eventually show it how to improve), and it is widely believed to converge to the optimum, but a general proof is still open; Sutton and Barto list it as an unsolved question, and Tsitsiklis (2002) settled some special cases. Run a million episodes, starting from “stick on 20+”, and watch the policy settle into basic strategy. Outlined cells still disagree with the exact optimum; click a cell to read its two averages.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\pi_0 \xrightarrow{\;E\;} q_{\pi_0} \xrightarrow{\;I\;} \pi_1 \xrightarrow{\;E\;} q_{\pi_1} \\ &\qquad \xrightarrow{\;I\;} \cdots \xrightarrow{\;I\;} \pi_* \\ &q_{\pi_k}\big(s, \pi_{k+1}(s)\big) = \max_a q_{\pi_k}(s,a) \\ &\qquad \ge v_{\pi_k}(s) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: E is an evaluation step (here, Monte Carlo averaging of returns), I an improvement step (make π greedy). The second line is the policy improvement theorem from chapter 3.1b: taking the greedy action once and then following πk is at least as good as following πk throughout, so the greedy policy πk+1 is at least as good as πk in every state. When no state changes, π is optimal. Monte Carlo ES interleaves E and I after every episode, using averages that mix returns from many past policies.
Say it“Evaluate, improve, evaluate, improve, until pi star; q of s and the new greedy action equals the max over a, which is at least v-pi-k of s.”

No exploring starts · rl 5.4

On-policy control with ε-soft policies

Exploring starts are fine in a simulator, but you cannot start a real game, or a real patient’s treatment, in a state of your choosing. The alternative is never to stop exploring. A policy is ε-soft if every action has probability at least ε/|𝒜(s)| in every state; the usual choice is ε-greedy, which takes the greedy action except, with probability ε, an action chosen uniformly at random. This is an on-policy method: the policy being improved is the one generating the data. Generalised policy iteration still works, with one change: making the policy fully greedy would end exploration, so the improvement step moves it only to the ε-greedy policy. The price is that it converges to the best ε-soft policy, which still makes random moves, not to the optimal one. Change ε, then run, and compare the expected return of the ε-soft policy that is learning with that of its greedy part.

\[ \begin{aligned} \pi(A^*\mid s) &= 1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon/|\mathcal A(s)| \\ \pi(a\mid s) &= \varepsilon/|\mathcal A(s)|, \quad a \ne A^* \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: A* = argmaxa Q(s, a) is the greedy action. With two actions (hit, stick), |𝒜(s)| = 2, so the greedy action gets 1 − ε/2 and the other ε/2. Every action keeps being tried, so every Q(s, a) keeps receiving returns.
Say it“The greedy action gets one minus epsilon plus epsilon over the number of actions; every other action gets epsilon over the number of actions.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\sum_a \pi'(a\mid s)\, q_\pi(s,a) \\ &\quad = \frac{\varepsilon}{|\mathcal A|}\sum_a q_\pi(s,a) \\ &\qquad + (1-\varepsilon)\max_a q_\pi(s,a) \\ &\quad \ge v_\pi(s) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: π′ is ε-greedy with respect to qπ, and π is any ε-soft policy. The inequality holds because a maximum is at least any weighted average, here with weights (π(a|s) − ε/|𝒜|)/(1 − ε), which are nonnegative and sum to 1. So π′ is at least as good as π, with equality everywhere only when π is already the best ε-soft policy.
Say it“Following pi-prime for one step is worth epsilon over A times the sum of the q's plus one minus epsilon times the max, and that is at least v-pi of s.”

Learning from another policy · rl 5.5

Off-policy prediction by importance sampling

Every control method faces a dilemma: we want the values of the best policy, which never explores, but we must behave in an exploratory way to find out what is best. The on-policy answer was a compromise. The off-policy answer uses two policies: a target policy π that we want to learn about, and a behaviour policy b that generates the episodes. It needs coverage: every action π might take must have some probability under b. The returns from b have the wrong expectation, vb, so each is reweighted by the importance-sampling ratio, the relative probability of its trajectory under the two policies. The environment’s transition probabilities appear in both and cancel, so the ratio needs no model.

There are two ways to average the reweighted returns. Ordinary importance sampling divides by the number of returns: unbiased, but its variance can be huge, even infinite. Weighted importance sampling divides by the sum of the weights: biased, but the bias vanishes as data accumulates and its variance stays bounded when returns are bounded, so it is usually preferred. Here, as in Sutton and Barto’s Example 5.4, the target policy is “stick on 20+”, the behaviour policy tosses a coin at every decision, and every episode starts from sum 13 with a usable ace against a dealer’s 2. Compare the two error curves, then switch to the looping task, where ordinary importance sampling never settles.

\[ \begin{aligned} \rho_{t:T-1} &= \prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \frac{\pi(A_k\mid S_k)\;p_k}{b(A_k\mid S_k)\;p_k} \\ &= \prod_{k=t}^{T-1} \frac{\pi(A_k\mid S_k)}{b(A_k\mid S_k)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the probability of the rest of the episode under π, divided by its probability under b. Each step also carries the environment’s factor pk = p(Sk+1 | Sk, Ak), the same under both policies, so the unknown dynamics cancel. Here π is deterministic and b tosses a coin, so each step contributes 1/0.5 = 2 when b happened to do what π would do, and 0 the moment it did not: ρ is 2(number of decisions) or 0. The key fact is Eb[ρt:T−1 Gt | St = s] = vπ(s).
Say it“Rho from t to T minus one is the product over the remaining steps of pi of the action over b of the action.”
\[ \begin{aligned} V_{\text{ord}}(s) &= \frac{\sum_{t\in\mathcal T(s)} \rho_{t:T(t)-1}\,G_t}{|\mathcal T(s)|} \\ V_{\text{wtd}}(s) &= \frac{\sum_{t\in\mathcal T(s)} \rho_{t:T(t)-1}\,G_t}{\sum_{t\in\mathcal T(s)} \rho_{t:T(t)-1}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: T(t) is the end of the episode containing step t. Ordinary IS scales each return by its ratio and takes a plain average. Weighted IS takes a weighted average with the ratios as weights; with a single return its estimate is just that return (the ratio cancels), which is why it is never wildly off. Before any return has a nonzero weight, the weighted estimate is undefined; this page reports 0 then.
Say it“Ordinary: the sum of rho times G over the number of visits. Weighted: the same sum over the sum of the rhos.”
ordinary IS: MSE at the end
weighted IS: MSE at the end

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb E_b\big[(\rho\,G)^2\big] \\ &\quad = \sum_{L=1}^{\infty} 0.5^{L}\,0.9^{\,L-1}\,0.1\cdot 2^{2L} \\ &\quad = 0.2\sum_{L=1}^{\infty} 1.8^{\,L-1} = \infty \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the looping task (Sutton and Barto’s Example 5.5): one state, two actions. Right ends the episode with reward 0; left stays with probability 0.9 and ends with reward +1 with probability 0.1. The target policy always goes left, so vπ(s) = 1; b picks each action half the time. A return counts only if every action was left, say L of them; that episode has probability 0.5L·0.9L−1·0.1 under b, and its ratio is 2L. Squaring it gives 4L, which grows faster than the probability 0.5L·0.9L−1·0.1 shrinks, so the second moment, and hence the variance, is infinite. The mean is still 1; the average just never settles.
Say it“The expected square of rho G is the sum over L of the probability of the episode times two to the two L, which diverges.”
Bookkeeping · rl 5.6

Incremental implementation

Keeping every return just to average them would waste memory. On-policy, the familiar running-mean trick from the bandits chapter does it in constant memory: V ← V + (1/n)(G − V). Ordinary importance sampling is the same with ρG in place of G. Weighted importance sampling needs one more number per state, the running total of the weights, C; each new return then moves the estimate towards itself by its share of the total weight. A return with weight zero changes nothing, and a heavy return moves the estimate a long way. Step through episodes one at a time and check that the incremental value always equals the batch formula of the previous section.

\[ \begin{aligned} C_n &= C_{n-1} + W_n, \qquad C_0 = 0 \\ V_{n+1} &= V_n + \frac{W_n}{C_n}\big(G_n - V_n\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Gn is the n-th return from the state and Wn = ρ its weight. Cn is the total weight so far, and Wn/Cn is the step size. If every W were 1, Cn would be n and this would be the plain running mean. The result after n returns equals Σ WkGk / Σ Wk exactly, without storing any of them.
Say it“Add the new weight to C; move V towards G by W over C of the gap.”

Control, off-policy · rl 5.7

Off-policy Monte Carlo control

Put the pieces together. The target policy π is greedy with respect to Q, so it is deterministic and can become optimal; the behaviour policy b is any soft policy, here ε-greedy with respect to the same Q. Because π(a|s) is 1 for the greedy action and 0 otherwise, the ratio is simple: walking backwards from the end of an episode, each step multiplies the weight by 1/b(At|St) as long as b took the greedy action, and the first non-greedy action makes every earlier ratio zero, so the update loop stops there. The method therefore learns only from the tails of episodes, the part after the last exploratory move. When episodes are long and exploration is frequent, most of the experience is thrown away, which is one motivation for the temporal-difference and tree-backup methods of later chapters. A blackjack hand involves only one or two decisions, so here the tails are most of the data. Raise ε all the way to 1, a behaviour policy that plays completely at random, and the greedy target still learns basic strategy.

\[ \begin{aligned} C &\leftarrow C + W \\ Q &\leftarrow Q + \frac{W}{C}\,\big(G - Q\big) \\ \pi(S_t) &\leftarrow \arg\max_a Q(S_t,a) \\ &\quad \text{stop if } A_t \ne \pi(S_t) \\ W &\leftarrow W \,/\, b(A_t\mid S_t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: C and Q in the first two lines mean C(St, At) and Q(St, At). This is one pass backwards through an episode, t = T−1 down to 0, with W = 1 at the start and G accumulated as G ← γG + Rt+1. Each pair is updated by the weighted incremental rule of the previous section. The weight for step t covers only the actions after t; the action at t itself needs no correction, because Q(St, At) already conditions on it. That is why W is updated last.
Say it“Add W to C, move Q by W over C of the error, make pi greedy, stop if the action wasn't greedy, otherwise divide W by b.”

Cutting the variance · rl 5.8–5.9 (optional sections)

Discounting-aware and per-decision importance sampling

Ordinary importance sampling multiplies the whole return by the product of all the ratios. That wastes information. Suppose episodes last 100 steps and γ = 0: the return is just the first reward, which depends only on the first action, yet its weight is a product of 100 ratios, 99 of which only add noise. Two refinements use the structure of the return. Discounting-aware importance sampling reads γ as a probability of the episode ending at each step: the return becomes a mixture of flat partial returns, undiscounted sums up to a horizon h, and each one needs only the ratios up to h. Per-decision importance sampling notices that each reward needs only the ratios of the actions taken before it; the later ratios have expectation 1 and can be dropped.

The test bed is a corridor of T steps. At each step the target policy always takes forward, which pays +1; the behaviour policy takes forward with probability β and back, which pays 0, otherwise. So vπ = 1 + γ + … + γT−1. All three estimators below are unbiased; they differ only in variance. Each bar is the exact standard deviation of one episode’s estimate, computed by summing over the possible episodes; the dot is the spread measured over 20,000 simulated episodes. Lengthen the corridor and lower γ.

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar G_{t:h} &= R_{t+1} + R_{t+2} + \dots + R_h \\ G_t &= (1-\gamma)\sum_{h=t+1}^{T-1}\gamma^{h-t-1}\,\bar G_{t:h} \\ &\quad + \gamma^{T-t-1}\,\bar G_{t:T} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: treat γ as the chance of carrying on at each step. With probability (1 − γ)γh−t−1 the episode “ends” after horizon h and you collect the flat (undiscounted) partial return up to h; with the remaining probability γT−t−1 it runs to the true end T. The weighted mixture equals the ordinary discounted return exactly.
Say it“G-t is one minus gamma times the gamma-weighted sum of flat partial returns, plus gamma to the T minus t minus one times the full flat return.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde G^{\,\gamma}_t &= (1-\gamma)\sum_{h=t+1}^{T-1}\gamma^{h-t-1} \\ &\qquad \times \rho_{t:h-1}\,\bar G_{t:h} \\ &\quad + \gamma^{T-t-1}\rho_{t:T-1}\,\bar G_{t:T} \\ V(s) &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal T(s)|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal T(s)} \tilde G^{\,\gamma}_t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the discounting-aware ordinary estimator. Each flat partial return up to h is weighted only by the ratios ρt:h−1 of the actions that produced it, not by ratios for steps beyond its horizon. With γ = 0 it uses just ρtRt+1; with γ = 1 it is ordinary importance sampling again.
Say it“Weight each flat partial return by the ratios up to its own horizon, mix them with the gamma weights, and average.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb E_b\big[\rho_{t:T-1}\,R_{t+k}\big] \\ &\qquad = \mathbb E_b\big[\rho_{t:t+k-1}\,R_{t+k}\big] \\ &\tilde G_t = \rho_{t}R_{t+1} + \gamma\rho_{t:t+1}R_{t+2} + \dots \\ &\qquad + \gamma^{T-t-1}\rho_{t:T-1}R_T \\ &\phantom{\tilde G_t} = \rho_t\big(R_{t+1} + \gamma\,\tilde G_{t+1}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a ratio for a step after the reward has expectation Σa b(a|s)·π(a|s)/b(a|s) = 1 and, given everything before it, is independent of that reward, so it can be dropped without changing the expectation. The per-decision return G̃t keeps, for each reward, only the ratios up to the action just before it, and averaging it over visits, V(s) = Σ G̃t / |𝒯(s)|, is still unbiased. The last line is the same thing computed backwards, one ratio per step.
Say it“Each reward only needs the ratios up to its own action; the per-decision return is rho-t times R plus gamma times the next per-decision return.”

Real-world project

Project: learn blackjack from millions of hands, then check it against basic strategy

The environment is Gymnasium’s Blackjack-v1 (Farama Foundation, version 1.0.0, MIT licence), registered with sab=True so that it follows Sutton and Barto’s rules exactly: infinite deck, the dealer draws to 17 and stands on every 17, a natural (ace + ten-card) beats anything but a dealer natural, no doubling, splitting or insurance. This page re-implements those rules in JavaScript (read from the environment’s source) so that millions of hands can be played in your browser, and checks the re-implementation two ways: against the exact dynamic-programming values, and against 50,000 hands per policy played by Gymnasium itself in Python.

The yardstick is the basic-strategy table in Wikipedia’s “Blackjack” article (revision 1382551223, 29 October 2026, citing the Wizard of Odds), licensed CC BY-SA 4.0; the hit/stand extract embedded in this page is shared under the same licence. That table is for a casino game with 4–8 decks in which the dealer hits soft 17 and players may double, split and surrender. Blackjack-v1 allows none of those, so each cell is read by its fallback: “double, else hit” counts as hit, “double, else stand” as stand, “surrender, else hit/stand” as hit/stand, and pairs by their total. The article lists six cells that change when the dealer stands on soft 17; none of them changes a hit/stand fallback among our 200 states. The deck size is not adjusted for, and the question is whether the strategy survives anyway.

The same experiment in Python, on Gymnasium itself

import sys, json, time, numpy as np
sys.path.insert(0, "x069_py")                     # gymnasium 1.0.0 (MIT licence), installed with pip --target
import gymnasium as gym

# ---- the exact model of Blackjack-v1: infinite deck, dealer draws to 17 and stands on all 17s ----
P = {c: (4 if c == 10 else 1) / 13 for c in range(1, 11)}          # ace = 1; 10, J, Q, K all count 10
def dealer(up):                                    # distribution of the dealer's final total; 22 = bust
    out = dict.fromkeys(range(17, 23), 0.0)
    def go(h, ace, p):
        t = h + 10 if ace and h + 10 <= 21 else h
        if t >= 17: out[min(t, 22)] += p; return
        for c, q in P.items(): go(h + c, ace or c == 1, p * q)
    go(up, up == 1, 1.0); return out
D = {d: dealer(d) for d in range(1, 11)}
PNAT = {d: P[10] if d == 1 else P[1] if d == 10 else 0.0 for d in range(1, 11)}   # dealer natural, given the up card
def stick(s, d): return sum(p * ((t == 22 or t < s) - (t != 22 and t > s)) for t, p in D[d].items())
def nxt(s, u, c):                                  # (sum, usable ace) after drawing card c; None = bust
    if u: return (s + c, 1) if s + c <= 21 else (s + c - 10, 0)
    if c == 1 and s + 11 <= 21: return (s + 11, 1)
    return (s + c, 0) if s + c <= 21 else None
ORDER = [(s, u) for u in (0, 1) for s in range(21, 11, -1)]       # every successor of a state comes before it
def solve(hitp=None, eps=0.0):                     # evaluate policy hitp(s,u,d) = P(hit); None = best eps-soft policy
    V, Q = {}, {}
    for d in range(1, 11):
        for s, u in ORDER:
            qs = stick(s, d); qh = sum(p * (-1 if nxt(s, u, c) is None else V[nxt(s, u, c) + (d,)]) for c, p in P.items())
            Q[s, u, d] = (qs, qh)
            h = hitp(s, u, d) if hitp else (1 - eps / 2 if qh > qs else eps / 2)
            V[s, u, d] = h * qh + (1 - h) * qs
    return V, Q
def start(V, Q, hitp=None, eps=0.0):               # expected return of a fresh deal; below 12 the player always hits
    tot = 0.0
    for d in range(1, 11):
        def low(h): return sum(p * (V[s, u, d] if s >= 12 else low(s)) for c, p in P.items()
                               for s, u in [(h + 11, 1) if c == 1 and h + 11 <= 21 else (h + c, 0)])
        for c1, p1 in P.items():
            for c2, p2 in P.items():
                if {c1, c2} == {1, 10}:            # a natural: +1 unless the dealer has one too
                    qs, qh = 1 - PNAT[d], Q[21, 1, d][1]
                    h = hitp(21, 1, d) if hitp else (1 - eps / 2 if qh > qs else eps / 2)
                    v = h * qh + (1 - h) * qs
                else:
                    t, ace = c1 + c2, 1 in (c1, c2); s, u = (t + 10, 1) if ace and t + 10 <= 21 else (t, 0)
                    v = V[s, u, d] if s >= 12 else low(s)
                tot += P[d] * p1 * p2 * v
    return tot

The browser’s exact model is a separate JavaScript implementation of the same recursion; its dealer probabilities, optimal values and policy values agree with Python’s to within 10−15, including the value of the policy Python learnt (−0.043532 both ways). The learnt policies do not match cell for cell, and should not: the browser uses its own random-number generator (mulberry32, seeded), Python uses Gymnasium’s NumPy generator, and the cells where they differ are the close calls, where q(hit) and q(stick) differ by less than 0.02 and a few hundred thousand episodes cannot reliably tell them apart. Hard 12 against a 4 is the closest, a gap of 0.0025; separating it at two standard errors needs millions of visits to that one pair. The Python side runs Gymnasium’s own step() for every hand (about 60 s in all); exploring starts are set up by writing the starting hand into the environment, since Gymnasium has no option for it.

Wrap-up · rl 5.10

Five lines to remember

  • Monte Carlo methods estimate values by averaging the returns of complete episodes: no model, no bootstrapping, and each state’s estimate stands on its own.
  • Without a model you need action values, and action values need exploration: exploring starts, or ε-soft policies that converge to the best ε-soft policy.
  • Control is generalised policy iteration: evaluate by averaging returns, improve greedily, and interleave the two episode by episode.
  • Off-policy learning reweights a behaviour policy’s returns by ρ = ∏ π/b: ordinary IS is unbiased but can have infinite variance; weighted IS is biased but far steadier.
  • Store running sums, not returns (C and W/C steps), and cut variance by giving each reward only the ratios it needs (discounting-aware and per-decision IS).

Compared with dynamic programming, Monte Carlo methods have four advantages: they learn from actual or simulated experience without a model; a simulator is often far easier to build than a table of probabilities; they can spend their effort on the states that matter, such as the start states, without sweeping the rest; and, because they do not bootstrap, they are less harmed when the Markov property only roughly holds. The price is that nothing is learnt until an episode ends, which the temporal-difference methods of the next chapter remove.

Check your understanding

Why does model-free control estimate q(s, a) rather than v(s)?

Choosing an action from v needs a one-step look-ahead, Σ p(s′, r | s, a)[r + γv(s′)], which needs the model. With q the greedy action is simply argmaxa q(s, a), no model required.

In blackjack, first-visit and every-visit MC give identical estimates. Why?

No state can occur twice in one hand: a hit either raises your sum or turns a usable ace into a non-usable one, so each state is visited at most once per episode and the two definitions coincide.

Your ε-greedy agent has converged with ε = 0.1. Is its policy optimal?

It is the best policy among ε-soft ones, not the optimal policy, because it still acts at random 10% of the time. Its greedy part is often optimal or nearly so; you can deploy that, or decay ε towards 0 during learning (the GLIE condition).

A behaviour policy never sticks on 20. Can you evaluate “stick on 20+” off-policy from its episodes?

No. Coverage fails: the target takes an action that b never takes, so every episode that reaches 20 has ρ = 0 for the target’s trajectory, and no reweighting can recover what was never observed.

Why does off-policy MC control stop its backward loop at the first non-greedy action?

The target policy is greedy, so π(At|St) = 0 for a non-greedy action. Every earlier step’s ratio includes that factor and is zero, so those steps would receive zero weight; stopping saves the work. It also means only the tail of each episode is learnt from.
