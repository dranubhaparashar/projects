POMDPs: Acting on Beliefs

When you cannot see the state, make your uncertainty the state

A machine on a factory floor wears out, but nobody can see wear. A gauge gives a noisy hint every cycle, a proper inspection gives a much better one at a price, and a repair costs more still. A partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) is the model of exactly this situation, and its central trick is simple: stop pretending to know the state and act on a belief, a probability for every state, updated by Bayes’ rule after each observation. The triangle on the right is the space of all beliefs about a three-state machine (new, worn, broken); its colours are a policy that point-based value iteration has just computed in your browser. Press Run, Inspect or Repair, or let the policy choose, and watch the belief move.

The setting

Decisions in sequence

Earlier chapters judged one action at a time. Most real decisions come in a chain: what you do now changes the situation you face next, and the payoff is spread over the whole chain. That is a sequential decision problem. With a fully observable world and Markov dynamics it is a Markov decision process: states s, actions a, a transition model P(s′ | s, a), a reward R(s, a), and a discount γ that makes a reward t steps away worth γt of one today. A solution is a policy, an action for every state, and the best one maximises the expected discounted sum of rewards, the state’s utility.

Our running example is a machine with three wear levels: new, worn and broken. Running it earns 10 a cycle when new, 5 when worn and costs 25 when broken (scrap, damage). Each cycle a new machine becomes worn with probability 0.15 and a worn one breaks with probability 0.2. Repairing makes it new and costs the repair price. There is also an inspect action: run the cycle and pay 3 extra for a careful look. For now, pretend the wear level is printed on the machine. Step value iteration and move the repair cost: watch the utilities settle and the policy appear.

\[ U^{\pi}(s) = \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^{t} R(S_t, \pi(S_t)) \;\Big|\; S_0 = s\Big] \]
Read it as: start in state s, follow policy π for ever, add up the rewards with the t-th one shrunk by γt, and average over all the ways the random transitions could go. With γ < 1 and bounded rewards the sum is finite: at most Rmax/(1 − γ).
Say it“The utility of s under pi is the expected discounted sum of rewards, starting from s and following pi.”
\[ \begin{aligned} U_{k+1}(s) = \max_{a}\Big[\, & R(s,a) \\ & + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s,a)\, U_k(s')\Big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the Bellman update. The best you can get from s in k + 1 steps is the best action’s immediate reward plus the discounted, probability-weighted best you can get in k steps from wherever you land. Repeating it from U0 = 0 converges to the true utilities; the maximising action is the optimal policy.
Say it“U k-plus-one of s is the max over a of R of s, a plus gamma times the sum over s-prime of P of s-prime given s, a times U k of s-prime.”

Representing MDPs

Describing a world compactly

Value iteration above needed a table of P(s′ | s, a): one number for every pair of states and every action. That is fine for three states. Now run a workshop of n machines, each with d wear levels. The state is the whole vector of wear levels, so there are dn states, and the flat table has |A| · d2n entries: ten machines with three levels already need more than 38 billion numbers. Yet the world is simple: each machine wears on its own, perhaps shaken by its neighbour. A dynamic decision network (DDN) writes the state as variables X1, …, Xn, draws each variable at time t + 1 with arrows from the few things it depends on, and stores one small conditional table per variable. Rewards are often additive across machines too. Add machines and wear levels, and couple neighbours; the flat matrix on the right is rebuilt from the small tables whenever it is small enough to draw.

\[ \begin{aligned} P(\mathbf X_{t+1} \mid \mathbf X_t, A_t) &= \prod_{i=1}^{n} P\big(X^{i}_{t+1} \mid \text{Pa}(X^{i}_{t+1})\big) \\ R(\mathbf X_t, A_t) &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} R_i(X^i_t, A_t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the chance of the next joint state is the product of each machine’s own chance, given only its parents Pa in the network (its own wear, the action and, if coupled, its neighbour’s wear). A table for a variable with d values and k state parents, under |A| actions, holds d · dk · |A| numbers, so the whole model holds at most n · |A| · dk+1 instead of |A| · d2n. The saving is in describing the problem; solving it can still be hard, because the utility function need not factor.
Say it“The probability of the next state is the product over variables of each one's probability given its parents; the reward is a sum of local rewards.”

Partial observability

When you cannot see the state

Take the label off the machine. Now there are two wear levels, good and worn, and all the agent gets each cycle is a gauge reading, ok or alarm, that matches the truth only with probability q. A good machine wears with probability 0.1 a cycle, and the routine replaces it about one cycle in ten. What should the agent treat as “the state” now? Its raw information is the history of everything it did and saw, which grows without end. Sutton and Barto put the requirement this way: a summary of the history is a good enough state, a Markov state, when it predicts everything the full history would predict about the future. Two practical recipes follow. Keep the last k observations, a crude approximate state that gets better with k but costs 4k contexts; or keep a belief, the posterior probability of each hidden state, which a two-line recursive update maintains exactly.

Below, all three predict the next gauge reading on a fresh stretch of 20,000 cycles, after the window predictor has learned its counts from a separate training run. A predictor that knew the hidden state would do best of all, but no agent can have it. Lengthen the window and change the gauge’s accuracy.

\[ \begin{aligned} H_t &= A_0, O_1, A_1, O_2, \ldots, A_{t-1}, O_t \\ S_{t+1} &= u(S_t, A_t, O_{t+1}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the history is the whole sequence of actions and observations so far. A state-update function u folds each new action and observation into the old state, so the agent never has to store the history itself. For a window of k steps, u drops the oldest pair; for a belief, u is Bayes’ rule (next section).
Say it“The history is every action and observation so far; the next state is u of the current state, the action and the new observation.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &f(h) = f(h') \;\Rightarrow\; p(\tau \mid h) = p(\tau \mid h') \\ &\text{log-loss} = -\tfrac{1}{T}\textstyle\sum_t \log_2 \hat p(o_{t+1}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a summary f is Markov if any two histories it maps to the same state give the same probability to every future sequence τ of actions and observations, so nothing useful was thrown away. We score predictors by log-loss: the average number of bits of surprise per reading. Lower is better; 1 bit is a coin toss.
Say it“If f of h equals f of h-prime, the futures must be equally likely; log-loss is the average of minus log two of the predicted probability.”

The definition

Beliefs become the state

A POMDP adds two things to an MDP: a set of observations and a sensor model P(e | s′, a), the chance of seeing e after action a lands the world in s′. It also needs a starting belief b0. The agent’s belief b is a probability distribution over the hidden states, and after each action and observation it is updated in two moves: predict (push the belief through the transition model) and correct (weight each state by how well it explains what was seen, then renormalise). That is the forward step of filtering from the chapters on probabilistic reasoning.

Here is the fact that makes POMDPs solvable in principle: the optimal action depends only on the belief, not on the history that produced it. So the POMDP becomes an MDP whose states are beliefs, the belief MDP, with a continuous state space. In the two-state machine the belief is one number, P(worn). Running gives a noisy gauge reading (accuracy 0.7); inspecting costs 4 and reads correctly 95% of the time; replacing costs 20 and makes the machine good. A good machine earns 10 a cycle; a worn one loses 10. Act, and choose what the gauge says or let it be sampled; the running example shows Bayes’ rule at work.

\[ b'(s') = \alpha\, P(e \mid s', a) \sum_{s} P(s' \mid s, a)\, b(s) \]
Read it as: the sum is the prediction, the chance of landing in s′ averaged over where we might have been. Multiplying by P(e | s′, a) is the correction. α rescales so that the new belief sums to 1; 1/α is exactly P(e | b, a), the chance that this observation would appear.
Say it“b-prime of s-prime is alpha times the probability of e given s-prime, times the sum over s of the transition probability times b of s.”
\[ \begin{aligned} P(b' \mid b, a) &= \sum_{e} P(e \mid b, a) \\ &\quad\times \mathbb 1\big[b' = \text{forward}(b,a,e)\big] \\ \rho(b, a) &= \sum_s b(s)\, R(s, a) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: from belief b, action a can lead to only as many next beliefs as there are observations, each reached with the probability of its observation. The reward of a belief is the reward averaged over the states you might be in. Together they define an ordinary (if continuous) MDP over beliefs.
Say it“The chance of b-prime is the total probability of the observations whose update gives b-prime; the belief reward is the belief-weighted reward.”

Solving offline

Value iteration over beliefs

The belief MDP has infinitely many states, so a table of utilities is out. What rescues value iteration is a structure first described by Smallwood and Sondik (1973). Think in conditional plans: “run; if the gauge says ok, run again; if alarm, inspect”. A plan’s expected value from a known state s is a number αp(s), and from a belief it is just the belief-weighted average b · αp, a straight line in b. The best you can do with h steps to go is to pick the best h-step plan for your belief, so the utility is the upper surface of a set of lines: piecewise linear and convex. Each backup builds every new plan (an action, then one old plan for each possible observation) and throws away the dominated ones, those never on top anywhere. Without that pruning the count explodes as |A| · |Γ||O| per step. Raise the horizon, then change the inspection cost and the gauge; move the pointer across the chart to read off the plan.

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha_p(s) = {} & R(s,a) \\ & + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) \\ & \qquad \times \sum_{e} P(e \mid s', a)\, \alpha_{p.e}(s') \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: plan p starts with action a and then follows sub-plan p.e when it sees e. Its value from state s is the immediate reward plus the discounted average, over where the machine goes and what the gauge then says, of the chosen sub-plan’s value. Nothing here depends on a belief: plans are evaluated state by state.
Say it“Alpha p of s is R of s, a plus gamma times the sum over s-prime and e of the transition and sensor probabilities times alpha of the sub-plan for e.”
\[ U_h(b) = \max_{p \,\in\, \Gamma_h}\; \sum_s b(s)\,\alpha_p(s) \]
Read it as: with h steps to go, the utility of belief b is the best plan’s belief-weighted value. A maximum of linear functions is convex, so being unsure is never worse than the average of the certain cases would suggest — it is where information has value. The first action of the winning plan is the policy.
Say it“U h of b is the max over plans of b dot alpha p.”

Solving offline, approximately

Point-based value iteration

Exact pruning needs a linear program for every candidate plan once there are more than two states, and even the pruned sets can grow exponentially with the horizon; Papadimitriou and Tsitsiklis (1987) showed that finite-horizon POMDPs are PSPACE-complete. The practical way out is to stop caring about every belief and care only about a sample of them. Point-based value iteration (Pineau, Gordon & Thrun, 2003) keeps a finite set B of belief points and, at each backup, builds only the single best new plan for each point: at most |B| vectors, whatever the horizon. Between the points the value is still a maximum of planes, and because each vector is the value of a real plan and the backups start from a pessimistic vector, the result never overstates the true utility; it tightens as the points cover the beliefs that matter. Below is the three-state machine from the hero; the reference is the same method on a 496-point grid. Coarsen and refine the grid, and stop the backups early.

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha_b^{a} &= R(\cdot,a) \\ &\quad + \sum_{e} \arg\max_{g \,\in\, \{g_{\alpha}^{a,e}\,:\,\alpha \in \Gamma\}} b\cdot g \\ g_{\alpha}^{a,e}(s) &= \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s,a) \\ &\quad \times P(e \mid s',a)\, \alpha(s') \\ \text{backup}(b) &= \arg\max_{a}\; b \cdot \alpha_b^{a} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for belief point b and action a, choose for each observation e the old plan that is best for this point after seeing e, and add the pieces to the immediate reward. Keep the action whose assembled vector scores highest at b. Each g is one old vector pushed back one step through the transition and sensor models.
Say it“For each point and action, add to the reward, for every observation, the back-projected old vector that is best at this point; keep the best action.”

Solving online

Planning online by lookahead

Offline methods solve for every belief in advance. An online agent only ever needs a decision for the belief it is in right now. So at each step it grows a tree from the current belief: a max node for its own choice of action, a chance node for nature’s choice of observation, each branch ending at an updated belief, down to depth d. At the leaves it guesses the remaining value with a heuristic, then backs the numbers up (expectimax) and takes the best root action. The tree has (|A| · |O|)d leaves, independent of how many states there are. A classic cheap leaf heuristic is QMDP (Littman, Cassandra & Kaelbling, 1995): pretend the state will become fully visible after this step and use the MDP’s values. For large problems, POMCP (Silver & Veness, 2010) replaces the exact tree with Monte Carlo tree search and the exact belief with a particle filter. Deepen the tree and swap the leaf heuristic; watch when the agent starts to value an inspection.

\[ \begin{aligned} Q_d(b,a) &= \rho(b,a) \\ &\quad + \gamma \sum_{e} P(e \mid b,a)\, V_{d-1}\big(b'_{a,e}\big) \\ V_d(b) &= \max_a Q_d(b,a), \qquad V_0(b) = h(b) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the value of an action is its expected reward now, plus the discounted average over observations of the best you can do from the belief that observation would produce, d − 1 levels further down. At depth 0, the heuristic h takes over.
Say it“Q d of b, a is rho of b, a plus gamma times the sum over e of the chance of e times V d-minus-one of the updated belief.”
\[ h_{\text{QMDP}}(b) = \max_a \sum_s b(s)\, Q_{\text{MDP}}(s, a) \]
Read it as: average the fully observable Q-values over the belief and take the best action. It is optimistic, an upper bound on the true value, because it assumes all uncertainty vanishes after one step. That is also its blind spot: if you will know the state anyway, paying to inspect is never worth it.
Say it“The QMDP heuristic is the max over a of the belief-weighted MDP Q-values.”

The frontier

Predictive state: a state you can learn

Everything so far assumed someone handed us the hidden states and their models. A learning agent has neither: it only ever sees actions and observations. Sutton and Barto list this among the field’s frontiers: ideally the state is built from, and judged by, predictions about future experience. One concrete version is the predictive state representation (PSR; Littman, Sutton & Singh, 2002). A test τ is a short future “do a1, see o1, do a2, see o2”; its prediction p(τ | h) is the chance of those observations if you take those actions after history h. Stack the predictions for every history and every test into a system-dynamics matrix D (here each row is also weighted by how often its history happens, so that rare histories, whose estimates are noisy, count for little). For a system with n hidden states, every row is a belief times a fixed matrix, so D has rank at most n, and the predictions of n well-chosen core tests are a Markov state, made of quantities the agent can check against what actually happens.

Below, D is built exactly from the machine’s model, and estimated by counting in one long stream of experience. Spectral learning methods (Boots, Siddiqi & Gordon, 2011) recover a state by reading off D’s leading singular directions. Switch the hidden system and feed it more data: does the number of large singular values reveal how many hidden states there are?

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\tau \mid h) &= \mathbf b_h^{\top} \mathbf u_\tau \;\Rightarrow\; \operatorname{rank} D \le |S| \\ p(\tau \mid h) &= p(Q \mid h)^{\top}\, \mathbf m_\tau \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: every prediction is the belief after h, dotted with a vector uτ that depends only on the test. So D (weighting rows changes nothing here) is a (histories × |S|) matrix times an (|S| × tests) matrix and its rank cannot exceed the number of hidden states. Choose core tests Q whose columns span D: then any test’s prediction is a fixed linear combination mτ of the core predictions p(Q | h).
Say it“The prediction of tau given h is the belief dotted with u tau, so the rank of D is at most the number of states; any prediction is the core predictions dotted with m tau.”
\[ p(q_i \mid h a o) = \frac{p(a\,o\,q_i \mid h)}{p(a\,o \mid h)} = \frac{p(Q \mid h)^{\top}\mathbf m_{a o q_i}}{p(Q \mid h)^{\top}\mathbf m_{a o}} \]
Read it as: the PSR’s state update. After doing a and seeing o, the new prediction for core test qi is the chance of the longer test “a, o, then qi” divided by the chance of “a, o”. Both are linear in the old core predictions, so this is the PSR’s version of u(St, At, Ot+1), built from nothing but observable quantities.
Say it“The new prediction of q i is the prediction of a, o, q i over the prediction of a, o.”

Real-world project

Project: planning inspections for turbofan engines

NASA’s C-MAPSS data set (Saxena et al., 2008) simulates a fleet of turbofan engines with the Commercial Modular Aero-Propulsion System Simulation, each starting with its own unknown amount of wear and running, cycle after cycle, until a fault in the high-pressure compressor grows into failure. Its first subset, FD001, holds 100 engines run to failure, 20,631 flight cycles in all, each with 21 noisy sensor readings. The data are a US Government work released through NASA’s Prognostics Data Repository and are in the public domain. Here they become a POMDP. The hidden state is how much life an engine has left, in whole 10-cycle epochs (0 to 36), plus failed. Every epoch the operator chooses: run it (earn 10; if it fails during the epoch, pay the failure cost instead, then replace it), inspect and run (pay extra for a reading of all the sensors), or replace it (pay the replacement cost; the next engine is a fresh one with its own unknown life). Between inspections you see nothing except that the engine is still running. The 50 odd-numbered engines build the model; the 50 even-numbered engines are held out and the policies are replayed on their real sensor data.

1 · What an inspection sees

An inspection averages, over the epoch, the 14 of the 21 sensors that vary (the other seven are constant or nearly so), and turns them into one health index: a least-squares fit, on the training engines, from the standardised sensor means to the remaining life, capped at 125 cycles because early wear is invisible in the sensors. The reading reported is the band the index falls in. Its error is large (an RMSE of about 19 cycles), which is exactly why the operator needs a belief rather than taking the reading at its word.

The same pipeline in Python

import json, numpy as np
data = json.load(open("x074-data.json"))                     # 100 run-to-failure engines, 14 sensors averaged per 10-cycle epoch
eng = data["eng"]; tr = [e for e in eng if e["u"] % 2]; te = [e for e in eng if e["u"] % 2 == 0]
DT, CUT, W = 10, [110, 80, 50, 30, 15], 2                  # epoch length, inspection bands, observation pooling
cI, cR, K, gam = 1.0, 30.0, 200.0, 0.97                     # inspection, replacement, failure cost; discount per epoch

# 1. inspection = a health index: least squares from the 14 sensor means to remaining life (capped at 125)
X = np.array([m for e in tr for m in e["m"]]); y = np.array([min(e["L"] - DT * (j + 1), 125) for e in tr for j in range(len(e["m"]))])
mu, sd = X.mean(0), X.std(0); A = np.c_[np.ones(len(X)), (X - mu) / sd]; w = np.linalg.lstsq(A, y, rcond=None)[0]
hi = lambda m: float(np.r_[1, (np.array(m) - mu) / sd] @ w)
band = lambda h: next((i for i, c in enumerate(CUT) if h >= c), len(CUT))
print(f"health index: rmse {np.sqrt(np.mean((A @ w - y) ** 2)):.4f} cycles on {len(y)} training epochs")

# 2. the POMDP: hidden state s = whole epochs of life left (0..36), 37 = failed
NS, F = 38, 37; NB = len(CUT) + 1; ALIVE, FAIL, NEW = 0, NB + 1, NB + 2; NO = NB + 3
b0 = np.bincount([e["L"] // DT for e in tr], minlength=NS) / len(tr)            # life of a fresh engine
cnt = np.zeros((NS, NB))
for e in tr:
    for j, m in enumerate(e["m"]): cnt[(e["L"] - DT * (j + 1)) // DT, band(hi(m))] += 1
cnt = np.array([cnt[max(0, s - W):min(F, s + W + 1)].sum(0) for s in range(NS)])   # pool neighbouring states
T = np.zeros((NS, NS)); T[0, F] = 1; T[np.arange(1, F), np.arange(0, F - 1)] = 1; T[F] = b0
Zrun, Zins, Zrep = np.zeros((NS, NO)), np.zeros((NS, NO)), np.zeros((NS, NO))
Zrun[:F, ALIVE] = 1; Zins[:F, 1:1 + NB] = (cnt[:F] + 0.5) / (cnt[:F].sum(1, keepdims=True) + 0.5 * NB)
Zrun[F, FAIL] = Zins[F, FAIL] = 1; Zrep[:, NEW] = 1
Rrun = np.r_[10 * (1 - T[:F, F]) - K * T[:F, F], -cR]
P = dict(T=[T, T, np.tile(b0, (NS, 1))], Z=[Zrun, Zins, Zrep], R=[Rrun, np.r_[Rrun[:F] - cI, -cR], np.full(NS, -cR)])

def update(b, a, o):
    u = (b @ P["T"][a]) * P["Z"][a][:, o]; return u / u.sum()
def mulberry32(a):
    def r():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF; t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return r
def sample(r, p):
    u, acc = r(), 0.0
    for j, x in enumerate(p):
        acc += x
        if u < acc: return j
    return len(p) - 1

# 3. belief points: the no-inspection path, then random run/inspect trajectories from the model
rnd, B, b = mulberry32(7), [b0], b0
while (b @ T)[:F].sum() > 0:                                  # until no engine could still be running
    b = update(b, 0, ALIVE); B.append(b)
while len(B) < 200:
    b, s = b0, sample(rnd, b0)
    for step in range(40):
        a = 0 if rnd() < 0.6 else 1
        s = sample(rnd, P["T"][a][s]); o = sample(rnd, P["Z"][a][s]); b = update(b, a, o)
        if s == F: break
        if not any(np.abs(b - c).max() < 1e-6 for c in B): B.append(b)
        if len(B) >= 200: break

# 4. point-based value iteration
Bm, G = np.array(B), np.full((1, NS), min(r.min() for r in P["R"]) / (1 - gam)); GA = [0]
pairs = [(a, o) for a in range(3) for o in range(NO) if P["Z"][a][:, o].max() > 0]
for it in range(120):
    acc = [np.tile(P["R"][a], (len(Bm), 1)) for a in range(3)]
    for a, o in pairs:
        g = gam * (P["T"][a] @ (P["Z"][a][:, o][:, None] * G.T)).T
        acc[a] = acc[a] + g[(Bm @ g.T).argmax(1)]
    best = np.stack([(Bm * acc[a]).sum(1) for a in range(3)], 1).argmax(1); NG, NA = [], []
    for i in range(len(Bm)):
        al = acc[best[i]][i]
        if not any(np.abs(al - e).max() < 1e-10 for e in NG): NG.append(al); NA.append(int(best[i]))
    G, GA = np.array(NG), NA
act = lambda b: GA[int((G @ b).argmax())]
print(f"PBVI: {len(B)} belief points, {len(G)} alpha vectors; value of a fresh engine {(G @ b0).max():.4f}")

# 5. replay each policy on the held-out engines' real sensor data
def replay(engines, policy):
    tot = cyc = ins = fail = used = 0
    for e in engines:
        L, b, t = e["L"], b0, 0
        while True:
            a = policy(b, t, L)
            if a == 2: tot -= cR; cyc += t + DT; used += t; break                     # planned replacement
            if t + DT > L: tot -= K + cR; cyc += t + 2 * DT; fail += 1; used += t; break  # failure, then replacement
            tot += 10 - cI * (a == 1); ins += a == 1
            o = 1 + band(hi(e["m"][t // DT])) if a == 1 else ALIVE
            b = update(b, a, o); t += DT
    return tot / cyc, ins / len(engines), fail, used / len(engines)
age = max(range(10, 370, 10), key=lambda A: replay(tr, lambda b, t, L: 2 if t >= A else 0)[0])

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A POMDP is an MDP whose state you cannot see: add observations and a sensor model P(e | s′, a), and a starting belief.
  • The belief, updated by predict-then-correct Bayes filtering, is a Markov state: the optimal action depends on the belief alone, which turns the POMDP into an MDP over beliefs.
  • Finite-horizon utilities are the upper surface of α-vectors, one per conditional plan: piecewise linear and convex, and convexity is where information (an inspection) earns its keep.
  • Exact value iteration explodes; point-based backups on a sample of beliefs, or online expectimax lookahead from the current belief (with QMDP or learned leaves, or POMCP’s sampling), are what scale.
  • Without a given model, the state must be learned from experience: windows of history are crude approximations; predictive states, built from checkable predictions, are one road at the frontier.

Check your understanding

Why does the fully observable MDP policy never inspect the machine, while the POMDP policy often does?

In the MDP the state is already known, so an inspection only costs money and changes nothing. In the POMDP an inspection changes the belief, and because the utility is convex in the belief, a sharper belief is worth more on average: the expected gain from better later decisions can exceed the inspection’s price.

The gauge reads “alarm”. Does the belief in “worn” always go up? What does it depend on?

It goes up whenever an alarm is likelier from a worn machine than from a good one, P(alarm | worn) > P(alarm | good). How far it moves depends on the ratio of these likelihoods and on the predicted belief; a completely uninformative sensor (equal likelihoods) leaves the prediction unchanged.

Why is a POMDP’s finite-horizon utility function piecewise linear and convex?

For a fixed conditional plan, the expected value is linear in the belief (a weighted average of the plan’s values in each state). The optimal utility is the maximum over finitely many plans, and a maximum of linear functions is piecewise linear and convex.

An online agent with a QMDP leaf heuristic and depth 0 never inspects. Why, and what fixes it?

QMDP assumes the state becomes known after one step, so information has no value to it and the inspection cost is pure loss. Looking one or more steps ahead with the true observation branches lets the agent see how an inspection changes its later choices, which is the value of information.

A system-dynamics matrix estimated from data has three large singular values and many tiny ones. What does that suggest?

That about three dimensions are enough to predict the future from the past: the system behaves like one with three hidden states (or fewer, in degenerate cases), and the predictions of three core tests could serve as its state. The tiny values are estimation noise, which shrinks as more data arrive.
