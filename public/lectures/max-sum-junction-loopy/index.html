Max-Sum, Junction Trees & Loopy BP

The best single answer, exact answers on any graph, and a fast guess when exact is too dear.

Chapter 6.5 passed messages on trees to get marginals. This chapter takes three steps further. Swap the sums for maxima and the same messages find the single most probable configuration. Group variables into cliques and any graph becomes a tree, at a price. Or ignore the loops, pass messages anyway, and hope. The question

The best answer is not the best marginals

The sum-product algorithm gives every variable its marginal, p(xi). Often we want something else: the single joint configuration that is most probable. A decoder wants the one message that was most likely sent, a speech recogniser the one sentence, a stereo matcher one depth for every pixel, a Sudoku solver one digit in every cell. This is called the MAP configuration (maximum a posteriori) or the most probable explanation.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{x}^{\max} &= \arg\max_{\mathbf{x}}\, p(\mathbf{x}) \\ p(\mathbf{x}^{\max}) &= \max_{\mathbf{x}}\, p(\mathbf{x}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: bold x is a value for every variable at once. Among all such joint settings, xmax is the one with the highest probability, and p(xmax) is that probability.
Say it“x-max is the arg-max over all x of p of x.”

The tempting shortcut is to run sum-product and give each variable its own most probable value. That answers a different question. Each marginal adds up probability over every setting of the other variables, so a value can win its marginal by being part of many moderately likely worlds, none of which is the best world. A better idea is the max-marginal: for each value of xi, the probability of the best complete configuration that uses it. If the overall maximum is unique, every variable’s max-marginal peaks at its value in xmax, so picking per variable now works. If two configurations tie for best, each variable may pick from a different winner and the mixture can be poor. The cure, which the next two sections build, is to pick one variable and then trace back to the choices that produced its maximum.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_i(x_i) &= \max_{\mathbf{x} \setminus x_i}\, p(\mathbf{x}) \\ \text{versus}\quad p(x_i) &= \sum_{\mathbf{x} \setminus x_i} p(\mathbf{x}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the max-marginal μi keeps xi fixed and takes the best setting of everything else; the ordinary marginal keeps xi fixed and adds up all settings of everything else. “x ∖ xi” means all variables except xi.
Say it“Mu-i of x-i is the max over everything except x-i of p; the marginal is the sum instead.”
The algorithm

Max-sum: swap the sum for a max

Sum-product was fast because multiplication distributes over addition: ab + ac = a(b + c), so a factor that does not depend on a variable can be pulled out of the sum over that variable. Maximisation obeys the same law, as long as the numbers are not negative, and probabilities never are.

\[ \max(ab,\, ac) = a \max(b,\, c) \quad (a \ge 0) \]
Read it as: scaling two numbers by the same non-negative a does not change which one is bigger, so you can take the maximum first and scale afterwards. With a = 2, b = 3, c = 5: max(6, 10) = 10 = 2 × max(3, 5).
Say it“The max of a-b and a-c is a times the max of b and c.”

So on a chain p(x) = (1/Z) ψ1,2(x1, x2) ψ2,3(x2, x3) … ψN−1,N, the maximum over all variables can be pushed inside, one variable at a time, exactly as the sums were. Every result of chapter 6.5 carries over with ∑ replaced by max.

\[ \begin{aligned} \max_{\mathbf{x}} p(\mathbf{x}) &= \frac{1}{Z} \max_{x_1} \Big[ \max_{x_2} \Big[ \psi_{1,2}(x_1, x_2) \\ &\qquad \cdots \max_{x_N} \psi_{N-1,N}(x_{N-1}, x_N) \\ &\qquad \cdots \Big] \Big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the innermost max over xN only involves the last factor, so do it first; its result is a function of xN−1 alone, which joins the next factor, and so on. A chain of N variables with K values costs about N K2 operations instead of KN.
Say it“Peel off the maximisations from the end of the chain, one variable at a time.”

Products of many probabilities get tiny. Thirty factors of 0.1 give 10−30, and a few hundred underflow a computer’s numbers altogether. Because the logarithm is increasing, ln max p = max ln p, so we can work with logs instead: products become sums, and the distributive law becomes max(a + b, a + c) = a + max(b, c). That is why the algorithm is called max-sum. Written on a factor graph, it has the same two messages as sum-product.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_{f \to x}(x) &= \max_{x_1, \dots, x_M} \Big[ \ln f(x, x_1, \dots, x_M) \\ &\qquad + \sum_{m=1}^{M} \mu_{x_m \to f}(x_m) \Big] \\ \mu_{x \to f}(x) &= \sum_{l \in \mathrm{ne}(x) \setminus f} \mu_{f_l \to x}(x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a factor f tells variable x, for each value of x, the best log score it can reach by choosing its other variables x1…xM, counting its own log value plus what those variables told it. A variable tells a factor the sum of what all its other factors told it. Leaves start the process: a leaf variable sends 0, a leaf factor sends ln f(x).
Say it“Factor to variable: max over the other variables of log f plus their incoming messages. Variable to factor: add up the messages from the other factors.”
\[ \ln p^{\max} = \max_{x} \Big[ \sum_{s \in \mathrm{ne}(x)} \mu_{f_s \to x}(x) \Big] - \ln Z \]
Read it as: once every message has reached a chosen root variable x, add up what its factors told it and take the best value. That is the log of the largest unnormalised product; subtract ln Z for the probability. Unlike sum-product, one pass from the leaves to the root is all that is needed for the maximum.
Say it“The best log probability is the max at the root of the summed incoming messages, minus log Z.”

Getting the configuration

Back-tracking on the trellis

Max-sum’s pass to the root gives the best score. To get the best configuration, keep a note while passing messages. Each time a factor takes a maximum over a variable, record which value won, for every value of the variable it is sending to. When the root has picked its best value, follow those notes back towards the leaves. This is back-tracking, and on a chain it is the Viterbi algorithm.

Take a sensor that reads Low, Mid or High along eight time steps. The hidden true level tends to stay put (probability ρ) and the sensor misreads with probability ε. The trellis (or lattice) below has one column per time step and one row per value; every path from left to right is one possible history.

\[ \begin{aligned} \omega_n(x_n) &= \ln p(y_n \mid x_n) \\ &\quad + \max_{x_{n-1}} \big[ \ln p(x_n \mid x_{n-1}) \\ &\qquad\qquad + \omega_{n-1}(x_{n-1}) \big] \\ \phi_n(x_n) &= \arg\max_{x_{n-1}} \big[ \ln p(x_n \mid x_{n-1}) \\ &\qquad\qquad + \omega_{n-1}(x_{n-1}) \big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ωn(xn) is the log probability of the best path that ends in value xn at step n, including the reading yn. It is the max-sum message passed along the chain. φn(xn) remembers which value at step n − 1 that best path came from. It starts with ω1(x1) = ln p(x1) + ln p(y1 | x1).
Say it“Omega-n of x-n is the log likelihood of the reading plus the best predecessor’s omega plus its log transition; phi-n remembers who that predecessor was.”
\[ \begin{aligned} x_N^{\max} &= \arg\max_{x_N} \omega_N(x_N) \\ x_{n-1}^{\max} &= \phi_n\big(x_n^{\max}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: pick the best last value, then walk backwards: the best value at step n − 1 is whichever one the best path into xnmax came from. The result is always one consistent path, even if several paths tie for best.
Say it“Take the best final state, then follow the phi pointers back.”

Click a reading in the bottom row to change it, move the sliders, and press Step to run the forward pass and then the back-tracking one column at a time. Thin lines are the stored pointers φ: every node keeps exactly one, to its best predecessor.

Back-tracking matters most when there are ties. Running max-sum twice, once from each end, gives every variable its max-marginal, and you could then pick each variable’s best value separately. When the maximum is unique this gives the same path. When two paths tie, the separate picks can stitch half of one path to half of another, as the table in section 1 showed with the “Two winners” preset. Back-tracking never does that, because each step follows a pointer that was stored by a real path.

Exact inference, any graph

Junction trees for any graph

Sum-product and max-sum are exact on trees. On a graph with loops a message can come back to where it started, and the argument that made them exact breaks. The junction tree algorithm (Lauritzen and Spiegelhalter, 1988) fixes this by changing the graph instead of the algorithm: it groups variables into clusters until the clusters form a tree, then passes messages between clusters. It works for directed and undirected graphs alike, in five steps.

  1. Moralise (directed graphs only). Join every pair of parents that share a child, then drop the arrow heads. Each conditional table p(x | parents) now sits inside a clique of the undirected graph.
  2. Triangulate. Add edges until every cycle of four or more nodes has a chord, an edge joining two nodes of the cycle that are not neighbours on it. The usual way is to eliminate nodes one at a time: when a node goes, connect all its remaining neighbours. The edges added are called fill-in.
  3. Find the maximal cliques of the triangulated graph. Each becomes one node of the new tree, and each original factor is assigned to a clique that contains all its variables.
  4. Join the cliques into a tree by choosing a maximum-weight spanning tree, where the weight of a link is the number of variables the two cliques share. The shared variables form the separator on that link.
  5. Pass messages between cliques with sum-product (for marginals) or max-sum (for the MAP), one pass in and one pass out, exactly as on any tree. A message from one clique to the next is a table over their separator.
\[ \begin{aligned} w(C_i, C_j) &= |C_i \cap C_j| \\ \text{cost} &\;\propto\; \sum_{C} \prod_{v \in C} K_v \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Ci and Cj are cliques (sets of variables); their link weight is how many variables they share. Every clique holds a table with one entry per joint value of its variables, so with Kv values for variable v the work grows with the product of Kv over each clique, summed over cliques. One large clique dominates everything.
Say it“Link weight is the size of the intersection; the cost is the sum over cliques of the product of their variables’ sizes.”

Why the maximum-weight tree? It guarantees the running intersection property: if a variable appears in two cliques, it appears in every clique on the path between them. Without it, two distant cliques could hold different beliefs about the same variable and no message would ever reconcile them. With it, local agreement between neighbours implies global agreement, so the final clique tables are exact marginals.

\[ \begin{aligned} &v \in C_i \;\text{and}\; v \in C_j \\ &\Longrightarrow\; v \in C_k \text{ for every } C_k \\ &\qquad \text{on the path from } C_i \text{ to } C_j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the cliques that contain any given variable v form one connected piece of the tree. The panel below checks this for every variable of the graph you build.
Say it“If two cliques share a variable, every clique between them has it too.”

The size of the largest clique, minus one, minimised over all triangulations, is the graph’s treewidth. A tree has treewidth 1, a single loop 2, and an n × n grid n. Finding the best triangulation is itself NP-hard, which is why heuristics such as min-fill are used, and on graphs with large treewidth no triangulation helps: the junction tree is exact but its tables are exponentially large. That is the gap that loopy belief propagation, later in this chapter, tries to fill.

The same idea, from AIMA

Clustering into meganodes

Variable elimination (chapter 6.4) answers one query per run. If you want the posterior of every variable, running it once per variable repeats most of the work. Russell and Norvig describe the junction tree idea from the Bayesian-network side and call it clustering, or join-tree, algorithms: merge some nodes into meganodes until the network is a polytree (a directed graph with no undirected cycles). Exact message passing on a polytree costs time linear in the size of the network, and it delivers all posteriors at once.

Their example is the cloudy network. Cloudy influences both Sprinkler and Rain, and both influence WetGrass, so there is an undirected cycle C–S–W–R–C. Merge Sprinkler and Rain into one meganode S+R with four values (tt, tf, ft, ff), and the cycle is gone: C → S+R → W is a chain. The meganode needs a new table, which is easy here because S and R are independent given C.

\[ P(S{+}R = (s, r) \mid C) = P(s \mid C)\, P(r \mid C) \]
Read it as: the meganode’s conditional table has one row per value of its parent C and four columns, one per joint value of Sprinkler and Rain. Each entry is the product of the two original tables. With the standard numbers, the row for C = true is 0.1 × 0.8 = 0.08, 0.1 × 0.2 = 0.02, 0.9 × 0.8 = 0.72 and 0.9 × 0.2 = 0.18.
Say it“The probability of the pair given Cloudy is the product of each one given Cloudy.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &P(S{+}R = k \mid \mathbf{e}) \\ &\quad\propto\; \underbrace{\sum_{c} P(k \mid c)\, \pi(c)}_{\text{from above}} \times \underbrace{\lambda(k)}_{\text{from below}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: on a chain the meganode’s posterior is a message from its parent side times a message from its child side. π(c) is P(c) times 1 or 0 depending on whether c agrees with any evidence on C. λ(k) is the probability of the evidence on W given meganode value k: P(W = true | k) if the grass was seen wet, 1 if W was not observed. Evidence on S or R alone zeroes the meganode values it rules out.
Say it“Meganode posterior: the parent’s message times the child’s message, then normalise.”

Set what you have observed. The clustered chain computes the posterior of all four variables in one pass in and one pass out. The table checks every number against brute-force enumeration of the 16 worlds of the original network.

Clustering moves the cost rather than removing it. The message passing is linear in the size of the clustered network, but a meganode’s table has one column per joint value of the variables inside it, so merging k binary variables gives 2k columns. Exact inference in general Bayesian networks is NP-hard, and clustering does not escape that: it is fast exactly when the meganodes can stay small. When meganodes share variables, the algorithm must also make sure they agree on those shared variables’ posteriors, which is what the separators of the junction tree do.

Approximate inference

Loopy belief propagation

When the cliques are too big for a junction tree, there is a bolder option: run sum-product on the loopy graph anyway. Nothing in the message rules mentions trees. What the tree gave us was a place to start (the leaves) and a place to stop (when every message has been sent once in each direction). Without leaves, we start all messages at 1, meaning “no information yet”, and keep updating them in some order until they stop changing, or we give up. This is loopy belief propagation.

The labs here use the simplest loopy model: binary spins si = +1 or −1 on the nodes of a grid, with a local field hi pulling each spin one way and a coupling J pulling neighbours to agree. Factor-graph messages between a node and a pairwise factor can be folded into one message per edge.

\[ p(\mathbf{s}) \;\propto\; \prod_{i} e^{h_i s_i} \prod_{(i,j)} e^{J_{ij}\, s_i s_j} \]
Read it as: each node has a factor ehisi that favours si = +1 when hi > 0, and each edge has a factor that favours agreement (si = sj) when J > 0 and disagreement when J < 0. In the labs, hi is a fixed pattern of values between −1 and 1, scaled by the field slider.
Say it“p of s is proportional to the product over nodes of e to the h-i s-i, times the product over edges of e to the J s-i s-j.”
\[ \begin{aligned} m^{(t+1)}_{i \to j}(s_j) &\propto \sum_{s_i = \pm 1} e^{h_i s_i + J_{ij} s_i s_j} \\ &\qquad \times \prod_{k \in \mathrm{ne}(i) \setminus j} m^{(t)}_{k \to i}(s_i) \\ b_i(s_i) &\propto e^{h_i s_i} \prod_{k \in \mathrm{ne}(i)} m_{k \to i}(s_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the new message from i to j at step t + 1 sums over i’s two values: i’s own field, the edge factor, and everything i heard from its other neighbours at step t. Each message is rescaled to add to 1. The belief bi is i’s field times all incoming messages, normalised. On a tree, bi is the exact marginal; on a loopy graph it is only an estimate.
Say it“The message from i to j is the sum over s-i of the local and edge factors times the messages into i from everyone but j.”

On a loopy graph the order matters, so we need a schedule. The flooding schedule sends every message at once, each computed from the previous round’s messages. A serial schedule sends one message at a time, always using the latest values. A message from i to j is pending if i has received a new message on one of its other links since it last sent to j: only pending messages carry anything new, so efficient schedules send only those. On a tree, pending messages run out after a finite number of steps, and the beliefs are then exact. On a loopy graph, news keeps coming round the loops, and in our labs a message counts as new only if it changed by more than 10−9. Step through both schedules on both graphs. Theory and practice

When loopy BP works, and when it fails

Loopy BP is not guaranteed to converge, and when it does converge its beliefs are not guaranteed to be right. Yet it is the decoder inside turbo codes and LDPC codes, which came close to the theoretical limit of reliable communication, and it was the core of leading stereo-vision methods. Here is what is known, in plain terms.

  • TreesExact, after a number of steps equal to the diameter of the tree. This is chapter 6.5.
  • One loopWeiss (2000) showed that on a graph with a single loop, BP converges, and that for binary variables each belief picks the same most probable value as the exact marginal, even though the probabilities themselves can be off.
  • Weak couplingsMooij and Kappen (2007) proved sufficient conditions for convergence to a unique fixed point. For binary models like ours, one of them is that for every node i and neighbour j, the sum of tanh|Jik| over i’s other neighbours k stays below 1. It is sufficient, not necessary.
  • OvercountingAround a loop, a node’s own evidence comes back to it as if it were new, so loopy beliefs are typically too confident. Short loops with strong couplings are the worst case.
  • FrustrationWhen the couplings around a loop cannot all be satisfied at once (an odd number of negative couplings), messages can circle without settling. Damping helps some oscillations and not others.
  • What a fixed point isYedidia, Freeman and Weiss (2001) showed that BP’s fixed points are the stationary points of the Bethe free energy, an approximation to the true free energy. That explains why the answers are often good and why there can be several fixed points.
\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{i,\; j \in \mathrm{ne}(i)} \sum_{k \in \mathrm{ne}(i) \setminus j} \tanh |J_{ik}| \;<\; 1 \\ &\qquad\Longrightarrow\;\; \text{BP converges} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: look at every node i and every neighbour j, and add up tanh of the coupling strengths to i’s other neighbours. If every such sum is below 1, BP converges to a unique fixed point from any starting messages. On a single loop each node has one other neighbour, so the condition holds for every J. On a 4 × 4 grid the inner nodes have four neighbours, so it needs 3 tanh J < 1, that is J < 0.347.
Say it“If, at every node, the tanh of the couplings to the other neighbours adds to less than one, BP converges.”
\[ m_{i \to j} \;\leftarrow\; \delta\, m^{\text{old}}_{i \to j} + (1 - \delta)\, m^{\text{new}}_{i \to j} \]
Read it as: damping with δ between 0 and 1 moves each message only part of the way towards its new value. It does not change the fixed points, only how the iteration approaches them, and it can stop the flip-flopping that the flooding schedule causes on grids.
Say it“The new message is delta times the old one plus one minus delta times the freshly computed one.”

Real-world project

Project: Sudoku and stereo vision by loopy BP

Two real problems, two kinds of loopy BP. A Sudoku is 81 variables bound by 27 all-different constraints, a factor graph full of short loops; we run sum-product and read off each cell’s most probable digit. Stereo matching is a grid with one variable per pixel; we run min-sum, which is max-sum written with energies (negative logs), to find the depth map with the lowest total cost.

The puzzles are the first 30 of each grade (easy, medium, hard, diabolical) from the Sudoku Exchange Puzzle Bank (Grant McLean, 2020, github.com/grantm/sudoku-exchange-puzzle-bank), dedicated to the public domain. Its puzzles were generated by QQWing, have unique solutions, and were sorted into grades by the difficulty rating of the Sukaku Explainer solver. The stereo pair is Tsukuba from the Middlebury Stereo Vision 2001 data sets (Scharstein and Szeliski, 2002, vision.middlebury.edu/stereo/data), whose site grants permission to use and publish its images and disparity maps and asks for that citation. The page embeds the two 384 × 288 views as greyscale and the true disparity map, unchanged otherwise.

1 · Sudoku: all-different factors

Each cell is a variable with nine values. Each row, column and box is one factor that is 1 if its nine cells hold nine different digits and 0 otherwise. Givens are fixed by a one-hot local factor. The factor-to-variable message is the interesting part: for cell i and digit v, it adds up, over every way of placing the other eight digits in the other eight cells, the product of those cells’ messages. That is the permanent of an 8 × 8 matrix, and the page computes all 81 of them per factor with a dynamic programme over subsets of digits.

\[ \mu_{f \to i}(v) \;=\; \sum_{\sigma:\; \sigma(i) = v} \;\prod_{j \in f,\; j \ne i} \mu_{j \to f}\big(\sigma(j)\big) \]
Read it as: σ runs over the 9! ways of giving the nine cells of factor f nine different digits, keeping only those that give cell i the digit v. Each way is scored by the product of what the other cells believe about the digit they get. The page then rescales each message to add to 1, and keeps every non-zero entry at least 10−30 so that products of eight tiny numbers never underflow to a false zero.
Say it“The message to cell i about digit v sums, over all fillings that put v in cell i, the product of the other cells’ messages.”

Pure BP can stall: the messages converge, but some cells are still undecided. Decimation is the common fix: fix the most confident undecided cell to its favourite digit and continue (after 200 iterations at first, then every 30, or sooner if the messages stop moving). It never backtracks, so a wrong guess ends in a contradiction: a cell with no digit left. Pick a grade and a puzzle and solve it, then run the whole benchmark.

2 · Stereo: min-sum on a pixel grid

A stereo pair is two photos taken side by side. A point at depth z appears shifted by a disparity d proportional to 1/z between the two views. For each pixel p of the left view we choose a disparity dp from 0 to 15. The data cost says how badly the left pixel matches the right-view pixel dp places to its left; the smoothness cost says neighbours should usually have similar disparities, but caps the penalty so that real depth edges are not smoothed away. This is the model of Felzenszwalb and Huttenlocher (2006).

\[ \begin{aligned} E(\mathbf{d}) &= \sum_{p} \lambda \min\!\big(|I_L(p) - I_R(p - d_p)|,\, \tau\big) \\ &\quad + \sum_{(p,q)} \min\!\big(|d_p - d_q|,\, \eta\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: IL and IR are grey levels (0 to 255) in the left and right views, and p − dp means the pixel dp columns to the left. A grey-level difference costs λ per level, capped at τ = 15 levels. Neighbouring pixels p, q pay one unit per step of disparity difference, capped at η. Minimising E is max-sum on p ∝ e−E, so messages are min-sums: each pixel tells each neighbour, for every disparity, the cheapest way to explain itself.
Say it“Energy: lambda times the capped intensity mismatch at every pixel, plus the capped disparity jump between every pair of neighbours.”

The page uses Felzenszwalb and Huttenlocher’s two speed-ups: the min over d of h(d) + |d − d′| is a distance transform computed in two passes instead of K2 work, and a checkerboard schedule updates the white pixels on even iterations and the black ones on odd iterations, in place. It keeps everything in single precision (float32), as the Python reference does, so the two give identical counts. Iteration 0 is “winner takes all”: every pixel picks its cheapest disparity on its own. Run BP and watch the noise drain out of the depth map.

The same computations in Python

One script reproduces every number on the page: the joint table of section 1, the max-sum tree and the underflow, the Viterbi path against brute force, the clustered cloudy network against enumeration, the loopy grids against exact marginals, the Sudoku benchmark and the stereo run. It needs only numpy. The page’s JavaScript adds numbers in the same order as the script, so the Sudoku outcomes (all 120, iteration for iteration) and the stereo pixel counts agree exactly.

"""Reference for every number on the page: max-sum, Viterbi, clustering, loopy BP,
Sudoku by loopy BP and stereo matching by loopy min-sum BP.  numpy only."""
import itertools, json, math, sys, time
import numpy as np

S = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "./"
T_START = time.time()

# ---------- 1. the best answer is not the best marginals ----------
W = np.array([[4, 1, 3], [0, 5, 0], [3, 0, 2]], float)   # weights for (x1, x2)
p = W / W.sum()
m1, m2 = p.sum(1), p.sum(0)

Honest notes. The Sudoku solver is plain loopy BP with greedy decimation and no backtracking, so it is far weaker than a real Sudoku program, which would solve all 120 in milliseconds by search; the point is to see where BP alone succeeds. The stereo scores count every pixel with ground truth, including occluded ones near depth edges, so they are not comparable with the Middlebury benchmark’s published tables, which use masks and multi-scale methods. Timings depend on your device. All other numbers are computed exactly as in the script.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • The most probable configuration is not the set of most probable values. Max-sum finds it by replacing sum-product’s sums with maxima, in logs so that long products do not underflow.
  • One pass to a root gives the best score; back-tracking through stored arg-max pointers gives one consistent best configuration, even with ties. On a chain this is the Viterbi algorithm, N K2 work instead of KN.
  • The junction tree algorithm makes any graph a tree of cliques: moralise, triangulate, take the maximal cliques, join them by a maximum-weight spanning tree so the running intersection property holds, and pass messages. It is exact, and exponential in the largest clique.
  • AIMA’s clustering algorithms are the same idea for Bayesian networks: merge nodes into meganodes until the network is a polytree, then get every posterior in one linear-time pass in each direction.
  • Loopy BP runs the tree messages on a graph with cycles, from uniform messages, under some schedule. It is exact on trees, converges on a single loop, converges for weak couplings, and can be overconfident or fail to converge with strong or frustrated couplings; damping helps some failures.

Check your understanding

Max-sum needs one pass to the root for the best score. Why does sum-product need two passes for all marginals, while max-sum with back-tracking still needs only one pass plus a walk back?

Sum-product wants a marginal at every node, and each node’s marginal needs messages from all directions, so messages must flow both ways. Max-sum wants one configuration: the root’s value is decided by the inward pass, and every other value is then a lookup in a stored pointer table. The walk back costs one table lookup per variable, not a second message pass.

In the cloudy network, why can’t sum-product be run directly on the moral graph, and what does the junction tree look like?

Moralising joins Sprinkler and Rain (they share the child WetGrass), so the moral graph has the triangles C–S–R and S–R–W, which contain cycles. The graph is already triangulated, its maximal cliques are {C, S, R} and {S, R, W}, and the junction tree is these two cliques joined by the separator {S, R}. That is exactly AIMA’s meganode S+R in a different notation.

Eliminating the centre of the 3 × 3 grid first is a bad idea. Why?

The centre has four neighbours that are not joined to each other, so removing it first adds up to six fill-in edges and creates a clique of five variables. Eliminating corners first adds at most one edge each and keeps the cliques small. The best orders keep the largest clique at four variables, matching the grid’s treewidth of 3.

On the 4 × 4 grid with J = 1, flooding BP flips back and forth forever but damping 0.5 converges. Does damping make the answer right?

No. Damping only changes the path to a fixed point, not where the fixed points are. Here the damped run settles on a fixed point whose beliefs are far too confident: the largest error against the exact marginals is still about 0.36. Converging and being correct are different things for loopy BP.

Easy Sudoku puzzles are all solved by BP alone, but many hard ones stall. What does that tell you?

Easy puzzles can be finished by simple local deductions, and BP’s exact zeros reproduce those deductions: a digit ruled out in one unit stays ruled out. Hard puzzles need longer chains of reasoning, where loopy BP’s soft, overcounted beliefs settle on a fixed point that leaves cells undecided or confidently wrong, so decimation sometimes guesses badly and hits a contradiction.
