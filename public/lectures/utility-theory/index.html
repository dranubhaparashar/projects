Rational Decisions & Utility Theory

Beliefs say what might happen; utilities say how much you care

An agent that acts in an uncertain world needs two things: probabilities for what each action might lead to, and a number for how good each outcome is. Multiply, add, pick the largest: that is the whole recipe, and this chapter shows why any agent with sensible preferences must follow it. The curve on the right is a utility of money. A gamble’s worth to you is the height of the chord, not the curve, so a bent curve makes a sure thing beat a gamble with the same average. Drag the two prizes and the probability dot, and watch the gap between the gamble’s average and what it is really worth to you.

The principle

Beliefs times desires: maximum expected utility

You have a flight to catch and must choose when to leave home. The traffic will be light, normal or jammed; you don’t know which, but you have beliefs about it. Each combination of departure time and traffic leads to an outcome you care about: catching the flight with a short wait, catching it after an hour in the lounge, or missing it. A utility function U gives each outcome a number, higher for better. Decision theory then says: for each action, average the utilities of its possible outcomes, weighted by how likely they are, and take the action with the largest average. That is the principle of maximum expected utility (MEU).

The utilities below come from a simple rule: you need the drive time plus 15 minutes at the airport; every spare minute costs half a point from 100, and missing the flight is worth 10. Each point of the triangle is one belief about the traffic: the corners are certainty, the middle is “no idea”. The colour shows which action wins there. Drag the dot around the triangle and watch the best action change.

\[ \begin{aligned} &EU(a \mid \mathbf{e}) = \sum_{s'} U(s') \\ &\quad \times P(\text{Result}(a) = s' \mid a, \mathbf{e}) \\ &a^{*} = \operatorname*{argmax}_{a}\; EU(a \mid \mathbf{e}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: e is everything the agent has observed. Result(a) is the uncertain state that action a leads to; the agent’s beliefs give a probability for each possible s′, and U(s′) says how much it wants s′. The expected utility of a is the probability-weighted average of the utilities, and a rational agent picks the action a* where it is largest. Here the state is the traffic, so P(Result(a) = s′) is just your belief about the traffic, the same for every action; in general the action changes the odds too.
Say it“The expected utility of action a given the evidence is the sum over outcomes of the probability that a leads to that outcome times its utility. Choose the a that makes it largest.”

MEU is a definition of rational behaviour, not an algorithm. Using it needs a model of how actions change the world, a probability for every state, and a utility for every outcome; each is hard to come by, and the chapters that follow (decision networks, Markov decision processes, reinforcement learning) are about getting them and computing with them. Why utilities at all? If an agent’s performance is judged by a measure averaged over all the situations it might face, then choosing by expected utility, with U matching the measure, is exactly what scores best on average.

The foundations

The rules a rational preference must follow

Why should anyone maximise an average? Utility theory answers by starting from preferences, which are easier to defend than numbers. Outcomes are bundled into lotteries: L = [p1, S1; p2, S2; …] gives outcome Si with probability pi (a sure thing is a lottery with one branch). We write A ≻ B when the agent prefers A to B, A ∼ B when it is indifferent, and A ≽ B for “prefers or indifferent”. Six constraints, due in this form to von Neumann and Morgenstern (1944), are the price of entry for being called rational:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\textbf{Orderability:}\ \text{exactly one of} \\ &A \succ B,\ \ B \succ A,\ \ A \sim B \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: given any two lotteries, the agent cannot refuse to compare them: it prefers one or is indifferent.
Say it“Either A is preferred to B, or B to A, or they are equally good; exactly one.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\textbf{Transitivity:} \\ &(A \succ B) \wedge (B \succ C) \Rightarrow (A \succ C) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: preferences do not go round in circles. If they did, the agent could be milked of money for ever; the lab below shows how.
Say it“If A beats B and B beats C, then A beats C.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\textbf{Continuity:}\ \ A \succ B \succ C \Rightarrow \\ &\exists\, p:\ [p, A;\ 1-p, C] \sim B \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: if B sits between A and C, some mix of the best and the worst is exactly as good as B. Dial p from 0 to 1 and the lottery slides from C to A, so somewhere it must pass B. This is how utilities will be measured.
Say it“If B is between A and C, there is a probability p at which a p-chance of A, otherwise C, is just as good as B.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\textbf{Substitutability:}\ \ A \sim B \Rightarrow \\ &[p, A;\ 1-p, C] \sim [p, B;\ 1-p, C] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: swapping a prize for one you like equally, inside a bigger lottery, changes nothing. The same holds with ≻ in place of ∼. The Allais paradox later in the chapter is a test of exactly this rule.
Say it“If A and B are equally good, a lottery with A in it is as good as the same lottery with B swapped in.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\textbf{Monotonicity:}\ \ A \succ B \Rightarrow \\ &\big(p > q \Leftrightarrow \\ &\quad [p, A;\ 1-p, B] \succ [q, A;\ 1-q, B]\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: between two lotteries over the same two outcomes, the agent prefers the one that gives the better outcome the higher chance.
Say it“If A beats B, then a bigger chance of A is better than a smaller one, and only then.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\textbf{Decomposability:} \\ &[p, A;\ 1-p, [q, B;\ 1-q, C]] \\ &\sim [p, A;\ (1-p)q, B; \\ &\qquad (1-p)(1-q), C] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a lottery whose prize is another lottery can be flattened by multiplying probabilities along each path. Only the final chances of each outcome matter, not how many coin flips produce them: there is “no fun in gambling”.
Say it“A lottery over lotteries is the same as the single lottery you get by multiplying the probabilities down each branch.”

The theorem

Rational preferences become numbers

Here is the payoff, proved by Ramsey (1931) and by von Neumann and Morgenstern (1944). If an agent’s preferences obey the six rules, then there is a function U that gives every outcome a real number such that the agent prefers A to B exactly when U(A) is bigger, and, more surprisingly, the number for a lottery is the probability-weighted average of the numbers for its prizes. Nothing was assumed about averages; the rules force them. That is the justification for the MEU principle. The theorem does not say the agent consciously computes anything: it only says its choices can be described as maximising some U.

\[ \begin{aligned} U(A) > U(B) &\Leftrightarrow A \succ B \\ U(A) = U(B) &\Leftrightarrow A \sim B \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a utility function is a numerical stand-in for the preference ordering: bigger number, preferred outcome; equal numbers, indifference.
Say it“U of A is bigger than U of B exactly when A is preferred, and equal exactly when the agent is indifferent.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &U([p_1, S_1;\ \dots;\ p_n, S_n]) \\ &\quad = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i\, U(S_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the utility of a lottery is its expected utility. Once you know the utilities of the sure outcomes, every lottery’s utility follows by averaging.
Say it“The utility of a lottery is the sum of each probability times the utility of its outcome.”

A utility function is not unique. Stretch it by any positive factor and shift it, U′(S) = aU(S) + b with a > 0, and every comparison between lotteries comes out the same, because averages stretch and shift along with it. But a bend that keeps the order of the sure prizes, such as squaring, can reverse the order of two lotteries. So utility under uncertainty is more than a ranking. Without uncertainty, only the ranking matters: any increasing function works, and it is called an ordinal utility or a value function. Drag the four utility dots, then try each transformation.

\[ \begin{aligned} &U'(S) = a\,U(S) + b,\ \ a > 0 \\ &\Rightarrow\ \text{same choices between lotteries} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: utility is measured on an interval scale, like temperature: you may choose where zero sits and how big one unit is, and nothing the agent does changes. Any other monotone transformation keeps the sure prizes in order but may change which lottery wins.
Say it“U-prime equals a times U plus b, with a positive, gives exactly the same decisions.”

Utility scales

Measuring a utility: the standard gamble

Continuity hands us a measuring instrument. Fix the best prize on the table as u⊤ = 1 and the worst as u⊥ = 0 (we are free to, since utility has no fixed zero or unit). For any outcome S in between, offer the choice: S for sure, or the standard lottery [p, best; 1 − p, worst]. Adjust p until the person cannot choose. That p is U(S), on this normalised scale. Medicine uses exactly this to measure how patients value health states, and the same idea gives the QALY (quality-adjusted life year: a year in a health state counts as U of that state, so one year at U = 0.6 is 0.6 QALY) and the micromort (a one-in-a-million chance of death; if you would pay at most £V to avoid a sure death, then, for small risks with linear utility, a micromort is worth V/1,000,000).

Answer the questions: each one halves the range for U(£X), and after five answers for each of four amounts the page fits a curve to your choices. It starts with an example respondent already filled in; press Answer it myself to replace them with yours.

\[ \begin{aligned} &S \sim [\,p,\ u_{\top};\ 1-p,\ u_{\perp}\,] \\ &\Rightarrow\ U(S) = p\cdot 1 + (1-p)\cdot 0 = p \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: if you are indifferent between S and a lottery that gives the best outcome with probability p and the worst otherwise, then S’s utility equals the lottery’s expected utility, which on the 0-to-1 scale is just p.
Say it“If S is as good as a p chance of the best outcome and otherwise the worst, then the utility of S is p.”

The utility of money

Why a sure £400 can beat a coin flip for £1,000

Money is the one attribute nearly everyone ranks the same way: more is better. That is a monotonic preference, but it says nothing about gambles. A lottery’s expected monetary value (EMV) is its average payout. Most people turn down a fair bet: they value a gamble at less than its EMV, which means their utility of money is concave, flattening as they get richer. They are risk-averse. A convex curve is risk-seeking (often seen in people already deep in debt, for whom only a long shot helps), and a straight line is risk-neutral. For stakes that are small compared with your wealth, any smooth curve is nearly straight over the range, so everyone is close to risk-neutral about small bets.

The amount you would accept for sure instead of the lottery is its certainty equivalent (CE). The gap EMV − CE is the risk premium: what you would pay to get rid of the risk, and the reason insurance companies can exist. Below, the offer is £400 for sure or a fair coin flip for £1,000. Utility is applied to your total wealth W after the prize, with constant relative risk aversion r (r = 1 is Bernoulli’s logarithm). Slide your wealth up and watch the risk premium melt.

\[ u_r(w) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{w^{\,1-r}}{1-r} & r \ne 1 \\[4pt] \ln w & r = 1 \end{cases} \]
Read it as: the constant relative risk aversion (CRRA) family. r = 0 is a straight line; bigger r bends the curve more. With r > 0 you would always stake the same fraction of your wealth, whatever its size. The project at the end measures r in 1,505 real people.
Say it“u of w is w to the one minus r over one minus r, or log w when r is one.”
\[ \begin{aligned} u_r(W + CE) &= \textstyle\sum_i p_i\, u_r(W + x_i) \\ \text{risk premium} &= \text{EMV} - CE \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the certainty equivalent is the sure gain whose utility matches the lottery’s expected utility. Subtract it from the lottery’s average to get the premium you would give up for certainty.
Say it“u of W plus CE equals the expected u of W plus the prize; the risk premium is the EMV minus the CE.”

The St Petersburg game

Daniel Bernoulli (1738) invented the logarithmic utility to settle a puzzle. Toss a fair coin until it lands heads; if that happens on toss n, you win £2n. The expected payout is infinite, yet nobody would pay more than a few pounds to play. With u = ln(wealth), each doubling of the prize adds the same utility while its chance halves, so the expected utility is finite and the game is worth little. Play a thousand games at a time: the average payout creeps up for ever, but very slowly, driven by rare huge wins.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{EMV} &= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \tfrac{1}{2^n}\, 2^n \\ &= 1 + 1 + \dots = \infty \\ EU &= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \tfrac{1}{2^n} \ln(W + 2^n) < \infty \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: every possible toss contributes exactly £1 to the average payout, so the sum never stops growing. Under log utility the terms shrink fast enough to add up to a finite number, and its certainty equivalent CE = eEU − W is a modest sum. With W = 0 (only the prize counts), EU = 2 ln 2 and the game is worth exactly £4.
Say it“The expected payout is the sum of one-half-to-the-n times two-to-the-n, which is infinite; the expected log utility is the sum of one-half-to-the-n times log of W plus two-to-the-n, which is finite.”

Post-decision disappointment

The optimiser’s curse

In practice an agent never knows expected utilities exactly; it has estimates, and even unbiased ones are noisy. Suppose you pick, among k options, the one whose estimate is highest. The winner is likely to be one whose noise happened to be positive, so its estimate is biased upwards, and when you find out its real value you will, on average, be disappointed. This is the optimiser’s curse (Smith & Winkler, 2006). It gets worse with more options and noisier estimates, and it strikes every manager choosing the best-looking project and every model selected by its validation score.

The cure is to treat estimates as evidence, not truth. If the true values are spread around 0 with spread τ and each estimate adds noise with spread σi, Bayes’ rule shrinks each estimate towards the prior mean, more so the noisier it is. Raise k and the noise, then switch on the correction. Try unequal noise too: then the naive chooser keeps falling for the noisiest options.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}\Big[\max_{i \le k} \hat U_i\Big] \\ &\quad = \sigma \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\, k\, \varphi(x)\, \Phi(x)^{k-1}\, dx \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the simplest case. All k options are truly worth 0, and the estimates Ûi are pure noise with spread σ. The largest of k draws has density kφ(x)Φ(x)k−1 (φ and Φ are the standard normal density and cumulative). Its mean is positive and grows with k, so the chosen option promises more than it will deliver.
Say it“The expected best estimate is sigma times the integral of x times k phi of x times Phi of x to the k minus one.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &U_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2),\ \ \hat U_i = U_i + \sigma_i \varepsilon_i \\ &\mathbb{E}[U_i \mid \hat U_i] = \frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2 + \sigma_i^2}\, \hat U_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with a Gaussian prior for the true values and Gaussian noise, the posterior mean is the estimate multiplied by a shrinkage factor between 0 and 1. Precise estimates (small σi) are trusted; noisy ones are pulled hard towards 0. Choose by the shrunk values and, on average, what you expect is what you get.
Say it“The expected true value given the estimate is tau squared over tau squared plus sigma-i squared, times the estimate.”

Human judgment

Where people break the rules

Decision theory is normative: it says how a rational agent should choose. Psychologists asked whether it is also descriptive, and found systematic departures. The most famous is due to Maurice Allais (1953); the version here is Kahneman and Tversky’s (1979). Answer both questions honestly before reading on. Pick one option in each pair.

Most people choose B and C. But question 2 is question 1 with every chance divided by four: C is a 25% ticket to A, D a 25% ticket to B. By substitutability, anyone who prefers B should prefer D. No utility function at all can produce B and C together:

\[ \begin{aligned} &B \succ A \\ &\Leftrightarrow U(3000) > 0.8\, U(4000) \\ &\Leftrightarrow 0.25\, U(3000) > 0.2\, U(4000) \\ &\Leftrightarrow D \succ C \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: set U(£0) = 0 (allowed: it only shifts the scale). Multiplying both sides of the first inequality by 0.25 gives the second, which is the comparison of D with C. The two questions must get the same kind of answer, whatever the shape of U.
Say it“B beats A exactly when U of 3,000 exceeds 0.8 U of 4,000, which is exactly when 0.25 U of 3,000 exceeds 0.2 U of 4,000, which is when D beats C.”
\[ \begin{aligned} V &= w(p)\, x^{\alpha} \\ w(p) &= \frac{p^{\gamma}}{(p^{\gamma} + (1-p)^{\gamma})^{1/\gamma}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: prospect theory’s fix (Tversky & Kahneman, 1992). People weigh a probability p by w(p), which overweights small chances and underweights large ones, so the jump from 80% to 100% (certainty) feels far bigger than the jump from 20% to 25%. That is the certainty effect. With γ = 1, w(p) = p and the model is expected utility again. Here α = 0.88, their estimate.
Say it“The value of a gamble is w of p times x to the alpha, where w of p is p to the gamma over p to the gamma plus one minus p to the gamma, all to the one over gamma.”

The Ellsberg urn: ambiguity aversion

Daniel Ellsberg (1961) found a second kind of departure. An urn holds 90 balls: 30 red, and 60 that are black or yellow in an unknown mix. You win £100 if the ball drawn matches your bet. Most people prefer betting on red to black, and on black or yellow to red or yellow: they prefer odds they know. Make your two bets and slide your belief about the share of black: is there any belief that justifies both?

\[ \begin{aligned} R \succ B &\Leftrightarrow \tfrac13 > b \\ B{\cup}Y \succ R{\cup}Y &\Leftrightarrow \tfrac23 > \tfrac13 + (\tfrac23 - b) \\ &\Leftrightarrow b > \tfrac13 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: b is your probability that the ball is black, somewhere between 0 and 2/3; yellow then has 2/3 − b. With the prize’s utility set to 1, each bet’s expected utility is its chance of winning. The usual pair of choices needs b below 1/3 and above 1/3 at once. People are averse to ambiguity, not just to risk.
Say it“Red beats black when a third exceeds b; black-or-yellow beats red-or-yellow when two-thirds exceeds a third plus two-thirds minus b, which is when b exceeds a third.”

There is more. The framing effect: the same medical treatment is chosen far more often when described as giving a “90% survival rate” than a “10% death rate”, though the two say the same thing. Anchoring: people’s estimates and offers drift towards any number they have just seen, even an irrelevant one. Some departures may be partly rational: regret at losing a sure £3,000 is a real cost that a utility over money alone leaves out, and ambiguity may signal that the person offering the bet knows something you don’t. People also reason far better when chances are given as counts (“2 in 100”) than as percentages. For building agents, the lesson is that decision theory is the standard to aim at, and that utilities learned from human choices, as in reinforcement learning from human feedback, inherit these quirks.

Many attributes

Dominance: deciding without weights

Real outcomes have several attributes: a site for a new airport has a construction cost, a number of people disturbed by noise, a safety record. Write the outcome as a vector x = (x1, …, xn). Before weighing them against each other, look for options that can be thrown out with no weighing at all. Option B is strictly dominated if some option A is at least as good on every attribute and better on one: no sensible weights could ever choose B. What survives is the Pareto front. (In the flight example, leaving 150 minutes early was dominated by leaving at 110: worse whatever the traffic, which is why it never won.) Drag the sites and watch them join and leave the front.

\[ \begin{aligned} &A \text{ dominates } B \iff \\ &\quad x_j(A) \ge x_j(B)\ \text{ for all } j, \\ &\quad x_j(A) > x_j(B)\ \text{ for some } j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for attributes where more is better. Here both attributes are costs, so “better” means lower; flip the inequalities. Dominance is a partial order: many pairs are not comparable at all, and only the preferences can settle those.
Say it“A dominates B when A is at least as good on every attribute and strictly better on at least one.”
sites on the Pareto front
dominated sites

Costs are rarely known exactly. If each site’s cost is a probability distribution, strict dominance rarely applies, but a weaker test often does. Site S1 stochastically dominates S2 if, for every amount c, S1 is at least as likely to come in under c. Then every agent who prefers lower costs, whatever its utility curve and attitude to risk, prefers S1. Move the two cost distributions: dominance holds when one cumulative curve sits entirely above the other.

\[ \begin{aligned} &S_1 \text{ stochastically dominates } S_2 \\ &\iff P(C_1 \le c) \ge P(C_2 \le c)\ \ \forall c \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: plot each site’s cumulative distribution of cost. If S1’s curve is never below S2’s, S1 is cheaper in a very strong sense, and its expected utility is at least as high for every utility that decreases with cost. If the curves cross, it depends on the agent’s attitude to risk, and you need the utility function after all.
Say it“S1 dominates S2 when, for every cost c, the chance S1 costs at most c is at least the chance S2 does.”

Dominance can even be checked without any numbers. If you know only that more noise lowers utility and that a site near a city is likely to disturb more people, a qualitative model of these monotone influences can already show that one option dominates another.

Preference structure

Many attributes, one number

When dominance does not decide, we need a utility over the whole vector, U(x1, …, xn). With ten attributes of ten levels each that is ten billion numbers to elicit, unless the preferences have structure. Two attributes are preferentially independent of a third if your ranking of outcomes that differ only in those two does not depend on the third’s value. If every subset is independent of the rest (mutual preferential independence, MPI), Debreu (1960) showed that preferences are captured by a simple sum of one-attribute value functions. Without uncertainty, that is all you need.

Independence is not guaranteed. Whether you would rather drink red or white wine may depend on whether dinner is beef or fish: then wine is not preferentially independent of the dish, and no sum Vdish + Vwine can rank the four dinners, because it would need red to beat white and white to beat red.

\[ \text{MPI} \ \Rightarrow\ V(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} V_i(x_i) \]
Read it as: if the attributes are mutually preferentially independent, the agent’s ranking of deterministic outcomes is reproduced by adding up one value function per attribute. n small functions replace one huge table.
Say it“Under mutual preferential independence, the value of the outcome is the sum of the values of its attributes.”

Under uncertainty we need the stronger utility independence: your preferences between lotteries over some attributes do not depend on the fixed values of the others. If it holds for every subset (mutual utility independence, MUI), utility is multiplicative (Keeney, 1974): a sum of one-attribute utilities plus their products. For three attributes:

\[ \begin{aligned} U = {}& k_1U_1 + k_2U_2 + k_3U_3 \\ &+ k_1k_2\,U_1U_2 + k_2k_3\,U_2U_3 \\ &+ k_3k_1\,U_3U_1 + k_1k_2k_3\,U_1U_2U_3 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Ui is the utility of attribute i alone, scaled from 0 to 1, and the k’s are constants. The product terms let attributes interact: whether doing well on two attributes together is worth more or less than the sum of each. n one-attribute utilities and n constants describe the whole thing. In this form the product weights are tied to the k’s; the general Keeney form scales them by one more constant, which is how the interaction below can be positive or negative.
Say it“U is k-one U-one plus k-two U-two plus k-three U-three, plus the products of the pairs with their k’s, plus k-one k-two k-three times all three.”

The lab uses the two-attribute case for choosing a flat: U1 rewards a short commute, U2 a low rent. With k1 + k2 = 1 the interaction vanishes and the utility is additive; the indifference curves (outcomes you like equally) are then straight lines. Change the weights: with k1 + k2 < 1 the two goods are complements and the curves sag towards the worst corner; with k1 + k2 > 1 they are substitutes and bulge towards the best corner.

\[ \begin{aligned} U = {}& k_1 U_1 + k_2 U_2 \\ &+ (1 - k_1 - k_2)\, U_1 U_2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: scaled so that the worst flat on both counts has U = 0 and the best on both has U = 1. k1 is the utility of a flat that is best on commute but worst on rent; k2 the reverse.
Say it“U equals k-one U-one plus k-two U-two plus one minus k-one minus k-two times U-one U-two.”

Real-world project

Project: how risk-averse are 1,505 adults?

Holt and Laury (2002) designed what became the standard lottery test of risk attitude: ten rows, each a choice between a safe lottery A (​2.00 or 1.60) and a risky lottery B (3.85 or 0.10), with the chance of the higher prize rising from 10% to 100% down the rows. A risk-neutral person takes A for the first four rows and B from row 5 on; the later you switch, the more risk-averse you are. The Basel–Berlin Risk Study (Frey et al., 2017) put this exact list to 1,507 adults aged 20 to 36, in Basel and in Berlin, alongside dozens of other risk measures; its data are public under CC BY 4.0. Here, 1,505 people completed all ten rows.

From switch point to utility curve

Each row has a value of r at which a CRRA chooser is exactly indifferent between A and B. Someone who takes A on row k and B on row k + 1 has an r between those two cut-points. Row 10 is special: B pays 3.85 for sure against A’s 2.00, so B dominates A and any agent who likes money must pick B.

\[ \begin{aligned} &p_k\,u_r(2.00) + (1 - p_k)\,u_r(1.60) \\ &= p_k\,u_r(3.85) + (1 - p_k)\,u_r(0.10) \\ &\Rightarrow\ r = r_k, \quad p_k = k/10 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: on row k the high prize has probability k/10. Solve for the r that makes both lotteries’ expected utilities equal: that is the row’s cut-point rk. A person with r above rk takes A on row k. The cut-points rise down the rows, so the switch row pins r into an interval.
Say it“On row k, the r at which k-tenths of u of 2 plus the rest of u of 1.60 equals k-tenths of u of 3.85 plus the rest of u of 0.10 is the cut-point r-k.”

What 1,505 people chose

Real choices are noisy: some people switch back and forth, and a few even take the dominated A on row 10. So instead of reading off each person’s interval, fit one CRRA chooser with noise to everyone at once. The model says the chance of choosing B grows smoothly with how much more B is worth, measured in money as the difference of certainty equivalents; λ says how sharp the choice is. Maximising the likelihood over (r, λ) gives the population’s utility curve. Pick a group and compare the fitted curve with the risk-neutral prediction.

\[ \begin{aligned} &P(\text{B on row } k) \\ &\quad = \sigma\big(\lambda\,[\,CE_r(B_k) - CE_r(A_k)\,]\big) \\ &\ln L(r, \lambda) \\ &\quad = \sum_{\text{people}}\ \sum_{k=1}^{10} \ln P(\text{choice on row } k) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: CEr(L) = ur−1(EUr(L)) is the lottery’s certainty equivalent for a CRRA chooser; σ is the logistic function. When B is worth more, B is likely; the larger λ, the closer the model is to always picking the better lottery. The fit treats all 15,050 choices as independent, which they are not (ten come from each person), so it gives a good central estimate of r but would overstate its precision.
Say it“The chance of B on row k is the logistic of lambda times the certainty-equivalent gap between B and A; the log-likelihood sums the log chance of every choice actually made.”

The same fit in Python

import json, numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq, minimize

D = json.load(open("x065-data.json"))      # Basel-Berlin Risk Study (OSF rce7g, CC BY 4.0): mpl.csv, dp = 1
C = np.array([[c == "B" for c in s] for s in D["P"]], float)   # 1,505 people x 10 rows, 1 = risky B
p = np.arange(1, 11) / 10                  # chance of the high prize on row k
A, B = (2.00, 1.60), (3.85, 0.10)

def u(x, r):    return np.log(x) if abs(r - 1) < 1e-12 else x ** (1 - r) / (1 - r)
def uinv(v, r): return np.exp(v) if abs(r - 1) < 1e-12 else ((1 - r) * v) ** (1 / (1 - r))
def ce(L, r):   return uinv(p * u(L[0], r) + (1 - p) * u(L[1], r), r)   # certainty equivalent, per row

safe = (C == 0).sum(1)
clean = np.array([s == "A" * k + "B" * (10 - k) for s, k in zip(D["P"], safe)])
print("people:", len(C), "| one clean switch:", clean.sum(), "| chose A on row 10 (dominated):", int((C[:, 9] == 0).sum()))
print("share choosing B by row:", np.round(C.mean(0), 4))
print("safe-choice counts 0..10:", np.bincount(safe, minlength=11))
cuts = [brentq(lambda r: ce(A, r)[k] - ce(B, r)[k], -3, 3, xtol=1e-14) for k in range(9)]
print("CRRA cut-points r_1..r_9:", np.round(cuts, 3))

def nll(th, nB, n):                        # Fechner logit on the CE gap; only the per-row counts matter
    r, lam = th; z = lam * (ce(B, r) - ce(A, r))
    return np.sum(nB * np.logaddexp(0, -z) + (n - nB) * np.logaddexp(0, z))
def fit(nB, n):
    o = dict(method="Nelder-Mead", options={"xatol": 1e-10, "fatol": 1e-10, "maxiter": 4000})
    f = minimize(nll, [0.5, 2.5], args=(nB, n), **o)
    rn = minimize(lambda t: nll([0.0, t[0]], nB, n), [2.0], **o)
    return f.x[0], f.x[1], -f.fun, -rn.fun

def mulberry32(a):                         # the page's random numbers, bit for bit
    s = [a & 0xFFFFFFFF]
    def r():
        s[0] = (s[0] + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF; a = s[0]
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return r

groups = {"everyone": np.ones(len(C), bool), "clean switchers": clean,
          "Basel": np.array([c == "0" for c in D["L"]]), "Berlin": np.array([c == "1" for c in D["L"]]),
          "women": np.array([c == "0" for c in D["S"]]), "men": np.array([c == "1" for c in D["S"]])}
out = {"cuts": cuts, "groups": {}}
for gi, (g, mask) in enumerate(groups.items()):
    X = C[mask]; n = len(X)
    r, lam, ll, llrn = fit(X.sum(0), np.full(10, n))
    rng, boot = mulberry32(1000 + gi), []          # 400 resamples of whole people
    for b in range(400):
        idx = np.array([int(rng() * n) for _ in range(n)])
        boot.append(fit(X[idx].sum(0), np.full(10, n))[0])
    boot = np.sort(boot); lo, hi = boot[10], boot[389]
    out["groups"][g] = dict(n=n, r=r, lam=lam, ll=ll, llrn=llrn, lo=lo, hi=hi)
    print(f"{g:15s} n = {n:4d}  r = {r:.4f}  lambda = {lam:.4f}  log-lik = {ll:.3f}  (r = 0: {llrn:.3f})  95% bootstrap r: [{lo:.3f}, {hi:.3f}]")
json.dump(out, open("x065-py.json", "w"))

Honest caveats. The stakes are the small ones of Holt and Laury’s original list, so the measured r describes attitudes to small gambles; by the argument in the money section, a CRRA chooser should be nearly risk-neutral at these stakes, and r is fitted to the prizes alone, as is standard. Read as a utility of total wealth, these choices would need an implausibly large r: people are more averse to small risks than a smooth utility of wealth can explain (Rabin, 2000). The fit pools each group into one chooser and ignores how people differ within it. The 95% intervals resample whole people, so they respect the fact that each person made ten choices. Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A rational agent chooses the action with the maximum expected utility: EU(a) = Σ P(outcome | a) U(outcome). Beliefs and desires are combined by multiplying and adding.
  • Six constraints on preferences (orderability, transitivity, continuity, substitutability, monotonicity, decomposability) guarantee a utility function exists, and that lotteries are valued by their expected utility. It is unique only up to U′ = aU + b.
  • Utilities are measured with the standard gamble; for money they are usually concave (risk-averse), and the certainty equivalent falls short of the average by the risk premium.
  • Picking the best of many noisy estimates guarantees disappointment on average (the optimiser’s curse); shrink estimates towards a prior before choosing.
  • People violate the axioms (Allais, Ellsberg, framing), and many attributes need structure: dominance first, then additive or multiplicative utilities when independence holds. 1,505 real people chose like a CRRA agent with r ≈ 0.5.

Check your understanding

Action a gives utility 10 with probability 0.6 and 0 otherwise; action b gives 5 for sure. Which does an MEU agent pick? What if U is replaced by 3U + 7?

EU(a) = 0.6 × 10 = 6 and EU(b) = 5, so a. Under 3U + 7, EU(a) = 0.6 × 37 + 0.4 × 7 = 25 and EU(b) = 22: still a. Positive affine changes never alter choices.

Someone prefers tea to coffee, coffee to cocoa, and cocoa to tea. Which axiom is broken, and why should they care?

Transitivity. They can be money-pumped: offered each preferred drink for a small fee in turn, they pay every time and end up where they started, poorer.

You are indifferent between £300 for sure and a 50% chance of £1,000 (else nothing). What is your normalised U(£300), and are you risk-averse?

With U(£0) = 0 and U(£1,000) = 1, the standard gamble gives U(£300) = 0.5. The gamble’s EMV is £500 but its certainty equivalent is £300, so the risk premium is £200: risk-averse.

A firm picks the best of 20 projects by estimated return; the estimates are unbiased. Why will the chosen project usually underperform its estimate, and what should the firm do?

The optimiser’s curse: the maximum of 20 noisy estimates is biased upwards, because the winner is likely to have had lucky noise. Shrink each estimate towards a prior, more for noisier estimates, and choose by the shrunk values.
