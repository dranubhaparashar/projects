Prompt, Retrieve or Fine‑tune?

Model merging and fine-tuning tactics: the decision-making chapter

The last three chapters taught three ways to make a language model do your job: write a better prompt, retrieve the right passages into its context, or change its weights. This chapter is about choosing. Each lever changes a different thing, costs money at a different moment, and fails in a different way. The setting · from foundation models to AI engineering

Three levers: what each one actually changes

Ten years ago, building a language application meant collecting a labelled dataset and training a model for that one task. A foundation model changes the order of work. The model already exists, already reads and writes, and already knows a great deal; the engineering problem is to make a general model behave as a specialist on your inputs, at a price and latency you can live with. The craft of doing that with an existing model, rather than training a new one, is what practitioners have started to call AI engineering, to distinguish it from the machine-learning engineering of the previous decade. Its toolbox has exactly three levers, and the rest of this chapter is about pulling the right one.

The first lever is prompting (chapter 4.8, 4.8a): instructions, examples and formatting rules written into the context window. It changes nothing in the model; it changes what the model is conditioned on, at every call. The second is retrieval (chapter 4.9): a search index outside the model that picks, per query, the passages most likely to contain the answer and pastes them into the context. It still changes nothing in the model, but it lets the context be assembled from a corpus far larger than any window, and it lets the corpus be updated without touching the model. The third is fine-tuning (chapter 4.6, 4.6a): continuing training on your own examples so that the behaviour you want is stored in the weights and no longer needs to be spelled out.

The cleanest way to keep them apart is to ask where the change lives. Prompting lives in the context and is paid for in tokens on every call. Retrieval lives in an index and is paid for in index building, per-query search, and the retrieved tokens. Fine-tuning lives in the weights and is paid for once, in training compute and in the data you had to produce, after which the per-call prompt can be short. Switch the levers on and off under the figure and watch the context a single call carries change shape; the numbers are tokens, and they are the quantity that every cost model in this chapter multiplies by a price.

What each lever cannot do

Half of the bad decisions in this area come from asking a lever to do a job that belongs to another. The failures are predictable once you remember where each one lives.

  • Prompting cannot add knowledge the window will not holdInstructions can tell the model how to answer; they cannot tell it about ten thousand documents. A prompt that tries becomes a retrieval system assembled by hand on every call, at full price. Nor can a prompt make a small model reason like a large one: in-context learning (chapter 4.8) selects among abilities the model already has.
  • Retrieval cannot teach a skillThe retriever chooses what the model reads; the model still has to know how to read it, how to format the answer and when to abstain. A model that writes invalid JSON with no passages in the prompt writes invalid JSON with four passages in the prompt. Retrieval also cannot fix a retriever’s blind spots: if the passage is not in the index, or the query does not match its wording, the model answers from memory and nobody notices.
  • Fine-tuning cannot keep up with the worldWeights are a snapshot. A fact learned by fine-tuning is as current as the training set and no more; a fact that changed last week is wrong until the next training run, and the old value may survive the retrain. Fine-tuning also cannot make a model cite sources it never saw as passages, and it cannot be undone except by keeping the base model and the adapter apart.
  • None of them replaces evaluationEach lever needs the same thing before it is pulled: a set of real inputs with the outputs you want, held out from whatever you tune. Without it you cannot tell whether the prompt is good enough, whether the retriever finds the passages, or whether the fine-tune regressed something you cared about. The evaluation set is the one investment that transfers between levers; build it first.
Decision framework

When to fine-tune, and when not to

The most common mistake is to start with the most expensive lever. Fine-tuning feels like real machine learning, and it is, which is exactly the problem: it brings back a training loop, a dataset to curate, a model to version and evaluate, and a way to quietly get worse at everything you did not train on. The discipline that experienced teams converge on is a ladder. Prompt first, because a day of prompt engineering establishes what the base model can and cannot do and produces the evaluation set you will need anyway. Add retrieval when the failures are about facts the model does not have. Fine-tune when the failures are about how the model behaves and prompting has demonstrably plateaued: the format keeps slipping, the style is wrong, the prompt has grown to thousands of tokens to hold the behaviour in place, or the latency and cost of that long prompt have become the bottleneck.

The questions below are the ones that decide. Is the missing thing knowledge or behaviour? Does the knowledge change, and must the answer point to its source? How many good examples can you actually produce? Is the per-call budget tight? Must the data stay on your own machines? Answer them and the tree lights up a route and the reasons for it. The route is a recommendation, not a verdict; the reasons are what you should be able to say in the design review.

Recommendation

    Reasons to fine-tune

    • The output must follow a format or protocol exactly: JSON schemas, tool-call syntax, a house style, a fixed dialogue policy.
    • The prompt has become the product: thousands of tokens of instructions and examples re-sent on every call.
    • You need a small, fast, cheap model to do one thing as well as a large one does it with a long prompt.
    • The behaviour is stable: what you teach today will still be right next quarter.
    • You can produce hundreds of clean examples and an evaluation that would catch regressions.

    Reasons not to

    • The failures are about facts, especially facts that change or need a citation: retrieve instead.
    • You have not exhausted prompting: no few-shot attempt, no structured-output constraints, no evaluation set yet.
    • You have a few dozen examples: they teach more as demonstrations in the prompt than as gradient steps.
    • You cannot afford to re-evaluate the model’s general abilities after training: forgetting is silent.
    • The base model will be replaced soon: a fine-tune is tied to one base and does not transfer.

    • Support-ticket triageEach incoming ticket must be labelled with one of forty categories and a priority, as a two-field JSON object, 300,000 times a month, with a tight latency budget. A LoRA on a small open model, evaluated against a prompted large model on a held-out slice; the cost chart of section 3 crosses within weeks at this traffic.
    • Contract-clause extraction for a law firmPull defined terms, parties and termination clauses from contracts into a fixed schema. Form; about 300 reviewed examples; data cannot leave the firm. → Fine-tune (LoRA) on an open-weights model hosted in-house. The privacy answer rules out an API; the 300 examples are enough for LoRA and for a held-out evaluation; forgetting matters little because the model does one job.

Reaching for training here would spend a week to get a model that cannot be evaluated.
    Cost models

    Cost models and the break-even point

    Each lever has a different cost shape, and the shape matters more than the price list, which changes every few months. Prompting has no fixed cost and a per-call cost proportional to the tokens it sends and receives: with \(n\) calls a month, a prompt of \(L_{\text{in}}\) input tokens and \(L_{\text{out}}\) output tokens, and prices \(p_{\text{in}}, p_{\text{out}}\) per token, the monthly bill is \(n\,(p_{\text{in}} L_{\text{in}} + p_{\text{out}} L_{\text{out}})\). Retrieval adds a one-off index (embedding every chunk of the corpus), a monthly hosting cost, and a small per-query search cost, in exchange for a prompt that carries only the instructions, the question and the few retrieved passages instead of the whole corpus or a long list of examples. Fine-tuning is an up-front cost (data preparation, training tokens times a training price, evaluation) and then a per-call cost on a short prompt, sometimes at a higher per-token price than the base model.

    Write the three as functions of months \(m\) and the comparison becomes a picture: three lines with different intercepts and slopes. The line with the highest intercept and the lowest slope eventually wins, and the month in which it overtakes is the break-even point. Whether that month is 2 or 40 depends on one ratio: the fixed cost divided by the per-call saving. Edit any number below; the prices are illustrative round figures, not a quotation, and the point is how the answer moves, not the answer.

    \[ \begin{aligned} C_{\text{prompt}}(m) &= m\,n\,\big(p_{\text{in}}L^{P}_{\text{in}} + p_{\text{out}}L_{\text{out}}\big) \\ C_{\text{rag}}(m) &= C_{\text{index}} + m\,\big(h + n\,(c_q + p_{\text{in}}L^{R}_{\text{in}} + p_{\text{out}}L_{\text{out}})\big) \\ C_{\text{ft}}(m) &= C_{\text{train}} + m\,n\,\kappa\,\big(p_{\text{in}}L^{F}_{\text{in}} + p_{\text{out}}L_{\text{out}}\big) \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: cumulative cost after m months. n is calls per month. LP, LR, LF are the input tokens of one call under prompting (instructions + examples + question), retrieval (instructions + question + k retrieved passages) and fine-tuning (short instructions + question). Cindex is the embedding of the corpus, h the monthly index hosting, cq the per-query search cost. Ctrain covers training tokens times the training price plus the fixed engineering and evaluation effort; κ ≥ 1 is the price multiplier for serving a custom model.
    Say it“Prompting is all slope. Retrieval is a small intercept and a medium slope. Fine-tuning is a big intercept and the smallest slope.”
    \[ n^{*} = \frac{C_{\text{train}}}{c_{\text{prompt}} - c_{\text{ft}}}, \qquad m^{*} = \frac{n^{*}}{n} \]
    Read it as: the break-even number of calls between a long prompt and a fine-tuned short prompt is the training cost divided by the saving per call, cprompt − cft. Divide by the monthly traffic to get the break-even month. If the saving per call is zero or negative (a short prompt at a high custom-model price can cost more per call), fine-tuning never pays back on cost alone and must be justified by quality or latency.

    Worked by hand: when does the fine-tune pay for itself?

    Take the default numbers: $2.50 per million input tokens, $10 per million output tokens, a custom model at 1.5× those prices, $8 per million training tokens and $500 of fixed effort. The few-shot prompt carries 300 + 1,200 + 300 = 1,800 input tokens and produces 150; the fine-tuned prompt carries 100 + 300 = 400 and produces the same 150. Training uses 2,000 examples × 1,500 tokens (three epochs of 500) = 3 million tokens.

    1. 1 · Cost of one few-shot call
      \( c_{\text{prompt}} = 1{,}800 \times 2.5\!\times\!10^{-6} + 150 \times 10^{-5} = 0.0045 + 0.0015 = \$0.0060 \)
      how exactly?
      $2.50 per million tokens is $2.50 / 1,000,000 = $0.0000025 per token; $10 per million is $0.00001 per token.
      input: 1,800 × 0.0000025 = 0.0045 · output: 150 × 0.00001 = 0.0015 · total 0.0060, six tenths of a cent per call.
    2. 2 · Cost of one fine-tuned call
      \( c_{\text{ft}} = 1.5 \times \big(400 \times 2.5\!\times\!10^{-6} + 150 \times 10^{-5}\big) = 1.5 \times 0.0025 = \$0.00375 \)
      how exactly?
      input: 400 × 0.0000025 = 0.0010 · output: 150 × 0.00001 = 0.0015 · base price total 0.0025 · times the custom-model multiplier 1.5 = 0.00375.
      Notice the output tokens are now 60% of the bill: shortening the prompt cannot touch them.
    3. 3 · The training bill
      \( C_{\text{train}} = 3\,\text{M tokens} \times \$8/\text{M} + \$500 = \$24 + \$500 = \$524 \)
      how exactly?
      2,000 examples × 1,500 tokens = 3,000,000 training tokens → 3 × $8 = $24 of compute. The fixed $500 (writing the examples, building the evaluation, a few failed runs) dwarfs it, which is typical for small fine-tunes: the data costs more than the GPU.
    4. 4 · Break-even
      \( n^{*} = \frac{524}{0.0060 - 0.00375} = \frac{524}{0.00225} \approx 232{,}900 \text{ calls}; \quad m^{*} = \frac{232{,}900}{50{,}000} \approx 4.7 \text{ months} \)
      how exactly?
      saving per call: 0.0060 − 0.00375 = 0.00225 dollars.
      524 / 0.00225 = 232,888.9 calls. At 50,000 calls a month that is 232,889 / 50,000 = 4.66 months; at 5,000 calls a month it would be 46.6 months, by which time the base model will have been replaced twice. The traffic, not the price list, decides.

    What the cost model leaves out

    Three lines with intercepts and slopes is the right first picture and the wrong last one. The model above is deliberately simple; these are the terms that change its answer in practice, and the direction in which they change it.

    • Prompt cachingMost APIs charge a fraction of the input price for a prefix that was seen recently. The system prompt and the few-shot examples are exactly such a prefix, so the prompting line’s slope falls, sometimes by more than half, and the break-even moves out. Put the cached price on the fixed part of the prompt and the full price on the question before you decide.
    • Self-hosting changes the shapeA GPU rented by the hour costs the same at 1,000 calls a month and at 1,000,000. For an in-house open-weights model every line becomes a monthly fixed cost plus nothing per call until the card saturates, at which point a second card is a step. The question becomes utilisation, not tokens (chapter 4.5a).
    • Engineering time dominates small fine-tunesThe $500 of fixed effort in the worked example stands for whatever it costs your team to write and review examples, build the evaluation and babysit the runs; for most teams it is several times larger, and it recurs with every base-model upgrade. The GPU bill is rarely the issue.
    • Quality is not in the modelA cheaper call that is wrong more often is not cheaper. Attach a value to a correct answer and a cost to a wrong one and the comparison becomes expected value per call, which is where the chapter’s other sections come in: the levers differ in which failures they remove.
    • The base model has a lifetimeA fine-tune is tied to one base checkpoint. If a better base arrives in nine months, the adapter must be retrained and re-evaluated; the up-front cost is paid again. A break-even beyond the expected life of the base is a loss, whatever the chart says at month 24.
    • Retrieval has hidden lines tooThe retriever itself (an embedding model, a re-ranker) may run on your own GPU; chunking and re-indexing pipelines need maintenance; and the retrieved passages lengthen every prompt, which the model above counts but engineers often forget when they compare “RAG” with “a prompt”.
    Fine-tuning versus RAG

    Knowledge: when retrieval beats fine-tuning, and when it does not

    The question people ask most is whether to put their documents into a fine-tune or into a retrieval index. Mechanically the two do different things. Fine-tuning on documents (continued pre-training on raw text, or supervised training on question–answer pairs derived from it) asks the model to store facts in its weights, where they are blended with everything else it knows and recalled by association. Retrieval asks the model to read facts at inference time, from a passage that is sitting in the context. Reading is what language models are best at; storing new facts reliably from a few exposures is what they are worst at. Pre-training sees most facts thousands of times in thousands of phrasings; a fine-tune on a company handbook sees each fact once.

    The evidence points the same way. Lewis et al. (2020) introduced retrieval-augmented generation precisely so that a model’s knowledge could be a swappable, inspectable index rather than frozen weights, and found the retrieval-augmented model more specific and more factual than its parametric-only counterpart on knowledge-intensive tasks. Ovadia et al. (2024) compared the two directly on knowledge questions, including questions about events after the models’ training cut-off: retrieval won consistently; unsupervised fine-tuning on the same documents helped only a little, and helped more when each fact was shown in many paraphrases, which is exactly the repetition that pre-training provides and a small fine-tune cannot. Kandpal et al. (2023) showed that even in pre-training, a model’s ability to answer a factual question tracks how many documents mentioned that fact: rare facts are not learned well. The lesson is not that fine-tuning cannot add knowledge; it is that it is an expensive, lossy way to do it, and the loss is silent.

    Three further properties push knowledge toward retrieval. Freshness: when a fact changes, re-indexing one document takes minutes, while a fine-tuned model has to be retrained, and the stale fact may survive in the weights alongside the new one. Attribution: a retrieved passage can be shown to the user as the evidence, and a claim that no passage supports can be refused (grounded answering); a fine-tuned model’s facts have no provenance. Access control: an index can answer different users from different document sets; weights cannot. The toy model below keeps the bookkeeping honest: facts change at a rate you set, the fine-tuned model is retrained on a schedule, the index is refreshed with a lag, and the curves are the fraction of facts each system would answer correctly. It is a cost-of-staleness model, not a measurement of any real system, and every knob is yours.

Retrieval answers correctly when the right passage is retrieved (recall ρ), the model reads it correctly (c), and the passage is not in the refresh lag of ℓ days. The hybrid uses the retrieved passage when there is one and falls back to the weights otherwise.

    Where fine-tuning wins

    None of this says fine-tuning is the weaker lever; it says it is the wrong lever for facts. It is the right lever for behaviour, and behaviour is most of what makes a product feel finished.

    Format

    Schemas and protocols

    JSON that always validates, tool calls in exactly the syntax the runtime parses, a dialogue policy followed without a 900-token reminder. A few hundred examples make this a reflex.

    Style

    Voice and register

    A brand’s tone, a legal house style, the way a particular support team phrases a refusal. Style is diffuse and hard to specify in words; it is easy to demonstrate in examples.

    Tools

    Tool-use conventions

    Which tool to call for which request, how to read its result, when to stop. Prompted models drift; a fine-tuned small model follows the protocol of the tool-use chapter reliably.

    Latency

    Short prompts, small models

    Every token of instruction is prefill time. Baking the instructions into the weights shortens the prompt; specialising a small model replaces a large one for one job.

    Scale

    High traffic

    At millions of calls, a per-call saving of a fraction of a cent is the whole budget. Section 3’s break-even arrives in weeks.

    Privacy

    In-house models

    With an open-weights base, fine-tuning keeps the data, the model and the traffic on your own machines. Retrieval can too; API prompting cannot.

    Telling a knowledge failure from a behaviour failure

    The first question of the decision tree is the one most often answered wrongly, because a model that lacks a fact and a model that ignores an instruction produce the same thing: a confident wrong answer. A few diagnostic habits separate them.

    • Give it the fact and ask againPaste the relevant passage into the prompt. If the answer becomes right, the model was missing knowledge and retrieval will fix it at scale. If the answer stays wrong, or right but in the wrong shape, the problem is behaviour.
    • Look at the wrongnessPlausible but outdated or specific-sounding fabrications (a product name, a date, a clause number) are knowledge failures. Right content in the wrong format, the wrong tone, an answer where a refusal was due, or a tool called with a mangled argument are behaviour failures.
    • Check whether a human expert could answer from memoryIf the answer depends on something only your organisation knows, or something that happened after the model’s training cut-off, no amount of prompting about style will produce it. If an expert would answer from general knowledge plus the question, the model probably can too, given the right instructions.
    • Count the failures by type on the evaluation setLabel every wrong answer in your held-out set as knowledge, behaviour or both. The proportions decide the route; a set that is 80% knowledge failures wants retrieval even if the remaining 20% are embarrassing.
    Fine-tuning tactics I

    Full fine-tuning, LoRA, adapters and prefixes

    Once fine-tuning is the right lever, the next decision is how much of the model to move. Full fine-tuning updates every weight. For a 7-billion-parameter model trained in mixed precision that means, per parameter, 2 bytes of bf16 weight, 2 bytes of gradient, and 12 bytes of fp32 master weight and Adam moments: about 16 bytes, so roughly 110 GB before a single activation is stored (chapter 4.4c does this arithmetic properly). It also produces a complete new copy of the model for every task, and it is the setting in which catastrophic forgetting is worst, because nothing stops the optimiser from rewriting whatever it likes.

    Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) keeps the base frozen and trains a small number of new parameters. Hu et al. (2022) observed that the weight change during fine-tuning has low intrinsic rank, and proposed LoRA: for a weight matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\), learn the update as a product of two thin matrices, \(\Delta W = BA\) with \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r}\), \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times k}\) and \(r \ll \min(d,k)\). \(B\) starts at zero so the model begins exactly as the base, and the update is scaled by \(\alpha/r\). The trainable count is \(r(d+k)\) per matrix instead of \(dk\); at inference \(BA\) can be added into \(W\) once, so there is no extra latency, or kept separate so that many adapters share one base. Chapter 4.6 derives it; here we count.

    Two older ideas sit beside it. Houlsby et al. (2019) inserted adapters: a small bottleneck network (project down to \(m\) dimensions, nonlinearity, project up, add to the residual stream) after each attention and feed-forward sublayer, about \(2dm + d + m\) parameters each. They reach near full-fine-tuning quality with a few percent of the parameters, at the cost of extra layers that cannot be folded away at inference. Li and Liang (2021) proposed prefix tuning: prepend \(p\) trainable vectors to the keys and values of every layer, so the model attends to a learned “virtual prompt” that never occupies real tokens; about \(2pd\) parameters per layer, and nothing inside the model changes. In practice LoRA has become the default because it is the one whose cost vanishes at serving time; the counter below shows why the choice of rank matters less than the choice of which matrices to adapt.

    \[ W' = W + \frac{\alpha}{r}\,BA, \qquad \#\text{LoRA} = \sum_{\text{layers}}\;\sum_{\text{targets } W_i}\; r\,(d_i^{\text{out}} + d_i^{\text{in}}) \]
    Read it as: the adapted weight is the frozen weight plus a rank-r product, scaled by α/r. The trainable count adds, over every layer and every targeted matrix, r times the sum of that matrix’s two dimensions. For a square d × d projection that is 2rd; for the feed-forward matrices it is r(d + dff), and with grouped-query attention the key and value projections are only d × dkv.

      \( \frac{4.19\,\text{M}}{6.74\,\text{B}} \approx 0.062\% \)
      how exactly?
      A 7B-class model has about 6.74 × 109 parameters (embeddings, 32 blocks of attention and feed-forward, the output head). 4,194,304 / 6,738,000,000 = 0.00062 = 0.062%. In bf16 the adapter is 4,194,304 × 2 bytes = 8.4 MB; the model is 13.5 GB.
    4. 4 · What the optimiser must hold
      \( \text{full: } 6.74\,\text{B} \times 12\,\text{B} \approx 81\,\text{GB}; \qquad \text{LoRA: } 4.19\,\text{M} \times 12\,\text{B} \approx 50\,\text{MB} \)
      how exactly?
      Adam keeps an fp32 master copy and two fp32 moments per trainable parameter: 12 bytes. Full fine-tuning: 6.74 × 109 × 12 = 80.9 × 109 bytes ≈ 81 GB, before weights, gradients and activations. LoRA: 4.19 × 106 × 12 ≈ 50 MB. The frozen weights (13.5 GB in bf16, or about 3.5 GB quantised to 4 bits as in QLoRA) and the activations now dominate, which is why a 7B LoRA fits on one consumer GPU and a full fine-tune does not.

    Which matrices, which rank

    The counter makes one thing visible: the number of trainable parameters is dominated by which matrices are adapted, not by the rank. Doubling the rank doubles the count; adding the three feed-forward matrices, each roughly three times as wide as an attention projection, multiplies it by several. Hu et al. (2022) reported that, for a fixed parameter budget, adapting more matrices at a lower rank beat adapting one matrix at a higher rank, and that very small ranks were already competitive on their tasks, which is the observation behind the default of r = 8 on the attention projections. Later practice has moved toward adapting the feed-forward matrices too when the task is far from the base model’s training distribution, and toward QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023), which keeps the frozen base in 4-bit and trains the adapter in higher precision, so that a 65B model fits a single 48 GB card.

    • TargetsStart with the attention projections q and v (or all four of q, k, v, o). Add gate, up and down when the task needs new computation rather than new routing: unfamiliar formats, a new language, dense domain vocabulary.
    • Rank8 to 16 for format and style tasks; 32 to 64 when adapting the feed-forward matrices for a distant domain. Beyond that the adapter is approaching a small full fine-tune in behaviour, forgetting included.
    • Scale αThe update is scaled by α/r, so with α fixed, raising r shrinks each entry’s effect; a common habit is α = 2r so that the effective scale stays at 2 as you sweep r.
    • Dropout and weight decaySmall data over-fits quickly; a little dropout on the adapter input and weight decay on A and B are cheap insurance. Neither touches the frozen base.
    • Merge or notMerging BA into W removes the extra matmul at serving time but ties the deployment to one adapter. Keeping adapters separate lets one base serve many tasks, swapping adapters per request; the cost is a few percent of latency.
    Fine-tuning tactics II

    Small data, learning rates, replay and templates

    Most production fine-tunes are small: a few hundred to a few thousand examples, one to three passes. That regime has its own rules. The learning rate is far below pre-training’s: practitioners typically use something in the region of 10−5 for full fine-tuning and 10−4 for LoRA (whose parameters start at zero and can take larger steps), with a short warm-up and a decay to zero. Too high and the model forgets faster than it learns; too low and three epochs change nothing. The number of epochs trades the same way: with 500 examples the training loss keeps falling for ten epochs, but the held-out loss turns upward after two or three, and after that the model is memorising your examples, including their mistakes. Hold out a slice before you start, evaluate every epoch, and stop at the minimum.

    The quieter risk is catastrophic forgetting (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017): gradient steps toward the new task overwrite weights that old abilities depended on, and nothing in the loss on the new data registers the damage. The cheapest defence is replay, also called data mixing: keep a slice of general data (instruction-following examples, or the base model’s own outputs on general prompts) in every batch. Ten to thirty percent is usually enough to hold the old ability, because the shared weights are now being pulled toward a point that serves both tasks rather than toward the new one alone. Regularising toward the base weights (the idea behind elastic weight consolidation) and training fewer parameters work in the same direction; Biderman et al. (2024) found that LoRA, besides learning somewhat less than full fine-tuning, also forgets markedly less. The experiment below is the whole story in a 16-unit network.     Instruction formats and chat templates

    A chat model is trained on conversations rendered into one token stream by a chat template: special tokens that mark where the system message, each user turn and each assistant turn begin and end. Three practical rules follow. First, train and serve with the identical template: a fine-tune rendered with one delimiter convention and served through another degrades silently, because the model has never seen the tokens it is now being asked to continue. Second, compute the loss only on the assistant’s tokens (the highlighted span below): the model should learn to produce answers, not to predict the user’s questions, and masking the prompt tokens out of the loss keeps a long prompt from dominating a short answer. Third, end every training response with the end-of-turn token; otherwise the fine-tuned model never learns to stop and will run on into an imagined next turn.     Evaluate before, evaluate after

    A fine-tune is a change to a system that already worked somewhere. The evaluation must therefore measure two things: the target task, where you expect to gain, and everything else, where you must not lose. Build both before training and run them on the base model with its best prompt, so that the comparison is fine-tuned model versus the lever it is replacing, not versus nothing. Chapter 4.10 covers the statistics; the checklist is what to measure.

    Target

    Rules of thumb for the small-data regime

    None of these is a law; each is the setting from which a sensible sweep starts, and the direction to move when the held-out curve tells you to.

    • Learning rateFull fine-tuning: 1 to 3 × 10−5. LoRA: 1 to 3 × 10−4. Sweep a factor of three either side; the held-out loss curve is usually U-shaped and the minimum is wide.
    • ScheduleLinear warm-up over the first few percent of steps (the optimiser’s moment estimates are noise at the start), then cosine or linear decay to zero. A learning rate that never decays leaves the model bouncing around the minimum.
    • EpochsOne to three. Evaluate at the end of each; stop when the held-out metric stops improving. With fewer than 500 examples, consider that the second epoch is already a repetition and watch for memorised outputs in the samples.
    • Batch sizeEffective batches of 16 to 64 sequences, with gradient accumulation if memory forces smaller micro-batches. Very small batches add noise that a small dataset cannot average out; very large ones give few update steps per epoch.
    • Replay share10 to 30% of each batch from general instruction data, or from the base model’s own answers to general prompts (self-distillation). More for full fine-tuning than for LoRA; more when the target task is narrow.
    • Data quality over quantityA hundred examples reviewed by someone who knows the task beat a thousand scraped ones. Deduplicate near-copies, remove examples that contradict each other, and make sure the hard cases are represented: the model learns the distribution it is shown, mistakes included.
    • Sequence lengthTruncate to what the task needs plus a margin; padding is wasted compute and long, mostly-prompt sequences dilute the assistant tokens the loss is computed on.
    Model merging & multi-task fine-tuning

    Model merging: soups, task vectors, TIES and DARE

    Suppose you have fine-tuned one base model twice: once for task A, once for task B. The classical route to a model that does both is multi-task fine-tuning, training once on a mixture of both datasets, and it works, but it means re-collecting the data, re-running the training, and redoing it for every new combination. Model merging asks a cheekier question: can we combine the two sets of weights directly, with no training and no data? For models trained from scratch the answer is no; two independently trained networks live in different basins of the loss landscape, with hidden units in different orders, and averaging them gives nonsense. For models fine-tuned from the same base the answer is often yes. Fine-tuning moves the weights only a little, and the fine-tuned models stay in one connected low-loss region around the base, so that straight lines between them stay low-loss too.

    Wortsman et al. (2022) made this a method: a model soup averages the weights of many fine-tunes of one base (different learning rates, seeds, augmentations) and gets a model that is more accurate and more robust than the best single run, at no extra inference cost, where an ensemble would cost one forward pass per member. Their greedy soup adds models one at a time and keeps each only if the held-out accuracy improves. Ilharco et al. (2023) then named the object that makes merging compositional: the task vector \(\tau = \theta_{\text{ft}} - \theta_0\), the direction fine-tuning moved the weights. Task vectors can be added to get a multi-task model, negated to remove an ability (subtract the toxic-text task vector and the model generates less of it), and combined by analogy. A scaling coefficient \(\lambda\), tuned on a little held-out data, sets how far along the summed direction to go; averaging two models is the special case \(\lambda = \tfrac12\).

    Adding task vectors interferes in two ways, and the two refinements most used in practice each attack one. Yadav et al. (2023) observed that most entries of a task vector are small noise that nevertheless adds up across models, and that where two task vectors disagree in sign the sum cancels or flips what each wanted. TIES-merging therefore trims each task vector to its largest-magnitude entries (keeping, say, the top 20%), elects a sign per parameter from the trimmed vectors, and takes the disjoint mean of only those entries that agree with the elected sign. Yu et al. (2024) showed that fine-tuning deltas of large models are so redundant that one can drop 90% or more of the entries at random and rescale the survivors by \(1/(1-p)\) with almost no loss (DARE); sparsified task vectors then collide far less when summed. A different family interpolates along the sphere rather than the line: SLERP (Shoemake, 1985) keeps the norm of the interpolated weights where linear interpolation would shrink it, and is popular for merging two models with large, non-parallel differences.

    \[ \theta_{\text{soup}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\theta_i, \qquad \tau_i = \theta_i - \theta_0, \qquad \theta_{\text{merge}} = \theta_0 + \lambda\sum_i \tau_i \]
    Read it as: a soup is the plain average of M fine-tuned weight vectors. A task vector is a fine-tuned model minus the base it came from. Task arithmetic adds the task vectors back onto the base with a scale λ; for two models, λ = ½ gives exactly the average, λ = 1 gives the full sum, and λ < 0 on a single task vector unlearns it.
    Say it“Base plus lambda times the sum of the differences.”
    \[ \hat\tau_i = \operatorname{trim}_k(\tau_i), \qquad \gamma = \operatorname{sgn}\Big(\sum_i \hat\tau_i\Big), \qquad \tau^{\text{TIES}}_j = \frac{\sum_{i:\,\operatorname{sgn}\hat\tau_{ij} = \gamma_j}\hat\tau_{ij}}{\big|\{i: \operatorname{sgn}\hat\tau_{ij} = \gamma_j\}\big|} \]
    Read it as: trim each task vector to its top-k% entries by magnitude (the rest become zero); elect, for each parameter j, the sign of the summed trimmed values; then average, parameter by parameter, only the entries whose sign agrees with the elected one. The result goes onto the base with a λ like any task vector.
    Say it“Trim, elect a sign, average the ones that agree.”
    \[ \tilde\tau_j = \frac{m_j\,\tau_j}{1-p},\; m_j \sim \text{Bernoulli}(1-p) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathbb{E}[\tilde\tau] = \tau; \qquad \text{slerp}(\theta_a,\theta_b;t) = \frac{\sin((1-t)\Omega)}{\sin\Omega}\,\theta_a + \frac{\sin(t\Omega)}{\sin\Omega}\,\theta_b \]
    Read it as: DARE keeps each entry with probability 1 − p and divides the survivors by 1 − p, so the sparse vector is unbiased for the dense one; the rescaling is what makes dropping 90% survivable. SLERP interpolates along the great circle between two weight vectors, where Ω is the angle between them; at t = ½ it is the normalised average scaled back up to the original norm, so the merged weights do not shrink the way a straight-line average does when the two vectors point in different directions.
    Say it“Drop at random and rescale; or walk along the arc instead of the chord.”

    Merging or multi-task fine-tuning?

    Merging competes with the older, surer route to a model that does several things: multi-task fine-tuning, one training run on the union of the task datasets, usually with the tasks balanced by sampling so that a large dataset does not drown a small one. Multi-task training lets the optimiser negotiate the shared weights directly, so it generally reaches higher accuracy on all tasks than any merge of separately trained models, and it is the right choice when the datasets are available together and a single retraining per release is acceptable. Merging wins on logistics: it needs no data at merge time (only the fine-tuned weights, which may come from different teams or from the open-weights community), it costs seconds instead of GPU-hours, it composes after the fact (add a task, remove a task, try a λ), and it is how one serving model can absorb adapters that were trained independently. The lab in the project shows the price: the merged model sits a few points below the specialists, and how many points depends on how much the task vectors overlap.

    A merging recipe

    1. Check the lineage. All models must descend from the same base checkpoint with the same tokenizer; otherwise the weights are not comparable and the merge is noise.
    2. Compute task vectors against the base and look at their norms and pairwise cosines. Near-orthogonal vectors (cosine near zero) merge well; strongly aligned or opposed ones will interfere.
    3. Start with the average (λ = 1/M) and evaluate on every task’s held-out set, plus the general-ability suite. This is the baseline every refinement has to beat.
    4. Sweep λ over a small grid (0.3 to 1.3 for two tasks); then try TIES with a 20% trim and DARE with a 90% drop at the best λ. Keep whichever has the best worst-task accuracy, not the best mean: a merge that sacrifices one task is a specialist with extra steps.
    5. Re-run the regression suite on the chosen merge. Merging can also unlearn safety behaviour if one ingredient was fine-tuned without it; the negation demo shows how easily a direction is removed.
    6. Record the recipe (ingredients, λ, trim, seed) with the merged weights. A merge is reproducible arithmetic; keep it so.
    Putting it together

    The hybrid system, and how to evaluate the whole thing

    The levers are not exclusive, and the pattern that most mature systems settle into uses two of them for the two different jobs: fine-tune for form, retrieve for facts. The retriever supplies the passages, which keeps knowledge fresh, cited and access-controlled. The fine-tuned model supplies the behaviour: it has learned, from a few hundred examples, how to read retrieved passages, how to answer in the house format with a citation field, and, importantly, how to say that the passages do not contain the answer. That last skill is the one prompting struggles hardest to hold in place and the one that most needs to be reliable. A prompt still exists, but it is short: the question and the passages, not three pages of instructions.

    A system with two components has to be evaluated as a system and as components, because an end-to-end number cannot tell you which part to fix. Decompose the end-to-end accuracy: a query is answered correctly if the output is well-formed (probability f), and either the right passage was retrieved (recall \(\rho\)) and read correctly (\(c_1\)), or it was not retrieved and the model’s own knowledge happened to suffice (\(c_0\)). Measuring the four factors separately, on the same evaluation set, tells you the marginal value of improving each: another five points of retriever recall is worth \(f\,(c_1 - c_0)\) × 0.05 end to end, another five points of reading accuracy is worth \(f\rho\) × 0.05. Set the four factors to what your measurements say and the flow shows where the next engineering week should go.

    \[ P(\text{correct}) = f\,\big[\rho\,c_1 + (1-\rho)\,c_0\big], \qquad \frac{\partial P}{\partial \rho} = f\,(c_1 - c_0), \quad \frac{\partial P}{\partial c_1} = f\rho, \quad \frac{\partial P}{\partial f} = \rho c_1 + (1-\rho)c_0 \]
    Read it as: end-to-end accuracy is the format-validity rate times the mixture of two conditional accuracies, weighted by how often the retriever finds the right passage. The three partial derivatives are the end-to-end gain per unit improvement of each component; multiply by the improvement you think you can buy.

    What to measure in a hybrid system

    Retriever

    Recall@k and MRR

    Does the gold passage appear among the k retrieved, and how high? Measured on questions with known source passages; see chapter 4.9.

    Reader

    Faithfulness

    Share of answer claims supported by the retrieved passages. An answer can be correct and unfaithful (the model knew it anyway) or faithful and wrong (the passage was wrong); both matter.

    Reader

    Abstention

    On questions whose answer is not in the corpus, does the model say so? A fine-tune for form should raise this; measure it on a deliberately unanswerable set.

    Output

    Format validity and citation accuracy

    Parses; cites; and the cited passage is the one the claim came from. Cheap to compute automatically on every call in production.

    End to end

    Answer correctness

    Against gold answers, by exact match, by a rubric, or by a judge model whose agreement with humans you have checked (chapter 4.10).

    Operations

    Latency, cost, drift

    p50 and p95 latency including the retrieval hop; dollars per thousand calls; and the same evaluation re-run whenever the index, the prompt, the adapter or the base model changes.

    Two levers you will rarely pull

    Retraining the base model

    • Pre-training from scratch costs millions of dollars and trillions of tokens (chapter 4.4), and produces a model that then needs all of the alignment and fine-tuning work again. For an application team the answer is almost always no.
    • The exception is a large in-house corpus in a language, modality or domain the base barely saw. Even then the move is continued pre-training of an open-weights model on that corpus, with replay of general text, not training from zero.
    • If the base model’s general ability is the problem, the cheaper fix is almost always a bigger or newer base, with your adapter retrained on top.

    Distilling into a small model

    • Once the hybrid system works, it becomes a teacher: run it over many inputs, keep the outputs that pass your checks, and fine-tune a small open model on them (chapter 4.7a).
    • The student inherits the format and much of the behaviour at a fraction of the latency and cost; it does not inherit the teacher’s knowledge, so it keeps the retriever.
    • Evaluate the student on the same suite as the teacher; a distilled model that is 95% as good at a tenth of the price is section 3’s break-even again, with a larger saving per call.

    Before the first real user

    1. Freeze the evaluation. Held-out task set, retrieval set with gold passages, unanswerable set, general-ability regression set, format checks. Numbers for the prompted baseline on all of them.
    2. Measure the four factors of the decomposition above on the same set, so that the end-to-end number has an explanation and the next week’s work has a target.
    3. Shadow traffic. Run the new system beside the old on live inputs without showing its answers; compare agreement, latency p95 and cost per call from real token counts rather than estimates.
    4. Staged rollout with a kill switch. A few percent of users first; the adapter, the index version and the prompt version all logged with every answer so that a regression can be traced to its cause.
    5. Watch the inputs, not only the outputs. Distribution shift in what users ask is the most common reason a system that passed its evaluation fails in the field; sample new inputs into the evaluation set every month.
    6. Schedule the re-decision. Base models change, traffic changes, prices change. Put a date on re-running this chapter’s workbench with the measured numbers.

        B · The merging lab

        The data: points x drawn uniformly from the cube [−1, 1]4. Task A asks whether x1 and x2 have the same sign (an exclusive-or, which a linear model cannot do). Task B asks whether (x3, x4) lies outside the circle of area 2 (radius √(2/π) ≈ 0.80, so the classes are balanced). The model is a 4 → 16 → 4 tanh network; task A is scored on logits 0 and 1, task B on logits 2 and 3, so each task has its own head and the two share the body. The base is pre-trained by alternating minibatches of both tasks for a few hundred steps, which leaves it mediocre at both, as a foundation model is at any particular job. Each specialist then fine-tunes the whole network from the base on its own task alone. Then we merge. Press run; the defaults reproduce the Python listing below.

        # merge_lab.py -- two specialists from one base, merged four ways (numpy only)
import math
import numpy as np

def mulberry32(a):                      # same generator as the page, bit for bit
    def nxt():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return nxt

rng = mulberry32(7)
H, N_TRAIN, N_TEST = 16, 600, 2000
PRE_STEPS, PRE_LR, FT_STEPS, FT_LR, BATCH = 400, 0.3, 1200, 0.2, 32

def uniform(n, d):                      # points in the cube [-1, 1]^d
    return np.array([[rng() * 2 - 1 for _ in range(d)] for _ in range(n)])

def label(X, task):                     # A: same sign of x1 and x2;  B: (x3, x4) outside the circle of area 2
    if task == 0:
        return (X[:, 0] * X[:, 1] > 0).astype(int)
    return (X[:, 2] ** 2 + X[:, 3] ** 2 > 2 / math.pi).astype(int)

XA, XB = uniform(N_TRAIN, 4), uniform(N_TRAIN, 4)
yA, yB = label(XA, 0), label(XB, 1)
XAt, XBt = uniform(N_TEST, 4), uniform(N_TEST, 4)
yAt, yBt = label(XAt, 0), label(XBt, 1)

def init():                             # 4 -> H -> 4 logits; task A reads logits 0-1, task B logits 2-3
    W1 = np.array([[(rng() * 2 - 1) * math.sqrt(3 / 4) for _ in range(H)] for _ in range(4)])
    W2 = np.array([[(rng() * 2 - 1) * math.sqrt(3 / H) for _ in range(4)] for _ in range(H)])
    return [W1, np.zeros(H), W2, np.zeros(4)]

def forward(th, X):
    W1, b1, W2, b2 = th
    Hh = np.tanh(X @ W1 + b1)
    return Hh, Hh @ W2 + b2

def accuracy(th, X, y, task):
    Z = forward(th, X)[1][:, 2 * task:2 * task + 2]
    return float(np.mean(Z.argmax(1) == y))

def sgd_step(th, X, y, task, lr):       # one minibatch of softmax cross-entropy on the task's two logits
    W1, b1, W2, b2 = th
    Hh, Z = forward(th, X)
    Zt = Z[:, 2 * task:2 * task + 2]
    Zt = Zt - Zt.max(1, keepdims=True)
    P = np.exp(Zt); P /= P.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    P[np.arange(len(y)), y] -= 1; P /= len(y)          # dL/dlogits
    G = np.zeros_like(Z); G[:, 2 * task:2 * task + 2] = P
    dH = (G @ W2.T) * (1 - Hh ** 2)
    return [W1 - lr * (X.T @ dH), b1 - lr * dH.sum(0), W2 - lr * (Hh.T @ G), b2 - lr * G.sum(0)]

def batch(n):
    return [int(rng() * n) for _ in range(BATCH)]

def train(th, X, y, task, steps, lr):
    th = [p.copy() for p in th]
    for _ in range(steps):
        idx = batch(len(y))
        th = sgd_step(th, X[idx], y[idx], task, lr)
    return th

# 1. the base: a short mixed pre-training on both tasks, alternating minibatches
base = init()
for _ in range(PRE_STEPS):
    idx = batch(N_TRAIN); base = sgd_step(base, XA[idx], yA[idx], 0, PRE_LR)
    idx = batch(N_TRAIN); base = sgd_step(base, XB[idx], yB[idx], 1, PRE_LR)

# 2. two specialists, each fine-tuned from the same base on one task
thA = train(base, XA, yA, 0, FT_STEPS, FT_LR)
thB = train(base, XB, yB, 1, FT_STEPS, FT_LR)

flat = lambda th: np.concatenate([p.ravel() for p in th])
def unflat(v):
    out, i = [], 0
    for p in base:
        out.append(v[i:i + p.size].reshape(p.shape)); i += p.size
    return out
tauA, tauB = flat(thA) - flat(base), flat(thB) - flat(base)      # task vectors

def report(name, th):
    print('%-32s A %.3f   B %.3f' % (name, accuracy(th, XAt, yAt, 0), accuracy(th, XBt, yBt, 1)))

print('%d weights per model; |tauA| = %.3f, |tauB| = %.3f, cosine = %.3f'
      % (tauA.size, np.linalg.norm(tauA), np.linalg.norm(tauB),
         tauA @ tauB / (np.linalg.norm(tauA) * np.linalg.norm(tauB))))
report('base (mixed pre-training)', base)
report('specialist A', thA)
report('specialist B', thB)

# 3. merges
report('weight average (soup)', unflat((flat(thA) + flat(thB)) / 2))
for lam in (0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.25):
    report('task arithmetic, lambda = %.2f' % lam, unflat(flat(base) + lam * (tauA + tauB)))

def ties(keep, lam):                    # trim, elect sign, disjoint mean
    def trim(t):
        k = int(round(keep * t.size))
        cut = np.sort(np.abs(t))[::-1][k - 1]
        return np.where(np.abs(t) >= cut, t, 0.0)
    tA, tB = trim(tauA), trim(tauB)
    sign = np.sign(tA + tB)                                  # elected sign per weight
    agreeA, agreeB = (np.sign(tA) == sign) & (tA != 0), (np.sign(tB) == sign) & (tB != 0)
    total = np.where(agreeA, tA, 0) + np.where(agreeB, tB, 0)
    count = agreeA.astype(int) + agreeB.astype(int)
    merged = np.where(count > 0, total / np.maximum(count, 1), 0.0)
    return unflat(flat(base) + lam * merged)
for keep in (0.2, 0.5):
    report('TIES, keep %d%%, lambda = 1' % round(keep * 100), ties(keep, 1.0))

def dare(p, lam):                       # drop a share p of each task vector at random, rescale the rest
    def sparsify(t):
        mask = np.array([rng() >= p for _ in range(t.size)])
        return np.where(mask, t / (1 - p), 0.0)
    return unflat(flat(base) + lam * (sparsify(tauA) + sparsify(tauB)))
report('DARE, drop 50%, lambda = 1', dare(0.5, 1.0))
report('DARE, drop 90%, lambda = 1', dare(0.9, 1.0))

# 4. negation: subtract a task vector to unlearn
report('base - 1.0 * tauA (forget A)', unflat(flat(base) - tauA))

        Wrap-up

        Five lines to remember

        • Three levers, three places: prompting changes the context, retrieval changes the context from an index, fine-tuning changes the weights. Ask where the change should live before asking which tool to use.
        • Prompt first, always. Retrieve when the failures are about facts, especially facts that change or must be cited. Fine-tune when the failures are about form, style or protocol, prompting has plateaued, and you can produce hundreds of clean examples and an evaluation.
        • Costs have shapes: prompting is all slope, retrieval a small intercept and a medium slope, fine-tuning a large intercept and the smallest slope. Break-even calls = fixed cost / saving per call; traffic decides.
        • Fine-tune with care: LoRA on a few projections trains a fraction of a percent of the weights; use small learning rates and few epochs on small data; mix in 10–30% general data against forgetting; train and serve with one chat template; evaluate the target and a regression suite before and after.
        • Fine-tunes of one base can be merged: average them (soups), add their task vectors with a scale λ (negate to forget), trim and sign-elect to cut interference (TIES), or drop-and-rescale (DARE). It is free multi-tasking when the task vectors interfere little, and a measured compromise when they do not.

        Check your understanding

        A support bot answers from a product manual that is revised monthly. The team proposes fine-tuning on the manual. What do you say?

        Retrieve instead. The knowledge changes, so a fine-tune is stale within a month and must be retrained; fine-tuning stores facts unreliably from single exposures; and the answers should cite the manual section, which only retrieval can do. If the bot’s answers are badly formatted or too chatty, that is a form problem, and a small fine-tune on a few hundred example answers (with retrieved passages in the prompt) is the right second step.

        Your few-shot prompt is 3,000 tokens; the fine-tuned alternative would use 300 at 1.5× the price. Output is 200 tokens either way at $10/M; input is $2/M. Training costs $800 all in. How many calls to break even?

        Prompt call: 3,000 × 2 × 10−6 + 200 × 10−5 = 0.006 + 0.002 = $0.008. Fine-tuned call: 1.5 × (300 × 2 × 10−6 + 0.002) = 1.5 × 0.0026 = $0.0039. Saving $0.0041 per call; 800 / 0.0041 ≈ 195,000 calls. At 10,000 calls a month that is 19.5 months; at 500,000 a month, under two weeks.

        Why does LoRA start with B = 0 rather than both A and B random?

        With B = 0 the update BA is zero at the start, so the adapted model is exactly the base model and training begins from a known-good point; a random BA would perturb every adapted layer before the first gradient step. A is random so that the product has a direction to grow along; if both were zero the gradient with respect to each would be zero and nothing would ever move.

        Rank 16 on q, k, v and o of a 7B model (32 layers, d = 4,096, k and v also 4,096 wide). How many trainable parameters?

        Each matrix: 16 × (4,096 + 4,096) = 131,072. Four matrices: 524,288 per layer. Times 32: 16,777,216, about 16.8 M, which is 0.25% of 6.74 B. Doubling the rank doubles the count; adding targets multiplies it. The feed-forward matrices would add far more, because dff is almost three times d.

        In the forgetting lab, why does 10% replay recover almost all of task A when 0% loses so much of it?

        With 0% replay the gradient only ever says “improve task B”, and any change to the shared hidden units that helps B is accepted regardless of what it does to A. With 10% of each batch from A, a step that damages A raises the loss on those examples, and the optimiser is pulled toward the region that serves both. The A examples do not need to be many; they need to be present at every step so that the damage is registered as it happens.

        Two task vectors have entries +0.6 and −0.5 at the same weight. What do plain task arithmetic and TIES do with it, and why does it matter?

        Task arithmetic adds them: +0.1, which is almost nothing, so neither task gets what it wanted at that weight. TIES elects the sign of the sum (+), keeps only the entries that agree (+0.6), and uses 0.6: task A gets its full move and task B’s opposite wish is dropped at this weight rather than half-cancelling both. Sign disagreement is one of the two interference mechanisms TIES was designed around; the other is the accumulation of many small entries, handled by trimming.

        Why did DARE with a 50% drop wreck the 148-weight toy model when the paper drops 90% of a 7B model’s delta without harm?

        DARE relies on redundancy: in a billion-dimensional task vector, the function the delta computes is spread over so many entries that a random half of them, rescaled, still points in nearly the same direction, and the rescaling keeps the expectation right. In 148 weights there is no such redundancy; dropping half the entries of a small network’s update removes specific features it needs, and rescaling the rest by two overshoots on others. The expectation argument is exact but the variance is enormous relative to the signal. Unbiased is not the same as harmless.

        You have adapters for three tasks trained by three teams from the same base, and you need one serving model by Friday. Merge or multi-task fine-tune?

        Merge, this week: compute the three task vectors, check their cosines, average them, sweep λ and try TIES, and evaluate every task on its held-out set plus the regression suite; this is minutes of arithmetic and an afternoon of evaluation. Schedule a multi-task fine-tune on the union of the three datasets for the next release if the merged model is more than a few points below the specialists on any task; it needs the data in one place and a training run, but the optimiser will negotiate the shared weights better than any merge.
