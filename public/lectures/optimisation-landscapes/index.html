Why Deep Networks Are Hard to Optimise

Learning is not quite optimisation, and the landscape fights back

Training a network means walking downhill on a surface with millions of dimensions that you can only feel under your feet. Two things make that hard. First, the surface is a stand-in: we want low error on data we have never seen, but we can only measure error on data we have. Second, the surface is rough: long narrow valleys, flat plateaus, saddles, cliffs, and gradients that are only noisy estimates. This chapter takes each obstacle in turn, then measures them on a real convolutional network. The figure shows a small landscape. Click or tap anywhere on it to drop a handful of balls and watch where they settle.

The goal

Learning is not pure optimisation

In pure optimisation the function you minimise is the goal. In machine learning it isn't. What we care about is the risk: the average loss over the whole data-generating distribution, including every example we will meet in future. We cannot compute that, because we don't know the distribution. So we minimise the empirical risk instead: the average loss over the training set, and hope the two are close. This swap is the root of everything in this chapter. The training loss is a proxy, so its exact minimum is not the prize, and an algorithm that stops early may be doing the right thing.

Below, two classes on a line: class 1 is centred at +1, class 0 at −1, both with spread 1, and 40% of points are class 1. The classifier says “class 1” whenever x > θ. Because we chose the distribution, we can compute the true risk exactly (the smooth curve) and compare it with the empirical risk on a sample (the staircase). Slide the sample size and resample a few times.

\[ J^*(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim p_{\text{data}}}\, L\big(f(x;\theta),\,y\big) \]
Read it as: the true risk of the parameters θ is the loss you would suffer on average over every example the world could produce, weighted by how often it produces it. This is the quantity we actually want to be small.
Say it“J-star of theta is the expected loss over pairs x, y drawn from the data distribution.”
\[ \hat J(\theta) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} L\big(f(x_i;\theta),\,y_i\big) \]
Read it as: replace the unknown distribution by the m examples you have, each counted once. Minimising this is empirical risk minimisation. With large m it tracks J* well; with small m its minimum can sit far from J*'s.
Say it“J-hat of theta is one over m times the sum of the losses on the m training examples.”

A loss we can descend

Surrogate losses and early stopping

Look again at the staircase above. The 0–1 loss (count the mistakes) is flat almost everywhere and jumps at each data point. Its gradient is zero wherever it exists, so gradient descent cannot use it, and minimising it exactly is intractable even for a linear classifier. So we optimise a surrogate loss instead: a smooth function that upper-bounds the one we care about. The favourite is the negative log-likelihood of the correct class, the logistic loss. Here the model is f(x) = w(x − θ) and the label is coded y = ±1. Press Descend to run gradient descent on the surrogate, then switch to separable data.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell(z) &= \log_2\!\big(1 + e^{-z}\big) \\ &\ge \mathbf{1}[\,z \le 0\,], \quad z = y\,f(x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: z is the score of the correct class: positive when the point is on its correct side, larger the more confidently so. The logistic loss, in bits, equals 1 at z = 0 and lies above the 0–1 loss everywhere, so pushing it down pushes the error count down too. Unlike the 0–1 loss, it keeps falling as z grows, so it keeps rewarding a point for moving further from the boundary.
Say it“ell of z is log base two of one plus e to the minus z, which is at least the indicator that z is at most zero.”

The second big difference from pure optimisation is when to stop. A pure optimiser stops when the gradient is (nearly) zero. A learning algorithm usually stops when the error on held-out validation data stops improving, because that is the best estimate of the true risk. That moment often comes while the training loss is still falling and its gradient is still far from zero. Below, a flexible model (a logistic regression on 20 bumps) learns from 30 noisy points and is checked on 300 fresh ones. Press Train, then change the patience.

\[ \begin{aligned} \tau^\star &= \arg\min_{\tau}\, \hat J_{\text{val}}\big(\theta^{(\tau)}\big) \\ \text{stop once } &\tau - \tau^\star \ge p \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: after every step τ, measure the loss on the validation set. Remember the step τ* with the lowest value so far. If p further steps pass without beating it, stop and return θ(τ*). The training gradient plays no part in the rule.
Say it“Tau-star is the step with the smallest validation loss; stop when you are p steps past it.”

The landscape

Parameter optimisation on the error surface

Think of the error E(w) as a surface over weight space, with one axis per weight. Small steps in w change E by roughly δw · ∇E, so the gradient points straight uphill and we move against it. Wherever the surface is flat, ∇E = 0: a stationary point. It can be a minimum, a maximum or a saddle, and the Hessian (the matrix of second derivatives) tells them apart. A surface can hold many minima; the lowest is the global minimum, the rest are local minima. There is no formula for the bottom, so every method is iterative: start somewhere, update, repeat. Click anywhere on the map to start gradient descent there, and turn on the basins.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\nabla E(\hat{\mathbf{w}}) = \mathbf{0} \\ &E(\mathbf{w}) \approx E(\hat{\mathbf{w}}) + \tfrac12\boldsymbol\Delta^{\!\top}\mathbf{H}\,\boldsymbol\Delta \\ &\boldsymbol\Delta = \mathbf{w}-\hat{\mathbf{w}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: at a stationary point ŵ the slope vanishes in every direction, so near it the surface is a quadratic bowl, dome or saddle in the displacement Δ = w − ŵ, shaped by the Hessian H. All eigenvalues of H positive: a minimum. All negative: a maximum. Mixed signs: a saddle.
Say it“The gradient of E is zero; near w-hat, E is E of w-hat plus a half delta-transpose H delta, where delta is w minus w-hat.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{w}^{(\tau+1)} &= \mathbf{w}^{(\tau)} + \Delta\mathbf{w}^{(\tau)} \\ \Delta\mathbf{w}^{(\tau)} &= -\eta\,\nabla E\big(\mathbf{w}^{(\tau)}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: every training algorithm is a recipe for the update Δw. Gradient descent uses the simplest one: a small step of size η straight downhill. Where you end up depends on where you start, because each minimum drains its own basin.
Say it“w at step tau plus one is w at step tau plus delta w, and delta w is minus eta times the gradient.”

Obstacle 1

Ill-conditioning: one step too far

Expand the loss to second order around the current point and take a gradient step of size ε. The first-order term promises a decrease of εg⊤g. The second-order term charges back ½ε²g⊤Hg for the curvature along the gradient. When the Hessian is ill-conditioned, its largest curvature is far bigger than its smallest, and the charge can exceed the promise: the step goes uphill unless ε is shrunk, and then progress along the flat directions crawls. In networks this shows up as a gradient norm that refuses to shrink while the loss falls only slowly. Raise the condition number, then drag the start point and the step size.

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\boldsymbol\theta - \epsilon\mathbf{g}) \approx f(\boldsymbol\theta) &- \epsilon\,\mathbf{g}^{\!\top}\mathbf{g} \\ &+ \tfrac12\,\epsilon^2\,\mathbf{g}^{\!\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{g} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the step lowers f only while ½ε²g⊤Hg < εg⊤g, that is ε < 2g⊤g / g⊤Hg. The best single step is half of that. For this quadratic bowl the expansion is exact, so the prediction and the real change agree to the last digit. The condition number κ = λmax/λmin measures how lopsided the bowl is.
Say it“f at theta minus epsilon g is about f at theta, minus epsilon g-transpose-g, plus a half epsilon squared g-transpose-H-g.”

Obstacle 2

Local minima, and why most of them are harmless

A convex problem has no local minima that aren't global. Neural networks are not convex, and they are guaranteed to have huge numbers of local minima for a simple reason: they are not identifiable. Swap two hidden units, along with their incoming and outgoing weights, and the network computes exactly the same function. With tanh units you can also flip the signs of a unit's incoming and outgoing weights together, since tanh(−a) = −tanh(a). Every minimum therefore comes in a family of identical copies. Those copies all have the same loss, so they do no harm.

The worrying minima are the ones with high loss. Below, a network with one input, M tanh units and one output is fitted by full-batch gradient descent with momentum to 21 points, from 40 random starts. Run the restarts for M = 2, then for M = 4.

\[ N_{\text{equivalent}} = M!\cdot 2^{M} \]
Read it as: M! ways to order the M hidden units, times 2 sign choices for each unit. For one hidden layer of 10 units that is already 3,715,891,200 copies of every minimum; deep networks multiply this layer by layer.
Say it“The number of equivalent weight vectors, N-equivalent, is M factorial times two to the M.”

Obstacle 3

Saddle points, plateaus and flat regions

At a saddle point the gradient is zero, but the surface curves up in some directions and down in others. Gradient descent is pushed away from a saddle along the downward directions, yet the push is proportional to the distance from it: start very close and you spend a long time on an almost flat plateau before escaping. Newton's method is worse: it jumps to the nearest point where the gradient vanishes, and a saddle qualifies. The surface below has a saddle at the origin and two minima at y = ±1. Shrink the offset δ and compare the three methods.

\[ \begin{aligned} &f(\boldsymbol\theta^\star + \boldsymbol\Delta) \\ &\approx f(\boldsymbol\theta^\star) + \tfrac12\sum_i \lambda_i\,(\mathbf{v}_i^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\Delta)^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: near a stationary point, write the displacement in the Hessian's eigenvectors vi. Each direction with λi > 0 curves up, each with λi < 0 curves down. Gradient descent multiplies the i-th coordinate by (1 − ηλi) per step: shrinking it when λi > 0, growing it when λi < 0.
Say it“f near theta-star is f at theta-star plus a half the sum of lambda-i times the squared component of delta along v-i.”
\[ \begin{aligned} T_{\text{escape}} &\approx \frac{\ln(c/\delta)}{\ln(1 + \eta\,|\lambda_{\min}|)} \\ \boldsymbol\Delta_{\text{Newton}} &= -\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{g} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: starting a distance δ from the saddle along its down direction, gradient descent needs this many steps to grow that coordinate to size c. Halving δ adds a fixed number of steps, so the plateau lengthens without limit as δ → 0. Newton's step solves for where the local quadratic is flat, and goes there whether that is a minimum or a saddle.
Say it“Escape time is about log of c over delta, over log of one plus eta times the size of lambda-min.”

Why should saddles, not minima, dominate in high dimensions? Here is a heuristic. At a stationary point, the Hessian has n eigenvalues. If their signs were independent coin flips, the chance that all n are positive, making a minimum, would be 2−n: vanishing for a network with millions of weights. Random symmetric matrices make the point even more strongly. Studies of random functions (Bray & Dean 2007) and of real networks (Dauphin et al. 2014) add a twist: the eigenvalues drift positive as the loss falls, so the rare minima tend to sit low and high stationary points are mostly saddles. Shift the spectrum to imitate going lower on the surface.

\[ \Pr\big[\lambda_1>0,\dots,\lambda_n>0\big] = 2^{-n} \]
Read it as: the probability that a stationary point is a minimum, under the crude model of independent coin flips that each curvature is up or down at random. The lab compares it with random symmetric matrices A/√(2n) + sI, where s shifts every eigenvalue up by the same amount.
Say it“The probability that all n eigenvalues are positive is two to the minus n.”

Obstacles 4 and 5

Cliffs, and gradients you can't trust

Networks that multiply many weights together, such as deep or recurrent ones, can have cliffs: thin regions where the loss changes enormously over a tiny distance. The gradient on a cliff face is huge, and a normal-sized learning rate turns it into a leap that throws away all the progress so far. The standard cure is gradient clipping: keep the gradient's direction but cap its length. Turn clipping off and on, and slide the start towards the edge.

\[ \mathbf{g} \leftarrow \mathbf{g}\cdot\min\!\Big(1,\ \frac{v}{\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert}\Big) \]
Read it as: if the gradient is longer than the threshold v, rescale it to length v; otherwise leave it alone. The step can then never be longer than ηv, however steep the cliff. The direction is still downhill, so near the cliff the method simply takes careful steps.
Say it“g becomes g times the smaller of one and v over the norm of g.”
longest step
loss after 12 steps

Most of the theory above assumes we know the gradient. We never do. With millions of examples we estimate it from a mini-batch, and the estimate scatters around the truth with a spread that shrinks only like 1/√m: four times the batch buys half the noise. (Some objectives are even worse: their gradients are intractable and can only be approximated.) Below, a logistic regression with weight w and bias b is fitted to 400 points. The thick arrow is the true full-data gradient at the dot; the thin ones are 40 mini-batch estimates. Change the batch size and drag the dot.

\[ \hat{\mathbf{g}} = \frac1m\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}} \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta} L_i, \qquad \mathrm{sd}(\hat{\mathbf{g}}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt m} \]
Read it as: average the per-example gradients over a random batch B of m examples. On average this equals the true gradient, but any one batch is off by roughly σ/√m, where σ is the spread of single-example gradients. Here “sd” is the root-mean-square length of the error vector.
Say it“g-hat is the batch average of the example gradients; its standard deviation is sigma over root m.”

The big picture

Local moves, global trouble, and hard limits

Even if every local feature were benign, there is a deeper problem: gradient descent only sees the ground under its feet. The direction that is best here can lead on a long detour if a mountain stands between the start and the goal, and some losses have no minimum at all, so the walk never ends. Much of what makes training work in practice is choosing a starting point from which a short, well-behaved path exists. Pick a scenario and drag the start.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\hat J(w) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\!\big(1+e^{-w\,s_i}\big) > 0 \\ &\hat J(w)\to 0 \ \text{ as } w\to\infty \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: when every training point has a positive score si (the data are separable), the log-loss can always be lowered by scaling w up, but never reaches zero. There is no minimum to converge to: the weights grow without bound, roughly like the logarithm of the number of steps, while the gradient shrinks towards zero.
Say it“J-hat of w, the average log of one plus e to the minus w s-i, is positive for every w and tends to zero as w goes to infinity.”

Are there theoretical limits? Yes, in the worst case. Blum & Rivest (1992) showed that even for a network of three threshold units, deciding whether some weights classify a given training set perfectly is NP-complete, so no algorithm is known that always solves it in polynomial time. Similar hardness results exist for many other architectures. But these results are about worst cases, about exact solutions, and about small, fixed networks. In practice we want a good enough point, not a provably optimal one, and we are free to make the network larger, which gives it many more settings that reach a low loss. The results warn us not to expect guarantees; they do not say training will fail.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{Given } \{(\mathbf{x}_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n\text{:} \\ &\exists\,\boldsymbol\theta \ \text{ with } \ \hat J_{0\text{-}1}(\boldsymbol\theta) = 0\,? \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the decision problem “is there any setting of the weights that makes zero training mistakes?” For a two-layer network of two hidden threshold units and one output unit, this question is NP-complete in the input dimension.
Say it“Given the training pairs, does there exist a theta whose training 0-1 error is zero?”

3 · The straight line from start to finish

Goodfellow, Vinyals & Saxe (2015) asked a simple question: if you walk in a straight line from the initial weights θ0 to the trained weights θ*, what does the loss do? For many networks it falls smoothly, with no barrier to climb, which suggests that the hard part of training is not local minima but the route. Slide α along the line, including a little before the start and past the end.

\[ \boldsymbol\theta(\alpha) = (1-\alpha)\,\boldsymbol\theta_0 + \alpha\,\boldsymbol\theta^\star \]
Read it as: α = 0 is the random start, α = 1 the trained network, values in between are blends, and values outside [0, 1] continue the line. The weights move along a straight line in 1,490 dimensions; SGD itself took a winding path between the same two points.
Say it“Theta of alpha is one minus alpha times theta-zero plus alpha times theta-star.”

4 · Curvature: the Hessian's extreme eigenvalues

A 1,490 × 1,490 Hessian has 2.2 million entries, too many to build in a page. We don't need them: the Lanczos method finds the most extreme eigenvalues using only Hessian-vector products Hv, and Hv is the change in the gradient when the weights move a tiny step along v. Twenty products give the top and bottom eigenvalues to five digits. Python built the full matrix and diagonalised it, so the two can be compared. Switch between the start and the trained weights.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{H}\mathbf{v} &\approx \frac{\nabla\hat J(\boldsymbol\theta + \varepsilon\mathbf{v}) - \nabla\hat J(\boldsymbol\theta - \varepsilon\mathbf{v})}{2\varepsilon} \\ \eta &< \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}} \ \text{ for stability} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: nudge the weights forward and back along v, take the difference of the two exact gradients, and divide by the nudge: that is the curvature's action on v, at the price of two gradient evaluations (here ε = 10−4). The largest eigenvalue sets the step-size limit from the ill-conditioning section: on a quadratic, steps longer than 2/λmax diverge along the sharpest direction.
Say it“H v is about the gradient at theta plus epsilon v minus the gradient at theta minus epsilon v, over two epsilon; stable SGD needs eta below two over lambda-max.”

5 · Slices through the trained network

A Hessian eigenvector is a direction in weight space, so we can walk along it and plot the loss. Near θ* the second-order model predicts the shape, slope term included, because the trained point is not a stationary point. Along the sharpest direction the walls are steep; along the most negative one the loss falls on both sides at first, so θ* sits on a gentle ridge, a saddle-like spot; along a random direction almost nothing happens, because most of the 1,490 directions are nearly flat. Pick a direction.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat J(\boldsymbol\theta^\star + t\,\mathbf{d}) \approx \hat J(\boldsymbol\theta^\star) &+ t\,\mathbf{g}^{\!\top}\mathbf{d} \\ &+ \tfrac12\,t^2\,\mathbf{d}^{\!\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{d} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: step a distance t along the unit direction d. The loss changes by the slope along d times t, plus half the curvature along d times t². For an eigenvector, d⊤Hd is its eigenvalue. The dashed curve is this prediction; the solid curve is the real loss, measured at 41 points.
Say it“J-hat at theta-star plus t d is about J-hat at theta-star, plus t times g-dot-d, plus a half t squared d-transpose-H-d.”

import json
import numpy as np

D = np.load("x050_dl/small.npz")                # first 2000 train / 500 test images, 14x14, 16 grey levels
Xtr, ytr, Xte, yte = D["qtr"] / 15, D["ytr"], D["qte"] / 15, D["yte"]
def patches(X):                                 # every 3x3 window of every image: (N, 144, 9)
    return np.stack([X[:, i:i+12, j:j+12] for i in range(3) for j in range(3)], -1).reshape(len(X), 144, 9)
Ptr, Pte = patches(Xtr), patches(Xte)
P = 4*9 + 4 + 10*144 + 10                       # 4 conv filters 3x3, tanh, 2x2 average pool, dense 144 -> 10

def loss_grad(t, X, y, grad=True):
    Wc, bc, Wd, bd = t[:36].reshape(4, 9), t[36:40], t[40:1480].reshape(10, 144), t[1480:]
    N = len(y); A = np.tanh(X @ Wc.T + bc)                        # (N, 144, 4)
    H = A.reshape(N, 6, 2, 6, 2, 4).mean((2, 4)).reshape(N, 144)  # pooled features
    Z = H @ Wd.T + bd; Z = Z - Z.max(1, keepdims=True)
    p = np.exp(Z); p /= p.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    L = -np.log(p[np.arange(N), y]).mean()
    if not grad: return L, (p.argmax(1) == y).mean()
    dZ = p.copy(); dZ[np.arange(N), y] -= 1; dZ /= N
    dH = (dZ @ Wd).reshape(N, 6, 1, 6, 1, 4).repeat(2, 2).repeat(2, 4).reshape(N, 144, 4) / 4
    dA = dH * (1 - A**2)
    return L, np.concatenate([np.einsum("npk,npj->kj", dA, X).ravel(), dA.sum((0, 1)), (dZ.T @ H).ravel(), dZ.sum(0)])

def mulberry32(s):                              # the page's random generator, bit for bit
    imul = lambda a, b: (a * b) & 0xFFFFFFFF
    def r():
        nonlocal s
        s = (s + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = imul(s ^ (s >> 15), 1 | s)
        t = ((t + imul(t ^ (t >> 7), 61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return (t ^ (t >> 14)) / 4294967296
    return r

init = json.load(open("x050-init.json"))      # the same initial weights the page uses
theta0 = np.array(init["theta0"]); theta = theta0.copy()
rnd, idx, eta, B, EPOCHS = mulberry32(7), np.arange(2000), 0.5, 50, 12
for ep in range(1, EPOCHS + 1):
    for i in range(1999, 0, -1):                # Fisher-Yates shuffle
        j = int(rnd() * (i + 1)); idx[i], idx[j] = idx[j], idx[i]
    for b in range(0, 2000, B):
        theta -= eta * loss_grad(theta, Ptr[idx[b:b+B]], ytr[idx[b:b+B]])[1]
    Ltr, g = loss_grad(theta, Ptr, ytr); Lte, acc = loss_grad(theta, Pte, yte, grad=False)
    if ep % 3 == 0: print(f"epoch {ep:2d}  train loss {Ltr:.6f}  |grad| {np.linalg.norm(g):.5f}  test loss {Lte:.6f}  test acc {acc:.3f}")

# exact Hessian of the training loss: the dense block in closed form, the 40 conv rows by differences of gradients
DI = np.array([[40 + a*144 + j if j < 144 else 1480 + a for j in range(145)] for a in range(10)])
def hessian(t, eps=1e-4):
    Wc, bc, Wd, bd = t[:36].reshape(4, 9), t[36:40], t[40:1480].reshape(10, 144), t[1480:]
    N = len(ytr); A = np.tanh(Ptr @ Wc.T + bc)
    Hf = np.hstack([A.reshape(N, 6, 2, 6, 2, 4).mean((2, 4)).reshape(N, 144), np.ones((N, 1))])
    Z = Hf[:, :144] @ Wd.T + bd; p = np.exp(Z - Z.max(1, keepdims=True)); p /= p.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    H = np.zeros((P, P))
    for a in range(10):
        for b in range(10):
            H[np.ix_(DI[a], DI[b])] = Hf.T @ (((a == b) * p[:, a] - p[:, a] * p[:, b])[:, None] / N * Hf)
    for k in range(40):
        e = np.zeros(P); e[k] = eps
        H[k] = H[:, k] = (loss_grad(t + e, Ptr, ytr)[1] - loss_grad(t - e, Ptr, ytr)[1]) / (2 * eps)
    return H
spec = {}

What differs, and why. The page and numpy start from the same rounded initial weights, shuffle with the same generator and add up the same numbers, so training agrees to about 10−12; only the order of floating-point additions differs. The page never builds the Hessian: it runs 20 Lanczos steps with finite-difference Hessian-vector products, which pins down the extreme eigenvalues but not the whole spectrum, so the histogram's bars come from numpy's full matrix and the page draws only its own estimates on top. numpy builds the dense-layer block of the Hessian exactly and the 40 convolution rows by central differences of the exact gradient, accurate to about 10−9. The network is deliberately tiny so that all of this runs in seconds; real CNNs have millions of weights, and there only Hessian-vector methods like Lanczos are feasible.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Learning minimises the empirical risk as a proxy for the true risk, usually through a smooth surrogate such as the log-loss, and it stops when validation error stops improving, often far from any stationary point.
  • The error surface has minima, maxima and saddles, told apart by the signs of the Hessian's eigenvalues; gradient descent ends in whichever basin it starts in.
  • Ill-conditioning caps the step size at about 2/λmax, so flat directions crawl; the trained CNN here sat at λmax ≈ 2.53, below its limit of 4.
  • Weight-space symmetry creates M!·2M equal copies of every minimum; bad minima with high loss are what matter, and they fade as networks grow wider.
  • In high dimensions saddles and flat regions outnumber minima; add cliffs (clip the gradient), noisy mini-batch gradients (spread σ/√m) and local moves that miss the global picture, and a good starting point matters as much as the optimiser.

Check your understanding

Why don't we minimise the 0–1 loss directly with gradient descent?

As a function of the weights it is piecewise constant: its gradient is zero wherever it exists and undefined at the jumps, so it gives no direction to move. Minimising it exactly is also computationally intractable in general. A smooth surrogate that upper-bounds it, such as the log-loss, gives useful gradients.

Early stopping halted training while the gradient norm was still large. Is that a failure of the optimiser?

No. The goal is low true risk, estimated by validation loss, not a stationary point of the training loss. Continuing would lower the training loss but raise the validation loss: the model would be fitting noise.

A network with 3 hidden tanh units has a minimum at loss 0.12. How many other weight vectors are guaranteed to have the same loss, and are they a problem?

3!·2³ = 48 equivalent vectors in total, so 47 others. They compute the same function, so they are exactly as good: no problem at all.

Why can Newton's method be attracted to a saddle point when gradient descent is pushed away from it?

Newton's step −H−1g jumps to the stationary point of the local quadratic, whatever its type. Along a negative-curvature direction, dividing by the negative eigenvalue reverses the step, so it moves towards the saddle. Gradient descent multiplies that coordinate by 1 + η|λ| each step and so moves away.

You quadruple the batch size. What happens to the noise in the gradient estimate, and to the cost per step?

The standard deviation halves (σ/√m), while the cost per step quadruples. That poor trade is why moderate batches and more steps usually win.
