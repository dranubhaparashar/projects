Estimators, Maximum Likelihood & MAP

How to turn a handful of data into the best possible guess

Every model you train is an estimate: a number, or a million numbers, worked out from a finite, noisy sample. This chapter asks what makes one estimate better than another, and arrives at the two recipes behind nearly every loss function in machine learning: maximum likelihood, which trusts only the data, and MAP, which also brings what you already believed. The figure uses real counts from the bicycle counter on Seattle’s Fremont Bridge. We want the average number of cyclists in one weekday hour of 2025, and last year’s figure is our prior belief. The idea

An estimator is a guess you can repeat

Machine learning is, at heart, estimation. There is some quantity we cannot see directly (the average traffic in an hour, the weights of the best network, the function that maps an x-ray to a diagnosis) and a sample of data that carries clues about it. A point estimator is any rule that turns the sample into a single best guess. Any rule at all: the definition does not demand that the guess be good, which is exactly why we need ways to grade estimators.

The key shift in thinking is this. The data was drawn at random, so the estimate is random too: collect a different sample and you get a different number. An estimator therefore has a distribution, its sampling distribution, and that distribution is what we judge. The same idea covers function estimation: when we fit a model f̂ to predict y from x, the fitted function is an estimate of the true relation, and it would also come out differently on a different training set.

Below, the population is all 261 weekdays of 2025 at the Fremont Bridge, 5–6 pm, and the true value is their mean. Four rules each guess it from the same few days. Draw 1,000 samples and see whose guesses cluster tightest around the truth.

\[ \hat{\theta}_m = g\big(x^{(1)}, x^{(2)}, \dots, x^{(m)}\big) \]
Read it as: θ is the true, fixed, unknown quantity. x(1) to x(m) are m data points drawn at random. g is any function of them, and its output θ̂m (“theta hat”) is the estimate. Because the x’s are random, θ̂m is a random variable, while θ is not.
Say it“Theta-hat-m is some function g of the m data points.”

Grading estimators

Bias, variance and the divisor in s²

Two questions grade an estimator. Is it right on average? That is its bias, the gap between the centre of its sampling distribution and the truth. And how much does it wobble from sample to sample? That is its variance; the square root is called the standard error. An estimator with zero bias is called unbiased.

Some familiar estimators are unbiased. The fraction of heads in m coin flips has expected value exactly θ, the coin’s true chance of heads, and the sample mean of a Gaussian sample has expected value exactly μ. The sample variance is the classic counter-example. Measured around the sample mean, the squared spread is a little too small, because the sample mean is by construction the point that the sample huddles around most tightly.

\[ \begin{gathered} \operatorname{bias}(\hat\theta_m) = \mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_m] - \theta \\ \text{coin flips: } \mathbb{E}\big[\tfrac1m \textstyle\sum_i x^{(i)}\big] = \theta \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: the expectation is over the random sample. For coin flips x ∈ {0, 1} with P(x = 1) = θ, each flip has expected value θ, so their average does too, whatever m is. The same one-line argument shows the Gaussian sample mean is unbiased for μ.
Say it“The bias is the expected estimate minus the true value.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\sigma^2_m &= \tfrac1m \textstyle\sum_i \big(x^{(i)} - \hat\mu_m\big)^2 \\ \mathbb{E}\big[\hat\sigma^2_m\big] &= \tfrac{m-1}{m}\,\sigma^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: averaging the squared deviations from the sample mean μ̂m underestimates the true variance by a factor (m − 1)/m, so its bias is −σ²/m. Dividing by m − 1 instead removes the bias exactly; that is the “unbiased sample variance” that statistics packages print.
Say it“The expected value of sigma-hat squared is m minus one over m, times sigma squared.”

Is unbiased best, then? Not necessarily. A sensible overall score is the mean squared error, the average squared distance from the truth, and it splits cleanly into bias squared plus variance. Shrinking an estimate a little towards zero adds bias but can remove more variance. For Gaussian data the exact numbers are known, so the curves below are not simulations. Slide the divisor from m − 1 to m + 3 and find the lowest MSE.

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{MSE} &= \mathbb{E}\big[(\hat\theta_m - \theta)^2\big] \\ &= \operatorname{bias}(\hat\theta_m)^2 + \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta_m) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the typical squared miss has two sources: aiming at the wrong place (bias) and a shaky hand (variance). Here the estimator is S/d, where S = Σ(x − μ̂)² and d is the divisor; for Gaussian data E[S] = (m − 1)σ² and Var(S) = 2(m − 1)σ4, which gives both terms exactly.
Say it“The mean squared error is the bias squared plus the variance.”

More data

Consistency: why more data helps

Bias and variance describe an estimator at one fixed sample size. The deeper promise we want is about what happens as data piles up: that the estimate homes in on the truth. An estimator is consistent if, for any tolerance ε you name, the chance of missing by more than ε shrinks to zero as m grows. That is called convergence in probability.

Consistency and unbiasedness are different promises. Using only the first data point is unbiased (its expected value is the truth) yet it never improves, however much data you collect. Laplace’s rule, (heads + 1)/(m + 2), is biased at every m but consistent, because its bias fades like 1/m. Each line below is one person flipping a coin with P(heads) = 0.3, two thousand times, recomputing the estimate after every flip. Switch estimators and narrow the band.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\lim_{m\to\infty} P\big(\lvert\hat\theta_m - \theta\rvert > \epsilon\big) = 0 \\ &\text{for every } \epsilon > 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: pick any band of half-width ε around the truth. The share of people whose estimate after m flips lies outside the band must head to zero as m grows. Written compactly, plim θ̂m = θ.
Say it“As m goes to infinity, the probability that theta-hat misses by more than epsilon goes to zero.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}\big(\hat\theta_m\big) &= \frac{\theta(1-\theta)}{m} \\ \operatorname{SE} &= \sqrt{\frac{\theta(1-\theta)}{m}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for the fraction of heads, the variance falls like 1/m, so the standard error falls like 1/√m: four times the data halves the typical error. An unbiased estimator whose variance goes to zero is consistent.
Say it“The variance is theta times one minus theta, over m; the standard error is its square root.”

The recipe

Maximum likelihood: the most believable setting

So far we judged estimators someone handed us. Where do good ones come from? The most widely used principle is maximum likelihood. Choose a family of distributions pmodel(x; θ), one for each setting of the parameters θ. For each setting, ask how probable it makes the data you actually observed, and keep the setting that makes it most probable.

With independent examples, that probability is a product of one term per example. Products of many numbers below 1 are numerically hopeless (a thousand factors of 0.1 is 10−1000, far below the smallest number a computer can store), so we take the logarithm. The log turns the product into a sum, and because it only ever increases it does not move the peak. Drag the dots, then move μ and σ to make the data as likely as you can; the map below shows where the peak is.

\[ \begin{aligned} \theta_{\text{ML}} &= \arg\max_\theta \prod_{i=1}^{m} p_{\text{model}}(x^{(i)};\theta) \\ &= \arg\max_\theta \sum_{i=1}^{m} \log p_{\text{model}}(x^{(i)};\theta) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each data point casts a vote: the height of the model’s density at that point. Multiply the votes (or add their logs) and pick the parameters with the biggest total. The sum of logs is the log-likelihood ℓ(θ). For a Gaussian the answer comes out in closed form: μML is the sample mean and σ²ML is the average squared deviation, divided by m. That is the biased variance estimator from the previous section.
Say it“Theta-M-L is the theta that maximises the product of the model probabilities of the data, or equally the sum of their logs.”

Another view

MLE as matching the data: the KL divergence

Divide the log-likelihood by m and it becomes an average over the data, that is, an expectation under the empirical distribution p̂data, which puts probability 1/m on each example. Maximising it is the same as minimising the KL divergence from p̂data to the model, a measure of how far apart two distributions are (chapter 0.8 builds it properly). So maximum likelihood is an act of matching: it moves the model until its distribution looks as much as possible like the data’s.

The bars are real: how often 0, 1, 2, … cyclists crossed the bridge in one night-time hour, over the 261 weekdays of 2025. The model is a Poisson distribution with rate λ, the textbook model for counts of independent events. Slide λ and watch the cross-entropy: its lowest point sits exactly at the sample mean.

\[ \begin{aligned} &D_{\text{KL}}\big(\hat p_{\text{data}} \,\Vert\, p_{\text{model}}\big) \\ &\quad = \mathbb{E}_{\hat p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log \hat p_{\text{data}}(x) \\ &\qquad\qquad - \log p_{\text{model}}(x)\big] \\ &\theta_{\text{ML}} = \arg\min_\theta \\ &\quad -\mathbb{E}_{\hat p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log p_{\text{model}}(x;\theta)\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first term inside the expectation depends only on the data, not on θ, so minimising the KL divergence only has to make the second term small. That second part, −E[log pmodel], is the cross-entropy between the data and the model, and it is exactly the negative average log-likelihood. Any loss written as a negative log-likelihood is a cross-entropy in this sense, including the mean squared error.
Say it“Maximum likelihood minimises the cross-entropy between the empirical distribution and the model, which is the same as minimising their KL divergence.”

Supervised learning

Conditional likelihood: where MSE and cross-entropy come from

In supervised learning we do not model the inputs, only the target given the input. The same principle applies to the conditional distribution: choose θ to make the observed targets most probable given their inputs. This is the single idea behind almost every loss function in deep learning. Pick an output distribution, take its negative log-likelihood, and the loss falls out; there is no need to invent a new loss for each model.

If we say the target is Gaussian around the model’s prediction, the negative log-likelihood is the mean squared error, plus terms that do not depend on the line. If we say a 0–1 label is a biased coin whose chance of heads is the model’s output, the negative log-likelihood is the binary cross-entropy of logistic regression. Steer the line or curve, and change σ: σ moves the log-likelihood up and down, but never moves the best line.

\[ \begin{aligned} \theta_{\text{ML}} &= \arg\max_\theta \\ &\qquad \textstyle\sum_{i} \log P(y^{(i)} \mid x^{(i)};\theta) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same recipe, now conditioned on the inputs. A neural network is then simply a machine that turns x into the parameters of a distribution over y, and training maximises the log-probability of the right answers.
Say it“Theta-M-L maximises the sum over examples of the log-probability of y-i given x-i.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &-\tfrac1m \textstyle\sum_i \log p\big(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}\big) \\ &\quad= \log\sigma + \tfrac12\log 2\pi + \tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\,\text{MSE} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with p(y | x) = N(y; ŷ(x; w), σ²), each example’s negative log-density is log σ + ½ log 2π + (ŷ − y)²/(2σ²). Averaged, the only part that depends on w is MSE = (1/m) Σ (ŷ(i) − y(i))². So least squares is maximum likelihood, and the best σ comes out as √MSE.
Say it“The average negative log-likelihood is log sigma, plus half log two pi, plus MSE over two sigma squared.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &-\tfrac1m \textstyle\sum_i \log P\big(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}\big) \\ &\quad= -\tfrac1m \textstyle\sum_i \big[\,y^{(i)}\log\hat y^{(i)} \\ &\qquad\quad + \big(1-y^{(i)}\big)\log\big(1-\hat y^{(i)}\big)\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with P(y = 1 | x) = ŷ = σ(wx + b), the probability of the observed label is ŷ if y = 1 and 1 − ŷ if y = 0. Its negative log is the binary cross-entropy: a confident wrong prediction costs a lot, a confident right one almost nothing. (Here σ(·) is the logistic sigmoid, not a standard deviation.)
Say it“The loss is minus the average of y log y-hat plus one minus y, times log of one minus y-hat.”

Why maximum likelihood

Consistent, efficient, and the Cramér–Rao bound

Why is maximum likelihood the default? Because of what it does as data grows. Under two conditions it is consistent: the true distribution must be one of the model family (some θ really produced the data), and only one θ may produce it (otherwise the data cannot tell the candidates apart). Then θML converges to the true value.

Many estimators are consistent, so the stronger property is efficiency: how little data an estimator needs for a given accuracy. The Cramér–Rao bound sets a floor on the variance of any unbiased estimator, and for large m no consistent estimator beats maximum likelihood’s mean squared error. Below, 2,000 experiments estimate the centre of a distribution with two rules: the sample mean and the sample median. For Gaussian data the mean is the maximum-likelihood estimator; for Laplace data (a peaked distribution with heavier tails) the median is. Both distributions have variance 1. Switch the data and grow m: the winner swaps, and as m grows the winner settles onto the bound.

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}\big(\hat\theta\big) &\ge \frac{1}{m\,I(\theta)} \\ I(\theta) &= \mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log p(x;\theta)\Big)^2\Big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: I(θ), the Fisher information, measures how sharply one data point’s log-likelihood reacts to a change in θ: the sharper, the more each point tells you. No unbiased estimator can have variance below 1/(m I). Maximum likelihood reaches this floor as m grows.
Say it“The variance of any unbiased estimator is at least one over m times the Fisher information.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Gaussian: } & I = \tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}, \\ \ \operatorname{Var} \ge \tfrac{\sigma^2}{m} \\ \text{Laplace: } & I = \tfrac{1}{b^2}, \\ \ \operatorname{Var} \ge \tfrac{b^2}{m} = \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2m} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a Laplace distribution with scale b has variance 2b². Its sharp peak makes each point twice as informative about the centre as a Gaussian point with the same variance, so the bound is half as large. The sample mean ignores the shape and always has variance σ²/m, so on Laplace data it wastes half the information.
Say it“For a Gaussian the bound is sigma squared over m; for a Laplace it is b squared over m.”

Output units

Output units: what the loss makes a network learn

A network’s last layer decides which distribution it is predicting, and the loss decides which statistic of that distribution it learns. Suppose the model is flexible enough to output any value for any input. Then minimising squared error drives its output, at every x, to the mean of y given x. Minimising absolute error drives it to the median. The “pinball” loss, which charges under-predictions τ per unit and over-predictions 1 − τ, drives it to the τ-quantile. (Proving this for every x at once uses the calculus of variations; for one x it is the one-variable minimisation in the small plot.)

The simplest output unit is a plain linear unit, ŷ = W⊤h + b, read as the mean of a Gaussian. It does not saturate, so gradients flow freely, and maximising its log-likelihood is minimising the MSE. A second output can predict the Gaussian’s spread too, so the network says how unsure it is at each input. Below, x is the hour of the day and y is the real count of cyclists on each weekday of 2025; the model has one free output per hour, the most flexible model possible. Switch the loss and watch which line it learns.

\[ \begin{aligned} f^* &= \arg\min_f \ \mathbb{E}\big(y - f(x)\big)^2 \\ &\Rightarrow\; f^*(x) = \mathbb{E}[\,y \mid x\,] \\ f^* &= \arg\min_f \ \mathbb{E}\,\big\lvert y - f(x)\big\rvert \\ &\Rightarrow\; f^*(x) = \operatorname{median}(y \mid x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for each x separately, the number c that minimises the average squared miss to the y’s is their mean, and the number that minimises the average absolute miss is their median. A model trained on MSE therefore learns conditional means, and one trained on absolute error learns conditional medians.
Say it“The function that minimises expected squared error predicts the mean of y given x; for absolute error, the median.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf y} &= \mathbf W^{\top}\mathbf h + \mathbf b \\ p(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x) &= \mathcal N\big(\mathbf y;\ \hat{\mathbf y},\ \mathbf I\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: h is whatever the hidden layers computed from x. The linear unit maps it to ŷ, treated as the mean of a Gaussian over y. Its negative log-likelihood is ½‖y − ŷ‖² plus a constant, so this output unit plus maximum likelihood is exactly “minimise the squared error”. Replace the fixed I by a learned variance and the network also learns the spread.
Say it“Y-hat is W-transpose h plus b, and y given x is Gaussian with mean y-hat and identity covariance.”

The Bayesian view

Priors and MAP: regularisation, explained

Maximum likelihood treats θ as a fixed unknown and lets the data speak alone. The Bayesian view treats θ itself as uncertain: before seeing data you hold a prior p(θ), and Bayes’ rule turns it into a posterior p(θ | x) once the data arrives. A fully Bayesian prediction does not pick one θ: it averages the predictions of every θ, weighted by the posterior, which also tells you how unsure to be. Chapter 0.9 does this in depth.

Averaging over all θ is often too expensive, so a popular shortcut keeps a single point: the peak of the posterior, the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate. Its objective is the log-likelihood plus the log-prior. With a Gaussian prior centred on zero, the log-prior is a penalty on the squared weights, so MAP is weight decay (ridge regression), and the regularisation strength is the ratio of the noise variance to the prior variance. The fit below is a degree-9 polynomial, 10 weights, through 8 points: maximum likelihood can thread every point, and it swings wildly between and beyond them. Turn up the prior and drag the points.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\theta \mid x) &= \frac{p(x \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(x)} \\ \theta_{\text{MAP}} &= \arg\max_\theta \\ \log p(x\mid\theta) \\ &\qquad\quad + \log p(\theta) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the posterior is likelihood times prior, rescaled to sum to one. p(x) does not depend on θ, so it drops out of the arg max. MAP is maximum likelihood with one extra term, the log-prior, which pulls the estimate towards what you believed in advance. With lots of data the likelihood term grows with m and swamps the prior.
Say it“Theta-MAP maximises the log-likelihood plus the log-prior.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &p(\mathbf w) = \mathcal N\big(\mathbf w;\,\mathbf 0,\,\tau^2\mathbf I\big) \;\Rightarrow \\ &\mathbf w_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf w}\ \textstyle\sum_i (y_i - \mathbf w^{\top}\boldsymbol\phi_i)^2 \\ &\qquad\qquad + \lambda\,\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf w, \quad \lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: φ is the feature vector (here 1, x, x², …, x9), σ² the noise variance, τ² the prior variance of each weight. Multiply the negative log-posterior by 2σ² and this is what is left: squared error plus λ times the squared weights. A tight prior (small τ) means a big λ and small, smooth weights.
Say it“W-MAP minimises the squared errors plus lambda times w-transpose w, with lambda equal to sigma squared over tau squared.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol\Sigma_{\text{post}} &= \Big(\tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\boldsymbol\Phi + \tfrac{1}{\tau^2}\mathbf I\Big)^{-1} \\ \boldsymbol\mu_{\text{post}} &= \tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\,\boldsymbol\Sigma_{\text{post}}\boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\mathbf y \\ p(y_\ast \mid x_\ast) &= \mathcal N\big(y_\ast;\ \boldsymbol\mu_{\text{post}}^{\top}\boldsymbol\phi_\ast, \\ &\qquad\quad \sigma^2 + \boldsymbol\phi_\ast^{\top}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{\text{post}}\boldsymbol\phi_\ast\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: this is Bayesian linear regression. With a Gaussian prior and Gaussian noise the posterior over the weights is Gaussian too, with mean μpost (equal to wMAP, since a Gaussian’s peak is its mean) and covariance Σpost. A new prediction at x* averages over that posterior: its variance is the noise σ² plus the model’s own uncertainty, which grows away from the data. That is the shaded band.
Say it“The posterior covariance is the inverse of Phi-transpose Phi over sigma squared plus the identity over tau squared; predictions add the noise variance to phi-transpose Sigma phi.”

Real-world project

Project: counting cyclists on the Fremont Bridge

Since October 2012 the City of Seattle has counted every bicycle crossing the Fremont Bridge, hour by hour, with sensors on both sidewalks; the data is published on the city’s open-data portal. We take the 261 weekdays of 2025 (all complete, holidays included) and ask a planner’s question: what is the average number of cyclists in each hour of a weekday? Here the truth is known, because we have the whole year, so we can test how well a planner with only a few days of counts would do.

The planner’s model is the textbook one for counts: each day’s count in an hour is Poisson with rate λ. Maximum likelihood gives the sample mean. For MAP, the planner’s prior belief is the same hour’s weekday average from 2024, worth b “pseudo-days” of data, encoded as a Gamma distribution (the conjugate prior of the Poisson, so the posterior is a Gamma too). Pick an hour, draw a few days, and compare the two guesses with the truth.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\lambda) &\propto \lambda^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta\lambda} \\ \alpha &= b\,m_0 + 1,\quad \beta = b \\ \lambda_{\text{MAP}} &= \frac{\alpha - 1 + \sum_i x^{(i)}}{\beta + n} \\ &= \frac{b\,m_0 + n\,\bar x}{b + n} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the Poisson likelihood is proportional to λΣxe−nλ. Multiplying by the Gamma prior adds Σx to α and n to β, and the peak of the result is the MAP. With α and β chosen this way the prior’s peak is at last year’s rate m0, and the MAP is a weighted average: b parts last year, n parts this year’s sample mean x̄ (which is λML). As n grows the data wins.
Say it“Lambda-MAP is b times m-nought plus n times x-bar, all over b plus n.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{MSE}(\lambda_{\text{MAP}}) &= \frac{b^2\,\delta^2 + n^2\operatorname{Var}(\bar x)}{(b+n)^2} \\ \operatorname{Var}(\bar x) &= \frac{s^2}{n}\cdot\frac{N-n}{N-1} \\ \delta &= m_0 - \lambda \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: bias² plus variance again. The prior adds bias in proportion to how wrong last year is (δ), and divides the variance of the sample mean by (b + n)²/n². s² is the day-to-day variance over the N = 261 days; the last factor is there because a hand of days is drawn without replacement. Ignoring that factor, the best prior strength is b = s²/δ²: a trustworthy prior (small δ) on a noisy hour deserves many pseudo-days.
Say it“The MAP's mean squared error is b squared delta squared plus n squared times the variance of x-bar, over b plus n, squared.”

All 24 hours at once

One hour can be lucky. The fair test repeats the experiment for every hour of the day, 2,000 hands each, and compares errors relative to the size of each hour’s traffic (a miss of 10 cyclists matters more at 3 am than at 8 am). The prior strength and prior year are the ones you set above.

The same experiment in Python

import json, numpy as np
D = json.load(open("x007-data.json"))            # Fremont Bridge, 261 weekdays of 2025, counts per hour
X = np.array(D["d25"]); H, N = X.shape            # 24 hours x 261 days
mu, var = X.mean(1), X.var(1)                      # the truth: each hour's 2025 rate (population of days)
m24, m20 = np.array(D["m24"]), np.array(D["m20"])  # priors: the same hour's weekday rate in 2024, 2020

def mulberry32(a):                                 # the page's random-number generator, bit for bit
    a &= 0xFFFFFFFF
    def r():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return r

NS, R, rng, perm = [1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 10, 14, 20, 30], 2000, mulberry32(2025), list(range(N))
xbar = np.zeros((H, len(NS), R))
for h in range(H):                                 # R random hands of n days, without replacement
    for k, n in enumerate(NS):
        for r in range(R):
            s = 0
            for i in range(n):
                j = i + int(rng() * (N - i)); perm[i], perm[j] = perm[j], perm[i]; s += X[h, perm[i]]
            xbar[h, k, r] = s / n

def rmse(prior, b):                                # Monte Carlo RMSE, MAP = (b*m0 + n*xbar)/(b + n)
    n = np.array(NS)[None, :, None]
    est = xbar if b == 0 else (b * prior[:, None, None] + n * xbar) / (b + n)
    return np.sqrt(((est - mu[:, None, None]) ** 2).mean(2))
def exact(prior, b):                               # the bias-variance formula, finite-population correction
    n = np.array(NS)[None, :]; v = var[:, None] / n * (N - n) / (N - 1)
    return np.sqrt((n**2 * v + b**2 * (prior - mu)[:, None] ** 2) / (b + n) ** 2)

b = 5
mle, m4, m0 = rmse(mu, 0), rmse(m24, b), rmse(m20, b)
print(f"{N} weekdays x {H} hours; R = {R} hands per (hour, n); MAP prior strength b = {b} days")
print("  n | MLE rel. RMSE | MAP(2024) | MAP(2020) | hours MAP(2024) wins | exact MLE  exact MAP(2024)")
for k, n in enumerate(NS):
    rel = lambda e: (e[:, k] / mu).mean()
    print(f"{n:3d} |     {rel(mle):.4f}    |  {rel(m4):.4f}   |  {rel(m0):.4f}   |        {(m4[:, k] < mle[:, k]).sum():2d} of 24       |"

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • An estimator is a rule from data to a guess. Its guesses vary from sample to sample, and we grade that spread: MSE = bias² + variance. Unbiased is not automatically best.
  • Consistency is the promise that more data drives the error to zero. An estimator can be unbiased and never improve, or biased and still consistent.
  • Maximum likelihood picks the parameters that make the observed data most probable. Equivalently, it minimises the cross-entropy, and so the KL divergence, from the data’s empirical distribution to the model.
  • Conditional maximum likelihood generates the losses: a Gaussian output gives MSE (and learns the conditional mean), a Bernoulli output gives cross-entropy. As data grows, MLE is consistent and reaches the Cramér–Rao floor.
  • MAP adds a log-prior to the log-likelihood. A Gaussian prior on the weights is weight decay, and a good prior beats the MLE on small samples: on the Fremont Bridge, last year’s counts cut the error of a five-day estimate by more than a third.

Check your understanding

You estimate a variance by dividing by m and a colleague divides by m − 1. Who has the lower mean squared error on Gaussian data?

You do. Dividing by m − 1 removes the bias but increases the variance more than it saves; for Gaussian data the MSE is lowest at a divisor of m + 1, and m is closer to that than m − 1 is. Both are consistent, and the difference vanishes as m grows.

Why does nobody maximise the product of the likelihoods directly?

Because the product of many numbers below 1 underflows to zero in floating point, and its gradient is useless. The log turns the product into a sum of manageable numbers and, being increasing, keeps the same maximiser.

A regression network is trained with mean absolute error instead of mean squared error. What does its output estimate?

The conditional median of y given x, not the mean. On skewed targets, like hourly traffic, the median sits below the mean, so the network will predict systematically lower numbers than an MSE-trained one.

What prior turns maximum likelihood into L2 weight decay, and how does λ relate to it?

An independent Gaussian prior N(0, τ²) on every weight. The negative log-prior is w⊤w/(2τ²); with Gaussian noise of variance σ² the penalty in a squared-error loss is λ = σ²/τ². A tighter prior means stronger decay.

MAP with last year’s counts beat the MLE at every hour for small n. Why does that advantage shrink as n grows, and when would the prior hurt?

The data term grows with n while the prior is worth a fixed b pseudo-days, so MAP converges to the MLE. The prior hurts when it is wrong by much more than the day-to-day noise allows: once n is large, the bias bδ/(b + n) outweighs the variance it saves, as the 2020 pandemic prior shows.
