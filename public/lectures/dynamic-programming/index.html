Dynamic Programming: Evaluate, Improve, Iterate

When you know the rules of the world, you can plan instead of learn

A robot crosses an icy garden: one move in five slips sideways, pits cost a point, the flag earns one. If you know those odds exactly, you need no trial and error at all. You can evaluate any plan by solving a set of equations, improve it by acting greedily on what you learned, and repeat. Each round is guaranteed to be no worse than the last, and the loop stops at an optimal policy, usually within a handful of rounds. Every colour in the garden is a computed value; every arrow is the current policy. Press Play and watch policy iteration think, then click any square to turn it into a wall, a pit or a flag, and solve again.

The setting

Planning when you know the rules

Many decisions come in chains: what you do now changes where you will be, and so what you can do next. A sequential decision problem under uncertainty is modelled as a Markov decision process (MDP): states s, actions a, a transition model p(s′, r | s, a) that gives the chance of landing in s′ with reward r, and a discount γ for the future. A plan (“up, up, right, right, right”) is no good when moves can fail, because you may not be where the plan assumed. You need a policy π(a | s): an instruction for every state.

Dynamic programming (DP) is the family of algorithms that compute good policies when the model is known exactly. That is a strong assumption, and the rest of reinforcement learning is largely about doing without it, but DP is the foundation they all imitate. Its key tool is a consistency condition. The value vπ(s), the expected discounted return from s when following π, must equal the expected reward of one step plus the discounted value of wherever that step lands.

Below is the classic 4×3 world from Russell and Norvig’s Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach: a wall in the middle, an exit worth +1, another worth −1, and a cost of 0.04 on every other move. A move goes where you aimed with probability q and slips to either side otherwise. Here γ = 1: nothing is discounted, but every step costs. Click squares to turn their arrows, and see what your policy is worth.

\[ \begin{aligned} v_\pi(s) &= \textstyle\sum_a \pi(a\mid s) \\ &\quad\times \textstyle\sum_{s',\,r} p(s',r\mid s,a) \\ &\quad\times \big[\,r + \gamma\, v_\pi(s')\,\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the value of s under π is an average over what can happen next: over the actions π might choose, then over the next state s′ and reward r the world might return. Each outcome is worth its reward plus γ times the value of where it lands. One equation per state; together they pin down vπ. This is the Bellman expectation equation.
Say it“v-pi of s is the sum over actions of pi of a given s, times the sum over s-prime and r of p of s-prime, r given s, a, times r plus gamma v-pi of s-prime.”
\[ \begin{aligned} v_*(s) &= \max_a \textstyle\sum_{s',\,r} p(s',r\mid s,a) \\ &\quad\times \big[\,r + \gamma\, v_*(s')\,\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the best achievable value v* obeys the same equation with the average over actions replaced by the best action. A policy that picks, in every state, an action achieving this maximum is optimal. All of DP is a set of ways to solve these two equations.
Say it“v-star of s is the max over a of the sum over s-prime and r of p times r plus gamma v-star of s-prime.”

Move 1 · prediction

Policy evaluation: sweep until nothing changes

The Bellman equation gives one linear equation per state, so vπ could be found by solving a linear system, as the previous lab did. DP usually does something simpler that scales better: iterative policy evaluation. Start from any guess v0 (zeros), and use the right-hand side of the Bellman equation as an update rule, applied to every state in turn. One pass over the states is a sweep. Each sweep pushes information one step further back from the exits, and the guesses converge to vπ. Two bookkeeping choices: keep the old values in a separate array until the sweep ends (two arrays), or overwrite as you go (in place), so later states in the sweep already see new values. This is the 4×4 world of Sutton and Barto’s Example 4.1: the shaded corners end the episode, every move costs 1, moves are certain, and the policy picks each of the four directions with probability ¼. Step through the sweeps, then compare the two bookkeeping schemes.

\[ \begin{aligned} v_{k+1}(s) &= \textstyle\sum_a \pi(a\mid s) \\ &\quad\times \textstyle\sum_{s',\,r} p(s',r\mid s,a) \\ &\quad\times \big[\,r + \gamma\, v_k(s')\,\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the new guess for s is the Bellman right-hand side evaluated with the old guesses vk. The exact answer vπ is a fixed point: plug it in and it comes back unchanged. Stop when the largest change in a sweep, Δ, falls below a small threshold θ.
Say it“v k-plus-one of s is the expected value of r plus gamma v-k of s-prime, under pi and the model.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf v_\pi &= \mathbf r_\pi + \gamma P_\pi \mathbf v_\pi \\ \Vert \mathbf v_{k+1} - \mathbf v_\pi \Vert_\infty &\le \gamma\, \Vert \mathbf v_k - \mathbf v_\pi \Vert_\infty \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: stacked over all states, the equation is linear: rπ holds each state’s expected reward and Pπ its next-state probabilities, so vπ = (I − γPπ)−1rπ. With γ < 1, each sweep shrinks the worst error by at least a factor γ. At γ = 1 it still converges here, because under this policy every episode ends with probability 1.
Say it“v-pi equals r-pi plus gamma P-pi v-pi; and the largest error after a sweep is at most gamma times the largest error before it.”

Move 2 · control

Policy improvement: act greedily, never get worse

Knowing vπ tells you more than how good π is. In any state you can ask: what if, just this once, I took action a and then went back to following π? That is the action value qπ(s, a), and it costs one look-ahead through the model. If some action has qπ(s, a) > vπ(s), switching to it in s is better, and the policy improvement theorem says something stronger: switching to it every time s is visited is better too, and no state anywhere gets worse. Doing this in every state at once gives the greedy policy. Each square below is split into four triangles, one per action, coloured by qπ; the policy’s own choice has the dark edge. This lab discounts with γ = 0.9, so that even a hopeless policy has finite values to compare. Click a square, then make it greedy, here or everywhere.

\[ \begin{aligned} q_\pi(s,a) &= \textstyle\sum_{s',\,r} p(s',r\mid s,a) \\ &\quad\times \big[\,r + \gamma\, v_\pi(s')\,\big] \\ \pi'(s) &= \arg\max_a\; q_\pi(s,a) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: qπ(s, a) is the value of taking a first and following π afterwards: one step through the model, then vπ. The greedy policy π′ picks, in each state, the action with the largest qπ (keeping the old action on ties).
Say it“q-pi of s, a is the expected r plus gamma v-pi of s-prime after taking a; pi-prime of s is the arg-max over a of q-pi.”
\[ \begin{aligned} & q_\pi(s,\pi'(s)) \ge v_\pi(s)\ \ \forall s \\ & \Longrightarrow\; v_{\pi'}(s) \ge v_\pi(s)\ \ \forall s \\[6pt] v_\pi(s) &\le q_\pi(s,\pi'(s)) \\ &= \mathbb E\big[R_{1} + \gamma\, v_\pi(S_{1})\big] \\ &\le \mathbb E_{\pi'}\big[R_{1} + \gamma\, q_\pi(S_1,\pi'(S_1))\big] \\ &= \mathbb E_{\pi'}\big[R_{1} + \gamma R_{2} + \gamma^2 v_\pi(S_{2})\big] \\ &\le \cdots \le v_{\pi'}(s) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: every expectation starts from S0 = s and takes A0 = π′(s); Eπ′ means later actions follow π′ too. If following π′ for one step and then π is at least as good as π everywhere, then following π′ for ever is at least as good. The proof keeps replacing the vπ at the end of the chain by the qπ it is bounded by, which pushes π′ one step further each time. If the greedy policy changes nothing, π already satisfies the optimality equation and is optimal.
Say it“If q-pi of s, pi-prime of s is at least v-pi of s for every s, then v-pi-prime is at least v-pi everywhere.”

Putting them together

Policy iteration, and how much evaluation is enough

Policy iteration simply alternates the two moves: evaluate π, make it greedy, evaluate the new policy, make that greedy, and so on. Every new policy is strictly better than the last unless it is already optimal, and a finite MDP has only finitely many deterministic policies, so the loop must stop, and it stops at an optimal policy. In practice it stops after very few improvements. But evaluating each policy to convergence is expensive, and mostly wasted: the next improvement only needs values good enough to rank the actions. Modified policy iteration runs just m evaluation sweeps before each improvement. With m = 1 it becomes value iteration, the algorithm you met in chapter 3.1; with m = ∞ it is policy iteration. The garden here discounts with γ = 0.95. Pick m, then alternate Evaluate and Improve by hand, or run it to the end, and see which m needs the fewest sweeps in total.

\[ \begin{aligned} & \pi_0 \xrightarrow{\;E\;} v_{\pi_0} \xrightarrow{\;I\;} \pi_1 \xrightarrow{\;E\;} v_{\pi_1} \\ & \xrightarrow{\;I\;} \pi_2 \;\cdots\; \xrightarrow{\;I\;} \pi_* \xrightarrow{\;E\;} v_* \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: E is a complete policy evaluation, I a greedy improvement. Each πk+1 is at least as good as πk in every state, strictly better somewhere unless πk is optimal. There are at most |A||S| deterministic policies, so the chain cannot run for ever.
Say it“Evaluate pi-zero, improve to pi-one, evaluate, improve, until the policy stops changing; then it is pi-star.”
\[ \mathbf v \leftarrow \big(T^{\pi}\big)^{m}\,\mathbf v, \qquad \pi \leftarrow \text{greedy}(\mathbf v) \]
Read it as: Tπ is one evaluation sweep (the Bellman expectation backup applied to every state), so (Tπ)m is m sweeps, starting from the values left by the previous policy rather than from zero. Then improve. Any m ≥ 1 converges to v*; m only trades sweeps against improvements.
Say it“Apply the evaluation sweep for pi m times, then make pi greedy with respect to v.”

Breaking the sweep

Asynchronous DP: back up states in any order

A sweep touches every state once, in a fixed order, before anything else happens. For a game like backgammon, with about 1020 states, a single sweep would take longer than anyone can wait. Asynchronous DP drops the sweep: back up one state at a time, in any order you like, using whatever values the other states have right now. As long as every state keeps being backed up (and γ < 1), the values still converge to v*. The freedom is the point: you can spend backups where they matter. Back up states near the goal first and good news travels outward in one pass. Back up the state whose value is most wrong (prioritised). Or back up only the states an agent actually visits while acting greedily (real-time DP), which interleaves planning with acting and may never touch states that don’t matter. Choose an order and slide the budget of backups.

\[ \begin{aligned} v(s_k) &\leftarrow \max_a \textstyle\sum_{s',\,r} p(s',r\mid s_k,a) \\ &\quad\times \big[\,r + \gamma\, v(s')\,\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: at step k, pick one state sk by any rule and replace its value by the Bellman optimality backup, using the current values of its successors. Nothing else changes. A full in-place sweep is the special case where sk runs through the states in order.
Say it“Replace v of s-k by the max over a of the expected r plus gamma v of s-prime.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \delta(s) = \Big|&\max_a \textstyle\sum_{s',\,r} p(s',r\mid s,a) \\ &\times \big[r + \gamma\, v(s')\big] - v(s)\Big| \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the Bellman error of s, how much its value would move if you backed it up now. Prioritised ordering always backs up the state with the largest δ. When every δ is 0, v satisfies the optimality equation: it is v*.
Say it“Delta of s is the gap between the backed-up value of s and its current value.”

The big picture

Generalised policy iteration: two processes, one fixed point

Policy iteration, value iteration, modified and asynchronous versions all share one structure. Two processes run side by side. Evaluation pulls the value estimate v towards vπ, the true value of the current policy. Improvement pulls the policy towards greedy(v). They interfere: making π greedy changes vπ, so v is suddenly wrong again; fixing v makes π non-greedy again. Sutton and Barto call any such interleaving generalised policy iteration (GPI). The only way both processes can stop is when v is the value of π and π is greedy for v; that pair satisfies the Bellman optimality equation, so it is optimal. Almost every RL method in this book, from Monte Carlo control to actor–critic, is GPI with sampled experience in place of the model. Below, both gaps are measured exactly after every half-step, on the garden from the policy-iteration lab. Change how hard each process works and watch the zig-zag close in on the corner.

\[ \begin{aligned} e_{\text{eval}} &= \Vert \mathbf v - \mathbf v_\pi \Vert_\infty \\ e_{\text{greedy}} &= \max_s \Big[\max_a q_{\mathbf v}(s,a) \\ &\qquad - q_{\mathbf v}(s,\pi(s))\Big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: eeval is how far v is from the true value of the current policy; evaluation shrinks it. egreedy is how much better than π’s own action the best action looks under v (qv is the one-step look-ahead through the model using v); improvement sets it to 0. GPI is finished when both are 0.
Say it“The evaluation gap is the largest distance between v and v-pi; the greedy gap is the largest amount by which the best action beats pi's action.”
\[ \begin{aligned} & e_{\text{eval}} = 0 \;\text{ and }\; e_{\text{greedy}} = 0 \\ & \Longrightarrow\; \mathbf v = \mathbf v_* ,\;\; \pi = \pi_* \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: if v = vπ and π already takes a best action under v, then vπ(s) = maxa qvπ(s, a) for every s. That is the Bellman optimality equation, whose only solution is v*.
Say it“When both gaps are zero, v is v-star and pi is optimal.”

What the numbers mean

Reward scales: which changes alter the policy

Rewards are a design choice, and it matters which choices change the answer. Multiplying every reward by a positive number a changes the units of value and nothing else: every comparison between actions is scaled by the same a. Adding a constant b to every reward is subtler. In a task that never ends, it adds the same b/(1 − γ) to every value, and again no choice changes. But when episodes can end, the constant is collected once per step, so actions that finish sooner gain or lose relative to the rest. That is exactly why the 4×3 world’s small cost per move, its living reward, decides the route: make it harsh enough and the agent dives into the −1 exit just to stop paying; make it positive and the agent avoids both exits for ever. The one transformation that is always safe is potential-based shaping (Ng, Harada & Russell, 1999): add γΦ(s′) − Φ(s) for any function Φ of the state (with Φ = 0 at the exits). It can make learning faster, but it never changes which policy is optimal. Slide the living reward, then try to change the policy by scaling, shifting or shaping.

\[ \begin{aligned} r' &= a\,r + b, \quad a > 0 \\ q'_\pi(s,\alpha) &= a\,q_\pi(s,\alpha) \\ &\quad + b\,\mathbb E\big[\textstyle\sum_{t \lt T}\gamma^t\big] \\ \text{no exits: } q'_\pi &= a\,q_\pi + \tfrac{b}{1-\gamma} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: scaling and shifting every reward (α is the action, to avoid a clash with the scale a) turns each action value into a times the old one plus b times the expected discounted number of steps left, T being the step on which the episode ends. If episodes never end, that count is 1/(1 − γ) for every action, so the ranking of actions cannot change. If they do end, it depends on the action, and the ranking can flip.
Say it“Scale by a and shift by b: q-prime is a q plus b times the expected discounted number of remaining steps.”
\[ \begin{aligned} r' &= r + \gamma\,\Phi(s') - \Phi(s) \\ \Longrightarrow\; q'_\pi(s,\alpha) &= q_\pi(s,\alpha) - \Phi(s) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: along any trajectory the shaping terms telescope: γΦ(s1) − Φ(s0) + γ2Φ(s2) − γΦ(s1) + … leaves only −Φ(s0). Every action from s loses the same Φ(s), so the best action is the same. Here Φ(s) = −k × (steps from s to the +1 exit).
Say it“Add gamma Phi of s-prime minus Phi of s to every reward, and every action value just drops by Phi of s.”

Another road to the optimum

The linear-programming view of an MDP

The max in the Bellman optimality equation makes it non-linear, but it can be traded for inequalities. Call v a feasible value function if, in every state, it is at least as large as the one-step look-ahead of every action. Any feasible v is an upper bound on v*, and v* itself is feasible, with equality for its best actions. So v* is the smallest feasible v, and finding it is a linear program (LP): |S| unknowns, one inequality per state–action pair. LPs are solvable in polynomial time, which is the cleanest proof that MDPs are tractable. In practice LP solvers are usually slower than policy iteration on big MDPs, but the view is rich: the LP’s dual has one variable per state–action pair, which turns out to be how often (discounted) the optimal policy takes that action there. The machine below has two states, so every value function is a point on the plane. A Fresh machine can Run (earns 10, wears out with probability w) or be Serviced (earns 2, stays fresh); a Worn one can Run (earns 4, stays worn) or be Serviced (costs C, becomes fresh). Drag the point around the feasible region, then change the costs and watch the corner move.

\[ \begin{aligned} \min_{\mathbf v}\;& \textstyle\sum_s v(s) \;\;\text{ s.t., for all } s, a: \\ v(s) &\ge \textstyle\sum_{s'} p(s'\mid s,a) \\ &\quad\times \big[r(s,a,s') + \gamma\, v(s')\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: push every value as low as it will go, while each state stays at least as valuable as each of its actions. At the optimum the constraint of the best action in each state is tight (holds with equality), and v = v*. The constraints that are tight tell you the optimal policy.
Say it“Minimise the sum of the values, subject to v of s being at least the look-ahead value of every action in every state.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \max_{x \ge 0}\;& \textstyle\sum_{s,a} x(s,a)\, \bar r(s,a) \;\;\text{ s.t., } \forall s': \\ & \textstyle\sum_a x(s',a) = 1 \\ &\quad + \gamma \textstyle\sum_{s,a} p(s'\mid s,a)\, x(s,a) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the dual. x(s, a) is the discounted number of times action a is taken in state s, counted over runs that start once from every state (the 1 on the right), and r̄(s, a) is the expected reward of the pair. The constraint says visits are conserved: visits to s′ are its one start plus the discounted flow arriving from elsewhere. Maximising reward over such flows gives the same number as the primal.
Say it“Maximise total reward over discounted visit counts x, where visits into each state equal one plus gamma times the flow in.”

Is it practical?

How efficient is dynamic programming?

DP has a reputation for being impractical, and half of it is deserved. The good half: its cost is polynomial in the number of states and actions. A brute-force search over deterministic policies would have to compare |A||S| of them, which for 100 states and 4 actions is a number with 61 digits; policy iteration typically needs a handful of improvements, and each improvement or sweep costs about |S|2|A| operations at worst. Value iteration’s sweep count depends on γ: the closer γ is to 1, the further information has to travel and the more sweeps it takes. The bad half is the curse of dimensionality (Bellman’s phrase): the number of states grows exponentially with the number of variables that describe a state, so a problem described by twenty yes/no facts already has a million states. That is a property of the problems, not a weakness of DP, and DP copes with millions of states on a laptop. The MDPs below are random: every state–action pair has a reward between 0 and 1 and three random successors. Grow the MDP, add actions, push γ towards 1.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{policies:}\;& |A|^{|S|} \\ \text{one sweep:}\;& O\big(|S|\,|A|\,b\big) \\ & \le O\big(|S|^2|A|\big) \\ \text{exact evaluation:}\;& O\big(|S|^3\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: b is the number of possible successors of a state–action pair (3 here). A sweep looks at every pair and its successors. Exact evaluation solves an |S| × |S| linear system by elimination. All polynomial; only the policy count is exponential, and DP never enumerates it.
Say it“There are A to the power S policies, but a sweep costs only S times A times b.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \Vert \mathbf v_k - \mathbf v_* \Vert_\infty &\le \gamma^k\,\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma} \le \varepsilon \\ \iff k &\ge \frac{\ln\!\big(R_{\max} / (\varepsilon(1-\gamma))\big)}{\ln(1/\gamma)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: starting from v0 = 0, the error of value iteration starts at most at Rmax/(1 − γ) (the largest possible value, for rewards between 0 and Rmax) and shrinks by γ per sweep. So the number of sweeps needed for accuracy ε grows roughly like 1/(1 − γ) as γ approaches 1.
Say it“The error after k sweeps is at most gamma to the k times R-max over one minus gamma; so k must be at least the log of R-max over epsilon one-minus-gamma, over the log of one over gamma.”

Real-world project

Project: restocking a warehouse shelf with dynamic programming

Inventory control was one of the first problems dynamic programming was applied to. Here is a real one. The UCI Online Retail data set (Chen, Sain & Guo, 2012; licensed CC BY 4.0) holds every invoice line of a UK-based online gift wholesaler between 1 December 2010 and 9 December 2011, 541,909 of them. From it this page takes one product, stock code 22423, listed in the data as “REGENCY CAKESTAND 3 TIER”, which sold on all but 4 of the shop’s 305 trading days. Its daily demand is the total quantity on that day’s invoices (cancellations and returns removed). Demand from January to June 2011 (147 trading days) estimates the model; July to 9 December 2011 (138 days) is held back to test the plan on demand it has never seen.

The MDP: each evening the manager sees the stock s left on the shelf (0 to 250, the shelf’s capacity) and chooses how many units to order; the delivery arrives overnight, so the next day opens with y = s + order. That day’s demand D is drawn from the empirical distribution; sales are min(y, D), and unmet demand is lost. The price, £12.75, is the product’s median unit price in the data. The costs are assumptions you can change, since the data set doesn’t record them: a unit cost of half the price, a fixed charge K per delivery, a holding cost h per unit left overnight, and an optional goodwill penalty g per unit of unmet demand. There are 251 states and 31,626 state–action pairs, and 10494.9 deterministic policies to choose from.

\[ \begin{aligned} r(s,y,D) &= p\,\min(y,D) \\ &\quad - c\,(y-s) - K\,\mathbb 1[y>s] \\ &\quad - h\,(y-D)^+ \\ &\quad - g\,(D-y)^+ \\ s' &= (y - D)^+ \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the day’s reward is revenue from what was sold, minus the cost of the units ordered, minus the delivery charge if anything was ordered, minus holding cost on what is left and the goodwill penalty on what was missed. The next state is what is left: (x)+ means max(x, 0). The model averages the reward and the next state over the 147 training days’ demands.
Say it“Reward is p times sales minus c times the order minus K if you ordered, minus h times leftovers minus g times shortfall; next state is what's left.”

The same computation in Python

import json, time, numpy as np, scipy.sparse as sp
from scipy.optimize import linprog
J = json.load(open("x068-data.json"))
train = np.array([q for t, q in zip(J["dates"], J["q"]) if "2011-01-01" <= t <= "2011-06-30"])
test = np.array([q for t, q in zip(J["dates"], J["q"]) if t >= "2011-07-01"])
M, p, K, h, g, gam = 250, J["price"], 40.0, 0.20, 0.0, 0.99   # capacity, price (median in data), assumed costs
c = 0.5 * p                                     # assumed unit cost: half the selling price
pd = np.bincount(train) / len(train); dv = np.arange(len(pd))   # empirical demand pmf
S = np.arange(M + 1)                            # state s = units on the shelf at closing time
y_ = S[:, None]                                 # y = s + order = units on the shelf next morning
R = (pd * (p * np.minimum(y_, dv) - h * np.maximum(y_ - dv, 0) - g * np.maximum(dv - y_, 0))).sum(1)
P = np.zeros((M + 1, M + 1))                    # P[y, s'] = Pr(next closing stock s' | morning stock y)
for y in S: np.add.at(P[y], np.maximum(y - dv, 0), pd)
Q_of = lambda v: np.where(S[None, :] >= S[:, None],          # Q[s, y]: order y - s units tonight
    (R + gam * P @ v)[None, :] - c * (S[None, :] - S[:, None]) - K * (S[None, :] > S[:, None]), -np.inf)

def evaluate(y):                                # exact policy evaluation: solve (I - gamma P_pi) v = r_pi
    r = R[y] - c * (y - S) - K * (y > S)
    return np.linalg.solve(np.eye(M + 1) - gam * P[y], r)
t = time.perf_counter(); y = S.copy(); it = 0        # start from "never order"
while True:
    v = evaluate(y); it += 1; Q = Q_of(v); best = Q.argmax(1)
    new = np.where(Q[S, y] >= Q[S, best] - 1e-9, y, best)    # keep the old action on ties
    print(f"policy iteration {it}: v(0) = {v[0]:.4f}, actions changed in {int((new != y).sum())} states")
    if np.array_equal(new, y): break
    y = new
tPI = time.perf_counter() - t
s_lo = int(S[y > S].max()); S_up = int(y[0])
print(f"optimal policy: (s, S) = ({s_lo}, {S_up}); is it exactly (s, S)? {bool(np.all(y == np.where(S <= s_lo, S_up, S)))}")
print(f"v*(0) = {v[0]:.6f}, v*(60) = {v[60]:.6f}, v*(150) = {v[150]:.6f}   ({tPI * 1000:.0f} ms)")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Dynamic programming computes optimal policies when the model p(s′, r | s, a) is known, by turning the Bellman equations into update rules.
  • Policy evaluation sweeps the Bellman expectation backup until values stop changing; policy improvement makes the policy greedy, and the improvement theorem guarantees no state gets worse.
  • Policy iteration alternates the two and stops at an optimal policy in a few rounds; modified policy iteration and value iteration evaluate only partially, and asynchronous DP backs up states in any order it likes.
  • All of these are generalised policy iteration: evaluation and improvement pulling against each other until v = v*; the Bellman optimality equation is also a linear program, minimising ∑v subject to v ≥ every action’s look-ahead.
  • Positive scaling and potential-based shaping never change the optimal policy; adding a constant can, whenever episodes end. DP is polynomial in |S| and |A|; the hard part is that |S| itself explodes with the number of state variables.

Check your understanding

Why does a sequential decision problem with slippery moves need a policy rather than a plan?

A plan is a fixed list of actions that assumes you know where each one leaves you. When moves can fail, you may end up somewhere the plan never considered. A policy says what to do in every state, so it stays useful whatever happens.

In-place policy evaluation usually converges in fewer sweeps than the two-array version. Why?

Within a sweep, states updated later already use the new values of states updated earlier, so information can travel several steps in one sweep instead of exactly one. How much this helps depends on the order of the sweep relative to where the rewards are.

Policy improvement made the policy greedy with respect to vπ, and nothing changed. What can you conclude?

That π takes a best action under its own value function in every state, so vπ satisfies the Bellman optimality equation. Hence vπ = v* and π is optimal.

You add +5 to every reward in an episodic maze. Can the optimal policy change? What if you multiply every reward by 5 instead?

Adding 5 can change it: each step now earns 5, so routes that take longer gain relative to short ones, and the agent may even avoid finishing. Multiplying by 5 cannot: every action value is scaled by the same positive factor, so the ranking of actions is unchanged.

In the LP view of an MDP, what do the tight constraints at the optimum tell you?

A constraint v(s) ≥ look-ahead of action a is tight when that action achieves v*(s), so the tight constraints mark the optimal actions. In the dual, they are the state–action pairs with positive visit counts x(s, a).
