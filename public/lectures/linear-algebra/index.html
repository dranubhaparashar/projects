Linear Algebra for Machine Learning

Vectors, matrices and the handful of ideas every model is built from

A photograph, a sentence turned into embeddings, the weights of a neural network: to a computer each one is a grid of numbers, and linear algebra is the grammar for working with grids. This chapter builds it from the ground up, one picture at a time. With this introduction is a real photograph from the Library of Congress, the Wright Flyer leaving the ground on 17 December 1903, stored as a 229 × 320 matrix of grey levels. Your browser has just split that matrix into simple layers with the singular value decomposition. Drag the slider under the photograph: two dozen layers already show the aeroplane, from a sixth of the numbers.

The objects

Scalars, vectors, matrices and tensors

Linear algebra has four kinds of object, and they differ only in how many indices you need to pick out one number. A scalar is a single number, written in italic: s = 3.5. A vector is an ordered list of numbers, written in bold, x; its i-th entry is xi, and if it has n real entries we say x ∈ ℝn. A matrix is a rectangular grid, a bold capital A ∈ ℝm×n with m rows and n columns; Ai,j is the entry in row i and column j, Ai,: is the whole of row i and A:,j the whole of column j. A tensor is the same idea with three or more axes: a colour image is height × width × 3, and a batch of 64 of them adds a fourth axis.

The photograph in the hero is literally a matrix: 229 rows, 320 columns, one grey level per entry. Below is a small patch of it, cut down to 6 × 8 so you can read the numbers. Two operations come up constantly. The transpose A⊤ mirrors a matrix across its main diagonal, so rows become columns; a column vector transposed is a row vector. And a scalar can be multiplied into, or added to, every entry at once; deep-learning code also adds a vector to every row of a matrix, a shortcut called broadcasting. Switch between the four views, hover the numbers, and turn the two dials: you are changing the photograph’s contrast and brightness with a scalar times a matrix plus a scalar.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A} &= \begin{bmatrix} A_{1,1} & A_{1,2} & \cdots \\ A_{2,1} & A_{2,2} & \cdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{bmatrix} \\ (\mathbf{A}^\top)_{i,j} &= A_{j,i} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first index picks the row, the second the column. Transposing swaps the two indices, so an m × n matrix becomes n × m and the entry at (i, j) moves to (j, i). The diagonal entries A1,1, A2,2, … stay where they are.
Say it“Entry i, j of A-transpose is entry j, i of A.”
\[ \mathbf{D} = a\,\mathbf{B} + c, \qquad D_{i,j} = a\,B_{i,j} + c \]
Read it as: multiplying a matrix by a scalar a scales every entry; adding a scalar c adds it to every entry. Here a is the contrast and c the brightness. The shapes of B and D are the same; nothing moves, every number just changes.
Say it“D is a times B plus c, entry by entry.”

Multiplying

Matrix times vector: a map of the plane

The matrix product C = AB is defined when A has as many columns as B has rows: an m × n matrix times an n × p matrix gives an m × p one, and each entry is a sum of products along a row of A and a column of B. It is not the entry-by-entry product; that one also exists, is called the Hadamard product and is written A ⊙ B. When both factors are vectors of the same length, x⊤y is a single number, the dot product.

Here is the view that makes the rest of the chapter easy. Multiply A by a vector x and you get a weighted sum of A’s columns, with the entries of x as the weights. So the first column is where A sends the arrow (1, 0) and the second column is where it sends (0, 1); once you know those two landing spots, you know where every point goes, because a straight grid stays a grid of parallel, evenly spaced lines. That is what “linear” means. Drag the tips of the two column arrows, and drag the point x; then compose A with a quarter-turn B in both orders and see that the order matters.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} &= x_1\,\mathbf{A}_{:,1} + x_2\,\mathbf{A}_{:,2} \\ (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})_i &= \textstyle\sum_j A_{i,j}\,x_j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: two ways to compute the same vector. Column view: take x1 steps along the first column and x2 along the second. Row view: entry i of the answer is the dot product of row i of A with x.
Say it“A x is x-one times column one plus x-two times column two.”
\[ \begin{aligned} C_{i,j} &= \textstyle\sum_k A_{i,k}\,B_{k,j} \\ (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^\top &= \mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{A}^\top \\ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} &\ne \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A} \ \text{ (in general)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: entry (i, j) of AB is row i of A dotted with column j of B. As maps, ABx means “apply B first, then A”. The product is associative, A(BC) = (AB)C, and distributes over addition, A(B + C) = AB + AC, but swapping the order usually changes the answer. Transposing a product reverses it.
Say it“C i j is the sum over k of A i k times B k j.”

What it costs. Multiplying two n × n matrices this way takes n3 multiply-adds: n2 entries, each a sum of n products. In the big-O notation of algorithm analysis that is O(n3): double n and the work grows eightfold. A matrix-vector product is O(n2). That gap is why a neural network layer, which is a matrix times a whole batch of vectors, is what GPUs are built to do fast.

Undoing a map

Identity, inverse and solving Ax = b

The identity matrix In has 1s on the diagonal and 0s elsewhere; it leaves every vector alone, Inx = x. The inverse A−1 undoes A: A−1A = I. It matters because a set of linear equations is one matrix equation, Ax = b, and if the inverse exists the answer is x = A−1b, one unique solution. Each equation is a line in the plane (a plane, in three unknowns), and the solution is where they all meet. Move the sliders and watch the two lines; then press the button that makes the second row a multiple of the first.

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{I} \\ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b} \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: multiply both sides of Ax = b by A−1 on the left. A−1A collapses to I and Ix is x. This only works when A is square and no column is a combination of the others.
Say it“A-inverse times A is the identity, so x is A-inverse b.”
\[ \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix} \]
Read it as: for a 2 × 2 matrix: swap the diagonal, negate the other two, divide by ad − bc. That divisor is the determinant, and when it is 0 there is no inverse: the two lines are parallel.
Say it“The inverse is one over a d minus b c, times d, minus b, minus c, a.”

How a computer actually solves it

Software almost never forms A−1; it is slower and loses more accuracy to rounding than solving directly. The workhorse is Gaussian elimination: subtract multiples of one row from the rows below it until the matrix is upper triangular, then solve from the bottom up (back-substitution). Before each column it swaps the row with the biggest entry into the pivot position (partial pivoting), which keeps rounding errors small. For n unknowns it takes about n3/3 multiply-adds, O(n3) again. Press Step to watch it solve three equations.

\[ \text{row}_i \;\leftarrow\; \text{row}_i - \frac{A_{i,k}}{A_{k,k}}\,\text{row}_k \]
Read it as: to clear the entry below the pivot Ak,k in a lower row i (i > k), subtract the multiple of the pivot row that makes it zero. The same multiple is applied to the right-hand side, so the system keeps the same solution.
Say it“Row i becomes row i minus A i k over A k k times row k.”

What can you reach?

Linear dependence, span and rank

Ax = b asks: which mix of A’s columns lands on b? All the places any mix can reach form the span of the columns, also called A’s column space. A system has a solution exactly when b lies in it. Two columns that point in different directions span the whole plane; two that point along the same line span only that line, and every b off the line is out of reach.

A set of vectors is linearly dependent when one of them is a mix of the others, so it adds no new direction. The number of genuinely independent columns is the matrix’s rank. A square matrix has an inverse exactly when its columns are independent (full rank); a matrix with dependent columns is called singular. With more columns than dimensions, a dependence is guaranteed, and then any reachable b can be reached in infinitely many ways. Drag the arrows and the target b, line a₂ up with a₁, and add a third column.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{a}_1,\dots,\mathbf{a}_n\} \\ &\quad = \Big\{ \textstyle\sum_i c_i\,\mathbf{a}_i \;:\; c_i \in \mathbb{R} \Big\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the span is every point you can make by stretching each vector by some amount ci (negative allowed) and adding the results. Ax = b is solvable exactly when b is in the span of A’s columns.
Say it“The span is the set of all sums of c-i times a-i.”
\[ \begin{gathered} \textstyle\sum_i c_i\,\mathbf{a}_i = \mathbf{0} \ \text{ with some } c_i \ne 0 \\ \iff\ \text{dependent} \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: if some mix that is not all zeros adds up to the zero vector, one vector can be written in terms of the others, and the set is dependent. If the only way to get zero is c = 0, the vectors are independent, and the rank equals their number.
Say it“The vectors are dependent if a non-zero mix of them gives zero.”

Measuring size

Norms: how big is a vector?

A norm turns a vector into one non-negative number, its size. Any function deserves the name if it is zero only for the zero vector, obeys the triangle inequality ‖x + y‖ ≤ ‖x‖ + ‖y‖, and scales with the vector, ‖αx‖ = |α| ‖x‖. The family of Lp norms covers the ones machine learning uses. L2 is ordinary straight-line length, and its square is simply x⊤x. L1 adds absolute values; it grows at the same rate near zero as far from it, which is why it is used when small entries should become exactly zero (lasso, sparse codes). L∞, the max norm, is the largest absolute entry. Machine-learning papers also speak of an “L0 norm”, the number of non-zero entries, though it fails the scaling rule and is not a true norm.

Each norm has its own unit ball, the set of vectors of size 1: a diamond for L1, a circle for L2, a square for L∞. Slide p and drag x and y: the bold curve is the ball of the chosen norm that passes through x.

\[ \begin{aligned} \lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_p &= \Big(\sum_i |x_i|^p\Big)^{1/p} \\ \lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_\infty &= \max_i |x_i| \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: raise every entry’s size to the power p, add, take the p-th root. p = 1 gives the sum of sizes, p = 2 the usual length; as p grows the biggest entry dominates and the norm approaches the max norm.
Say it“The p-norm of x is the p-th root of the sum of the absolute entries to the p.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{y} &= \lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_2\,\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert_2 \cos\theta \\ \lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_F &= \Big(\sum_{i,j} A_{i,j}^2\Big)^{1/2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the dot product is the two lengths times the cosine of the angle between the vectors: zero when they are at right angles. The Frobenius norm measures a matrix the way L2 measures a vector: square every entry, add, take the root. Here A has x and y as its columns.
Say it“x dot y is the length of x times the length of y times cos theta; the Frobenius norm is the root of the sum of squared entries.”

Matrices with shortcuts

Diagonal, symmetric and orthogonal matrices

Some matrices are much cheaper and better behaved than the general kind, and they turn up everywhere. A diagonal matrix is zero off its diagonal; diag(v) times x just multiplies entry by entry, v ⊙ x, and its inverse simply inverts each diagonal entry. A symmetric matrix equals its own transpose, A = A⊤; distance tables and covariance matrices are symmetric because the (i, j) and (j, i) entries measure the same thing.

A unit vector has length 1. Two vectors are orthogonal when x⊤y = 0, at right angles; orthonormal when they are also unit vectors. An orthogonal matrix has orthonormal columns (and rows), so Q⊤Q = I and its inverse is just its transpose, free to compute. Geometrically it is a rotation or a reflection: it moves vectors without changing any length or angle. Pick a kind of matrix and turn its dials: watch which ones keep the circle a circle.

\[ \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{v})\,\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{v} \odot \mathbf{x}, \qquad \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}^\top \]
Read it as: a diagonal matrix stretches each axis on its own, by its own factor. A symmetric matrix is a mirror image of itself across the diagonal: Ai,j = Aj,i.
Say it“diag v times x is v times x entry by entry; a symmetric A equals its transpose.”
\[ \mathbf{Q}^\top\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^\top = \mathbf{I}, \qquad \mathbf{Q}^{-1} = \mathbf{Q}^\top \]
Read it as: entry (i, j) of Q⊤Q is column i dotted with column j. Getting the identity says each column has length 1 and every pair is at right angles. Because ‖Qx‖2 = x⊤Q⊤Qx = x⊤x, lengths never change.
Say it“Q-transpose Q is the identity, so Q’s inverse is its transpose.”

Natural axes

Eigenvectors: directions a matrix only stretches

Most vectors get knocked off their line by a matrix: Ax points somewhere else. A few special directions do not. An eigenvector v of a square matrix A is a non-zero vector that A only scales, Av = λv, and the scale factor λ is its eigenvalue. Any rescaled eigenvector is still an eigenvector, so we usually quote unit ones. Collect the eigenvectors as the columns of V and the eigenvalues in a diagonal Λ, and A = VΛV−1: change to the eigenvector axes, stretch each one, change back. That is the eigendecomposition.

Not every matrix has one made of real numbers: a rotation leaves no direction on its own line, and a shear has only one eigen-direction where it needs two. Real symmetric matrices are the happy case. Their eigenvalues are always real, and their eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormal, so A = QΛQ⊤ with an orthogonal Q. If every eigenvalue is positive the matrix is positive definite: x⊤Ax > 0 for every x ≠ 0. Covariance matrices and the curvature of a loss at a minimum are of this kind (with ≥ 0, positive semidefinite). Turn x round the circle, or press Spin, and watch for the moments when x and Ax line up.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} &= \lambda\mathbf{v} \\ \det(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I}) &= 0 \\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda^2 - \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A})\,\lambda + \det\mathbf{A} &= 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: (A − λI)v = 0 has a non-zero solution v only if A − λI is singular, i.e. its determinant is zero. For a 2 × 2 matrix that determinant is a quadratic in λ whose coefficients are the trace and the determinant, so the two eigenvalues come from the quadratic formula.
Say it“A v equals lambda v; the lambdas are the roots of the determinant of A minus lambda I.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A} &= \mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{Q}^\top = \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i\,\mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^\top \\ \mathbf{A}^{-1} &= \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i^{-1}\,\mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^\top \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for symmetric A, the matrix is a sum of simple pieces, one per eigenvector: qiqi⊤ picks out the part of x along qi, and λi scales it. The inverse uses the same eigenvectors with every eigenvalue flipped to 1/λi, so it exists only if no λi is zero.
Say it“A is the sum of lambda-i q-i q-i transpose; its inverse swaps each lambda for one over lambda.”
\[ \begin{gathered} \lambda_{\min} \;\le\; \mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x} \;\le\; \lambda_{\max} \\ \text{for symmetric } \mathbf{A} \text{ and } \lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_2 = 1 \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: x⊤Ax is a quadratic form, one number per direction. Over all unit vectors it is largest along the top eigenvector, where it equals λmax, and smallest along the bottom one. This is the fact behind principal component analysis: the direction of greatest variance is the top eigenvector of the covariance matrix.
Say it“For a unit x, x-transpose A x lies between the smallest and largest eigenvalues.”

Any matrix at all

The singular value decomposition

Eigendecomposition needs a square matrix and can fail even then. The singular value decomposition never fails: every real matrix, of any shape, can be written A = UDV⊤. U and V are orthogonal, and D is diagonal with entries σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ … ≥ 0, the singular values. The columns of V are the right singular vectors and those of U the left singular vectors. Read from right to left it says that every linear map does the same three things: turn (V⊤), stretch along the axes (D), turn again (U). The unit circle always becomes an ellipse whose half-axes are the singular values.

The two decompositions are cousins. The right singular vectors are the eigenvectors of A⊤A, the left ones are the eigenvectors of AA⊤, and the singular values are the square roots of their eigenvalues. Keeping only the biggest terms gives the best possible low-rank copy of a matrix, which is exactly what the hero photograph is doing. (Inverting the non-zero σs gives the pseudoinverse, A+ = VD+U⊤, a stand-in for the inverse of a matrix that has none.) Drag the column arrows, then play the three stages.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A} &= \mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^\top, \quad \mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i\,\mathbf{u}_i \\ \mathbf{A}^\top\mathbf{A} &= \mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}^2\mathbf{V}^\top \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: A sends its i-th right singular vector to its i-th left singular vector, stretched by σi. Substituting A = UDV⊤ into A⊤A, the U⊤U in the middle becomes I, leaving an eigendecomposition with eigenvalues σi2.
Say it“A is U D V-transpose; A sends v-i to sigma-i times u-i.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{A}_k &= \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i\,\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^\top \\ \lVert \mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}_k \rVert_F^2 &= \textstyle\sum_{i>k} \sigma_i^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the SVD writes A as a sum of rank-one layers, each an outer product uivi⊤ scaled by σi. Stop after k layers and the leftover error, in the Frobenius norm, is exactly the singular values you dropped. Eckart and Young proved (1936) that no rank-k matrix gets closer.
Say it“A-k is the sum of the first k layers; its squared error is the sum of the dropped sigmas squared.”

Two summary numbers

Trace and determinant: what a matrix does to area

Two single numbers summarise a square matrix surprisingly well. The trace is the sum of the diagonal entries. It has a handy property: inside a trace you may rotate a product, Tr(ABC) = Tr(CAB) = Tr(BCA), even though the products themselves differ. It also gives the Frobenius norm a second formula, which is often how it is used in derivations.

The determinant measures how the map changes size. The unit square, with sides along the axes, lands on the parallelogram spanned by A’s columns, and that parallelogram’s area is |det A|. Every other shape’s area is scaled by the same factor. In three dimensions it is the volume of the box the columns span, and so on upwards. A negative determinant means the map also flips space over (the F comes out mirrored); a determinant of zero means space is squashed flat onto a line, which is exactly when there is no inverse. And both numbers connect to the eigenvalues: the trace is their sum and the determinant their product. Drag the columns, and flatten the square.

\[ \begin{aligned} \det\mathbf{A} &= \textstyle\prod_i \lambda_i \\ \det\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} &= ad - bc \\ \det(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}) &= \det\mathbf{A}\,\det\mathbf{B} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the area scale factor is the product of the eigenvalues, and for a 2 × 2 matrix it is ad − bc, the signed area of the parallelogram. Doing two maps in a row multiplies their area factors.
Say it“The determinant is the product of the eigenvalues; for two by two, a d minus b c.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A}) &= \textstyle\sum_i A_{i,i} = \sum_i \lambda_i \\ \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}) &= \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}) \\ \lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_F &= \sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^\top)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: add up the diagonal and you have added up the eigenvalues. A product can be cycled inside the trace. The diagonal of AA⊤ holds each row’s squared length, so its trace is the sum of every squared entry. Here B is the fixed shear with rows (1, 1) and (0, 1).
Say it“The trace is the sum of the diagonal, which equals the sum of the eigenvalues.”

Real-world project

Project: the first flight in k layers

On 17 December 1903 at Kill Devil Hills, near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, John T. Daniels squeezed the shutter of a camera Orville Wright had set up, and caught the Wright Flyer a moment after take-off, with Wilbur running alongside. The glass-plate negative is in the Library of Congress (reproduction number LC-DIG-ppprs-00626) and is in the public domain. We took the library’s 1024 × 738 scan, trimmed its black border, and resized it once to 320 × 229 grey levels between 0 and 1: a matrix A with 73,280 entries.

When the page loaded, your browser computed A’s full SVD with the one-sided Jacobi method: it rotates pairs of columns until every pair is at right angles, and the lengths of the columns that remain are the singular values.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{stored} &= k\,(m + n + 1) \ \text{ vs } \ mn \\ \text{rel. error} &= \frac{\lVert\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{A}_k\rVert_F}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_F} \\ &= \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2}{\sum_i \sigma_i^2}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each layer costs one column of U (m = 229 numbers), one row of V⊤ (n = 320) and one σ. The error is the size of what was left out, relative to the size of the photograph; by Eckart–Young it can be read straight off the singular values, because ‖A‖F2 = Σ σi2.
Say it“We store k times m plus n plus one numbers, and the relative error is the root of the dropped sigma-squareds over all of them.”

Error against k, and why photographs compress

The left plot shows the singular values on a log scale; the right one the relative error as k grows. The first singular value is huge because the photograph is mostly mid-grey sky and sand: layer 1 is little more than the average brightness of each row times that of each column. After that the values fall away steadily, because neighbouring rows of a photograph are nearly alike. To check that this is structure and not luck, shuffle the pixels: the same 73,280 grey levels in random places, decomposed again from scratch.

The same project in NumPy

import numpy as np

A = np.fromfile("x002-wright.u8", dtype=np.uint8).reshape(229, 320) / 255   # grey levels in [0, 1]
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)                            # A = U diag(s) Vt
m, n = A.shape
print(f"A is {m} x {n}, rank {np.linalg.matrix_rank(A)}, ||A||_F = {np.linalg.norm(A):.4f}")
print("first singular values:", np.round(s[:5], 4), " smallest:", f"{s[-1]:.6f}")

for k in [1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 30, 50, 100]:
    Ak = (U[:, :k] * s[:k]) @ Vt[:k]                                         # sum of k rank-one layers
    err = np.linalg.norm(A - Ak) / np.linalg.norm(A)
    tail = np.sqrt((s[k:] ** 2).sum()) / np.linalg.norm(A)                   # Eckart-Young: same number
    psnr = 10 * np.log10(1 / np.mean((A - Ak) ** 2))
    print(f"k = {k:3d}  stored {k*(m+n+1)/(m*n):6.1%}  relative error {err:.4f}"
          f"  (tail of s: {tail:.4f})  PSNR {psnr:5.2f} dB")

def mulberry32(a):                         # the page's random generator, so both shuffle alike
    def r():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = (t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return r
r, p = mulberry32(1903), list(range(m * n))
for i in range(m * n - 1, 0, -1):          # Fisher-Yates shuffle of the pixels
    j = int(r() * (i + 1)); p[i], p[j] = p[j], p[i]
B = A.ravel()[p].reshape(m, n)              # same pixels, structure destroyed
sb = np.linalg.svd(B, compute_uv=False)
for k in [10, 30]:
    print(f"shuffled pixels, k = {k}: relative error {np.sqrt((sb[k:]**2).sum()) / np.linalg.norm(B):.4f}")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Scalars, vectors, matrices and tensors are numbers on 0, 1, 2 or more axes; a photograph is a matrix and a batch of colour images is a 4-axis tensor.
  • A matrix is a map: Ax is a mix of A’s columns weighted by x. Products compose maps, in an order that matters, and cost O(n3) for square matrices.
  • Ax = b has a solution when b is in the span of the columns, exactly one when the columns are also independent (A invertible, det A ≠ 0). Solve it by elimination, not by forming A−1.
  • Norms measure size: L1, L2, max and Frobenius. Orthogonal matrices keep every length; symmetric ones have real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors, A = QΛQ⊤.
  • Every matrix has an SVD, A = UDV⊤; dropping the small σs gives the best low-rank copy, which rebuilt the 1903 photograph to 5% error from 13 layers, under 10% of its numbers. Trace = Σλ, det = Πλ = area scale.

Check your understanding

A is 3 × 5 and B is 5 × 2. Which of AB, BA and A⊤B⊤ exist, and what shape are they?

AB exists and is 3 × 2 (A’s 5 columns match B’s 5 rows). BA does not: B has 2 columns but A has 3 rows. A⊤B⊤ is 5 × 3 times 2 × 5, which does not match either; B⊤A⊤ = (AB)⊤ is the one that works, 2 × 3.

The columns of a 3 × 3 matrix are (1, 0, 1), (0, 1, 1) and (1, 1, 2). Is it invertible?

No. The third column is the sum of the first two, so the columns are dependent, the rank is 2 and the determinant is 0. The three columns span only a plane, and any b off that plane has no solution.

Why is the inverse of an orthogonal matrix so cheap, and what does that say about lengths?

Q⊤Q = I means Q⊤ already undoes Q, so the inverse is a transpose: no arithmetic at all. And ‖Qx‖2 = x⊤Q⊤Qx = ‖x‖2, so lengths (and angles) are preserved: Q is a rotation or a reflection.

A symmetric 2 × 2 matrix has trace 5 and determinant 4. What are its eigenvalues, and is it positive definite?

They solve λ2 − 5λ + 4 = 0, so λ = 4 and 1. Both are positive, so yes: x⊤Ax > 0 for every non-zero x, and the unit circle maps to an ellipse with half-axes 4 and 1.

A 229 × 320 photograph has singular values whose squares add to 33,378. The ones you drop at k = 20 have squares adding to 55. What is the relative error?

√(55 / 33,378) ≈ 0.041, about 4%. By Eckart–Young that is the Frobenius error of the best possible rank-20 copy, and it costs 20 × (229 + 320 + 1) = 11,000 numbers instead of 73,280.
