Kernel PCA & Spectral Clustering

With principal curves and sparse PCA: four ways to bend, thin out or rewire the principal axes

Principal component analysis finds the best straight lines and flat planes through a cloud of points. Real data is rarely that tidy: it curls into arcs, nests in rings and hangs together in chains. This chapter keeps PCA’s central tool, an eigenvector problem, and changes what it is applied to. Kernel PCA runs it in a feature space you never build. Principal curves let the line bend. Spectral clustering runs it on a graph of who is close to whom. Sparse PCA forces most loadings to zero so you can read them. On the right, the points are coloured by spectral clustering. Drag dots into the gap between the two groups to build a bridge, and watch the eigenvalue gap close until the split goes wrong.

The starting point

Where a straight line falls short

Chapter 1.13 met principal components as directions of greatest variance. Here is the other way to see them, the one this chapter will bend. Look for the flat surface, a line when q = 1 or a plane when q = 2, that passes as close as possible to every point. Each point is replaced by its nearest point on that surface, and the cost is the total squared distance between the two. The best surface goes through the mean, and its directions are the leading eigenvectors of the covariance matrix: the principal components.

This works beautifully for elongated, roughly elliptical clouds. It fails quietly when the cloud bends. Every residual is measured straight to a line, so on an arc the line cuts across the chord and the tips and the middle are all far from it. Switch between the shapes and drag the dots: watch how much variance one straight line can capture.

\[ \begin{aligned} &f(\boldsymbol\lambda) = \boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf{V}_q\boldsymbol\lambda \\ &\min_{\boldsymbol\mu,\,\{\boldsymbol\lambda_i\},\,\mathbf{V}_q}\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\big\lVert x_i - \boldsymbol\mu - \mathbf{V}_q\boldsymbol\lambda_i\big\rVert^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: f is a flat surface of dimension q: start at μ and move λ units along the q orthonormal columns of Vq. Each point xi gets its own coordinates λi on that surface. Choose the surface and the coordinates to make the total squared miss as small as possible. The answer: μ = x̄, λi = Vq⊤(xi − x̄), and Vq holds the top q principal directions.
Say it“f of lambda is mu plus V-q lambda; minimise the sum of squared distances from each x-i to its point on the surface.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^\top \\ &\frac1N\sum_{i}\big\lVert x_i - \hat x_i\big\rVert^2 = \sum_{j>q}\lambda_j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the singular value decomposition of the centred data matrix X gives the directions V and the scores UD. Keep q directions and the average squared miss is the sum of the covariance eigenvalues you threw away, λj = dj²/N. In two dimensions with q = 1, the miss is just the smaller eigenvalue λ2.
Say it“X equals U D V-transpose; the mean squared miss is the sum of the eigenvalues beyond q.”

Bishop 12.3

PCA in a feature space you never build

Chapter 1.9 bent a straight classifier by lifting each point x into a richer space φ(x), then cutting with a straight line up there. The same trick works for PCA. Map every point to φ(x) and do ordinary PCA on the lifted points: a straight principal axis in feature space becomes a curved one back in the plane.

The snag is that φ(x) may have thousands of coordinates, or infinitely many. Schölkopf, Smola and Müller (1998) noticed that every eigenvector of the feature-space covariance is a weighted sum of the lifted data points. Substitute that sum into the eigenvector equation and every φ appears inside a dot product, which a kernel k(x, x′) = φ(x)⊤φ(x′) computes directly. The D-dimensional eigenproblem becomes an N×N one, built from kernel values alone. For now, assume the lifted points have mean zero; the next section removes that assumption.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{C} &= \frac1N\sum_{n=1}^{N}\phi(x_n)\,\phi(x_n)^\top \\ \mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{v}_i &= \lambda_i\,\mathbf{v}_i \\ \mathbf{v}_i &= \sum_{n=1}^{N} a_{in}\,\phi(x_n) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: C is the covariance matrix of the lifted points, and its eigenvectors vi are the principal axes in feature space. Because C vi is a combination of the φ(xn), so is vi itself (when λi > 0): it is fixed by N numbers ai1, …, aiN, however large the feature space.
Say it“C is one over N times the sum of phi of x-n times its transpose; C v-i equals lambda-i v-i; and v-i is a weighted sum of the phis.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{K}\,\mathbf{a}_i = \lambda_i N\,\mathbf{a}_i \\ &\lambda_i N\;\mathbf{a}_i^\top\mathbf{a}_i = 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: put the sum into C vi = λivi and take dot products with each φ(xl). What remains is an eigenproblem for the N×N Gram matrix K, Knm = k(xn, xm). Its eigenvalues are N times the feature-space variances. The second line rescales ai so that vi has length 1, since vi⊤vi = ai⊤K ai.
Say it“K a-i equals lambda-i N a-i, with lambda-i N times a-i dot a-i equal to one.”
\[ y_i(x) = \phi(x)^\top\mathbf{v}_i = \sum_{n=1}^{N} a_{in}\,k(x, x_n) \]
Read it as: the i-th principal component score of any point, old or new, is a weighted sum of its kernel values with the training points. Again φ never appears. With the plain dot product k(x, z) = x⊤z this is ordinary PCA, worked through an N×N matrix instead of a D×D one.
Say it“y-i of x is phi of x dot v-i, which is the sum over n of a-i-n times k of x and x-n.”

Check the trick yourself. The quadratic kernel (x⊤z)² is exactly the dot product of the 3-dimensional lift φ(x) = (x1², √2 x1x2, x2²), and (1 + x⊤z)² is the dot product of a 6-dimensional lift. Below, the page does PCA twice: once by building the lifted points explicitly, once from the kernel matrix alone. Pick a kernel and drag the points: the two sets of variances agree to the last digit, and the kernel route never reports more non-zero variances than the lift has dimensions.

Bishop 12.3, continued

Centring, new points and the Gaussian kernel

PCA needs centred data, but we cannot subtract the mean of the φ(xn) when we never compute them. We can do the next best thing: work out what the kernel matrix would be after centring. Expanding (φ(xn) − φ̄)⊤(φ(xm) − φ̄) gives four terms, each a kernel value or an average of kernel values. The same correction applies to a new point’s kernel values before projecting it.

The most popular choice is the Gaussian kernel, whose feature space has infinitely many dimensions. Then kernel PCA can produce as many components as there are data points, far more than the 2 coordinates we started with. The early components tell the clusters apart; later ones split single clusters in two, then in quarters, like the first few vibration patterns of a drum. Click any of the eight small maps to enlarge it, and turn the width c to see how the components change.

\[ \begin{aligned} \widetilde{\mathbf{K}} &= \mathbf{K} - \mathbf{1}_N\mathbf{K} \\ &\quad - \mathbf{K}\mathbf{1}_N + \mathbf{1}_N\mathbf{K}\mathbf{1}_N \\ (\mathbf{1}_N)_{nm} &= 1/N \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: from every kernel value subtract its row average and its column average, then add back the grand average. The result is the Gram matrix of the centred lifted points, so its eigenvectors give properly centred principal components. A new point x is treated the same way: from each k(x, xn) subtract x’s own average kernel value and column n’s average, and add the grand average.
Say it“K-tilde is K minus one-N K minus K one-N plus one-N K one-N, where every entry of one-N is one over N.”
\[ k(x, x') = \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\lVert x - x'\rVert^2}{c}\Big) \]
Read it as: the similarity is 1 for identical points and falls towards 0 as they separate; c sets the distance scale (it plays the role of 1/γ in the SVM chapter). Small c: each point only “sees” its close neighbours, so components pick out tight groups. Large c: everything looks alike, and the components start to resemble ordinary PCA’s smooth gradients.
Say it“k of x and x-prime is e to the minus squared distance over c.”

Two prices come with the trick. First, cost: the eigenproblem is N×N, so it grows with the number of points, not features; for a million points you need approximations such as the Nyström method or random features. Second, there is no easy way back. Ordinary PCA reconstructs a point as x̄ + Vqλ. Kernel PCA’s reconstruction lives in feature space, and in general no point x in the original space maps onto it exactly. Finding the closest one, the pre-image problem, needs its own optimisation. That is why kernel PCA is used to describe and separate data far more often than to compress and rebuild it.

ESL 14.5.2

Principal curves: a line that bends to the data

Kernel PCA bends the axes indirectly, through the kernel. Hastie and Stuetzle (1989) bent them directly. A straight principal component has a quiet property: take all the points that project to the same spot on the line, average them, and you land on that spot. They called this self-consistency and made it the definition. A principal curve is any smooth curve with the same property: every point on it is the average of the data that projects there. The straight line is the special case; a curve is free to follow an arc or a wave.

The definition suggests an algorithm. Start with the first principal component line. Then alternate two steps: project every point onto the current curve to read off its position λi along it, and average, replacing the curve by a scatterplot smooth of each coordinate against λ. Repeat until the average squared distance D² stops falling. Press Step a few times, then try a small and a large span for the smoother.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\lambda_f(x) = \arg\min_{\lambda}\ \lVert x - f(\lambda)\rVert \\ &f(\lambda) = \mathrm{E}\big(X \mid \lambda_f(X) = \lambda\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: λf(x), the projection index, is the position along the curve f of the point closest to x (the largest such λ if there is a tie). The curve is self-consistent when, at every position λ, f(λ) equals the average of all the X that project there. The algorithm estimates that conditional average with a smoother, one coordinate at a time.
Say it“Lambda-f of x is the position of the nearest point on the curve; f of lambda is the expected X given that X projects to lambda.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\{m_j\}_{j=1}^{k} = \arg\min \\ &\quad\mathrm{E}\,\min_j \lVert X - m_j\rVert^2 \\ &f(\lambda_1,\lambda_2) = \\ &\quad\mathrm{E}\big(X \mid \lambda_f(X) = (\lambda_1,\lambda_2)\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first line defines k principal points: the k centres that make the expected squared distance to the nearest centre smallest. For a sample they are the k-means centres, and they are self-consistent too, since each centre is the mean of the points closest to it. Principal curves are the smooth, continuous version. The second line is a principal surface: the same condition with two coordinates. A self-organising map is a discretised principal surface, a grid of prototypes constrained to lie on a smooth sheet.
Say it“Principal points minimise the expected squared distance to the nearest centre; a principal surface f of lambda-one, lambda-two is the expected X given that X projects there.”

ESL 14.5.3, part 1

A graph and its Laplacian

Spectral clustering starts by forgetting coordinates. It keeps only how similar each pair of points is, as a weighted graph: points are nodes, and an edge of weight wii′ ≥ 0 joins similar pairs. A group is then a set of nodes with heavy edges inside it and light edges to everything else. The graph’s Laplacian turns that idea into linear algebra.

Give each node a number fi. The quadratic form f⊤L f adds up wii′(fi − fi′)² over the edges: it is small when strongly connected nodes get similar numbers. A constant f costs nothing, so L always has eigenvalue 0. If the graph falls into separate pieces, any f that is constant on each piece also costs nothing, so the number of zero eigenvalues counts the pieces. When the pieces are joined by a few weak edges, the second-smallest eigenvalue is small and its eigenvector, the Fiedler vector, is nearly constant on each piece with opposite signs. Lower the bridge weights to zero and watch λ2 fall to zero with them.

\[ \begin{aligned} g_i &= \sum_{i'} w_{ii'} \\ \mathbf{G} &= \mathrm{diag}(g_1,\dots,g_N) \\ \mathbf{L} &= \mathbf{G} - \mathbf{W} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: W is the N×N matrix of edge weights (zero on the diagonal). gi, the degree of node i, is the total weight of its edges. The unnormalised graph Laplacian L subtracts W from the diagonal matrix of degrees, so every row of L sums to zero.
Say it“g-i is the sum of node i’s edge weights; L equals G minus W.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{f}^\top\mathbf{L}\,\mathbf{f} &= \sum_i g_i f_i^2 - \sum_{i,i'} f_i f_{i'} w_{ii'} \\ &= \frac12\sum_{i,i'} w_{ii'}\,(f_i - f_{i'})^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the cost of a labelling f is half the weighted sum of squared differences across every edge (each edge appears twice in the double sum, hence the half). It is never negative, so L’s eigenvalues are all ≥ 0; for a unit-length eigenvector f, the cost equals its eigenvalue.
Say it“f-transpose L f equals one half the sum over pairs of w-i-i-prime times f-i minus f-i-prime, squared.”

ESL 14.5.3, part 2

Spectral clustering: cut the graph, not the space

k-means draws straight boundaries between centres, so it cannot separate two nested rings. Spectral clustering sidesteps the geometry. Build a similarity graph from the points, take the m eigenvectors of its Laplacian with the smallest eigenvalues, and use them as new coordinates: point i becomes row i of the N×m matrix Z. In those coordinates, points in the same well-connected group nearly coincide, and plain k-means finishes the job.

There are three choices to make. The graph: a Gaussian similarity between every pair (scale c), or a K-nearest-neighbour graph that keeps only local edges. The Laplacian: L itself, or a normalised one that divides each row by the degree, so that a few busy nodes cannot dominate. And the number of groups, which the eigenvalues suggest: look for a jump, the eigengap, after the k-th eigenvalue. Try the rings with each graph, then shrink the neighbourhood until the graph falls apart.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{Gaussian: } w_{ii'} = \exp\!\big(-d_{ii'}^2/c\big) \\ &\text{mutual } K\text{-NN: } w_{ii'} = 1 \text{ if} \\ &\quad i' \in \mathcal{N}_K(i) \text{ and } i \in \mathcal{N}_K(i') \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: two ways to weight an edge. The Gaussian graph links every pair, with weight decaying in the squared distance dii′². The mutual K-nearest-neighbour graph keeps an edge only when each point is among the other’s K nearest neighbours, which keeps the graph sparse and local. The “symmetric” K-NN variant keeps the edge when either point is among the other’s neighbours.
Say it“w-i-i-prime is e to the minus d squared over c; or one when i and i-prime are each among the other’s K nearest neighbours.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \widetilde{\mathbf{L}} &= \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{G}^{-1}\mathbf{W} \\ \mathbf{Z} &= [\,\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_m\,] \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times m} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the normalised Laplacian divides each node’s row by its degree, so it measures differences relative to how connected a node is. Take its m eigenvectors with the smallest eigenvalues as the columns of the N×m matrix Z, and cluster the N rows of Z with k-means: those clusters are the answer. With m = k and a graph of k separate pieces, the rows of Z take just k distinct values, and k-means cannot fail.
Say it“L-tilde is I minus G-inverse W; stack the bottom m eigenvectors into Z and run k-means on its rows.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{\mathbf{f}}\ \mathbf{f}^\top\mathbf{L}\,\mathbf{f} \\ &\text{s.t.}\ \ \mathbf{f}^\top\mathbf{1} = 0,\ \ \mathbf{f}^\top\mathbf{f} = 1 \\ &\Longrightarrow\ \mathbf{f} = \mathbf{u}_2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: why the eigenvectors? Labelling nodes ±1 and minimising f⊤L f would find the lightest cut, but that is a hard combinatorial problem, and it happily chops off a single node. Relax the labels to any real numbers, insist they average to zero (so the constant vector is excluded) and have unit length, and the minimiser is the Fiedler vector u2. Its signs give the two-way split; more eigenvectors give more groups. Normalising by degree turns the lightest cut into a balanced one, the normalised cut of Shi and Malik.
Say it“Minimise f-transpose L f over unit-length f orthogonal to the ones vector; the answer is the second eigenvector.”

ESL 14.5.4

Kernel PCA and spectral clustering are relatives

The last two ideas look different, one about variance in a feature space and one about cuts in a graph, but they meet. ESL writes kernel PCA in the language of the singular value decomposition. Double-centre the kernel matrix, factor it as U D² U⊤, and the m-th kernel principal component, evaluated at the N training points, is the column um scaled by dm. (Bishop’s λmN is ESL’s dm².)

Now choose the kernel to be the pseudo-inverse of a graph Laplacian, K = L+. It has the same eigenvectors as L with the eigenvalues inverted, 1/λ, and since L+1 = 0 it is already centred. So its leading kernel principal components are exactly the Laplacian’s smallest non-trivial eigenvectors: spectral clustering is kernel PCA with a graph kernel (Ham, Lee, Mika and Schölkopf, 2004). One catch: the graph must be connected. If it falls into pieces, the vectors that tell the pieces apart have eigenvalue 0, and L+ throws them away. So here the graph links every pair with Gaussian weight e−d²/s, where a small scale s leaves far-apart pairs almost, but not quite, unlinked. The Gaussian kernel PCA on the left, by contrast, measures similarity through straight-line distance alone, so it can struggle where the graph succeeds. Turn both dials and compare the two maps.

\[ \begin{aligned} \widetilde{\mathbf{K}} &= (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{M})\,\mathbf{K}\,(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{M}) \\ &= \mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}^2\mathbf{U}^\top \\ \mathbf{z}_m &= \mathbf{u}_m d_m \\ z_{im} &= \sum_{j=1}^{N} \alpha_{jm}\,\widetilde{K}(x_i, x_j) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: M = 11⊤/N, so (I − M) subtracts means and the first line is the same double-centring as before, written as a matrix product. zm, the N-vector of the m-th component’s scores, is the m-th eigenvector times dm. The weights αm = um/dm write each score as a kernel expansion, which also scores new points.
Say it“K-tilde is I minus M, times K, times I minus M, which equals U D-squared U-transpose; z-m is u-m times d-m.”
\[ \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{L}^{+} = \sum_{j:\,\lambda_j > 0} \frac{1}{\lambda_j}\,\mathbf{u}_j\mathbf{u}_j^\top \]
Read it as: the pseudo-inverse keeps L’s eigenvectors, flips each non-zero eigenvalue to 1/λj, and drops the zero ones. The smallest Laplacian eigenvalue that is not zero becomes the largest kernel eigenvalue, so kernel PCA with this kernel ranks the Fiedler vector first. Its score vector is u2 scaled by 1/√λ2.
Say it“K equals L-plus, the sum over positive eigenvalues of one over lambda-j times u-j u-j transpose.”

ESL 14.5.5

Sparse principal components

The other weakness of PCA is not shape but readability. Each principal component mixes every variable, with loadings that are rarely zero, so “what does PC1 mean?” gets a vague answer. Sparse PCA asks for directions that capture nearly as much variance while using only a few variables, by adding the lasso’s L1 penalty from chapter 1.6, which sets small coefficients exactly to zero.

Jolliffe, Trendafilov and Uddin (2003) put the L1 bound straight into the variance-maximising problem (SCoTLASS), which is hard to optimise. Zou, Hastie and Tibshirani (2006) instead used the reconstruction view from the first section: approximate each xi by θ v⊤xi, and penalise v. With θ fixed, finding v is an elastic-net regression; with v fixed, θ comes from a small SVD. Alternate until both settle. Below, the data are 13 chemical measurements of 178 Italian wines, standardised so the analysis uses their correlation matrix. Raise the penalty and watch the loadings drop out one by one, while the variance explained barely moves at first.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{\mathbf{v}}\ \mathbf{v}^\top(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})\,\mathbf{v} \\ &\text{s.t.}\ \ \sum_{j=1}^{p}|v_j| \le t,\ \ \mathbf{v}^\top\mathbf{v} = 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: SCoTLASS: the usual “direction of greatest variance” problem, with an extra budget t on the sum of absolute loadings. A tight budget pushes most loadings to exactly zero. The trouble is that maximising a convex function under these constraints has many local optima.
Say it“Maximise v-transpose X-transpose-X v, subject to the absolute loadings summing to at most t and v having unit length.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \min_{\boldsymbol\theta,\,\mathbf{v}}\ &\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big\lVert x_i - \boldsymbol\theta\,\mathbf{v}^\top x_i\big\rVert^2 \\ &+ \lambda\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^2 + \lambda_1\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert_1 \\ \text{s.t.}\ \ &\lVert\boldsymbol\theta\rVert = 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: v⊤xi is a one-number summary of xi, and θ maps it back to a reconstruction. With λ1 = 0 the best v points along the first principal component (for any ridge λ > 0). The lasso term λ1‖v‖1 then zeroes loadings, and the ridge term keeps the problem well posed even when p > N. For K components, θ and v become p×K matrices Θ and V with Θ⊤Θ = I, and each column of V gets its own penalties.
Say it“Minimise the reconstruction error of x-i by theta v-transpose x-i, plus lambda times v squared, plus lambda-one times the absolute values of v, with theta of unit length.”

Choosing

When each beats plain PCA

Plain PCA remains the default: it is fast, unique, invertible and easy to explain. Reach for one of its relatives only when its specific weakness bites. The table sums up when that is.

PCAbest flat surface; uncorrelated directions of greatest variance(the baseline) data are roughly elliptical; you need to compress and reconstructmisses curved structure; every loading non-zero
Kernel PCAstraight axes in a feature space, curved in the original onegroups or structure that are separable after a nonlinear lift: rings, clusters, curved manifoldsN×N eigenproblem; kernel width must be tuned; no direct reconstruction (pre-image problem)

Real-world project

Project: a karate club and the US flight network

Spectral clustering was made for data that are already graphs. We try it on two real networks, one tiny and famous, one large and messy.

In 1970–72 the anthropologist Wayne Zachary watched a university karate club in the United States. He recorded who socialised with whom outside the club: 34 members and 78 friendships. During his study, a dispute between the instructor, “Mr Hi” (member 1), and the club officer (member 34) split the club in two, and Zachary recorded which side each member joined. The same project in Python

import json, numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import SpectralClustering, KMeans

D = json.load(open("x041-data.json"))     # karate club (from NetworkX) + OpenFlights US routes

def laplacian_eig(W, kind):
    """kind 'un': L = G - W.   kind 'rw': L_rw = I - G^-1 W (via the symmetric form)."""
    g = W.sum(1)
    if kind == "un":
        return np.linalg.eigh(np.diag(g) - W)
    s = 1 / np.sqrt(g)
    ev, U = np.linalg.eigh(np.eye(len(g)) - s[:, None] * W * s[None, :])
    U = U * s[:, None]                       # eigenvectors of L_rw
    return ev, U / np.linalg.norm(U, axis=0)

# ---- Zachary's karate club: 34 members, 78 friendships ----
A = np.zeros((34, 34))
for i, j, w in D["karate"]["edges"]:
    A[i, j] = A[j, i] = 1
fac = np.array(D["karate"]["fac"])           # 0 = Mr Hi's club, 1 = the officer's club
for kind in ("un", "rw"):
    ev, U = laplacian_eig(A, kind)
    lab = (U[:, 1] > 0).astype(int)
    if (lab == fac).sum() < 17: lab = 1 - lab
    wrong = [int(i) + 1 for i in np.nonzero(lab != fac)[0]]
    print(f"karate {kind}: lambda2 = {ev[1]:.4f}, Fiedler split matches {(lab == fac).sum()}/34, wrong: {wrong}")
sc = SpectralClustering(2, affinity="precomputed", random_state=0).fit_predict(A)
print(f"karate sklearn SpectralClustering: matches {max((sc == fac).sum(), (sc != fac).sum())}/34")

# ---- US airports (OpenFlights): routes between US airports, largest connected piece ----
air = D["air"]; N = len(air["code"])
W = np.zeros((N, N))
for i, j in air["edges"]:
    W[i, j] = W[j, i] = 1
for name, keep in (("all US", np.arange(N)), ("lower 48", np.nonzero(air["l48"])[0])):
    Wk = W[np.ix_(keep, keep)]
    ev, U = laplacian_eig(Wk, "rw")
    print(f"\n{name}: {len(keep)} airports, {int(Wk.sum() / 2)} routes")
    print("  smallest eigenvalues:", np.round(np.clip(ev[:8], 0, None), 4))
    s = 1 / np.sqrt(Wk.sum(1))                     # Ng-Jordan-Weiss: symmetric form, unit rows
    V = np.linalg.eigh(np.eye(len(s)) - s[:, None] * Wk * s[None, :])[1]
    for k in (2, 3, 4, 5, 6):

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • PCA is the best flat surface through the data. Its relatives change what the eigenvector problem is applied to: a kernel matrix, a graph Laplacian, a penalised regression, or a smoother.
  • Kernel PCA solves K̃a = λN a on the double-centred N×N kernel matrix and scores any point by Σ ank(x, xn); with a Gaussian kernel it can return up to N components, but cannot easily map back (the pre-image problem).
  • A principal curve is self-consistent: each point on it is the average of the data that project there. Fit it by alternating projection and smoothing, starting from the first principal component.
  • Spectral clustering builds a similarity graph, takes the bottom eigenvectors of its Laplacian L = G − W (or a normalised version), and runs k-means on them; zero eigenvalues count connected pieces and the eigengap suggests k. It is kernel PCA with the kernel L+.
  • Sparse PCA adds an L1 penalty so most loadings are exactly zero: a little variance traded for components you can name.

Check your understanding

Kernel PCA with the linear kernel k(x, z) = x⊤z on 500 points in 3 dimensions. How many non-zero eigenvalues does K̃ have, and how do they relate to ordinary PCA?

At most 3, because the feature space is the original 3-dimensional space. Divided by N they are exactly ordinary PCA’s covariance eigenvalues, and the scores are the same up to sign. The kernel route just solves a 500×500 problem instead of a 3×3 one.

Why must the kernel matrix be double-centred, and why not just subtract the mean of each column?

PCA needs the lifted points φ(xn) to have mean zero. Expanding the dot product of two centred lifted points gives four terms: the kernel value, minus its row mean, minus its column mean, plus the grand mean. Subtracting only column means would centre on one side of the dot product and leave a non-symmetric matrix.

A similarity graph has three zero eigenvalues in its Laplacian. What does that tell you, and what does spectral clustering with k = 3 do?

The graph has exactly three connected pieces. The three bottom eigenvectors are constant on each piece, so every row of Z takes one of three distinct values, and k-means recovers the three pieces exactly.

On the US flight network, Shi–Malik spectral clustering of the lower 48 states returns one cluster of 395 airports and one of 5. Is the method broken?

No: it found a genuinely weak cut. A few small airports connect to the rest only through one hub, so separating them costs almost nothing, and the eigenvalues show no clear gap that would favour a big, balanced split. Spectral clustering finds the weakest cuts, which on hub-and-spoke networks are often small, peripheral groups; row-normalising (Ng–Jordan–Weiss) or looking at more eigenvectors changes what you see.

Sparse PCA with a moderate penalty keeps 5 of 13 loadings on PC1 and explains 95% as much variance as ordinary PCA. What did you gain and what did you give up?

You gained a component defined by 5 named variables instead of a mixture of all 13, which is far easier to interpret and to measure. You gave up 5% of the variance and exact orthogonality: sparse components can be correlated, which is why their variance has to be “adjusted” before adding it up.
