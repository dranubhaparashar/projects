Variational Inference: Mean Field

When the posterior is out of reach, find the closest thing you can compute

Sampling (chapters 6.7–6.9) answers a hard posterior with a crowd of random draws. Variational inference answers it with an optimisation: pick a family of simple distributions, then search that family for the member closest to the posterior. Inference as optimisation: the ELBO again

A latent-variable model gives you a joint p(x, z) that is easy to write down: a prior over the hidden z and a likelihood for the visible x. Inference wants the posterior p(z | x) = p(x, z) / p(x), and the trouble is the denominator. p(x) = ∫ p(x, z) dz is a sum or integral over every setting of the hidden variables, which is exponential in their number for discrete z and usually has no closed form for continuous z. Chapter 19 of Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville calls every method that works around this approximate inference.

The way around it is an identity you met in the EM chapter (1.12a). Pick any distribution q(z) over the hidden variables. Then log p(x) splits exactly into two parts: the evidence lower bound (ELBO) L(q), which only needs the joint p(x, z), and the KL divergence from q to the true posterior, which is never negative. So L(q) ≤ log p(x), with equality exactly when q is the posterior. Maximising L over q therefore does two things at once: it tightens a bound on the evidence and it pushes q toward the posterior. Inference has become optimisation.

EM took this to the limit: its E-step sets q to the exact posterior, so the KL term is zero. Variational inference is what you do when the exact posterior is out of reach: you restrict q to a family you can compute with (here, Gaussians) and maximise L inside that family. MAP inference is the extreme case where q is a single point. The running example is one count x from a Poisson distribution whose log-rate z has a standard normal prior. The posterior is not Gaussian, so no Gaussian q can make the KL zero. Move the mean and width of q, then press “Best Gaussian”: the bar shows how much of log p(x) the bound has recovered.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln p(x) &= \mathcal{L}(q) + \mathrm{KL}\big(q \,\Vert\, p(z \mid x)\big) \\ \mathcal{L}(q) &= \int q(z) \ln \frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)}\, dz \\ \mathrm{KL}(q \Vert p) &= -\int q(z) \ln \frac{p(z \mid x)}{q(z)}\, dz \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: substitute p(x, z) = p(z | x) p(x) into L(q) and the ln p(x) comes out of the integral (because ∫ q = 1), leaving minus the KL. The KL is zero only when q equals the posterior everywhere, so L(q) is a lower bound that touches log p(x) exactly at the posterior.
Say it“Log evidence equals the ELBO plus the KL from q to the posterior.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(q) &= \mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\ln p(x, z)\big] + H[q] \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\ln p(x \mid z)\big] \\ &\quad - \mathrm{KL}\big(q \,\Vert\, p(z)\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: two readings of the same number. First: q should put its mass where the joint is high, while keeping as much entropy H[q] as it can (it pays for collapsing to a point). Second: q should explain the data well on average, while staying close to the prior. Neither form needs p(x).
Say it“The ELBO is the expected log joint plus the entropy of q; equivalently, the expected log-likelihood minus the KL from q to the prior.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), \quad x \mid z \sim \text{Poisson}(e^{z}) \\ &q = \mathcal{N}(m, s^2) \\ &\mathcal{L}(m, s) = x m - e^{\,m + s^2/2} - \ln x! \\ &\qquad\qquad - \tfrac12\big(m^2 + s^2 - 1\big) + \ln s \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for this model every expectation under a Gaussian q has a closed form: E[z] = m, E[ez] = em + s²/2, and the KL from N(m, s²) to N(0, 1) is ½(m² + s² − 1) − ln s. So the ELBO is an ordinary function of two numbers. Setting its derivatives to zero gives m = x − em + s²/2 and s² = 1 / (1 + em + s²/2), which the page solves by bisection. log p(x) itself needs a numerical integral; the bound does not.
Say it“The ELBO of the Poisson model is x m, minus e to the m plus s squared over two, minus log x factorial, minus the KL to the prior.”

Factorised distributions: mean field takes turns

Which family should q come from? One of the most useful answers assumes nothing about the shape of q and only cuts it into independent pieces. Split the hidden variables into groups Z1, …, ZM and require q to be a product of one factor per group. Physicists call this mean-field theory: each variable feels only the average effect of the others. Goodfellow et al. write the same thing as q(h | v) = ∏i q(hi | v), and call the general case, where the factors are larger blocks chosen to respect the model’s structure, structured variational inference.

Hold every factor but one fixed and look at the ELBO as a function of the remaining factor qj. It turns out to be minus a KL divergence between qj and a distribution built from the log joint, so the best qj is that distribution: the exponential of the log joint, averaged over all the other factors. The functional form of each factor is not chosen in advance; it falls out of the model. The factors depend on one another, so you cycle through them. Each update can only raise the ELBO, and the ELBO can never pass log p(x), so the cycle converges, though only to a local optimum.

Bishop’s first test is a correlated two-dimensional Gaussian p(z) = N(z | μ, Λ−1), approximated by q(z1)q(z2). Each optimal factor comes out Gaussian, with precision equal to a diagonal entry of Λ, and a mean that depends on the other factor’s mean. Drag the purple starting point, then step: each update moves along one axis only, so the path zig-zags toward the true mean. Raise the correlation and the zig-zag slows down.

\[ \begin{aligned} q(Z) &= \prod_{i=1}^{M} q_i(Z_i) \\ \ln q_j^{\star}(Z_j) &= \mathbb{E}_{i \neq j}\big[\ln p(X, Z)\big] \\ &\quad + \text{const} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the log of the best factor for group j is the log joint, averaged over the current factors of every other group, plus whatever constant makes it normalise. You never need p(X): the constant absorbs it.
Say it“Log q-j star is the expectation, over all the other factors, of the log joint, plus a constant.”
\[ \begin{aligned} q_1^{\star}(z_1) &= \mathcal{N}\big(z_1 \mid m_1,\ \Lambda_{11}^{-1}\big) \\ m_1 &= \mu_1 - \Lambda_{11}^{-1}\Lambda_{12}\big(\mathbb{E}[z_2] - \mu_2\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ln p(z) is a quadratic in z1, so averaging it over z2 leaves a quadratic in z1: a Gaussian. Its precision is Λ11 regardless of what q(z2) is; only q(z2)’s mean enters, through the cross term Λ12. The z2 update is the same with 1 and 2 swapped.
Say it“q-1 star is normal with mean mu-1 minus Lambda-1-1 inverse Lambda-1-2 times the expected z-2 minus mu-2, and precision Lambda-1-1.”
Where the update comes from, in three lines

Write the ELBO with q = qj ∏i≠j qi and collect what depends on qj: L = ∫ qj Ei≠j[ln p(X, Z)] dZj − ∫ qj ln qj dZj + const.

Define ln p̃(X, Zj) = Ei≠j[ln p(X, Z)] + const, normalised over Zj. Then L = −KL(qj ‖ p̃) + const.

A KL divergence is smallest (zero) when its two arguments agree, so qj☆ = p̃. Each such update raises L or leaves it unchanged; that is why coordinate ascent never goes downhill.

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta_1' &= \frac{\Lambda_{12}^2}{\Lambda_{11}\Lambda_{22}}\,\Delta_1 = \rho^2\, \Delta_1 \\ \Delta_1 &= m_1 - \mu_1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Δ1 is the error in q(z1)’s mean before a sweep and Δ1′ the error after it. Chain the two updates together: one full sweep multiplies the error in each mean by the squared correlation ρ² of p. At ρ = 0 one sweep is exact; at ρ = 0.99 each sweep removes only 2% of the error. The means always end at the true mean μ.

Properties of factorised approximations: compact and mode-seeking

The last lab already showed the first property. Mean field gets the mean of the correlated Gaussian exactly right, but its factors have variance 1/Λii, which is smaller than the true marginal variance (Λ−1)ii whenever there is correlation. The approximation is too compact: it sits inside the narrow direction of the posterior and misses its long direction. Switch on the KL(p ‖ q) fit in the previous lab to see the opposite behaviour: minimising the KL the other way round gives each factor the full marginal variance, and the ellipse spills into regions where p has almost no mass.

The reason is in where each divergence pays. KL(q ‖ p) averages ln(q/p) under q, so it is ruinous for q to put mass where p is tiny: q is zero-forcing and hides inside p. KL(p ‖ q) averages under p, so it is ruinous for q to be tiny where p has mass: q is zero-avoiding and stretches to cover everything. Goodfellow et al. explain why variational learning still uses KL(q ‖ p): its expectations are under q, the distribution you chose to be simple, so they can be computed; the other direction needs expectations under the posterior you could not compute in the first place.

The difference is starkest for a posterior with two separated modes, the common case in mixture models. A single Gaussian under KL(q ‖ p) locks onto one mode (and which one depends on where you start), while under KL(p ‖ q) it matches the overall mean and variance and lands in the empty valley between them. Both are members of the α-family of divergences: α → −1 gives KL(q ‖ p), α → 1 gives KL(p ‖ q), and α = 0 is symmetric and closely related to the Hellinger distance. Slide α from −1 to 1 with the bumps far apart: the fits from different starting points merge into one wide Gaussian.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{KL}(q \Vert p) &= \int q \ln \frac{q}{p}\, dz \\ \mathrm{KL}(p \Vert q) &= \int p \ln \frac{p}{q}\, dz \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same log-ratio, averaged under different distributions; the first is mode-seeking, the second mass-covering. In the first, regions where q > 0 but p ≈ 0 make ln(q/p) huge, so the optimum keeps q out of them. In the second, regions where p > 0 but q ≈ 0 are the expensive ones.
Say it“KL of q from p averages under q; KL of p from q averages under p.”
\[ \begin{aligned} D_\alpha(p \Vert q) = \frac{4}{1-\alpha^2}\Big(1 &- \int p(x)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}} \\ &\times q(x)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\, dx\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a family of divergences indexed by α in (−1, 1). It is zero exactly when p = q and positive otherwise. Low α leans toward zero-forcing, high α toward zero-avoiding; the two KLs are its limits at −1 and +1, and at α = 0 it becomes 2∫(√p − √q)² dx.
Say it“D-alpha is four over one minus alpha squared, times one minus the integral of p to the one-plus-alpha-over-two times q to the one-minus-alpha-over-two.”

Example: the univariate Gaussian, one factor at a time

Here is mean field on a real inference problem, small enough that the exact answer is known. You have N numbers from a Gaussian with unknown mean μ and unknown precision τ (the inverse variance). The conjugate prior is Normal–Gamma: τ ~ Gam(a0, b0) and, given τ, μ ~ N(μ0, (λ0τ)−1). The exact posterior is Normal–Gamma too, so you can see precisely what the factorised q(μ)q(τ) gets right and wrong. The page uses μ0 = 0, λ0 = 1, a0 = b0 = 1, and draws the data from a Gaussian with mean 1 and standard deviation 0.8.

Apply the general recipe. ln q(μ) collects the terms of the log joint that involve μ, averaged over τ; they are quadratic in μ, so q(μ) is Gaussian, and τ enters only through its mean E[τ]. ln q(τ) collects the terms that involve τ, averaged over μ; they are a multiple of ln τ plus a multiple of τ, so q(τ) is a Gamma distribution. Nobody told the algorithm to use a Gaussian and a Gamma. Step through the updates from a bad starting guess for E[τ] and compare the purple contours with the exact posterior in teal.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(D \mid \mu, \tau) &= \Big(\frac{\tau}{2\pi}\Big)^{N/2} \\ &\quad \times e^{-\frac{\tau}{2}\sum_{n}(x_n - \mu)^2} \\ p(\mu \mid \tau) &= \mathcal{N}\big(\mu \mid \mu_0, (\lambda_0 \tau)^{-1}\big) \\ p(\tau) &= \mathrm{Gam}(\tau \mid a_0, b_0) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the likelihood of N independent Gaussian observations, and a prior in which the uncertainty about the mean scales with the noise. The mean and the precision are coupled in the true posterior: a large τ (little noise) pins μ down more tightly.
Say it“The data are Gaussian given mu and tau; mu is Gaussian given tau with precision lambda-nought tau; tau is Gamma.”
\[ \begin{aligned} q^{\star}(\mu) &= \mathcal{N}\big(\mu \mid \mu_N, \lambda_N^{-1}\big) \\ \mu_N &= \frac{\lambda_0 \mu_0 + N \bar{x}}{\lambda_0 + N} \\ \lambda_N &= (\lambda_0 + N)\, \mathbb{E}[\tau] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the mean of q(μ) is a weighted average of the prior mean and the sample mean; it doesn’t depend on q(τ) at all. Its precision is the total count times the current guess of the noise precision.
Say it“q of mu is normal with mean mu-N and precision lambda-nought plus N times the expected tau.”
\[ \begin{aligned} q^{\star}(\tau) &= \mathrm{Gam}(\tau \mid a_N, b_N) \\ a_N &= a_0 + \tfrac{N+1}{2} \\ b_N &= b_0 + \tfrac12\, \mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Big[\sum_{n}(x_n - \mu)^2 \\ &\qquad + \lambda_0(\mu - \mu_0)^2\Big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the shape counts N observations plus one for μ’s prior. The rate adds the expected squared misfit, averaged over q(μ): E[(x − μ)²] = (x − μN)² + 1/λN. The new E[τ] = aN/bN feeds back into λN.
Say it“q of tau is Gamma with shape a-nought plus N-plus-one over two and rate b-nought plus half the expected misfit.”

Discrete latent variables: binary sparse coding

With discrete hidden units the factors are simple: each q(hi | v) is a Bernoulli with one parameter, the probability ĥi that unit i is on. Goodfellow et al. work through the binary sparse coding model: each binary cause hi switches on independently with probability σ(bi), and the visible vector v is the sum of the dictionary columns W:,i of the active causes, plus Gaussian noise of precision β. The exact posterior over 2m on/off patterns is hopeless for large m because the causes compete to explain v (“explaining away”), so the posterior does not factorise.

Setting the derivative of the ELBO with respect to each ĥi to zero gives a fixed-point equation: ĥi is a sigmoid of how well column i matches v, minus a penalty for its own length, minus how much of v the other active columns already explain. Updating the units one at a time is coordinate ascent and never lowers the ELBO. Updating them all at once from the same old values is not guaranteed to: units that overlap can all switch on together, then all switch off, and oscillate. Damping (moving only part of the way to the new value) is the usual cure. Unrolled for a fixed number of steps, these updates behave like a small recurrent network, which is how some models do inference.

Here m = 3 causes in two dimensions: two axis directions and a diagonal one. When v sits near the diagonal it can be explained by the diagonal cause alone or by the two axis causes together, and the exact posterior splits between those explanations. Drag v (the star) or the tips of the three columns, and switch between the update schemes.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(h_i = 1) &= \sigma(b_i) \\ p(v \mid h) &= \mathcal{N}\big(v;\ W h,\ \beta^{-1} I\big) \\ q(h \mid v) &= \prod_i \hat h_i^{\,h_i}\,(1 - \hat h_i)^{1 - h_i} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a sparse prior over binary causes, a linear-Gaussian likelihood, and a fully factorised Bernoulli q. With ĥ fixed, the ELBO has a closed form: Eq‖v − Wh‖² needs only the ĥi (cross terms use ĥiĥj, squared terms use ĥi because hi² = hi).
Say it“Each cause is on with probability sigma of b-i; v is W h plus noise; q is a product of Bernoullis with parameters h-hat.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat h_i = \sigma\Big(&b_i + \beta\, v^{\top} W_{:,i} - \tfrac12 \beta\, W_{:,i}^{\top} W_{:,i} \\ &- \beta \sum_{j \neq i} W_{:,j}^{\top} W_{:,i}\, \hat h_j \Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: unit i’s prior log-odds, plus its match to the input, minus half its own squared length, minus its overlap with the other units weighted by how likely they are to be on. Two units with overlapping columns inhibit each other: that is explaining away, approximated.
Say it“h-hat-i is the sigmoid of b-i plus beta v dot W-i, minus half beta W-i squared, minus beta times the overlap with the other active units.”

Calculus of variations: optimising over functions

In the mean-field derivation we optimised the ELBO over a whole function qj, not over a few numbers. The tool for that is the calculus of variations. A functional J[f] takes a function and returns a number: the entropy H[p] = −∫ p ln p is one, the ELBO is another. Its functional derivative δJ/δf(x) says how much J changes when you nudge f up a little at the single point x. Ordinary calculus sets a gradient to zero; here you set the functional derivative to zero at every x at once.

Goodfellow et al.’s worked example asks which density has the largest entropy among all densities with a given mean μ and variance σ². Add Lagrange multipliers for the three constraints (it integrates to 1, it has mean μ, it has variance σ²), take the functional derivative and set it to zero: ln p(x) must be a quadratic in x, so p is Gaussian. Change the constraints and the answer changes: with only a fixed support [a, b] and no moment constraint, the same calculation gives the uniform density on [a, b], and with a fixed mean on the positive half-line it gives the exponential. Bend the unit-variance density away from the Gaussian in either family: its entropy always drops, and the drop equals its KL divergence from the Gaussian.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\frac{\delta}{\delta f(x)} \int g\big(f(x), x\big)\, dx \\ &\quad = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\, g(y, x)\Big|_{y = f(x)} \\ &\frac{\partial G}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial G}{\partial y'} = 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: when the integrand depends only on the value f(x), the functional derivative is just its ordinary partial derivative, evaluated pointwise. When it also depends on the slope y′ = f′(x), the stationarity condition is the Euler–Lagrange equation on the second line. Every functional in this chapter is of the first kind.
Say it“The functional derivative of the integral of g of f is the partial derivative of g with respect to its first argument.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{J}[p] &= H[p] + \lambda_1\Big(\!\int p - 1\Big) \\ &\quad + \lambda_2\big(\mathbb{E}_p[x] - \mu\big) \\ &\quad + \lambda_3\big(\mathbb{E}_p[(x-\mu)^2] - \sigma^2\big) \\ \frac{\delta \mathcal{J}}{\delta p(x)} &= -\ln p(x) - 1 + \lambda_1 \\ &\quad + \lambda_2 x + \lambda_3 (x - \mu)^2 = 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the entropy plus three constraints, each times a multiplier. Setting the derivative to zero gives p(x) = exp(λ1 − 1 + λ2x + λ3(x − μ)²); choosing λ2 = 0 and λ3 = −1/(2σ²) to meet the constraints makes it N(μ, σ²).
Say it“Minus log p minus one plus the multipliers times the constraint functions equals zero, so log p is quadratic.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\frac{\delta}{\delta q_j(z_j)}\Big[\int q_j\, \mathbb{E}_{i\neq j}[\ln p]\, dz_j \\ &\qquad - \int q_j \ln q_j\, dz_j + \lambda\Big(\!\int q_j - 1\Big)\Big] \\ &\quad = \mathbb{E}_{i\neq j}[\ln p] - \ln q_j - 1 + \lambda = 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ln p is short for the log joint ln p(x, z). The same calculation on the ELBO, with only the normalisation constraint, gives ln qj = Ei≠j[ln p(x, z)] + const: the mean-field update of the second section, derived without the KL argument.
Say it“Set the functional derivative of the ELBO in q-j to zero and log q-j equals the expected log joint plus a constant.”

Continuous latent variables: free-form factors

For continuous hidden variables the general fixed point says the same thing as before, now read as a statement about whole functions: the unnormalised optimal factor for hi is the exponential of the log joint, averaged over all the other factors. You normalise it, and if the result is a familiar distribution you only need to track its parameters; if not, you can keep it as a table of values on a grid, which is what this lab does.

Goodfellow et al.’s example is two standard normal causes and one visible number, v = w1h1 + w2h2 + unit-variance noise. Averaging the log joint over q(h2) leaves a quadratic in h1, so the factor comes out Gaussian although nobody said it should be. Swap the prior for a Laplace (the sparse prior of sparse coding, scaled to unit variance) and the same recipe produces a factor that is not Gaussian: peaked at zero with a kink. The page computes both factors on a 481-point grid with no assumption about their shape. Move v and the weights, and switch the prior; the marginal strips compare each factor with the exact marginal of the posterior.

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde q(h_i \mid v) &= \exp\Big(\mathbb{E}_{h_{-i} \sim q(h_{-i} \mid v)} \\ &\qquad\quad \ln \tilde p(v, h)\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: h−i is every hidden variable except hi, and p̃ is the unnormalised joint. Average its log over the current factors of the others, exponentiate, normalise: that is the new q(hi | v). Any constant in ln p̃ disappears in the normalisation.
Say it“q-tilde of h-i is the exponential of the expected log joint, with the expectation over all the other hidden variables.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \ln q(h_1 \mid v) &= \ln p(h_1) + \text{const} \\ &- \tfrac12\big(v - w_2 \hat h_2 - w_1 h_1\big)^2 \\ q(h_1 \mid v) &= \mathcal{N}\big(h_1;\ m_1,\ s_1^2\big) \\ m_1 &= \tfrac{w_1 (v - w_2 \hat h_2)}{1 + w_1^2} \\ s_1^2 &= \tfrac{1}{1 + w_1^2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: only the mean ĥ2 of the other factor enters (its variance adds a constant). With a Gaussian prior (the last two lines), −½h1² plus the quadratic gives precision 1 + w1². With the Laplace prior ln p(h1) = −√2|h1| + const, and the factor has a kink at 0.
Say it“Log q of h-1 is the log prior minus half the squared leftover, v minus w-2 h-hat-2 minus w-1 h-1.”

Learning and inference interact

So far the model was fixed. In variational learning you also choose the parameters θ by maximising the ELBO, alternating between improving q (inference) and improving θ (learning), exactly like EM but with a restricted q. The catch is that the ELBO is log p(v; θ) minus the KL gap, and the gap depends on θ too. So learning is rewarded for two different things: making the data likely, and making the posterior easy to approximate. It will trade some of the first for the second, and it pushes the model toward exactly the kind of posterior the approximation assumes. The learned model is shaped by the inference method.

The previous section’s model shows this cleanly. With Gaussian priors, the evidence for v depends on the weights only through ‖w‖²: any w on a circle of the right radius fits the data equally well, so exact maximum likelihood is happy anywhere on the circle. The mean-field gap is zero only when the posterior of (h1, h2) is factorial, which here means w1w2 = 0. Drag the starting weights and run both learners: exact EM (teal) walks straight out to the circle and stops; variational EM (purple) ends on the circle too, but it turns as it goes, until one weight is zero and one cause has been switched off. A further difficulty: in a real model you cannot compute log p(v; θ), so you cannot even measure how much the approximation is costing you.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(v, \theta, q) &= \ln p(v; \theta) \\ &\quad - \mathrm{KL}\big(q \,\Vert\, p(h \mid v; \theta)\big) \\ \text{gap}(w) &= \tfrac12 \ln \frac{(1 + w_1^2)(1 + w_2^2)}{1 + w_1^2 + w_2^2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: q is q(h | v). Learning maximises the left-hand side over θ, so it is pulled by both terms on the right. For the two-cause model the best mean-field q leaves a gap that depends on w alone (not on v): zero when either weight is zero, largest along the diagonals, where the two causes are perfectly confusable.
Say it“The ELBO is the log-likelihood minus the gap; here the gap is half the log of one plus w-1 squared, times one plus w-2 squared, over one plus the squared length of w.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\tfrac{1}{N}\ln p(v_{1:N}; w) \\ &\quad = -\tfrac12 \ln 2\pi\big(1 + \Vert w\Vert^2\big) \\ &\qquad - \frac{S}{2(1 + \Vert w\Vert^2)} \\ &S = \tfrac1N \textstyle\sum_n v_n^2 \end{aligned} \]

Bishop 10.1.4

Model comparison with the bound

The ELBO is a lower bound on the evidence ln p(D | m), the quantity Bayesian model comparison ranks models by (chapter 1.2e covered it in detail). So a natural move is to compare models by their ELBOs. Bishop puts this on a firm footing by treating the model index m as one more hidden variable, with prior p(m), and a joint q(Z, m) = q(Z | m) q(m). Note that q(Z | m) must be allowed to depend on m, because different models have different latent variables. Maximising the bound over q(m) gives a clean answer: q(m) is proportional to p(m) times the exponential of that model’s best ELBO. When the bounds are tight, q(m) is the posterior over models.

When are they tight? The gap is a KL divergence, and it can differ from model to model. Here the models are polynomials of degree M = 0, …, 9 fitted to noisy samples of sin(2πx), with unknown noise precision β: the same Normal–Gamma structure as the univariate Gaussian, so exact evidences are known. With q(w)q(β) the gap is tiny and grows slowly with M, and q(m) almost equals the exact posterior. Factorise further, q(w0)…q(wM)q(β), and the gap grows with M because polynomial coefficients are strongly correlated: the bound now penalises big models for being hard to approximate, not for being bad. Switch the factorisation and compare the bars; click a bar to see that degree’s fit.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln p(X) &= \mathcal{L} + \mathrm{KL}\big(q \,\Vert\, p(Z, m \mid X)\big) \\ \mathcal{L} &= \sum_{m}\sum_{Z} q(Z \mid m)\, q(m) \\ &\qquad \times \ln \frac{p(Z, X, m)}{q(Z \mid m)\, q(m)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: q is short for q(Z | m) q(m). This is the first section’s identity with (Z, m) as the hidden variables: the log evidence over all models is the bound plus a KL that is never negative. The sums over Z become integrals for continuous Z.
Say it“Log p of X is the bound L plus the KL from q to the joint posterior over latents and models.”
\[ \begin{aligned} q(m) &\propto p(m)\, \exp\{\mathcal{L}_m\} \\ \mathcal{L}_m &= \sum_{Z} q(Z \mid m) \ln \frac{p(Z, X \mid m)}{q(Z \mid m)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: optimise q(Z | m) separately inside each model, read off its ELBO Lm, and weight the models by prior times eLm. Replace Lm by the exact ln p(X | m) and this is Bayes’ rule over models.
Say it“q of m is proportional to the prior on m times e to the model’s ELBO.”

Real-world project

Project: Old Faithful, mean field against MCMC

The Old Faithful data are 272 eruptions of the geyser in Yellowstone National Park: the length of each eruption and the waiting time until the next, both in minutes (the faithful data frame in R’s datasets package; source W. Härdle, from observations studied by Azzalini & Bowman, 1990). Long eruptions are followed by long waits, and the model is the simplest that says so: waiting = w0 + w1 × duration + Gaussian noise of precision β. The priors are independent, w0, w1 ~ N(0, 100²) and β ~ Gam(0.01, 0.01). With independent priors the posterior has no closed form, which is the usual situation that sends people to MCMC or to variational inference.

Three methods attack the same posterior in your browser. Gibbs sampling (chapter 6.8) alternates exact draws of w given β (a 2-D Gaussian) and β given w (a Gamma). Mean-field VI cycles the factor updates of this chapter until E[β] stops moving. And a referee: for fixed β the posterior of w is Gaussian, so the exact posterior moments follow from a 1-D integral over β, done here on a grid of 3,001 points. Compare the methods with the duration as measured, then centre it, and switch to the structured q(w)q(β).

\[ \begin{aligned} q(w_j) &= \mathcal{N}(m_j, v_j) \\ v_j^{-1} &= \alpha + \mathbb{E}[\beta]\, \Vert X_{:,j}\Vert^2 \\ m_j &= v_j\, \mathbb{E}[\beta]\, X_{:,j}^{\top}\big(y - X_{:,k}\, m_k\big) \\ q(\beta) &= \mathrm{Gam}\big(a_0 + \tfrac N2,\ b_N\big) \\ b_N &= b_0 + \tfrac12\, \mathbb{E}\Vert y - Xw\Vert^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: X has a column of ones and a column of durations, and k is the other column. Each weight is refitted to what the other weight leaves unexplained, with a precision that ignores the other weight entirely. E‖y − Xw‖² is the residual sum of squares at the means plus Σj vj‖X:,j‖².
Say it“Each weight’s factor is Gaussian with precision alpha plus E-beta times its column’s squared length; q of beta is Gamma with the expected residual sum of squares in its rate.”
\[ \begin{aligned} w \mid \beta, y &\sim \mathcal{N}\big(\beta A^{-1} X^{\top} y,\ A^{-1}\big) \\ A &= \alpha I + \beta X^{\top} X \\ \beta \mid w, y &\sim \mathrm{Gam}\big(a_0 + \tfrac N2, \\ &\qquad\quad b_0 + \tfrac12 \Vert y - Xw\Vert^2\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the two conditionals Gibbs alternates between. The first is the mean-field update of q(w) with E[β] replaced by the current draw and with the full 2 × 2 covariance kept, which is why Gibbs keeps the correlation that fully factorised VI throws away.
Say it“Given beta, w is Gaussian with precision alpha I plus beta X-transpose X; given w, beta is Gamma.”

def bsc(v, W, b, beta):
    m = W.shape[1]; H = np.array([[(k >> i) & 1 for i in range(m)] for k in range(2 ** m)], float)
    lj = (H * np.log(expit(b)) + (1 - H) * np.log(expit(-b))).sum(1) - beta / 2 * ((v[None, :] - H @ W.T) ** 2).sum(1) + W.shape[0] / 2 * math.log(beta / (2 * math.pi))
    lz = logsumexp(lj); P = np.exp(lj - lz); return lz, P @ H, P

    w = np.array(w, float); out = [w.copy()]

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • For any q, ln p(x) = L(q) + KL(q ‖ p(z | x)). Maximising the ELBO L over a tractable family is inference by optimisation; it never needs p(x), and the bound is tight only when q is the posterior.
  • Mean field assumes q = ∏ qi and nothing else. The best factor is ln qj = Ei≠j[ln p(x, z)] + const, so the factor’s form comes from the model; cycling the updates raises the ELBO every time and converges to a local optimum.
  • Minimising KL(q ‖ p) makes q compact and mode-seeking: correct means but too-small variances for correlated posteriors, one mode out of several. KL(p ‖ q) covers mass instead; the α-divergences interpolate between them.
  • The calculus of variations turns “best function” problems into pointwise conditions: it gives the mean-field update, the Gaussian as the maximum-entropy density for a fixed variance, and free-form factors for discrete or continuous latents.
  • The bound is not neutral: learning by the ELBO bends the model toward posteriors the approximation can represent, and comparing models by their ELBOs is only fair when their gaps are similar.

Check your understanding

Why can you compute the ELBO when you cannot compute log p(x)?

The ELBO is an expectation under q of ln p(x, z) − ln q(z). The joint p(x, z) is the model itself, and q was chosen so that expectations under it are easy. log p(x) needs an integral of the joint over all z, which is the hard part; the ELBO only needs integrals against q.

In the correlated Gaussian, mean field gets the means exactly right. Why are the variances too small, and by how much?

Each factor’s precision is the diagonal entry Λii of the precision matrix, which measures the spread of zi with the other variable held fixed: a conditional variance. The marginal variance (Λ−1)ii is larger by a factor 1/(1 − ρ²) for two unit-variance variables with correlation ρ: about 5.3 at ρ = 0.9.

The univariate-Gaussian example ends with E[τ] exactly equal to the exact posterior mean. Does that mean the approximation is exact?

No. The means agree, but q(μ) has variance 1/((λ0 + N)E[τ]), smaller than the exact marginal variance of μ, which is a Student-t; and q(τ) has shape a0 + (N + 1)/2 instead of a0 + N/2, so it is too narrow as well. Above all, q has no correlation between μ and τ, which the exact posterior does. The KL gap is small but not zero.

Why may updating all units of binary sparse coding at once oscillate, when one at a time cannot?

One-at-a-time updates are exact coordinate ascent: each sets one ĥi to its best value given the rest, so the ELBO can only rise. Simultaneous updates use stale values for every other unit. Units whose columns overlap all see the same unexplained input, all switch on together, then all see the input over-explained and switch off together. Damping, or updating only non-interacting units together, prevents this.

In the Old Faithful project, why does centring the duration make mean field almost exact for the weights?

With the raw duration, the intercept is the predicted wait at zero minutes, far from the data, so intercept and slope trade off against each other: posterior correlation −0.95. A factorised q cannot represent that correlation and shrinks both standard deviations by about √(1 − 0.95²) ≈ 0.31. Centring makes the duration column orthogonal to the column of ones, the posterior correlation of the weights vanishes, and the factorised q loses almost nothing.
