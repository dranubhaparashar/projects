Bandits in Depth: UCB, Thompson Sampling, Gittins & Context

How to learn which option is best while you are still paying for every try

Chapter 3.1 met the k-armed bandit and its simplest cure, ε-greedy. This chapter goes after the real question: how much should you explore, and where? You will make estimates that follow a moving target, buy exploration with optimism, put error bars on every arm (UCB), sample from your own uncertainty (Thompson sampling), compute the exactly optimal answer when the problem allows it (the Gittins index), see where no such answer exists, give every user their own best arm (contextual bandits), and finally grade policies you never ran, on 4.5 million real recommendations from a fashion website. Nonstationary rewards · Sutton & Barto 2.5

Tracking a moving target

The sample average treats the first reward and the thousandth as equally informative. That is exactly right when an arm’s mean never changes, and exactly wrong when it does: a restaurant changes its chef, a news story goes stale, a product goes out of season. Most real bandits are nonstationary. The fix is one character long. Replace the shrinking step 1/n in the incremental update by a constant step size α, and the estimate becomes an exponential recency-weighted average: every reward still counts, but its weight fades geometrically with age, so the estimate keeps following the arm wherever it wanders.

Below, one arm’s true mean (the dark line) drifts as a random walk and every pull returns that mean plus unit noise. Move α and the drift speed, and compare the three estimates’ errors.

\[ \begin{aligned} Q_{n+1} &= Q_n + \alpha\,\big(R_n - Q_n\big) \\ &= (1-\alpha)^n\, Q_1 + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha\,(1-\alpha)^{n-i} R_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the new estimate is the old one nudged a fraction α of the way towards the latest reward Rn. Unrolling the recursion shows what that does: reward Ri, which is n − i pulls old, carries weight α(1 − α)n−i, and the initial guess Q1 keeps weight (1 − α)n. The weights add up to exactly 1, so this is a genuine weighted average whose memory is roughly 1/α pulls. With α = 1/n instead, every reward gets weight 1/n: the plain sample average.
Say it“New estimate equals old estimate plus alpha times the error; unrolled, it is a sum of past rewards with weights that shrink by a factor one minus alpha per step of age.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \alpha_n &= \infty \\ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \alpha_n^2 &< \infty \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the two conditions of stochastic approximation (Robbins and Monro, 1951) for the estimate to converge to the true mean. The first says the steps add up to enough to overcome any starting point and any run of bad luck; the second says they shrink fast enough for the noise to average away. The step 1/n passes both. A constant α fails the second on purpose: the estimate never settles, it keeps responding to recent rewards, which is what you want when the truth moves.
Say it“The step sizes must sum to infinity, but their squares must sum to something finite.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \beta_n &= \frac{\alpha}{\bar o_n} \\ \bar o_n &= \bar o_{n-1} + \alpha\,(1 - \bar o_{n-1}) \\ \bar o_0 &= 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a constant step inherits a bias from Q1. This variant (Sutton & Barto’s Exercise 2.7) uses step βn instead. The trace ōn = 1 − (1 − α)n climbs from 0 towards 1, so β1 = 1 wipes out the initial guess completely and later steps settle to α: no initial bias, and still recency-weighted.
Say it“Beta-n is alpha over o-bar-n, where o-bar creeps up from zero to one.”

Exploration for free · Sutton & Barto 2.6

Optimistic initial values

Every method so far needs a starting guess Q1(a), and with a constant step that guess never fully disappears. You can turn this bias into a feature. Start every arm at a value far above anything the arms can pay, say +5 when the true means are around 0, and act purely greedily. Whichever arm you pull disappoints you, its estimate drops, and the still-untouched arms now look better, so you try them next. A greedy learner with optimistic initial values explores every arm several times before its estimates come down to earth, with no ε at all.

The trick has a catch: its exploration is a one-off at the start. If the arms later change, nothing ever sends the learner back. Raise the starting value, then switch the arms to drifting.

\[ \begin{aligned} Q_1(a) &= Q_0 \ \text{ for every arm } a \\ A_t &= \arg\max_a Q_t(a) \\ \mathbb{E}\big[Q_{n+1}(a)\big] &- q_*(a) \\ &= (1-\alpha)^{n}\,\big(Q_0 - q_*(a)\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: every arm starts at the same optimistic value Q0 and the learner always takes the arm with the highest estimate. Taking expectations of the recency-weighted average above shows how the optimism wears off: after n pulls of arm a, the expected overestimate is the starting excess Q0 − q*(a) shrunk by (1 − α)n. An arm stays overrated, and so attractive, for about ln(excess)/(−ln(1 − α)) pulls.
Say it“Start every estimate at Q-nought and act greedily; the expected excess over the truth after n pulls is one minus alpha to the n, times the starting excess.”

Exploring where it pays · Sutton & Barto 2.7

Upper confidence bounds: optimism that is earned

ε-greedy explores blindly: when it explores, it is as likely to re-try an arm it already knows is dreadful as one it has hardly seen. Better to explore the arms that might be best. Give every estimate an error bar that shrinks as the arm is pulled and grows slowly with time, and pick the arm whose bar reaches highest: upper-confidence-bound action selection. An arm gets pulled either because its estimate is high (exploitation) or because its bar is wide (exploration), and the bar of an arm you neglect keeps growing, like ln t, until you look again. Arms that have never been pulled count as infinitely promising, so each is tried once first.

Step through one run and watch the bonuses, then change c and compare with ε-greedy over 400 bandits.

\[ \begin{aligned} A_t &= \arg\max_a\, U_t(a) \\ U_t(a) &= Q_t(a) + c\,\sqrt{\frac{\ln t}{N_t(a)}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Qt(a) is the arm’s sample average, Nt(a) how often it has been pulled, t the step number and c > 0 sets how wide the bars are. The square root is the uncertainty in the estimate: it shrinks like 1/√N as the arm is pulled and creeps up like √(ln t) while it is not.
Say it“Pick the arm with the largest upper bound U: its estimate plus c times the square root of log t over its pull count.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\Pr\big(\mu_a > \hat\mu_a + u\big) \le e^{-2 N_a u^2} \\ &u = \sqrt{2 \ln t / N_a} \ \Rightarrow\ \text{bound} = t^{-4} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: where the square root comes from. For rewards in [0, 1], Hoeffding’s inequality says the true mean exceeds the average of Na rewards by more than u with probability at most e−2Nau². Choosing u so that this is t−4 gives the bonus of UCB1 (Auer, Cesa-Bianchi & Fischer, 2002), which is the rule above with c = √2: the bar is wrong ever more rarely as time goes on.
Say it“The chance the truth sits more than u above the average is at most e to the minus two N u squared.”
\[ \begin{aligned} L_T &= T\mu^* - \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T} R_t\Big] \\ &= \sum_{a} \Delta_a\, \mathbb{E}\big[N_T(a)\big] \\ \mathbb{E}\big[N_T(a)\big] &\le \frac{8 \ln T}{\Delta_a^2} + 1 + \frac{\pi^2}{3} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the regret LT is what you lost against always playing the best arm (mean μ*). It splits into, for every worse arm, its gap Δa = μ* − μa times the number of times you pulled it. Auer et al. proved that UCB1 pulls a worse arm only O(ln T) times, so regret grows logarithmically, where ε-greedy with a fixed ε grows linearly.
Say it“Regret is the sum over arms of the gap times the expected pull count, and UCB1 pulls each bad arm at most about eight log T over gap squared times.”

Beliefs as the state · AIMA 17.3.2–17.3.3

The Bernoulli bandit and Thompson sampling

Many bandits pay 0 or 1: a click or no click, a cure or no cure. In the Bernoulli bandit arm a pays 1 with an unknown probability μa. Take a Bayesian view and give each μa a Beta prior; after s successes and f failures its posterior is again a Beta, with the counts simply added on. Those two numbers per arm summarise everything you have learned, so the pair (αa, βa) is the arm’s belief state, and the bandit becomes a sequential decision problem whose states are beliefs. That is the view AIMA takes, and it is what makes the exact solution of the next sections possible.

There is also a beautifully lazy way to act on those beliefs. Thompson sampling (Thompson, 1933) draws one plausible value of μa from each posterior and pulls the arm whose draw is highest. An arm is chosen exactly as often as you believe it is the best one: confident beliefs give exploitation, wide beliefs give exploration, with no knob to tune. Pull a few times, then pull a hundred, and watch the posteriors sharpen.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mu) &\propto \mu^{\alpha-1}(1-\mu)^{\beta-1} \\ p(\mu \mid s, f) &= \mathrm{Beta}(\mu;\ \alpha + s,\ \beta + f) \\ \mathbb{E}[\mu \mid s, f] &= \frac{\alpha + s}{\alpha + \beta + s + f} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a Beta(α, β) prior multiplied by the likelihood μs(1 − μ)f of s successes and f failures is a Beta(α + s, β + f): the prior behaves like α imaginary successes and β imaginary failures. Beta(1, 1) is flat, “any rate is equally likely”.
Say it“The posterior is Beta of alpha plus successes, beta plus failures; its mean is successes-plus-alpha over everything.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\theta_a \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_a, \beta_a), \quad A_t = \arg\max_a \theta_a \\ &\Rightarrow\ \Pr(A_t = a) = \Pr\big(\mu_a = \max\nolimits_b \mu_b \mid \text{data}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: draw a sample θa for every arm from its posterior and pull the arm with the largest sample. Because the samples come from the posteriors, the chance of pulling arm a equals the posterior probability that arm a really is the best: this is probability matching. Those probabilities can be computed exactly (here by numerical integration) and the stats below show them.
Say it“Sample a theta from every posterior, pull the arm with the biggest; each arm gets pulled with the probability that it is the best.”

\[ \begin{aligned} \liminf_{T \to \infty} \frac{\mathbb{E}\big[N_T(a)\big]}{\ln T} &\ge \frac{1}{\mathrm{KL}(\mu_a \,\Vert\, \mu^*)} \\ \mathrm{KL}(p \,\Vert\, q) &= p \ln \frac{p}{q} + (1-p) \ln \frac{1-p}{1-q} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the lower bound of Lai and Robbins (1985). Any policy that is good on every Bernoulli bandit must pull each worse arm at least about ln T / KL times, where KL measures how hard the two arms are to tell apart from samples. So logarithmic regret is the best possible, and the constant matters: Thompson sampling meets this bound asymptotically (Kaufmann, Korda & Munos, 2012; Agrawal & Goyal, 2012), UCB1’s Hoeffding bonus does not. AIMA calls such policies approximately optimal: optimal in the long run, cheap at every step.

Summing up chapter 2 of Sutton & Barto · 2.10

Which method wins? A parameter study

Every method so far has a knob: ε for ε-greedy, Q0 for optimism, c for UCB. A fair comparison must give each method its best setting, and also show how forgiving it is when the setting is wrong. The standard picture is a parameter study: run each method at a range of settings, spaced by factors of two, on many testbed bandits, and plot the average reward over the first 1000 steps. Four methods are compared here: the three above plus the gradient bandit, which learns a preference H(a) for each arm and picks arms with softmax probabilities (it is covered fully in chapter 3.1 and in Policy Gradients). Thompson sampling with a Gaussian prior has no knob, so it appears as a single line. Run the study (it computes in your browser, one setting at a time).

\[ \begin{aligned} \pi_t(a) &= \frac{e^{H_t(a)}}{\sum_b e^{H_t(b)}} \\ H_{t+1}(a) &= H_t(a) + \alpha\,\big(R_t - \bar R_t\big) \\ &\qquad \times \big(\mathbb{1}[a = A_t] - \pi_t(a)\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the gradient bandit keeps a preference Ht(a) per arm and chooses with softmax probabilities πt(a). After each reward it raises the preference of the arm it pulled if the reward beat the running average R̄t (and lowers it otherwise), moving the other arms the opposite way. Its knob is the step size α.

The exact answer · AIMA 17, 17.3, 17.3.1

The Gittins index: one number per arm

Everything above is a heuristic. Is there an optimal policy? AIMA’s chapter 17 treats sequential decisions as Markov decision processes, and a bandit is one: the state is the belief state of every arm, an action pulls one arm, only that arm’s state changes, and future rewards are discounted by γ per step. With K arms the joint state space is the product of the arms’ spaces, far too large to solve directly. The surprise, proved by Gittins and Jones (1974), is that you never need to: each arm can be scored on its own by a number, its Gittins index, and pulling the arm with the highest index is exactly optimal.

The index asks: if I could play this arm alone and stop whenever I like, what is the best average discounted reward per step I could lock in? For an arm whose future rewards are known in advance, that is a ratio you can compute by hand. Drag the bars of the arm below (from step 8 on, every reward is 0), move γ, and find the best stopping time.

\[ \lambda(s) = \max_{\tau \ge 1}\ \frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{t<\tau} \gamma^t R_t \mid s\big]}{\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{t<\tau} \gamma^t \mid s\big]} \]
Read it as: τ is a stopping rule (it may look at what has happened so far). The top is the discounted reward collected before stopping (the sums run over t = 0, …, τ − 1), the bottom the discounted time spent, so the ratio is a reward rate. The index is the best rate any stopping rule achieves. For a known reward sequence τ is just a number T and the expectations disappear. An arm paying a constant c every step has index c.
Say it“The index of a state is the best ratio, over stopping times, of expected discounted reward to expected discounted time.”

For a Bernoulli arm the future is uncertain, so the stopping rule has to react to what it sees. AIMA computes the index by calibration: offer the arm a rival that pays a fixed λ per step forever (“retirement”). For each λ, a small dynamic program over the arm’s belief states says whether it is worth playing on; the index is the λ at which you are exactly indifferent, found here by bisection. Click two cells to compare two arms.

\[ \begin{aligned} V_\lambda(\alpha,\beta) &= \max\big\{ \lambda/(1-\gamma),\ C_\lambda(\alpha,\beta) \big\} \\ C_\lambda(\alpha,\beta) &= \bar\mu + \gamma\,\bar\mu\, V_\lambda(\alpha{+}1,\beta) \\ &\quad + \gamma\,(1-\bar\mu)\, V_\lambda(\alpha,\beta{+}1) \\ \bar\mu &= \alpha/(\alpha+\beta) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: in belief state Beta(α, β) you can retire and take λ per step forever, worth λ/(1 − γ), or continue (Cλ) and pull once more: you expect μ̄ now, and the belief moves to (α + 1, β) after a success or (α, β + 1) after a failure. The index λ(α, β) is the λ at which both options are worth the same. Because pulling also teaches you something, it is always at least the mean μ̄; the excess is the value of information.
Say it“The value is the larger of retiring for lambda over one minus gamma, and the expected reward now plus gamma times the expected value of the next belief.”

Where the theorem stops · AIMA 17.3.4

Non-indexable variants: when no single number will do

The index theorem rests on four quiet assumptions: an arm you are not pulling stays frozen; future rewards are discounted geometrically with no deadline; you pull one arm at a time and switching is free; and you are paid for every pull, while you learn. Break any one and, in general, no index exists: the best choice depends on the arms jointly or on the clock, not on a score each arm can compute alone. Some famous examples:

  • A deadline. With a fixed number of pulls left, exploring is worth less as the end approaches, so the same two beliefs can call for different arms at different times.
  • The selection problem. Sometimes you only care which option you end up choosing: test new drugs or designs for a while, then commit. Sampling earns nothing, so it pays to sample whichever arm best resolves the comparison, not whichever looks best.
  • Restless bandits (Whittle, 1988). Arms change even when left alone, like the drifting arms of the first section. Whittle’s index is a good heuristic but is not optimal in general.
  • Bandit superprocesses. Each arm is itself a decision problem with its own choices. The index idea extends only under extra conditions; in general the choice of arm and the choices inside arms interact.
  • Switching costs, several pulls at once, and arms whose payouts are correlated also break the theorem.

For two Bernoulli arms and a short horizon, the exact Bayes-optimal policy is a small dynamic program over both arms’ beliefs, computed below for every number of pulls remaining. Set the two arms’ records, then compare the two kinds of problem.

\[ \begin{aligned} V_h(b) &= \max_{i \in \{1,2\}} Q_h(b, i) \\ Q_h(b, i) &= \bar\mu_i\big(\rho + V_{h-1}(b^{+i})\big) \\ &\quad + (1-\bar\mu_i)\,V_{h-1}(b^{-i}) \\ V_0(b) &= 0 \ \text{ when earning, } \rho = 1 \\ V_0(b) &= M \max\nolimits_i \bar\mu_i \ \text{ selecting, } \rho = 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: b = (b1, b2) holds the two arms’ Beta beliefs and h the pulls left; Qh(b, i) is the value of pulling arm i first and acting optimally afterwards. Pulling arm i pays ρ with probability μ̄i (its posterior mean) and moves the beliefs to b+i or b−i (arm i’s belief updated, the other unchanged). When earning while learning, every success pays 1 and nothing is left at the end. In the selection problem the pulls pay nothing; at the end you commit to the arm that looks best and collect its expected rate for M = 100 further pulls.
Say it“The value with h pulls left is the better of the two arms’ expected immediate payoff plus the value of the belief they lead to.”

Associative search · Sutton & Barto 2.9, 16.7

Contextual bandits: a different best arm for every visitor

So far there was one best arm for everybody. A news site, a shop or an ad server knows something about each visitor before choosing: their device, their past clicks, the time of day. Sutton and Barto call this associative search: the learner sees a signal, a context x, before each choice, and must learn a policy, a map from contexts to arms, rather than a single arm. It sits between the plain bandit and full reinforcement learning: actions depend on the situation, but do not change which situation comes next. Today the setting is called the contextual bandit, and it is how personalised web services were first put on a principled footing (Li, Chu, Langford & Schapire, 2010).

Below, each visitor is a point x = (x1, x2), and each of three items is clicked with a probability that depends on x in its own way. Press play: LinUCB learns a linear model of each item’s click rate with a confidence ellipsoid around it, and the map shows which item it would now show to every possible visitor.

\[ \begin{aligned} \pi^*(x) &= \arg\max_a\ \mathbb{E}[R \mid x, a] \\ V(\pi) &= \mathbb{E}_x\big[\,\mathbb{E}[R \mid x, \pi(x)]\,\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the best policy shows each visitor the arm with the highest expected reward for that visitor. A policy’s value V(π) is its expected reward averaged over the visitors who arrive. A context-free bandit can only find the single arm that is best on average, which may be best for nobody in particular.
Say it“Pi-star of x picks the arm with the highest expected reward given x; the value of a policy averages its reward over contexts.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\theta_a &= M_a^{-1} b_a \\ M_a &= \lambda I + \textstyle\sum x x^\top \\ b_a &= \textstyle\sum r\, x \\ a_t &= \arg\max_a\ x_t^\top \hat\theta_a \\ &\qquad + \alpha \sqrt{x_t^\top M_a^{-1} x_t} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for every arm, ridge regression on the contexts x (here x = (1, x1, x2)) and rewards r of the visitors who were shown that arm: Ma collects the contexts, ba the reward-weighted contexts, and λ = 1. The arm shown is at. The first term is the predicted reward for this visitor; the square root is the standard error of that prediction, large in regions of context space where the arm has rarely been shown. It is UCB with a separate, context-dependent confidence bar.
Say it“Show the arm whose predicted reward x-transpose theta-hat plus alpha times the prediction’s standard error is largest.”

Personalised web services · Sutton & Barto 16.7

Off-policy evaluation: grading a policy you never ran

A website cannot try every new idea on live visitors: a bad policy costs money and goodwill. But it has logs, millions of rows of (context, shown item, click). Can they tell you how a different policy would have done? Only if you know how the logging policy chose, and only by correcting for it: the log over-represents what the old policy liked. Sutton and Barto’s case study tells this story twice. Li, Chu, Langford and Wang (2011) evaluated news-recommendation policies for the Yahoo! front page offline by replay on a log gathered with uniformly random choices. Theocharous, Thomas and Ghavamzadeh (2015) went further for ad recommendation: they optimised long-term value rather than the click-through rate of a single visit, and only deployed a new policy when a high-confidence lower bound on its offline value beat the current one.

Below, the visitors of the last section are logged by a policy that mostly shows item 3 and explores with probability ε. Each estimator tries to recover the value of the personalised policy (the true best item for every visitor) from 200 independent logs. Shrink the logger’s exploration and watch which estimators stay honest.

\[ \hat V_{\text{IPS}}(\pi) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\, r_i, \qquad w_i = \frac{\pi(a_i \mid x_i)}{\mu(a_i \mid x_i)} \]
Read it as: μ is the logging policy, π the policy you want to grade. Each logged reward is re-weighted by how much more (or less) likely π was to choose the logged action than μ was. In expectation the weights exactly undo the logger’s preferences, so inverse propensity scoring (IPS) is unbiased whenever μ gives every action that π might take a non-zero chance. Replay is the special case of a uniform logger and a deterministic π: wi = K when the logged item is the one π would have shown and 0 otherwise.
Say it“Average the logged rewards, each multiplied by pi over mu of the action that was logged.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat V_{\text{SN}} &= \frac{\sum_i w_i r_i}{\sum_i w_i} \\ \hat V_{\text{DR}} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \Big[\hat q(x_i, \pi) + w_i\big(r_i - \hat q(x_i, a_i)\big)\Big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: self-normalised IPS divides by the total weight instead of n, trading a little bias for much less variance. The direct method fits a reward model q̂(x, a) and averages its predictions for π’s choices, q̂(x, π); it is only as good as the model. Doubly robust estimation starts from the model and adds the IPS-weighted residuals, so it stays unbiased if either the model or the propensities are right.
Say it“Self-normalised: weighted rewards over total weight. Doubly robust: model prediction plus weighted residual.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{deploy } \pi \text{ only if }\ \hat V(\pi) - z_{1-\delta}\,\frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt n} > V(\pi_{\text{now}}) \\ &\text{CTR} = \frac{\text{clicks}}{\text{visits}}, \qquad \text{LTV} = \frac{\text{clicks}}{\text{visitors}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: estimate the new policy’s value, subtract z standard errors to get a lower bound that is wrong with probability only about δ, and switch only if even that pessimistic number beats the policy you run now. (Theocharous et al. used concentration inequalities that need no normal approximation; the page uses z = 1.645, δ = 5%.) Their second lesson is the metric: CTR scores each visit alone; lifetime value LTV counts clicks per visitor across all their visits, which rewards policies that keep people coming back, a full reinforcement-learning objective rather than a bandit one.
Say it“Deploy only when the estimate minus z standard errors still beats the current policy.”

Real-world project

Project: choosing a recommender for ZOZOTOWN, offline

The data. ZOZOTOWN is a large fashion e-commerce site in Japan. For seven days in late November 2019 its recommendation widget showed three items to each visitor, chosen for each impression at random by one of two policies: uniform random, or Bernoulli Thompson sampling (BTS) over the items’ click rates. The resulting Open Bandit Dataset (Saito, Aihara, Matsutani & Narita, 2021) records every recommendation with its position, whether it was clicked, the exact probability with which the logging policy chose it, and hashed user features. It is released under CC BY 4.0 by ZOZO, Inc.; this page uses the men’s campaign of the full release (34 items; 452,949 rows logged by the random policy and 4,077,727 by BTS), reduced in Python to the click and impression counts embedded below. Item and feature names are not public, so items are shown by their dataset index and feature values by the first characters of their hashes. Each row is one item in one of the three slots; following the dataset’s authors, a click is assumed to depend only on the item and its slot.

Because the two policies ran side by side as an A/B test, we know the truth for both. That makes this the rare real dataset on which offline estimates can be checked.

5 · How long does learning take at these click rates?

The log cannot replay a learning policy fairly: with 34 items, a policy’s choice matches the random log’s only once in 34 rows, leaving a few dozen clicks per week to learn from. So this last step is a simulation, labelled as such: 34 Bernoulli arms whose rates are the items’ measured CTRs from the whole random log, played for 100,000 impressions by four of this chapter’s policies. Run it to see how slowly anything learns at half a percent.

def rank_probs(al, be, L=3, G=4000):          # P(theta_i is the m-th largest), theta_i ~ Beta(al_i, be_i), on one grid
    m, s = al / (al + be), np.sqrt(al * be / ((al + be) ** 2 * (al + be + 1)))
    x = np.linspace(max(0, (m - 12 * s).min()), min(1, (m + 12 * s).max()), G + 1); dx = x[1] - x[0]
    lp = (al[:, None] - 1) * np.log(np.maximum(x, 1e-300)) + (be[:, None] - 1) * np.log(np.maximum(1 - x, 1e-300))
    f = np.exp(lp - lp.max(1, keepdims=True)); f /= ((f[:, 1:] + f[:, :-1]) * dx / 2).sum(1, keepdims=True)
    F = np.minimum(1, np.concatenate([np.zeros((len(al), 1)), np.cumsum((f[:, 1:] + f[:, :-1]) * dx / 2, 1)], 1))
    P = np.zeros((len(al), L))
    for i in range(len(al)):
        e = [np.ones(G + 1)] + [np.zeros(G + 1) for _ in range(L - 1)]
        for j in range(len(al)):
            if j == i: continue
            for k in range(L - 1, 0, -1): e[k] = e[k] * F[j] + e[k - 1] * (1 - F[j])
            e[0] = e[0] * F[j]
        for k in range(L): g = f[i] * e[k]; P[i, k] = ((g[1:] + g[:-1]) * dx / 2).sum()
    return P

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A constant step size turns the sample average into a recency-weighted average that tracks a moving target; optimistic starts buy early exploration but only once.
  • UCB explores where the error bars are wide; its regret grows like ln T, and Lai and Robbins showed nothing can do better than a KL-determined multiple of ln T.
  • Thompson sampling draws from each arm’s posterior and pulls the best draw, so each arm is played with the probability that it is best; for Bernoulli arms it meets the Lai–Robbins bound.
  • With geometric discounting, frozen idle arms and free switching, the Gittins index (one number per arm, found by calibration) is exactly optimal; deadlines, selection problems, restless arms and superprocesses break it.
  • Contextual bandits learn a policy from context to arm; logs from a known, randomised logger let you grade new policies offline with IPS, self-normalised IPS and doubly robust estimates, but at low click rates the error bars are wide, so deploy on lower bounds.

Check your understanding

With α = 0.1, how much weight does a reward from 20 pulls ago carry, and why would you want that?

α(1 − α)20 = 0.1 × 0.920 ≈ 0.012, against 0.1 for the newest reward. If the arm’s mean drifts, old rewards describe an arm that no longer exists, so discounting them lets the estimate follow the change; the price is that the estimate never stops fluctuating, even if nothing changes.

Why does a greedy learner with optimistic starts show a spike in % optimal action around step 11 on the 10-armed testbed?

Starting optimistic, it tries all ten arms once in the first ten steps (each disappointment sends it elsewhere). At step 11 every arm has one observation, and the arm with the best one is quite likely the best arm, so many runs pick it at once. Then that arm too disappoints relative to its still-optimistic estimate and the runs scatter again, before settling.

UCB1 and Thompson sampling both have logarithmic regret. Why can Thompson sampling still be much better in practice, for example at a 0.5% click rate?

The constants differ. UCB1’s Hoeffding bonus assumes rewards could be anywhere in [0, 1], so at a 0.5% rate its error bars are many times wider than the real uncertainty and it keeps exploring almost uniformly for a very long time. Thompson sampling’s Beta posteriors know the variance of a rare-event rate and match the Lai–Robbins constant.

The Gittins index of Beta(1, 1) at γ = 0.9 is about 0.703, although the mean is 0.5. What does the extra 0.2 pay for?

Information. Pulling an untried arm tells you whether it is good; if it is, you keep pulling it, and if not you retire. That option to stop after bad news makes an uncertain arm worth more than a sure arm with the same mean. As the arm is pulled more, the bonus shrinks towards zero.

Your log was gathered by a policy that showed item 3 with probability 0.98. Why is a new policy that never shows item 3 hard to evaluate offline, and what would you do?

The rows where the logger showed other items, the only ones that tell you about the new policy, are rare and carry huge IPS weights (1/0.01 = 100 for each of the two other items), so estimates are dominated by a few rows and have high variance. Use self-normalised or doubly robust estimates, report a lower confidence bound rather than a point estimate, and for the future log with more exploration.
