Scoring Generated Text

BLEU, chrF, COMET and human ratings: deciding whether a sentence a machine wrote is any good

A classifier is right or wrong. A translation, a summary or a chatbot’s answer is neither: there are thousands of good ways to say the same thing and one reference sentence written by one person. Every metric in this chapter is a different compromise with that fact. Some count the words a candidate shares with the reference; some compare the meanings of its words; some are trained to predict what people would say; and in the end people have to be asked, carefully. The problem

Why scoring generated text is hard

When a model labels an email as spam, checking it is trivial: compare the label with the truth. When a model translates Le magasin ferme à six heures le samedi, what is the truth? “The shop closes at six on Saturdays” is right. So is “On Saturdays the shop shuts at six”, and “Saturday closing time is six o’clock”, and a few hundred other sentences. A translation task, a summarisation task or an open-ended question has a set of acceptable answers, and that set is far too large to write down. What we have instead is one or two references: answers a person wrote, which sample the set without covering it.

That single fact drives the whole chapter. An automatic metric compares the system’s candidate (also called the hypothesis) with the references and returns a number. Since the references are a sample, every metric must decide how to treat a candidate that is correct but differs from them, and how to treat one that is close to them but wrong. The two failure modes are symmetric and both are common: a good paraphrase scores low because it shares few words with the reference; a fluent sentence that flips one crucial word scores high because it shares every other word. Three things make this hard rather than merely fiddly. First, quality has several dimensions. Translators separate adequacy (does it say what the source says?) from fluency (is it good English?); summarisation adds coverage and faithfulness; dialogue adds helpfulness and safety. A single number must fold these together somehow, and different metrics weight them differently without saying so. Second, the reference is one person’s choice among many, so a metric that rewards closeness to it rewards that person’s style. Third, small edits can carry large meaning: “not” is one token out of ten, Sunday and Saturday differ in one word, and a number can be off by a digit. Surface overlap is nearly blind to all three.

The chapter follows the field’s own history. It starts with BLEU, which counts shared n-grams and has been the standard currency of machine translation since 2002; moves to chrF, which counts shared character n-grams; then to metrics for summarisation and question answering (ROUGE, exact match, TER); then to metrics that compare meanings through embeddings or are trained to predict human scores; then to the human evaluation all of them are trying to approximate; and finally to the question that makes any of this scientific: how do we know which metric to trust?

Overlap with the reference

BLEU, chrF, ROUGE, TER

Count the words, characters or edits the candidate shares with the reference. Fast, transparent, language-agnostic; blind to synonyms and to meaning.

Meaning through embeddings

BERTScore

Match candidate and reference tokens by the cosine similarity of their contextual embeddings. Sees paraphrase; still needs a reference and still inherits the embeddings’ blind spots.

Trained to predict people

COMET, BLEURT, LLM judges

Regress human ratings on the source, candidate and reference. Best agreement with people on the languages and domains they were trained on; opaque, and only as good as their training judgements.

The target itself

Human evaluation

Direct assessment, error annotation (MQM), pairwise preference. Expensive, noisy, and the only thing every metric is ultimately scored against.

The classic metric

The anatomy of BLEU

BLEU (the bilingual evaluation understudy; Papineni, Roukos, Ward & Zhu, 2002) was designed at IBM as a cheap stand-in for human translators during development: run it after every change, call the humans once a month. Its core idea is n-gram precision: of the word sequences of length n in the candidate, what fraction also appears in a reference? Unigram precision asks whether the candidate uses the right words; bigram, trigram and 4-gram precision ask whether it puts them in the right order and in fluent runs. BLEU is a geometric mean of the four, times a penalty for candidates that are too short. Each part fixes a way the previous part could be cheated, so it is worth building it up one fix at a time.

Fix 1: clipping. Plain precision is gamed by repetition. If the reference contains “the” twice, a candidate consisting of seven copies of “the” has unigram precision 7/7. BLEU therefore clips each n-gram’s count at the largest number of times it occurs in any single reference: those seven “the”s earn 2 matches, not 7. The result is called modified n-gram precision.

\[ p_n \;=\; \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{g \in \text{n-grams}(C)} \min\!\big(\mathrm{count}_C(g),\ \max_{R}\mathrm{count}_R(g)\big)}{\displaystyle\sum_{g \in \text{n-grams}(C)} \mathrm{count}_C(g)} \]
Read it as: for every distinct n-gram g in the candidate C, take how often it occurs in C, but no more than its largest count in any one reference R. Add these clipped counts up and divide by the number of n-grams in the candidate, which is (length − n + 1). The denominator is the candidate’s, so this is a precision: it never asks what the candidate left out.
Say it“p-n is the sum of clipped n-gram counts over the number of n-grams in the candidate.”

Fix 2: the geometric mean. Unigram precision alone rewards a bag of the right words in any order. Longer n-grams reward order and fluency, but 4-gram matches are rare, so the four precisions live on very different scales (in the workbench below p1 is typically 0.6–0.9 while p4 is 0.1–0.4). BLEU combines them with a geometric mean, exp of the average log, which is closer to the small ones than an arithmetic mean would be, and which is zero if any single precision is zero. The standard choice is N = 4 with equal weights wn = ¼.

Fix 3: the brevity penalty. A precision can be made perfect by saying very little: the candidate “the” has p1 = 1 if the reference contains “the”. The natural cure is recall, but with several references of different lengths recall is ill-defined (which one must the candidate cover?). BLEU instead multiplies by a brevity penalty BP that is 1 when the candidate is at least as long as the reference and decays exponentially as it gets shorter. With several references, the reference length r used is the one closest to the candidate’s length c (ties go to the shorter).

\[ \mathrm{BP} = \begin{cases} 1 & c > r \\ e^{\,1 - r/c} & c \le r \end{cases} \]
Read it as: c is the total candidate length and r the (effective) reference length. A candidate 10% shorter than the reference (r/c = 1.11) is multiplied by e−0.11 = 0.895; one half as long by e−1 = 0.368. Nothing penalises being too long: that already costs precision, because the extra n-grams are unmatched.
Say it“BP is one if the candidate is longer than the reference, otherwise e to the one minus r over c.”
\[ \mathrm{BLEU} = \mathrm{BP}\cdot\exp\!\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{4} \tfrac14 \log p_n\Big) = \mathrm{BP}\cdot\sqrt[4]{p_1 p_2 p_3 p_4} \]
Read it as: the geometric mean of the four modified precisions, scaled by the brevity penalty. It lies between 0 and 1 and is usually reported ×100. If any pn is 0 the log is −∞ and BLEU is exactly 0, which is why single sentences need smoothing (below).
Say it“BLEU is the brevity penalty times the fourth root of p-one p-two p-three p-four.”

Corpus level, not sentence level. BLEU was defined for a whole test set: the clipped counts and the n-gram totals are summed over all sentences before dividing, and BP uses the total lengths. This matters. A 4-gram precision of 0/5 on one short sentence is noise; summed over 2,000 sentences the ratio is stable. A corpus BLEU is therefore not the average of sentence BLEUs. When people need a score per sentence (for a correlation study, or to find the worst outputs) they compute sentence BLEU with a smoothing rule that keeps a zero precision from annihilating the product: the simplest adds 1 to the numerator and denominator of p2, p3 and p4 (Lin & Och, 2004); another replaces a zero count by a small floor such as 0.1; Chen and Cherry (2014) compare seven such rules.

Tokenisation is part of the metric. “Saturdays.” and “Saturdays” are different unigrams unless the full stop is split off; lower-casing merges “The” and “the”. For years papers reported BLEU scores computed with private tokenisers and were not comparable; Post (2018) built sacreBLEU to fix the tokeniser, the casing and the smoothing, and to print a signature string recording them. This page lower-cases and splits punctuation off words (keeping apostrophes inside words such as o’clock), and says so.

\[ \mathrm{BLEU}_{\text{corpus}} = \mathrm{BP}\!\left(\textstyle\sum_i c_i,\ \sum_i r_i\right)\cdot\exp\!\Big(\tfrac14\sum_{n=1}^{4}\log\frac{\sum_i m_{n,i}}{\sum_i t_{n,i}}\Big),\qquad p_n^{\,+1} = \frac{m_n + 1}{t_n + 1}\ \ (n \ge 2) \]
Read it as: mn,i is the clipped match count and tn,i the number of n-grams of order n in sentence i; the corpus score divides the sums, not the sentences. On the right, add-one smoothing for a single sentence: every order above 1 gets one free match out of one extra n-gram, so a pn of 0/4 becomes 1/5 rather than 0.
Say it“Sum the matches and the totals over the corpus first, then divide; for one sentence, add one to the top and bottom of the higher orders.”

What BLEU rewards and what it misses

The table takes one reference and six candidates I wrote to probe the metric, and scores them live. Read it as a list of known behaviours, each with its reason. Synonyms earn nothing: “turned down” for “rejected” is as wrong to BLEU as a random word, because matching is exact. Word order is only locally enforced: moving a whole clause breaks the two or three n-grams at the seam and keeps everything else, so a grammatical reordering costs about as much as an ungrammatical one of the same size. Meaning is invisible: flipping “rejected” to “accepted” costs one unigram, two bigrams, three trigrams and four 4-grams, exactly what any single-word substitution costs, whether or not it reverses the sentence. Shortness is punished by BP, length by precision: the truncated candidate has perfect raw precisions (every n-gram it contains is in the reference) and a brevity penalty of 0.51; the padded one has a penalty of 1 and lower precisions. And the degenerate candidate of nine “the”s shows clipping at work: without it p1 would be 1.

None of this means BLEU is useless. Averaged over a few thousand sentences, the overlap with a reference tracks genuine improvements in a system surprisingly well, especially when the systems being compared are similar in kind, and BLEU’s transparency (you can see exactly which n-grams matched) is a real virtue. What the examples show is the scale at which it can be trusted: a corpus-level difference of several points between two similar systems, not a verdict on one sentence, and not a comparison between a system that paraphrases freely and one that translates literally. Mathur, Baldwin and Cohn (2020) made this precise and we return to it in meta-evaluation.

Characters, not words

chrF and chrF++: character n-gram F-scores

A word-level metric treats garden and gardens, play and played, as unrelated strings. In English that is a nuisance; in Finnish, Turkish, Czech or Arabic, where a single word carries case, number, person and tense as suffixes, it is a disaster: a translation with every word stem right and a few endings wrong can share almost no whole words with the reference. Popović (2015) proposed scoring character n-grams instead. A candidate that writes gardens where the reference has garden still matches the character 1-grams to 6-grams inside “garden”; only the n-grams touching the final “s” are lost. Characters also make the metric independent of any tokeniser, which removed a whole class of reproducibility problems.

chrF differs from BLEU in a second way: it is an F-score, combining precision and recall, rather than precision with a brevity penalty. Recall is well-defined because chrF assumes a single reference. The standard setting weights recall twice as much as precision (β = 2, “chrF2”), since omitting content is judged worse than adding some, and uses character n-grams of order 1 to 6, computed on the text with the spaces removed so that n-grams can span word boundaries. chrF++ (Popović, 2017) adds word unigrams and bigrams to the average, which recovers a little sensitivity to word order and correlates slightly better with human judgements on most language pairs.

\[ \mathrm{chrP} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \frac{m_n}{|C|_n},\qquad \mathrm{chrR} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \frac{m_n}{|R|_n},\qquad \mathrm{chrF}_\beta = (1+\beta^2)\,\frac{\mathrm{chrP}\cdot\mathrm{chrR}}{\beta^2\,\mathrm{chrP} + \mathrm{chrR}} \]
Read it as: mn is the clipped number of character n-grams of order n that the candidate shares with the reference, |C|n and |R|n the numbers of n-grams of that order in each. Precision and recall are averaged over the N = 6 orders first, then combined; β = 2 makes recall count four times as much as precision in the denominator. chrF++ puts the word 1- and 2-gram precisions and recalls into the same averages, so N becomes 8. Orders where one side has no n-grams at all (a 6-gram order on a 4-character string) are left out of the average, following Popović’s implementation.
Say it“Average the character n-gram precisions, average the recalls, then take the F-beta with beta two.”

METEOR, in a paragraph

Between BLEU and chrF in time sits METEOR (Banerjee & Lavie, 2005), which attacked the synonym problem directly. It aligns candidate and reference words one-to-one in stages: exact matches first, then matches after stemming (gardens → garden), then WordNet synonyms (shut → close), later paraphrase tables. From the alignment it computes unigram precision and recall and an F-mean that weights recall nine times more than precision, and then multiplies by a fragmentation penalty: the aligned words are grouped into chunks, maximal runs that are adjacent and in the same order on both sides, and the fewer chunks the better. A candidate with the right words in the right order is one chunk; the same words scrambled are many. METEOR correlated with human judgements better than BLEU at the sentence level, but needs language resources (a stemmer, a synonym dictionary) for every language, which is the price chrF was designed not to pay.

\[ F_{\text{mean}} = \frac{10\,P\,R}{R + 9P},\qquad \text{Pen} = \tfrac12\Big(\frac{\#\text{chunks}}{\#\text{matched}}\Big)^{3},\qquad \text{METEOR} = F_{\text{mean}}\,(1 - \text{Pen}) \]
Read it as: P and R are unigram precision and recall over the aligned words. The penalty is at most ½ (when every matched word is its own chunk) and vanishes as the matches form one long run.
Other overlap metrics

ROUGE, exact match and TER

Translation is not the only task scored against references, and other tasks want different compromises. A summary must cover the important content, so the natural question is recall: how much of the reference summary did the candidate reproduce? A short answer to a factual question is either the right entity or not, so the natural question is exact match. A post-editor paid by the hour cares how many edits it takes to fix the output. Each view gives a metric.

ROUGE (Lin, 2004; recall-oriented understudy for gisting evaluation) is BLEU’s mirror image for summarisation. ROUGE-N is n-gram recall: the clipped n-gram matches divided by the number of n-grams in the reference. ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-2 are the usual pair. ROUGE-L replaces n-grams with the longest common subsequence (LCS): the longest sequence of words that appears in both sentences in the same order, not necessarily adjacent. It rewards order without demanding contiguous runs, so a summary that keeps the reference’s skeleton with other words in between still scores. The LCS is found by the same dynamic-programming table as edit distance in chapter 4.0b: cell (i, j) holds the LCS length of the first i candidate words and the first j reference words.

\[ \text{ROUGE-N} = \frac{\sum_{g} \min(\mathrm{count}_C(g), \mathrm{count}_R(g))}{\sum_{g} \mathrm{count}_R(g)} \]
Read it as: the same clipped matches as BLEU’s numerator, but divided by the reference’s n-gram count. A candidate that copies the whole reference and adds a paragraph still scores 1: ROUGE-N does not punish length, which is why systems tuned on it tend to write long.
Say it“ROUGE-N is the clipped n-gram matches over the n-grams in the reference.”
\[ L(i,j) = \begin{cases} 0 & i=0 \text{ or } j=0 \\ L(i-1,j-1)+1 & c_i = r_j \\ \max\big(L(i-1,j),\,L(i,j-1)\big) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]
Read it as: if the last words match, extend the best subsequence of the two prefixes; if not, drop one word from either side and keep the better. ROUGE-L takes P = L/|C| and R = L/|R| and reports their F-score (Lin used a strongly recall-weighted F; this page shows the plain F1, as most modern toolkits do by default).
Say it“L of i, j is L of i minus one, j minus one, plus one when the words match, otherwise the larger neighbour.”

TER, the translation edit rate (Snover et al., 2006), measures how much work a human would need to turn the candidate into the reference: the minimum number of word insertions, deletions, substitutions and shifts, divided by the reference length. Lower is better; 0 means identical, and values above 1 are possible. The shift is the new move: picking up a block of words and dropping it elsewhere counts as one edit, however far it travels, because that is one action for an editor. Finding the optimal mix of shifts and ordinary edits is NP-hard, so TER uses a greedy search for shifts followed by exact edit distance. The panel above shows the shift-free version, plain word-level Levenshtein distance over the reference length; for the default pair, the moved clause “on Tuesday” costs several edits here and would cost one shift in TER. HTER, with the reference replaced by a human post-edit of the candidate itself, is one of the most trusted automatic measures of translation quality, because the reference is no longer an arbitrary alternative but the nearest correct sentence.

Exact match and token F1. For extractive question answering (SQuAD and its descendants) and for many structured tasks, the reference is a short span and the metric is binary: after lower-casing, stripping punctuation and the articles a, an, the, and collapsing whitespace, does the prediction equal the gold answer? Exact match (EM) is the fraction of questions where it does. Because a prediction of “Lyon” against a gold “Lyon, France” deserves partial credit, it is paired with token F1: the F1 of the bag of normalised tokens, taking the maximum over the gold answers when there are several. For code and maths the same idea becomes execution match, running the generated program against tests, which is the one case where a reference-based metric has no paraphrase problem at all: the test is the specification.

Meaning, approximately

Embedding-based metrics: BERTScore and COMET

Every metric so far matches strings exactly. Shut and close share no characters that matter; purchase and buy share none at all. The obvious fix is to compare word vectors instead of word spellings. BERTScore (Zhang, Kishore, Wu, Weinberger & Artzi, 2020) does exactly that: run the candidate and the reference through a pre-trained encoder such as BERT (chapter 4.3a), take the contextual embedding of every token, and compute the cosine similarity between every candidate token and every reference token. Then match greedily: each reference token is paired with its most similar candidate token, and the average of those best similarities is the recall; each candidate token with its most similar reference token gives the precision; F1 combines them. Optionally each token is weighted by its inverse document frequency, so that matching “the” counts for less than matching “Lyon”.

\[ R_{\text{BERT}} = \frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{r_i \in R}\ \max_{c_j \in C}\ \mathbf{r}_i^{\top}\mathbf{c}_j,\qquad P_{\text{BERT}} = \frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{c_j \in C}\ \max_{r_i \in R}\ \mathbf{r}_i^{\top}\mathbf{c}_j,\qquad F_{\text{BERT}} = \frac{2\,P\,R}{P + R} \]
Read it as: ri and cj are unit-length embeddings, so the dot product is the cosine. Recall walks along the reference and asks, for each token, how well the candidate’s best token covers it; precision walks along the candidate and asks whether each token is accounted for. Greedy matching allows one candidate token to cover several reference tokens; the authors found that the optimal one-to-one assignment made no practical difference.
Say it“Recall averages, over reference tokens, the best cosine to any candidate token; precision the other way round; F is their harmonic mean.”

Even so, BERTScore is known to be gentle on word-order errors, and like every reference-based metric it inherits the reference’s choices.

COMET: a metric trained on human judgements

BERTScore uses an encoder but no evaluation data: it assumes that cosine similarity of embeddings is quality. COMET (Rei, Stewart, Farinha & Lavie, 2020; crosslingual optimised metric for evaluation of translation) drops that assumption and learns the mapping from text to quality from the human ratings that translation evaluation campaigns have been collecting for years. Its ingredients: a multilingual encoder (XLM-RoBERTa) that reads the source, the candidate and the reference separately into three pooled vectors s, h, r; a fixed set of features built from them; and a small feed-forward network on top that outputs one number. Only the top of the network and (lightly) the encoder are trained, by regression, to predict the human score of each training segment.

\[ \mathbf{x} = \big[\,\mathbf{h};\ \mathbf{r};\ \mathbf{s};\ \mathbf{h}\odot\mathbf{r};\ \mathbf{h}\odot\mathbf{s};\ |\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{r}|;\ |\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{s}|\,\big],\qquad \hat{y} = f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad \mathcal{L} = \sum_{\text{segments}} \big(\hat{y} - z\big)^2 \]
Read it as: the input to the regressor is the three sentence embeddings together with their element-wise products and absolute differences, so that “how does the candidate differ from the reference” and “from the source” are available directly. fθ is a two-layer network; the target z is the human direct-assessment score of the segment, standardised per annotator (see the next section). The training signal is nothing but thousands of (source, candidate, reference, human score) tuples from the WMT shared tasks.
Say it“Embed the three sentences, feed their products and differences to a small network, train it to reproduce the human scores.”

Three consequences follow from the training signal. First, COMET sees the source, which no overlap metric does, so it can notice that a fluent candidate says something the source did not; drop the reference input altogether and you get a quality-estimation metric (COMET-QE, CometKiwi) that needs no reference at all, which is how modern systems are scored on live traffic. Second, its scale is whatever the training data’s scale was: early COMET models output something like a z-score, later ones are calibrated to 0–1, and neither is a percentage of anything, so a COMET score is meaningful only relative to another COMET score from the same model. Third, it is only as broad as its training judgements: on language pairs, domains or error types that were rare in WMT data (literary text, very long documents, specific number errors) it can be confidently wrong, and because it is a neural network you cannot look inside to see which words it matched. In the WMT metrics shared tasks since 2020, COMET-style learned metrics have led the correlation tables by a clear margin, which is why the field has largely moved to them for system comparison while keeping chrF or BLEU alongside as a transparent sanity check. There is no COMET on this page, and I give no COMET numbers, because computing one requires the model.

  • BERTScoreNeeds: a reference and an encoder. Learns from: nothing (fixed similarity). Sees: token-level paraphrase. Misses: order (partly), antonyms, numbers, faithfulness to the source.
  • COMETNeeds: source, candidate and (optionally) reference, a model download. Learns from: human ratings. Sees: whatever those ratings reflected, including source faithfulness. Misses: anything rare in the training judgements; its scores are not interpretable in isolation.
  • BLEURT (Sellam et al., 2020)The same recipe for English generation tasks: a BERT fine-tuned on synthetic perturbations, then on human ratings. Reference-based; the model, not the formula, is the metric.
The target

Asking people: direct assessment, MQM and agreement

Every automatic metric is justified by its agreement with human judgement, so the human judgement has to be collected first, and collected well. Three designs dominate. Direct assessment (DA; Graham et al., 2013) shows an annotator the candidate, with the source or the reference, and asks for a number on a 0–100 slider: “how adequately does this express the meaning?” People use sliders differently (one person’s 70 is another’s 85), so each annotator’s scores are standardised: subtract that annotator’s mean and divide by their standard deviation, giving a z-score, and average the z-scores per segment. DA is cheap and scales to crowdworkers; its weakness is that untrained raters are swayed by fluency and miss meaning errors.

MQM, the multidimensional quality metrics framework (Lommel et al., 2014; applied to MT evaluation by Freitag et al., 2021), replaces the number with a list. A professional translator marks every error span in the candidate, assigns it a category from a typology (accuracy: mistranslation, omission, addition, untranslated; fluency: grammar, spelling, punctuation, register; terminology; style) and a severity: minor or major, with a special weight for a segment that is not a translation of the source at all. Each severity has a weight and the segment’s score is the weighted sum of its errors, so lower is better. Freitag et al. used major = 5, minor = 1, with a minor punctuation error at 0.1 and a non-translation at 25, and showed that MQM rankings of WMT systems disagreed sharply with the crowd DA rankings: the crowd had been rewarding fluent output and the experts were counting its meaning errors. Change the severities in the table to feel the weights.

\[ z_{a,i} = \frac{r_{a,i} - \bar r_a}{s_a},\qquad \text{DA}(i) = \frac{1}{|A_i|}\sum_{a \in A_i} z_{a,i} \]
Read it as: ra,i is annotator a’s raw slider score for segment i, r̄a and sa are the mean and standard deviation of everything a rated. Standardising removes each annotator’s personal scale and generosity; the segment score averages the standardised scores of the annotators Ai who saw it.
Say it“z is the score minus the rater’s mean over the rater’s standard deviation; average the z’s per segment.”

Do the annotators agree? Cohen’s κ

Human judgement is the gold standard, but humans disagree, and a rating that two experts would not reproduce cannot anchor anything. Inter-annotator agreement measures how far two annotators’ labels coincide beyond what chance would produce. For two raters and categorical labels the standard statistic is Cohen’s κ (Cohen, 1960). Raw agreement po is the fraction of items they label the same. Chance agreement pe is what two raters who each picked labels at random with their own marginal frequencies would achieve: if both say “acceptable” 90% of the time they will agree about 82% of the time without looking. κ is the fraction of the available room above chance that was actually used. It is 1 for perfect agreement, 0 for chance, and negative when raters agree less than chance. Landis and Koch’s rules of thumb (0.4–0.6 moderate, 0.6–0.8 substantial) are widely quoted and have no theoretical basis; for MT ratings, κ around 0.4–0.6 between trained annotators is typical and a crowd often sits below 0.3. Click any cell below to flip a label.

\[ \kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e},\qquad p_o = \frac{a + d}{n},\qquad p_e = \frac{(a+b)(a+c) + (c+d)(b+d)}{n^2} \]
Read it as: in the 2 × 2 table of two raters’ yes/no labels, a is both-yes, d both-no, b and c the disagreements. pe multiplies rater 1’s yes-rate by rater 2’s yes-rate, adds the same for no, and so predicts how often they would coincide by luck. With more raters the same idea becomes Fleiss’ κ; with ordered or numeric labels, weighted κ or Krippendorff’s α.

Relative judgements

Preferences, Bradley–Terry and Elo

People are bad at absolute scores and good at comparisons. Asked “how good is this translation, 0 to 100?” two raters drift apart; asked “which of these two is better?” they agree far more often. Pairwise preference evaluation therefore shows the same source with two systems’ outputs and records a choice (or a tie). The cost is that comparisons do not come with a scale: to turn a table of “A beat B 14 times out of 20” into a ranking, and into a statement about how much better A is, you need a model of how preferences arise.

The Bradley–Terry model (1952) is the standard one. Give every system a positive strength wi and assume the probability that i is preferred to j is wi/(wi + wj). Equivalently, on the log scale βi = log wi, the probability is the logistic function of the difference βi − βj, so Bradley–Terry is logistic regression with one indicator feature per system. Given a table of wins, the strengths are fitted by maximum likelihood; the iterative minorisation–maximisation update below (Zermelo, 1929; Hunter, 2004) is the classical way to do it and converges from any start. The scale is arbitrary (multiply every w by 2 and nothing changes), so one normalises, here to geometric mean 1. The same model, with people as the systems and chess games as the comparisons, is what Elo approximates; chapter 4.10 uses it for chatbot arenas. Here the question is MT systems.

\[ P(i \succ j) = \frac{w_i}{w_i + w_j} = \sigma(\beta_i - \beta_j),\qquad w_i \leftarrow \frac{W_i}{\sum_{j \ne i} \dfrac{n_{ij}}{w_i + w_j}} \]
Read it as: Wi is the total number of comparisons i won and nij the number of times i and j were compared. Each update sets i’s strength to its wins divided by its “expected wins per unit strength” under the current estimates: a system that won more than the current strengths predict is raised, one that won less is lowered. Iterate to a fixed point, renormalising each time.
Say it“The chance i beats j is w-i over w-i plus w-j; update w-i to its wins over the sum of n-i-j over w-i plus w-j.”
\[ E_A = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_B - R_A)/400}},\qquad R_A \leftarrow R_A + K\,(S_A - E_A) \]
Read it as: Elo’s online version of the same model. R are ratings on a scale where 400 points means 10:1 odds; EA is A’s expected score against B; SA is the actual result (1, ½, 0); K sets the step. Elo updates after every game and therefore depends on the order of games and never quite settles; Bradley–Terry fits all games at once and is what you want for a fixed table of judgements.
Say it“Expected score is one over one plus ten to the rating gap over four hundred; move the rating by K times actual minus expected.”

Judging the judges

Meta-evaluation: how a metric earns trust, and how it loses it

A metric is a prediction of human judgement, so it is evaluated like any predictor: collect human scores for a set of outputs, compute the metric on the same outputs, and measure the agreement. The annual WMT metrics shared task has done this since 2008, and its correlation tables are the reason BLEU gave way to chrF and chrF to COMET. Agreement is measured at two levels. System level: average both over all segments of each system and correlate the handful of system averages (does the metric rank the systems the way people do?). Segment level: correlate across individual sentences (does the metric know which sentences are better?). Segment-level correlations are always much lower, both because single human ratings are noisy and because metrics are poor at fine distinctions.

Three correlation coefficients are in use and they answer different questions. Pearson’s r measures linear agreement between the values; it is dominated by extreme points. Spearman’s ρ is Pearson’s r computed on ranks, so it asks only whether the ordering agrees. Kendall’s τ counts pairs: of all pairs of items, how many does the metric order the same way as the humans (concordant) minus how many it reverses (discordant), as a fraction of all pairs. τ has the most direct meaning for a metric that will be used to make decisions (“when the metric says A is better than B, how often is it right?”), and the WMT task has used variants of it at the segment level for years. Drag any point below and watch r swing while ρ and τ stay put.

\[ r = \frac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2 \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2}},\qquad \rho = r\big(\mathrm{rank}(x), \mathrm{rank}(y)\big),\qquad \tau = \frac{n_{\text{concordant}} - n_{\text{discordant}}}{\binom{n}{2}} \]
Read it as: x is the human score and y the metric score of each item. r rewards points that lie on a straight line; ρ replaces every value by its position in the sorted order first (ties get the average of the positions they span); τ looks at every pair (i, j) and asks whether x and y agree about which is larger. The τ-b variant used on this page discounts tied pairs in the denominator.

The pitfalls. A high correlation on last year’s systems does not make a metric safe, and the field has learned the failure modes the hard way.

  • Reference biasEvery reference-based metric rewards the reference’s wording. A system that translates literally, close to how references are usually written, beats a system that paraphrases well; and human references themselves vary in quality, so a bad reference punishes the good system. Multiple references help a little; comparing against post-edits (HTER) or scoring without a reference (COMET-QE) helps more.
  • Metric gamingOnce a metric is the objective, systems optimise it (Goodhart’s law). Minimum-risk training on BLEU produces output with more matched n-grams and no better translations; ROUGE-tuned summarisers write long; reinforcement learning against a learned metric finds the metric’s blind spots. The metric you tune on cannot also be the metric you evaluate on.
  • Length effectsBLEU’s brevity penalty, ROUGE’s recall and BERTScore’s recall all move with output length independently of quality. Comparing two systems whose outputs differ in length is partly comparing their length. Always report lengths alongside scores.
  • ContaminationIf the test sentences, or their references, were in a model’s training data, every reference-based metric is inflated for that model. With web-scale pre-training this is the default rather than the exception; the remedy is fresh test sets, which is why WMT builds new ones each year.
  • “Tangled up in BLEU”Mathur, Baldwin and Cohn (2020) showed that the high system-level correlations reported for BLEU were driven largely by a few very poor systems: remove the outliers and the correlation among the good systems collapses. They also found that differences of one or two BLEU points between similar systems carried essentially no information about which humans preferred. Their recommendation: report Kendall-type accuracy on system pairs, drop outliers, and stop treating a one-point gain as a result.
  • Averaging across the wrong thingsA system-level score averages over segments; a segment-level correlation averages over systems. A metric can rank systems correctly while being nearly random on segments (BLEU) or vice versa. Say which you measured.

LLM-as-a-judge

The newest evaluator is a large language model with a prompt: “here is a question and two answers; which is better, and why?” Zheng et al. (2023) studied this systematically with GPT-4 as the judge on open-ended chatbot conversations and found agreement with human experts above 80%, about the level at which the human experts agreed with each other. Since then a model-as-judge has become the default way to evaluate anything without a reference: chat answers, summaries, instruction following, translation. It is in effect a learned metric with zero task-specific training, which is its power and its risk. Three biases are well documented, and each has a partial fix.

  • Position biasShown two answers, judges prefer the first (or, for some models, the second) more often than content explains. Zheng et al. found GPT-4 reversed its verdict on a large share of pairs when the order was swapped. Fix: judge each pair in both orders and count only consistent verdicts, or average.
  • Verbosity biasLonger, more elaborate answers win more often than their content deserves; a judge can be fooled by padding an answer with restatements. Fix: instruct the judge to penalise irrelevant length, control length across systems, or report length alongside win rate (as length-controlled arena rankings now do).
  • Self-preferenceA model rates its own outputs, and outputs in its own style, higher than other judges do; it also recognises text it is likely to have produced. Fix: use a panel of judges from different families, or never let a model judge a competition it is in.
  • Limited reasoningA judge that cannot solve a maths problem cannot reliably grade a solution to it, and may side with a confident wrong answer. Fix: give the judge the reference solution, ask for step-by-step checking, or use execution and tests where possible.

Project: a metric bake-off

Three imaginary translation systems have each translated the same 20 French sentences into English. I wrote all 60 outputs by hand with one deliberate flaw per system: System A is fluent and natural but unfaithful, changing one fact in each sentence (a time, a day, a verb, a negation); System B is faithful to the source but stilted, choosing awkward or over-literal English; System C uses the reference’s own words but puts them in a wrong order. A fourth party, the simulated human raters, scores every output; the question is which automatic metric agrees with them. Because I built the systems, I know the answer in advance: people should prefer B, then C, then A. The point is to see what each metric makes of it, and to run the whole meta-evaluation pipeline of the previous section on data small enough to read.

# ---------- tokenisation and n-grams ----------
def tok(s):                                  # lower-case words (keeping o'clock), punctuation as tokens
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+(?:'[a-z]+)?|[^\sa-z0-9]", s.lower())

def ngrams(seq, n):
    return Counter(tuple(seq[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(seq) - n + 1))

# ---------- BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) ----------
def bleu_stats(cand, refs):                  # per-sentence sufficient statistics
    c, rs = tok(cand), [tok(r) for r in refs]
    match, total = [], []
    for n in range(1, 5):
        cg = ngrams(c, n)
        maxref = Counter()
        for r in rs:
            for g, k in ngrams(r, n).items():
                maxref[g] = max(maxref[g], k)
        match.append(sum(min(k, maxref[g]) for g, k in cg.items()))   # clipped counts
        total.append(max(0, len(c) - n + 1))
    # effective reference length: the closest in length to the candidate (shorter wins ties)
    r = min((abs(len(x) - len(c)), len(x)) for x in rs)[1]
    return match, total, len(c), r

def bleu_from_stats(match, total, c, r, smooth=None):
    logs = 0.0
    for n in range(4):
        m, t = match[n], total[n]
        if smooth == "add1" and n > 0:
            m, t = m + 1, t + 1
        if m == 0 or t == 0:
            return 0.0
        logs += math.log(m / t)
    bp = 1.0 if c > r else math.exp(1 - r / c) if c > 0 else 0.0
    return 100 * bp * math.exp(logs / 4)

def corpus_bleu(cands, refs_list):
    M, T, C, R = [0] * 4, [0] * 4, 0, 0
    for cand, refs in zip(cands, refs_list):
        m, t, c, r = bleu_stats(cand, refs)
        M = [a + b for a, b in zip(M, m)]; T = [a + b for a, b in zip(T, t)]; C += c; R += r
    return bleu_from_stats(M, T, C, R)

def sentence_bleu(cand, refs):
    return bleu_from_stats(*bleu_stats(cand, refs), smooth="add1")

# ---------- chrF / chrF++ (Popović, 2015, 2017): beta = 2, char orders 1..6, word orders 0 or 2 ----------
def chrf_stats(cand, ref, char_order=6, word_order=0):
    stats = []                                # per order: (matches, candidate count, reference count)
    c, r = "".join(cand.split()), "".join(ref.split())     # whitespace removed, n-grams may cross words
    for n in range(1, char_order + 1):
        cg, rg = ngrams(c, n), ngrams(r, n)
        stats.append((sum(min(k, rg[g]) for g, k in cg.items()), sum(cg.values()), sum(rg.values())))
    cw, rw = tok(cand), tok(ref)
    for n in range(1, word_order + 1):
        cg, rg = ngrams(cw, n), ngrams(rw, n)
        stats.append((sum(min(k, rg[g]) for g, k in cg.items()), sum(cg.values()), sum(rg.values())))
    return stats

def chrf_from_stats(stats, beta=2.0):
    P = R = 0.0; eff = 0
    for m, nc, nr in stats:
        if nc > 0 and nr > 0:                 # only orders where both sides have n-grams count
            P += m / nc; R += m / nr; eff += 1
    if eff == 0: return 0.0
    P /= eff; R /= eff
    if P + R == 0: return 0.0
    return 100 * (1 + beta ** 2) * P * R / (beta ** 2 * P + R)

def chrf(cand, ref, word_order=0):
    return chrf_from_stats(chrf_stats(cand, ref, 6, word_order))

def corpus_chrf(cands, refs, word_order=0):
    k = 6 + word_order
    S = [[0, 0, 0] for _ in range(k)]
    for cand, ref in zip(cands, refs):
        for i, st in enumerate(chrf_stats(cand, ref, 6, word_order)):
            for j in range(3): S[i][j] += st[j]
    return chrf_from_stats([tuple(s) for s in S])

# ---------- ROUGE-L (Lin, 2004) as an F1 of LCS-based precision and recall ----------
def lcs_len(a, b):
    prev = [0] * (len(b) + 1)
    for x in a:
        cur = [0]
        for j, y in enumerate(b):
            cur.append(prev[j] + 1 if x == y else max(prev[j + 1], cur[j]))
        prev = cur
    return prev[-1]

def rouge_l(cand, ref):
    c, r = tok(cand), tok(ref)
    l = lcs_len(c, r)
    if l == 0: return 0.0
    p, q = l / len(c), l / len(r)
    return 100 * 2 * p * q / (p + q)

def mulberry32(a):
    M = 0xFFFFFFFF
    def nxt():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & M
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & M
        t = (((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & M)) & M) ^ t) & M
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M) / 4294967296
    return nxt

def gauss(rnd):
    u = 0.0
    while u == 0.0: u = rnd()
    return math.sqrt(-2 * math.log(u)) * math.cos(2 * math.pi * rnd())

def bradley_terry(wins, iters=50):
    k = len(wins); w = np.ones(k); n = wins + wins.T
    for _ in range(iters):
        new = np.array([wins[i].sum() / sum(n[i, j] / (w[i] + w[j]) for j in range(k) if j != i) for i in range(k)])
        w = new / np.exp(np.log(new).mean())      # fix the scale: geometric mean 1
    return w

# ---------- correlations ----------
def rankdata(x):
    x = np.asarray(x, float); r = np.empty(len(x))
    order = np.argsort(x, kind="stable"); i = 0
    while i < len(x):
        j = i
        while j + 1 < len(x) and x[order[j + 1]] == x[order[i]]: j += 1
        r[order[i:j + 1]] = (i + j) / 2 + 1; i = j + 1
    return r

def pearson(x, y):
    x, y = np.asarray(x, float), np.asarray(y, float)
    x, y = x - x.mean(), y - y.mean()
    return float((x * y).sum() / math.sqrt((x * x).sum() * (y * y).sum()))

def spearman(x, y): return pearson(rankdata(x), rankdata(y))

def kendall(x, y):                            # tau-b, ties handled
    c = d = tx = ty = 0
    for i in range(len(x)):
        for j in range(i + 1, len(x)):
            sx, sy = np.sign(x[i] - x[j]), np.sign(y[i] - y[j])
            if sx == 0 and sy == 0: continue
            if sx == 0: tx += 1
            elif sy == 0: ty += 1
            elif sx == sy: c += 1
            else: d += 1
    return (c - d) / math.sqrt((c + d + tx) * (c + d + ty))

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Generated text has many correct forms and one reference, so every automatic metric is a compromise: it must forgive correct differences from the reference and catch incorrect similarities to it, and no metric does both well.
  • BLEU is a brevity-penalised geometric mean of clipped n-gram precisions, defined at the corpus level; it is transparent and language-agnostic, blind to synonyms and meaning, and only trustworthy for differences of several points between similar systems.
  • chrF scores character n-grams with a recall-weighted F-score and needs no tokeniser, which suits morphologically rich languages; ROUGE is recall for summaries, TER counts an editor’s work, exact match and token F1 score short answers.
  • Embedding metrics compare meanings: BERTScore by greedy cosine matching of contextual token vectors, COMET by a regressor trained on human ratings that also reads the source. They agree with people far better and are harder to inspect.
  • People are the target: direct assessment with per-rater standardisation, MQM error annotation by experts, pairwise preferences fitted by Bradley–Terry; check them with Cohen’s κ, and check every metric against them with Pearson, Spearman and Kendall, while remembering reference bias, gaming, length and contamination.

Check your understanding

A candidate is “the the the the” and the reference contains “the” twice. What are its plain and modified unigram precisions?

Plain precision is 4/4 = 1: every candidate unigram occurs in the reference. Modified precision clips the count of “the” at its reference count, 2, giving 2/4 = 0.5. Clipping is what stops a candidate from earning credit by repeating a correct word.

Why does BLEU use a brevity penalty rather than recall?

With several references of different lengths and wordings, recall is ill-defined: which reference must the candidate cover? BLEU instead compares the candidate’s length with the closest reference length and penalises shortness exponentially. The cost is that it never checks which content was omitted, only how much.

A test set of 2,000 sentences gives corpus BLEU 31.2. Averaging the sentence BLEUs (add-one smoothed) gives 28.7. Which is “the” BLEU, and why do they differ?

The corpus score is the one BLEU defines: matches and totals are summed over the set before dividing, and the brevity penalty uses total lengths. Sentence scores need smoothing to avoid zeros, and averaging ratios is not the ratio of sums, so the mean of sentence BLEUs is a different (and non-standard) number. Report the corpus score; use sentence scores only for per-sentence analysis.

chrF2 weights recall four times as much as precision in the denominator. What kind of error does that punish most, and what kind does it let through?

Omissions: content in the reference that the candidate lacks lowers recall, and recall dominates the F-score. Additions lower precision, which counts for less, so a candidate that says everything in the reference and then some more is penalised mildly.

Two raters label 50 outputs acceptable or not and agree on 45. Rater 1 says yes 40 times, rater 2 says yes 42 times. Compute κ.

po = 45/50 = 0.90. Yes-rates 0.80 and 0.84, no-rates 0.20 and 0.16, so pe = 0.80 × 0.84 + 0.20 × 0.16 = 0.672 + 0.032 = 0.704. κ = (0.90 − 0.704)/(1 − 0.704) = 0.196/0.296 = 0.66. High raw agreement, moderate-to-substantial κ, because both raters say yes most of the time and would agree often by chance.

In a Bradley–Terry fit, system X has strength 3 and system Y strength 1. What does the model predict for a comparison, and what would Elo call the gap?

P(X beats Y) = 3/(3+1) = 0.75. Elo expresses odds of 10:1 as 400 points; odds of 3:1 correspond to 400 × log10(3) ≈ 191 rating points.

A metric correlates with human judgements at r = 0.95 across 15 systems, but only 0.3 after removing the three worst systems. What happened, and what should you report?

The three outliers were far from everything else on both axes and a straight line through them and the cluster has a high r regardless of what happens inside the cluster. The 0.3 is the number that describes the metric’s ability to distinguish good systems, which is what you actually use it for. Report correlations with outliers removed, or pairwise ranking accuracy (Kendall-style), and give the confidence interval; this is Mathur, Baldwin and Cohn’s point.

You judge 500 answer pairs with an LLM, showing system P first every time. P wins 62%. What is wrong with the experiment, and how do you fix it?

Position bias is confounded with quality: a judge that favours the first answer will inflate P’s win rate whatever its quality. Judge every pair in both orders (or randomise the order) and either average the two verdicts or keep only the pairs where they agree. Then check for verbosity bias by comparing the systems’ output lengths.
