AI agents · chapter 5.1 · the agent loop

What Is an AI Agent?

A model in a loop: observe, think, act, and go round again

A chat model answers once. An agent keeps going: it looks at what has happened so far, chooses an action, takes it, sees the result and decides again, until the job is done or the budget runs out. In the figure an agent plays a five-letter word game against 2,000 real English words. Its controller is a transparent stand-in for a language model, a rule you can inspect, not an LLM. Press Step and follow it round the loop.

The idea

From answers to actions

A chat model maps a prompt to a reply in one pass. That is enough when everything it needs is already in the prompt. Many tasks are not like that: the information arrives only as you act. You run the tests and see which one fails; you search and read what comes back; you guess a word and learn which letters are right. Russell and Norvig’s textbook definition fits exactly: an agent is anything that perceives its environment through sensors and acts upon it through actuators. An AI agent in today’s sense puts a language model in charge of that loop, a system in which, in Anthropic’s phrase, the model “dynamically directs its own processes and tool usage”. Contrast a workflow, where the programmer fixes the sequence of model calls in code.

The environment for this whole chapter is a word game with Wordle’s rules. The secret is one of 2,000 common five-letter words. After each guess every letter is marked g right letter, right place; y in the word, elsewhere; or x not in the word. Three players get k guesses each: one who never sees the feedback, a careless agent that guesses any word that still fits the feedback, and the careful stand-in from the hero. Move the guess limit k and compare them.

\[ \begin{aligned} P_{\text{blind}}(k) &= \frac{k}{N} \\ P_{\text{loop}}(k) &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{w} \mathbb{1}\big[K_w \le k\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Pblind(k) is the chance of winning within k guesses without ever seeing feedback, Ploop(k) the same for an agent in the loop. The secret is one of N = 2,000 equally likely words. Without feedback each new guess rules out exactly one word, so k different guesses cover k of the N. With feedback, one guess rules out whole groups of words. Kw is the number of guesses an agent needs when the secret is w; it is counted by playing the agent against every one of the 2,000 secrets.
Say it“Without feedback you win with probability k over N; with the loop, it is the share of secret words the agent finds within k guesses.”

The loop

The agent loop: observe, think, act

Every agent, from this word-game player to a coding assistant, runs the same loop. The history holds everything so far: the task, every action, every observation. The controller reads the history and chooses the next action. The environment carries the action out and returns an observation, which is appended to the history. The loop stops when the goal is reached or a budget runs out. With an LLM as the controller the history is literally the prompt: each turn the whole transcript is sent again and the model writes the next action. ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) adds a written thought before each action; the loop is the same. Below is the transcript an LLM controller would receive in this game. Step through a game and watch the history grow while the list of words that fit shrinks.

\[ \begin{aligned} a_t &= \pi(h_t) \\ o_t &= E(a_t) \\ h_{t+1} &= h_t \oplus (a_t,\, o_t) \\ \text{stop if } & o_t = \text{goal or } t = T \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ht is the history before step t, starting with the task description h1; at is the action (a guess), chosen by the controller π; ot is the observation the environment E returns (the coloured feedback); ⊕ means “append”. T is the step budget, 6 guesses in the usual game.
Say it“The action is pi of the history; the observation is E of the action; the next history is the old one with the action and observation appended; stop at the goal or at step T.”

The controller · 1

    
Thinking: choosing the most informative action

    
Between observing and acting sits the decision. An LLM makes it implicitly, inside its forward pass and whatever reasoning it writes out. The stand-in makes it explicitly, in two moves you can check. First, keep what fits: a word stays a candidate only if it would have produced every piece of feedback seen so far. Second, score each possible action by what it is expected to teach. A guess sorts the remaining words into groups by the feedback each would give; if the groups are small and even, whatever comes back tells you a lot. Shannon’s entropy measures this in bits. The stand-in guesses the highest-scoring word among those that still fit, so every guess could also win (ties go to the first alphabetically). Pick a moment in a game, then try different guesses and see how each would split the words.

    
\[ \begin{aligned} C_{t+1} &= \{\, w \in C_t : \text{fb}(a_t, w) = o_t \,\} \\ H(a) &= -\sum_{f} \frac{n_f(a)}{|C_t|}\,\log_2 \frac{n_f(a)}{|C_t|} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Ct is the set of words that still fit; fb(a, w) is the feedback guess a would receive if the secret were w. nf(a) counts the candidates that would give feedback pattern f; there are 35 = 243 patterns. H(a) is the expected information of guess a, in bits. Finding the secret among |Ct| equally likely words takes log2|Ct| bits in all.
Say it“The new candidate set is the words whose feedback matches what we saw; the entropy of a guess is minus the sum over patterns of the share times log two of the share.”

The controller · 2

    
An LLM as the controller

    
Swap the stand-in for a language model and the loop is unchanged; only the decision is made differently. An LLM outputs a probability distribution over its next tokens, so its choice of action is a sample, sharpened or flattened by the sampling temperature, and coloured by whatever it learned in training rather than by an exact calculation. Two things make that workable inside an agent. Code can check every action before it runs: is the reply five letters, is it a real word? And the loop absorbs weak choices: a poor guess costs an extra step, and the next observation puts the agent back on course. To see the second effect, let a softmax over the information scores stand in for the LLM’s sampling. Raise the temperature and watch how many guesses the games take.

    
\[ \pi_\tau(a \mid h_t) = \frac{e^{H(a)/\tau}}{\sum_{a' \in C_t} e^{H(a')/\tau}} \]
Read it as: the controller samples guess a from the words that fit, with a probability that grows with its information score H(a). As τ → 0 it becomes the greedy stand-in; as τ → ∞ it picks any fitting word uniformly, the careless player of section 1. This is an analogy, not a model of any particular LLM: a real model’s distribution comes from its weights, but temperature acts on its logits in exactly this way.
Say it“The probability of guess a is e to the H of a over tau, divided by the same sum over all words that fit.”

Reliability

    
Why long tasks fail: compounding error

    
A weak choice costs a step. Some mistakes cost the whole task. Suppose the controller misreads one piece of feedback, taking a grey letter for a yellow one, and trusts its notes: from then on it filters with a false clue, crosses out the real answer, and nothing later can bring it back. If each step goes right independently with probability p and any failure is fatal, n steps all go right with probability pn, which falls off exponentially with the length of the task. Ord (2025) found that this constant-hazard model describes the measured success of AI agents on longer and longer tasks surprisingly well, which gives every agent a “half-life”. The defence is to check: a verifier that catches a share v of the errors before they do harm raises the effective reliability of every step. Move p, v and the task length n.

    

      
\[ \begin{aligned} P(\text{success}) &= p_{\text{eff}}^{\,n} \\ p_{\text{eff}} &= 1 - (1-p)(1-v) \\ n_{1/2} &= \frac{\ln 2}{-\ln p_{\text{eff}}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: p is the chance one step goes right, v the share of wrong steps a check catches and repairs, n the number of steps. The half-life n1/2 is the task length at which success falls to 50%; double the length and success is squared.
Say it“Success is p-effective to the power n, where p-effective is one minus the uncaught error rate; the half-life is log two over minus log p-effective.”

      
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{pass@}k &= 1-(1-P)^k \\ \text{pass}^k &= P^k \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: run the same task k times independently, each succeeding with probability P. pass@k is the chance that at least one run succeeds, useful only if you can tell which. passk (Yao et al., 2024, the τ-bench paper) is the chance that all k succeed: what a user sees from an agent that must work every time.
Say it“Pass at k is one minus the chance that all k fail; pass to the k is the chance that all k succeed.”

Budgets

    
Cost and latency budgets

    
An agent pays for every trip round the loop. With an LLM controller each turn sends the whole history again, so the prompt grows with every step and the total input grows with the square of the number of steps. Prompt caching, offered by the major API providers, bills the unchanged start of a prompt at a reduced rate; it softens the square but does not remove it, and every call still costs time. The clock runs step by step too: each turn waits for the model to start answering, for it to write its output, and for the tool to run. The prices below are examples, not any provider’s list price. Set your own numbers and see where the money and the minutes go.

    
\[ \begin{aligned} T_{\text{in}}(n) &= \sum_{t=1}^{n} \big(s + (t-1)\,m\big) \\ &= n\,s + m\,\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2} \\ \text{cost} &= c_{\text{in}}\,T_{\text{in}} + c_{\text{out}}\,n\,o \\ \text{time} &= n\,\big(\ell + o/r\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: s tokens of fixed prompt (instructions, tool descriptions, the task), m tokens added to the history per step (the action and its observation), o output tokens per step, cin and cout the prices per token, ℓ the waiting per step (time to the first token plus the tool’s own run time) and r the output speed in tokens per second. With caching, the part of each prompt already sent on the previous turn is billed at a fraction κ of cin (a simplification: real cache pricing also has a write charge and an expiry time).
Say it“Total input is n times s plus m times n times n minus one over two; cost is input price times input plus output price times output; time is n times waiting plus output over speed.”

Who decides?

    
Levels of autonomy

    
How much should an agent do without asking? Engineers have described automation in levels for decades, from the machine merely suggesting to the machine acting alone (Sheridan & Verplank, 1978; Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens, 2000), and cars have SAE’s levels 0 to 5. For a software agent a practical ladder has five rungs. L1: it only suggests; a person acts. L2: it acts, but a person approves every action. L3: it asks for approval only before risky actions. L4: it acts alone while a person watches and can stop it. L5: it acts alone, unwatched. Each rung up saves human time and lets more mistakes through. The task below is invented, with made-up numbers, to show the trade: twelve actions of an agent handling a refund request, each with a chance of being wrong and a cost if a wrong one goes through. Pick a level, or slide the approval threshold yourself.

    
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\text{cost}] = {} & \sum_{i} \big(1 - g_i\,r\big)\, e_i\, h_i \\ & + c_H \sum_i g_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: action i goes wrong with probability ei and costs hi if the mistake goes through. gi = 1 if a person must approve it first; a reviewer catches a share r of the wrong actions they see; each approval costs cH of a person’s time. The threshold rule gates action i when its expected harm eihi is at least θ.
Say it“Expected cost is the sum of the harm that slips past review plus the price of every approval.”

Judgement

    
When to use an agent, and when not to

    
An agent is not the default answer. Anthropic’s advice to builders (Schluntz & Zhang, 2024) is to find the simplest solution that works and add complexity only when it clearly pays: agents trade latency and cost for better performance on hard, open-ended tasks. The three options line up like this.

    

      
One model call
Everything needed is in the prompt
Summarise, classify, draft, answer from retrieved passages. Cheapest, fastest, easiest to test.

      
Workflow
The steps are known in advance
A fixed chain or branch of model calls written in code: extract, then check, then format. Predictable cost and behaviour.

      
Agent
The path depends on what you find
Debugging, research, multi-step tool use. Worth it when there are many steps, the next one cannot be known in advance, and success can be checked.

    

    
Putting this chapter’s formulas together gives a rough expected value for handing a task to an agent, to set against doing it by hand. Describe a task with the sliders and see which way the sum tips, and how the answer changes with the number of steps.

    
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\text{agent}] &= P\,V - (1-P)\,F - n\,c \\ P &= p_{\text{eff}}^{\,n} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: V is what a successful run is worth, F what a failed one costs (clean-up, or harm), n the steps it needs, c the cost per step (tokens, time, and the checks), and P the success probability from the compounding section. Doing the task by hand is worth V minus the human cost H, assuming a person always succeeds.
Say it“The agent is worth P times V, minus one minus P times F, minus n times c, where P is p-effective to the n.”

Real-world project

    
Project: an agent loop over 2,000 real words

    

      
The environment is real. The word list is the 2,000 most frequent five-letter words in the English subtitles of the OpenSubtitles 2018 corpus (the frequency lists of H. Dave’s FrequencyWords project, CC BY-SA 4.0) that are also in the ENABLE word list (public domain), after removing by hand 163 offensive words, words used mostly as names, and subtitle artefacts. The feedback follows Wordle’s rules exactly, repeated letters included: greens are marked first, then yellows from left to right while unmatched copies of the letter remain in the secret. The controller is the transparent stand-in of the sections above, not an LLM: it keeps the words that fit and guesses the one with the most expected information. Everything below runs in your browser: the 4,000,000 feedback patterns between every pair of words, every one of the 2,000 games, and the fallible versions of the controller.

3 · A fallible controller

    
Now let the controller misread: with probability ε per observation it changes one tile’s colour in its notes (one tile at random, to one of the other two colours at random). A verifier re-reads the raw transcript and catches a share v of those slips, at the price of one extra model call each. Because a slip always crosses out the true secret, an uncaught slip is always fatal, and the exact chance of winning is

    
\[ \begin{aligned} P(\text{win}) &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{w:\,K_w \le 6} q^{\,K_w - 1} \\ q &= 1 - \varepsilon(1-v) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a game won in Kw guesses involves Kw − 1 readings of feedback (the last one is the win itself, announced by the game). Each reading must avoid an uncaught slip, which it does with probability q: this is pn with a different n for every word. Games the careful agent cannot win within six guesses stay lost.
Say it“The win rate is the average, over winnable words, of q to the power K minus one, where q is one minus epsilon times one minus v.”

The same agent in Python


import json, math
words = json.load(open("wa-data.json", encoding="utf8"))["words"].split()
N = len(words)

def fb(g, s):                                 # Wordle feedback: 2 green, 1 yellow, 0 grey, as one base-3 number
    r, left = [0] * 5, {}
    for i in range(5):
        if g[i] == s[i]: r[i] = 2
        else: left[s[i]] = left.get(s[i], 0) + 1
    for i in range(5):
        if r[i] == 0 and left.get(g[i], 0) > 0: r[i] = 1; left[g[i]] -= 1
    return r[0] + 3 * r[1] + 9 * r[2] + 27 * r[3] + 81 * r[4]
PM = [bytes(fb(g, s) for s in words) for g in words]          # all 4,000,000 patterns

def entropy(g, C):
    n = len(C)
    if n <= 1: return 0.0
    b = [0] * 243
    for s in C: b[PM[g][s]] += 1
    return math.log2(n) - sum(c * math.log2(c) for c in b if c > 1) / n
ROOT = [entropy(g, range(N)) for g in range(N)]
def scores(C): return ROOT if len(C) == N else [entropy(g, C) for g in C]
def best(C):                                  # the stand-in: most informative word that still fits, ties alphabetical
    H, b = scores(C), 0
    for k in range(1, len(C)):
        if H[k] > H[b] + 1e-9: b = k
    return C[b], H[b]

def mulberry32(a):                            # the same seeded generator as the page
    a &= 0xFFFFFFFF
    def rnd():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return rnd

def corrupt(f, rnd):                          # one tile changes to one of the other two colours
    d = [(f // 3 ** i) % 3 for i in range(5)]; i = math.floor(rnd() * 5)
    d[i] = (d[i] + 1 + math.floor(rnd() * 2)) % 3
    return sum(d[j] * 3 ** j for j in range(5))

def play(secret, policy="best", tau=1.0, eps=0.0, v=0.0, T=6, rnd=None):
    C, calls = list(range(N)), 0
    for t in range(1, T + 1):
        if policy == "best": g = best(C)[0]
        elif policy == "random": g = C[math.floor(rnd() * len(C))]
        else:                                 # softmax over information scores
            H = scores(C); m = max(H); e = [math.exp((h - m) / tau) for h in H]
            u, k = rnd() * sum(e), 0
            while k < len(H) - 1 and u >= e[k]: u -= e[k]; k += 1
            g = C[k]
        calls += 1
        f = PM[g][secret]
        if f == 242: return "won", t, calls
        read = f
        if eps > 0 and rnd() < eps:
            read = corrupt(f, rnd)
            if v > 0 and rnd() < v: read = f; calls += 1
        if read == 242: return "claimed", t, calls
        C = [w for w in C if PM[g][w] == read]
        if not C: return "stuck", t, calls
    return "out", T, calls

o, h = best(list(range(N)))
top = sorted(range(N), key=lambda g: -ROOT[g])[:5]
print(f"{N} words; best opener {words[o]} H = {h:.6f} bits; top 5:", ", ".join(f"{words[g]} {ROOT[g]:.4f}" for g in top))
K = [play(s, T=20)[1] for s in range(N)]
dist = {k: K.count(k) for k in sorted(set(K))}
print("careful stand-in, guesses needed:", dist, f"mean {sum(K) / N:.4f}, won within 6: {sum(k <= 6 for k in K)}")
print("needs more than 6:", " ".join(words[s] for s in range(N) if K[s] > 6))
rnd = mulberry32(7); R = [play(s, "random", T=30, rnd=rnd)[1] for s in range(N)]
print("careless (seed 7):", {k: R.count(k) for k in sorted(set(R))}, f"mean {sum(R) / N:.4f}, won within 6: {sum(k <= 6 for k in R)}")
sub = range(0, N, 5)
for tau in (0.1, 0.5, 2.0):
    rnd = mulberry32(11); S = [play(s, "soft", tau=tau, T=30, rnd=rnd)[1] for s in sub]
    print(f"softmax tau {tau}: mean {sum(S) / len(S):.4f} over {len(S)} words, won within 6: {sum(k <= 6 for k in S)}")
for eps, v in ((0.05, 0.0), (0.1, 0.0), (0.1, 0.9), (0.2, 0.5)):
    rnd = mulberry32(2024); out = [play(s, eps=eps, v=v, rnd=rnd) for s in range(N)]
    c = {k: sum(x[0] == k for x in out) for k in ("won", "stuck", "claimed", "out")}
    pred = sum((1 - eps * (1 - v)) ** (k - 1) for k in K if k <= 6) / N
    print(f"eps {eps} v {v}: {c}, calls {sum(x[2] for x in out)}, simulated {c['won'] / N:.4f}, formula {pred:.4f}")

Wrap-up

    
Five lines to remember

    
    
      
  • An agent is a model in a loop: observe the history, choose an action, let the environment answer, append, repeat until the goal or the budget is reached.
    • 
      
  • The loop is what lets an agent solve tasks whose information only arrives as you act; a better controller then shortens the path.
    • 
      
  • Weak choices cost steps; uncaught errors cost the task. With independent steps, success is pn, so long tasks need checks that catch errors before they compound.
    • 
      
  • Each step re-sends the history: tokens grow with the square of the number of steps, and time adds up call by call. Budget both.
    • 
      
  • Choose the least autonomy and the simplest design that does the job: one call, then a workflow, then an agent, with approvals placed where the expected harm is high.
    • 
    

    
Check your understanding

    

      
What is the difference between a workflow and an agent?
In a workflow the programmer fixes the sequence of model calls and tools in code; the model fills in steps. In an agent the model itself decides, from the history, which action comes next and when to stop. A workflow’s path is predictable; an agent’s depends on what it observes.

      
Why can a player with no feedback win only k out of 2,000 games with k guesses, while the loop wins almost all of them in six?
Without feedback each guess eliminates one word. With feedback a guess sorts all remaining words into up to 243 groups and keeps only one group, so a good guess cuts 2,000 words to a few dozen. The observation, not the cleverness of the guess, carries most of the information.

      
Each step of an agent is right 98% of the time. Roughly how long can a task be before it fails more often than it succeeds?
The half-life is ln 2 / −ln 0.98 ≈ 34 steps. At 34 steps success is about 50%; at 68 steps about 25%. A check that catches half of the errors makes peff = 0.99 and doubles the half-life to about 69 steps.

      
Why does the total input of a long agent run grow faster than the number of steps?
Each call re-sends the whole history, and the history grows by m tokens per step, so call t sends s + (t − 1)m tokens. Summed over n calls that is n s + m n(n − 1)/2: quadratic in n. Caching lowers the price of the repeated part but not the number of tokens processed.

      
Where should a person approve an agent’s actions?
Where the expected harm of a mistake that slips through, chance times cost, exceeds the cost of the review: typically irreversible or external actions such as payments, deletions and messages to customers. Approving every read-only lookup wastes human time and trains people to click “yes” without looking.
