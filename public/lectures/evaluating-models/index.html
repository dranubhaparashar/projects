Evaluating Models

How to know a model is good before the world finds out

A model that scores 100% on the data it learned from may be useless on tomorrow's. Evaluation is the discipline of measuring honestly: on data the model has never seen, with the right yardstick for the job, and without letting any information leak from the answers into the training. On the right, raise the polynomial's degree. The training error keeps falling, but the error on fresh points tells the real story.

Rule one
Never grade a model on the data it trained on
Rule two
Pick the metric that matches the cost of each kind of mistake
Rule three
Anything that looks at labels belongs inside the validation loop

The trap

Training error lies

A student who memorises last year's exam answers scores perfectly on last year's exam. Training error is that exam. Every extra degree of the polynomial lets it bend closer to the 15 training points, noise included, so training error can only go down. The error on new points first falls (the model learns the real shape) and then climbs (it starts learning the noise). That rising part is overfitting, and the distance between the two curves is the generalisation gap.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{RMSE} &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(y_i - \hat f(x_i)\big)^2} \\ \text{gap} &= \text{RMSE}_{\text{test}} - \text{RMSE}_{\text{train}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the root-mean-square error is the typical size of a miss, in the units of y. Computed on the training points it measures how well the model memorised; computed on points it never saw, it measures how well it generalises. A big gap is the signature of overfitting.
Say it“RMSE is the square root of the average squared miss; the gap is test RMSE minus train RMSE.”

Rule one

Honest splits: train, validation, test

So hold some data back. The standard recipe has three parts: a training set to fit the model, a validation set to compare models and tune settings, and a test set, sealed in an envelope, opened exactly once at the end. Why not tune on the test set? Because every time you pick the winner by its test score, a little of the test set's luck leaks into your choice, and the final score comes out flattering. But a test set is also just a sample. Shrink it and watch how much its verdict wobbles from one random split to the next.

train · fit the modelvalidation · choosetest · sealed
\[ \text{SE}(\widehat{\text{acc}}) = \sqrt{\frac{\text{acc}\,(1-\text{acc})}{n_{\text{test}}}} \]
Read it as: each test example is a coin flip that comes up “right” with probability acc, so the measured accuracy has a standard error that shrinks only with the square root of the test size. Quadruple the test set to halve the wobble. Two models whose scores differ by less than about two standard errors are not clearly different.
Say it“The standard error of accuracy is the square root of acc times one-minus-acc over the test size.”

Using every point

Cross-validation: everyone gets a turn

With only 15 points, holding back a third is painful, and the score would hinge on which third. k-fold cross-validation fixes both problems: cut the data into k folds, train on k−1 of them, score on the one left out, and rotate until every fold has been the test fold once. Every point is used for training and for testing, never at the same time. Below, cross-validation picks the polynomial degree without touching the 300 test points. Change k and compare its choice with the truth.

\[ \text{CV}_k = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k} \text{Err}\big(\hat f^{(-j)},\ \text{fold}_j\big) \]
Read it as: f̂(−j) is the model trained without fold j; Err is its error on fold j. Average the k scores. k = 5 or 10 is the usual choice; k = n is leave-one-out: n fits, each tested on a single point. The spread of the k fold scores tells you how much to trust the average.
Say it“CV-k is the average over folds of the error of the model trained without that fold, measured on that fold.”

Rule two

The confusion matrix

For classifiers, “error” hides two very different mistakes. A screening test that misses a disease (false negative) and one that frightens a healthy person (false positive) do not cost the same. The confusion matrix counts all four outcomes. Below, 2,000 people get a risk score; everyone above the threshold is flagged. Make the disease rare and see what accuracy says about a test that flags nobody.

\[ \text{accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + FP + FN + TN} \]
Read it as: TP: sick and flagged. FN: sick and missed. FP: healthy but flagged. TN: healthy and cleared. Accuracy lumps the two kinds of mistake together and is dominated by the larger class, so on rare events a do-nothing model looks excellent.
Say it“Accuracy is true positives plus true negatives, over everyone.”

Two questions

Precision and recall

Split the confusion matrix into two questions a doctor would ask. Recall: of the people who are sick, how many did we catch? Precision: of the people we flagged, how many are actually sick? Lowering the threshold catches more cases (recall up) but flags more healthy people (precision down). The precision–recall curve traces every threshold at once. Same data, same threshold as above: the sliders are linked.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{recall} &= \frac{TP}{TP+FN} \\ \text{precision} &= \frac{TP}{TP+FP} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: recall (also called sensitivity or true-positive rate) looks only at the sick; precision looks only at the flagged. Precision depends on how common the disease is; recall does not.
Say it“Recall is TP over all actual positives; precision is TP over all predicted positives.”
\[ F_1 = \frac{2\cdot\text{precision}\cdot\text{recall}}{\text{precision}+\text{recall}} \]
Read it as: the harmonic mean of the two: it is high only when both are high. A model that flags everyone has recall 1 but a terrible F₁.
Say it“F-one is two times precision times recall over their sum.”

All thresholds at once

ROC curves and AUC

The ROC curve (receiver operating characteristic, a name from 1940s radar) plots the catch rate against the false-alarm rate for every threshold. A useless model runs along the diagonal; a perfect one hugs the top-left corner. The area under it, AUC, has a lovely meaning: pick one sick and one healthy person at random; AUC is the chance the model gives the sick one the higher score. Change how well the score separates the groups, then change the prevalence above: ROC doesn’t move, precision does.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{TPR} &= \frac{TP}{TP+FN} \\ \text{FPR} &= \frac{FP}{FP+TN} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: TPR is recall; FPR is the share of healthy people we wrongly flag (1 − specificity). Both are computed within one class, so neither depends on how common the disease is.
Say it“True-positive rate is TP over the sick; false-positive rate is FP over the healthy.”
\[ \text{AUC} = P\big(s(\text{sick}) > s(\text{healthy})\big) \]
Read it as: the probability that a randomly chosen positive outranks a randomly chosen negative (ties count half). 0.5 is a coin flip, 1 is perfect ranking. AUC judges the ranking, not any one threshold.
Say it“AUC is the probability that the sick person's score beats the healthy person's.”

Why models fail

Bias and variance

Imagine collecting a fresh training set many times and refitting. A too-simple model misses in the same way every time: that systematic miss is bias. A too-flexible model chases each set's noise and lands somewhere different every time: that scatter is variance. Below, 30 polynomials, each fitted to its own 15 noisy points. Slide the degree from a stiff line to a wild curve.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\big[(y - \hat f(x))^2\big] = {}&\underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)] - f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} \\ &+ \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)])^2\big]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the expected squared error on a new point splits exactly into three parts. Bias²: how far the average fit is from the truth. Variance: how much fits wobble around their average. Noise: the part no model can remove. Flexibility trades the first for the second; the best model balances them.
Say it“Expected squared error equals bias squared plus variance plus noise.”

Rule three

Leakage: 90% accuracy on pure noise

This mistake has appeared in published papers. Take 50 patients and 1,000 “gene” measurements that are pure random noise, with random labels: nothing can be predicted, so honest accuracy must be about 50%. Now pick the 20 genes most correlated with the labels, then cross-validate a classifier on them. The wrong way picks the genes using all 50 patients, so the test folds helped choose the features. The right way repeats the selection inside every training fold. Run it.

\[ \begin{gathered} \text{every step that sees } y \\ \subset\ \text{the cross-validation loop} \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: feature selection, scaling, imputation, choosing hyperparameters, oversampling: if a step learns anything from the data, it must be re-learned on each training fold, without the fold being scored. Otherwise the test fold has already whispered its answers. (Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman call this “the wrong and right way to do cross-validation”.)
Say it“Anything that looks at the labels goes inside the cross-validation loop.”

Real-world project

Project: designing a diabetes screening test

The Pima Indians Diabetes data (Smith et al., 1988; US National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases) records 768 women of Pima heritage near Phoenix, Arizona: pregnancies, plasma glucose, blood pressure, skin-fold thickness, insulin, BMI, a family-history score and age, and whether diabetes was diagnosed within five years. 268 (35%) were. You are designing a screening rule. We split once, stratified: 576 women to develop with and 192 sealed in the envelope. The same project in scikit-learn

import numpy as np
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score, cross_val_predict
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.naive_bayes import GaussianNB
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
from sklearn.dummy import DummyClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score, confusion_matrix

D = np.loadtxt("pima-indians-diabetes.csv", delimiter=",")
X, y = D[:, :8], D[:, 8].astype(int)
X_dev, X_test, y_dev, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=0)

cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5)
models = {"always 'healthy'": DummyClassifier(),
          "logistic regression": make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression()),
          "Gaussian naive Bayes": GaussianNB(),
          "decision tree (depth 4)": DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=4, random_state=0)}
for name, m in models.items():
    acc = cross_val_score(m, X_dev, y_dev, cv=cv)
    p = cross_val_predict(m, X_dev, y_dev, cv=cv, method="predict_proba")[:, 1]
    print(f"{name:24s} CV accuracy {acc.mean():.4f} ± {acc.std():.4f}   CV AUC {roc_auc_score(y_dev, p):.4f}")

final = models["logistic regression"].fit(X_dev, y_dev)      # opened once, at the end
p_test = final.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]
for t in (0.5, 0.3):
    tn, fp, fn, tp = confusion_matrix(y_test, p_test >= t).ravel()
    print(f"test, threshold {t}: recall {tp/(tp+fn):.3f}  precision {tp/(tp+fp):.3f}  TP {tp} FP {fp} FN {fn} TN {tn}")
print(f"test AUC {roc_auc_score(y_test, p_test):.4f}")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Training error always flatters. Measure on data the model never saw; seal a test set and open it once.
  • A score from a small test set wobbles by about √(acc(1−acc)/n); k-fold cross-validation uses every point and shows that spread.
  • Accuracy hides which mistakes you make. Use the confusion matrix, recall and precision, and choose the threshold from the real costs.
  • ROC and AUC judge the ranking and ignore prevalence; precision–recall shows what rare positives really cost.
  • Error = bias² + variance + noise; and any step that sees the labels (selection, tuning, scaling) must sit inside the validation loop.

Check your understanding

A fraud model has 99.5% accuracy, and 0.5% of transactions are fraud. Is it any good?

Not necessarily: flagging nothing also scores 99.5%. Look at recall (frauds caught) and precision (flags that are real), or the precision–recall curve.

You tried 50 settings and reported the best validation score. Why is that number optimistic, and what do you report instead?

Picking the maximum of 50 noisy scores selects luck as well as quality. Report the score of the chosen model on a test set that played no part in the choice (or use nested cross-validation).

Model A has AUC 0.85, model B 0.80, but at your chosen threshold B has the higher recall. Which do you deploy?

AUC averages over all thresholds; you operate at one. If that threshold is set by the costs, compare the models there (or by expected cost), not by AUC alone.

You standardise all features using the whole dataset, then cross-validate. Is that leakage?

Yes, mildly: the means and standard deviations were computed with the test folds. Put the scaler in a pipeline so it is re-fitted on each training fold. It is usually small for scaling and large for feature selection.

Adding data barely changes the training error but steadily lowers the test error. Is the model suffering more from bias or from variance?

Variance. More data steadies a flexible model; a high-bias model would plateau with both errors high and close together.
