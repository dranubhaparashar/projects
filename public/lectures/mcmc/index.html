Markov Chain Monte Carlo

Metropolis–Hastings and Gibbs sampling

Rejection and importance sampling, from chapter 6.7, need a proposal that already looks like the target, and in many dimensions nothing does. Markov chain Monte Carlo gives up on independent draws. It takes a random walk instead, built so that the walk spends time in each region in proportion to its probability, whatever the starting point. The walker in the figure explores a curved banana-shaped density it can only evaluate, never sample directly. Each step proposes a nearby point and accepts it with a probability that depends only on a ratio of densities, so the normalising constant never appears. Click anywhere on the figure to drop the walker there, and switch the step size: too small and it crawls, too large and it is almost always refused.

Markov chains and where they settle

A Markov chain is a sequence of random states z(1), z(2), … in which the next state depends only on the current one. It is described by its transition probabilities T(z, z′) = p(z(m+1) = z′ | z(m) = z); when they are the same at every step the chain is homogeneous. If we know the distribution of the current state, one step of the chain gives the distribution of the next: multiply by T. For a finite state space that is a vector times a matrix, and running the chain for t steps multiplies by Tt.

A distribution that one step leaves unchanged is invariant (or stationary). The trick behind every MCMC method is to design T so that the target is invariant, and so that the chain is ergodic: whatever the start, the distribution of z(t) converges to that one invariant distribution. Then running the chain long enough produces samples from the target, and averages along a single run converge to expectations under it. A sufficient condition for invariance, easy to check and easy to build, is detailed balance: in equilibrium, the flow of probability from z to z′ equals the flow back. A chain with that property is called reversible. Pick a chain, click a state to start there, and step it; then try the periodic chain, and rescue it with a little laziness.

\[ \begin{aligned} p^{(m+1)}(z') &= \sum_{z} T(z, z')\, p^{(m)}(z) \\ \mathbf v^{(t)} &= \mathbf v^{(0)} T^{\,t} \\ p^\star(z') &= \sum_{z} T(z, z')\, p^\star(z) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the probability of being at z′ next is the probability of being at each z now, times the chance of moving from z to z′, summed over z. Written as a row vector v of state probabilities, t steps multiply by T to the power t. The invariant distribution p⋆ is the one that comes back unchanged.
Say it“Next distribution equals current distribution times T; p-star is the one T leaves alone.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p^\star(z)\, T(z, z') &= p^\star(z')\, T(z', z) \\ \mathbf v^{(t)} - p^\star &\approx c\, \lambda_2^{\,t}\, \mathbf u_2 \\ T &= \sum_k \alpha_k B_k \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: detailed balance says probability flows equally both ways along every edge; summing it over z gives invariance at once. The largest eigenvalue of a stochastic matrix is 1, belonging to p⋆; every other part of the starting vector shrinks like the next-largest eigenvalue modulus |λ2| to the power t. The last line builds a chain as a mixture of base transitions Bk, each leaving p⋆ invariant, chosen with probabilities αk; the mixture leaves it invariant too.
Say it“Flow from z to z-prime equals flow back; the error shrinks like lambda-two to the t.”

Metropolis–Hastings: propose, then accept or stay

Suppose we can evaluate p̃(z), the target up to an unknown constant, p(z) = p̃(z)/Zp. The Metropolis algorithm builds a chain for it from any symmetric proposal, such as a Gaussian step around the current point. From the current state z(τ), draw a candidate z⋆. If it is more probable than where we are, move there. If it is less probable, move there only with probability p̃(z⋆)/p̃(z(τ)); otherwise stay put and record the current state again. That repeat is not a wasted step; it is how the chain gives high-density points their extra weight. Zp cancels in the ratio, which is why MCMC suits posteriors.

Metropolis–Hastings allows asymmetric proposals. If the proposal finds it easier to jump from z to z⋆ than back, the acceptance probability is corrected by the ratio of the two proposal densities. With that correction the chain satisfies detailed balance with respect to p, so p is invariant; ergodicity holds whenever the proposal can eventually reach every region where p is positive. Here the target lives on z > 0 and has two bumps. The log-normal proposal multiplies z by a random factor, so it is not symmetric: the correction is z⋆/z. Step it one proposal at a time, then run it; switch to the log-normal proposal and turn the Hastings correction off to see the chain converge, confidently, to the wrong distribution.

\[ \begin{aligned} A(z^\star, z) &= \min\!\Big(1,\ \frac{\tilde p(z^\star)}{\tilde p(z)}\Big) \\ A_k(z^\star, z) &= \min\big(1,\ r_k\big) \\ r_k &= \frac{\tilde p(z^\star)\, q_k(z \mid z^\star)}{\tilde p(z)\, q_k(z^\star \mid z)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first line is Metropolis’s rule for a symmetric proposal, q(a|b) = q(b|a). The second is the Hastings generalisation: multiply by how likely the reverse jump is, divided by how likely the forward jump was. Accept by drawing u uniform on (0, 1) and moving if u < A; otherwise z(τ+1) = z(τ).
Say it“Accept with probability the smaller of one and the density ratio times the reverse-over-forward proposal ratio.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &p(z)\, q_k(z' | z)\, A_k(z', z) \\ &= \min\big(p(z)\,q_k(z' | z), \\ &\qquad\quad p(z')\,q_k(z | z')\big) \\ &= p(z')\, q_k(z | z')\, A_k(z, z') \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the probability flow from z to z′ is the chance of being at z, proposing z′ and accepting. Multiplying out the minimum gives an expression that is symmetric in z and z′, so it equals the flow back: detailed balance. Drop the correction with an asymmetric q and the symmetry breaks. For the log-normal proposal on z, the uncorrected chain is Metropolis on log z, and it settles on p̃(z)/z instead of p̃(z).
Say it“Flow from z to z-prime is the minimum of the two one-way flows, which is the same both ways.”

PRML 11.2, 11.2.2 · random-walk behaviour

Too timid, too bold: the step size

A random-walk proposal has one dial, its step size ρ, and the dial cuts both ways. Small steps are almost always accepted but go nowhere: a random walk covers a distance that grows only like the square root of the number of steps, so to cross a distance L with steps of size ρ takes about (L/ρ)2 of them. Large steps would travel far, but they mostly land where p̃ is tiny and are rejected, and a rejected step travels nowhere at all.

The trouble is sharpest for a strongly correlated target, one that is long in some directions (width σmax) and thin in many others (width σmin). The lab uses ten dimensions: one long axis and nine short ones, and plots the long axis against one short one. An isotropic step much bigger than σmin leaves the thin slab in nine directions at once and is almost certainly rejected, so ρ must be of order σmin; but then exploring the long direction takes about (σmax/σmin)2 steps. (In two dimensions, with a single thin direction, the penalty for big steps is much milder, and bold steps can win; the argument is about the many-dimensional case that matters in practice.) The measure that matters is not the acceptance rate but the effective sample size (ESS): how many independent draws the correlated run is worth, defined in the diagnostics section below. Stretch the ellipse, then hunt for the step size that maximises the ESS; the curve on the right shows the whole trade-off at once.

\[ \begin{aligned} z^{(\tau+1)} &= z^{(\tau)} + \epsilon_\tau \\ \epsilon_\tau &= 0,\ +1,\ -1 \\ &\quad \text{w.p. } \tfrac12,\ \tfrac14,\ \tfrac14 \\ \mathbb E\big[(z^{(\tau)})^2\big] &= \tfrac{\tau}{2} \quad (z^{(0)} = 0) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a walk on the integers that stays put half the time and otherwise steps left or right. Each step adds an independent amount with mean 0 and variance ½, so after τ steps the variance is τ/2 and the typical distance from the start is √(τ/2). Doubling the distance costs four times the steps. That square root is the random-walk behaviour every MCMC design tries to escape.
Say it“The expected squared distance after tau steps is tau over two.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \rho &\sim \sigma_{\min} \\ \tau_{\text{indep}} &\sim \Big(\frac{\sigma_{\max}}{\sigma_{\min}}\Big)^{2} \\ \text{ESS} &= \frac{N}{1 + 2\sum_{k \ge 1} r_k} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: keep the step comparable to the narrowest width, and the number of steps between roughly independent samples grows like the square of the elongation. ESS discounts the N stored states by their autocorrelations rk at lag k: strongly correlated runs are worth few independent draws.
Say it“Step about sigma-min; independent samples every sigma-max over sigma-min, squared, steps.”

Gibbs sampling: one coordinate at a time

When the joint distribution is hard to sample but each variable’s conditional given all the others is easy, Gibbs sampling needs no proposal and no tuning. Cycle through the variables, or pick one at random, and replace zi by a fresh draw from p(zi | z\i), with every other variable held at its current value. Each update leaves p invariant, because the marginal of the variables it does not touch is unchanged, and the conditional it draws from is the exact one. In fact a Gibbs update is a Metropolis–Hastings step whose proposal is that conditional, and its acceptance probability works out to exactly 1.

The moves are axis-parallel, so for a strongly correlated pair they zigzag down a narrow valley in tiny steps: for a bivariate Gaussian with correlation r, the lag-one autocorrelation of each coordinate across sweeps is r². Over-relaxation (Adler’s method) fixes this for Gaussian conditionals by drawing the new value on the far side of the conditional mean, and blocking updates correlated variables together. Push the correlation towards 1 and watch the zigzag shrink, then slide α towards −1.

\[ \begin{aligned} z_i^{(\tau+1)} &\sim p\big(z_i \mid z_{\setminus i}^{(\tau)}\big) \\ z_1 \mid z_2 &\sim \mathcal N\big(r z_2,\ 1 - r^2\big) \\ A(z^\star, z) &= \frac{c(z_i^\star)\; m\; c(z_i)}{c(z_i)\; m\; c(z_i^\star)} = 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: replace one variable by a draw from its conditional given the rest. For a standard bivariate Gaussian with correlation r, that conditional is a Gaussian centred at r times the other coordinate, with variance 1 − r². Viewed as Metropolis–Hastings with the conditional as proposal, write c(·) for the conditional p(· | z\i) and m for the marginal p(z\i): the target is p(z) = c(zi) m, the proposal is c(zi⋆), z\i is unchanged by the move, every factor cancels, and the step is always accepted.
Say it“Draw z-i from its conditional given everything else; the acceptance ratio is one.”
\[ \begin{aligned} z_i' &= \mu_i + \alpha (z_i - \mu_i) \\ &\quad + \sigma_i \sqrt{1 - \alpha^2}\; \nu \\ \nu &\sim \mathcal N(0, 1) \\ \operatorname{corr}\big(z_1^{(\tau)}, z_1^{(\tau+1)}\big) &= r^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: μi and σi are the mean and spread of the conditional. With α = 0 this is plain Gibbs. With α near −1 the new value is the old one reflected through the conditional mean, plus a little noise; the conditional is still left invariant, but successive sweeps no longer creep. The last line is the lag-one autocorrelation of plain Gibbs on the bivariate Gaussian.
Say it“New z-i is the conditional mean plus alpha times the old offset plus fresh noise.”

Gibbs on a Bayes net: resample each node from its Markov blanket

In a graphical model the conditional of one variable given all the others involves only its Markov blanket: its parents, its children and its children’s other parents. That makes each Gibbs update cheap and local. Here is the flood-alarm network from chapter 6.7, with the alarm observed to be ringing. Rejection sampling there kept only the samples in which the alarm happened to ring, about one in five, and the rarer the evidence the worse that gets. Gibbs sampling clamps the evidence and walks among the states consistent with it, so no sample is wasted, although successive samples are correlated. Energy-based models in deep learning use the same idea in blocks: in a restricted Boltzmann machine the hidden units are conditionally independent given the visible ones, so a whole layer can be resampled at once. Resample one node at a time and watch which neighbours its probability depends on, then run a few thousand sweeps.

\[ \begin{aligned} &P\big(x_i \mid \text{mb}(X_i)\big) \\ &\propto P\big(x_i \mid \text{pa}(X_i)\big) \\ &\quad \times \prod_{Y_j \in \text{ch}(X_i)} P\big(y_j \mid \text{pa}(Y_j)\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: to resample Xi, evaluate this product for each of its values and normalise. The first factor is its own table entry given its parents; the others are its children’s table entries, which mention Xi as a parent. Every other factor of the joint distribution is the same for both values and cancels.
Say it“Probability of x-i given its blanket is proportional to its own table entry times its children’s.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(h_j = 1 \mid \mathbf v) &= \sigma\big(c_j + \mathbf W_{:,j}^{\top} \mathbf v\big) \\ p(\mathbf h \mid \mathbf v) &= \prod_j p(h_j \mid \mathbf v) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: block Gibbs in a restricted Boltzmann machine. Given the visible units v, each hidden unit is on with probability the logistic sigmoid σ of its bias cj plus its weighted input, and the hidden units are independent, so the whole hidden layer is one block update. The visible layer given h works the same way, and the chain alternates between the two layers.
Say it“Each hidden unit turns on with probability sigma of its bias plus its weighted input.”

ESL 8.6 · MCMC for sampling from the posterior

Sampling a posterior: Gibbs for a mixture

In Bayesian inference the target is a posterior, Pr(θ | data) ∝ Pr(data | θ) Pr(θ), with an evidence nobody can compute. MCMC needs only the right-hand side. Gibbs sampling is especially natural when the model has latent variables: add them to the state, and the conditionals often become standard distributions. The draws of θ after burn-in are then samples from its posterior, and any posterior summary (a mean, an interval, the probability that one mean exceeds another) is an average over them.

The classic case is a two-component Gaussian mixture. To keep the picture two-dimensional we fix the spreads at σ = 1 and the mixing weight at π = 0.6 and sample only the two means, giving each a wide N(0, 10²) prior. The latent label Δi says which component produced point i. Given the means, each label is a coin flip with the familiar responsibility as its probability. Given the labels, each mean is a Gaussian centred near the average of its points. This is EM with the averages replaced by draws: where EM computes responsibilities, Gibbs samples labels from them; where EM maximises, Gibbs samples. EM returns one point; Gibbs returns the whole posterior around it. The 40 points are synthetic, drawn with a fixed seed from the same model. Run a few hundred sweeps, then shrink the gap between the groups and watch the posterior widen and the two labels start to swap.

\[ \begin{aligned} \gamma_i &= \frac{\pi\, \phi_{\mu_2}(y_i)}{(1 - \pi)\, \phi_{\mu_1}(y_i) + \pi\, \phi_{\mu_2}(y_i)} \\ \Delta_i &\sim \text{Bernoulli}(\gamma_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: φμ is the Gaussian density with mean μ and spread σ. γi is the probability that point i came from component 2, given the current means; Gibbs flips a coin with that probability to set its label (1 = component 2). EM would keep γi as a soft weight instead.
Say it“Delta-i is a coin flip with heads probability gamma-i, the responsibility of component two.”
\[ \begin{aligned} n_k &= \#\{i : \text{point } i \text{ in } k\} \\ \mu_k \mid \Delta, y &\sim \mathcal N(m_k,\ v_k) \\ m_k &= \frac{\sum_{i \in k} y_i}{n_k + \sigma^2/s_0^2} \\ v_k &= \frac{\sigma^2}{n_k + \sigma^2/s_0^2} \\ \widehat{\Pr}(\mu_1) &= \frac{1}{M} \sum_{t} \Pr\big(\mu_1 \mid \Delta^{(t)}, y\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: given the labels, each mean has a Gaussian conditional: its mean mk is its points’ sum divided by their count plus a small prior term (s0 = 10), and its variance vk is σ² shrinking like 1/nk. The last line estimates the marginal density of μ1 by averaging its conditional density over the M kept sweeps, a smoother estimate than a histogram of the draws (often called Rao–Blackwellisation).
Say it“Mu-k is Gaussian around its group’s average, with variance sigma-squared over the group size.”

Separated modes: tempering and better representations

The guarantee that a chain converges says nothing about how long it takes. Write the target as p(x) ∝ exp(−E(x)) with an energy E. A local sampler moves easily downhill and along valleys, but to pass from one low-energy basin to another it must climb the barrier between them, and the chance of doing so falls roughly exponentially with the barrier’s height. With two well-separated modes, a chain can sit in one for longer than any run you can afford, and everything it reports looks converged.

Tempering flattens the landscape. The tempered distribution pβ ∝ exp(−βE) with an inverse temperature β < 1 has lower barriers; at β = 0 it is flat. Parallel tempering runs a ladder of chains at temperatures from β = 1 down to a small β, and now and then proposes to swap the states of two neighbours, accepting with a Metropolis ratio that keeps every rung correctly distributed. Hot chains cross barriers and pass their states down the ladder. Deep learning offers a second escape: sample in a learned representation. A good encoder maps the data distribution to a simpler one, closer to a single Gaussian, where a chain mixes fast; the decoder maps each latent sample back. Here the map is built exactly, x = F−1(Φ(z)), standing in for a perfectly trained model. Widen the gap and watch plain Metropolis stall, then switch on tempering.

\[ \begin{aligned} p_\beta(x) &\propto \exp\big(-\beta\, E(x)\big) \\ A_{\text{swap}} &= \min\!\Big(1,\ \frac{p_{\beta_i}(x_j)\, p_{\beta_j}(x_i)}{p_{\beta_i}(x_i)\, p_{\beta_j}(x_j)}\Big) \\ &= \min\!\Big(1,\ e^{(\beta_i - \beta_j)(E_i - E_j)}\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: raising the target to a power β < 1 lowers every barrier by the factor β. A swap, with Ei short for E(xi), proposes to exchange the states of the chains at βi and βj; it is a Metropolis move on the joint state of the whole ladder, whose target is the product of all the tempered distributions, so each rung keeps its own distribution. The swap is always accepted when it hands the lower-energy state to the colder chain.
Say it“Swap with probability e to the beta-i minus beta-j times E-i minus E-j, capped at one.”
\[ \begin{aligned} E(x) &= -\ln p(x) \\ p(x) &= 0.3\, \phi(x; -d) + 0.7\, \phi(x; d) \\ \Delta E &= E(0) - E(d) \\ x &= g(z) = F^{-1}\big(\Phi(z)\big) \\ z &\sim \mathcal N(0, 1) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the target is a 30 : 70 mixture of two narrow Gaussians (spread 0.5) at −d and +d. ΔE is the barrier a chain in the big mode must climb to reach the middle. In the latent version the chain walks on z, where the target is a plain standard normal, and g pushes each z through the normal CDF Φ and then the target’s inverse CDF, which lands it in the right mode with the right odds.
Say it“The barrier is the energy at zero minus the energy at the mode; x is g of z.”

Deep Learning 17.3 · PRML 11.2 · burn-in, autocorrelation, R̂

Has it converged? Burn-in, autocorrelation and R̂

No test can prove that a chain has converged, but several can catch one that has not. Burn-in: the first stretch of a run still remembers its starting point, so it is discarded; how long is long enough is judged from the traces. Autocorrelation: successive states are correlated, so N states carry less information than N independent draws. The autocorrelation function rk measures how alike states k steps apart are, and it gives the effective sample size. Storing only every m-th state (thinning) saves memory but never adds information. The Monte Carlo standard error of a posterior mean is sd/√ESS, not sd/√N.

The most useful check runs several chains from scattered starts and compares them. If they have all forgotten their starts, the spread between the chains’ means should match what the spread within each chain predicts. The Gelman–Rubin statistic R̂ is the square root of the ratio of a pooled variance estimate to the within-chain variance. It is near 1 when the chains agree; a common rule is to want R̂ < 1.01. Splitting each chain in half before computing it also catches a single chain that is still drifting. Shrink the step size until R̂ complains, then pick the two-mode target, where every chain can look fine on its own.

\[ \begin{aligned} \gamma_k &= \frac1N \sum_{t=1}^{N-k} (x_t - \bar x)(x_{t+k} - \bar x) \\ r_k &= \gamma_k / \gamma_0 \\ \text{ESS} &= \frac{N}{1 + 2 \sum_{k=1}^{2K} r_k} \\ \text{MCSE} &= \frac{\text{sd}}{\sqrt{\text{ESS}}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: γk is the autocovariance at lag k, and rk the correlation between the run and itself shifted by k steps. ESS divides N by the integrated autocorrelation time; the sum stops at the first lag pair whose sum r2m−1 + r2m turns negative (Geyer’s initial positive sequence), where the noisy tail of rk begins. MCSE is the standard error of the estimated mean.
Say it“Effective sample size is N over one plus twice the summed autocorrelations.”
\[ \begin{aligned} W &= \frac1M \sum_{m} s_m^2 \\ B &= \frac{n}{M-1} \sum_{m} (\bar x_m - \bar x)^2 \\ \hat V &= \frac{n-1}{n}\, W + \frac1n\, B \\ \hat R &= \sqrt{\hat V / W} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: M (split) chains of n states each. W is the average within-chain variance, B is n times the variance of the chain means. V̂ pools them into an estimate of the target’s variance that is too large while the chains disagree, and W is too small while each chain has explored only part of the target, so their ratio exceeds 1 until both settle.
Say it“R-hat is the square root of the pooled variance over the within-chain variance.”

Real-world project

Project: when did British coal mines get safer?

The page embeds the dates of 191 explosions in British coal mines that killed ten or more people, from 15 March 1851 to 22 March 1962, as compiled by Jarrett (1979) and distributed as the coal data set of the R package boot (whose documentation cites Hand et al., 1994, as its source; the package’s licence is “Unlimited”). The dates are historical facts. Counted by calendar year they give 112 yearly totals, from 4 in 1851 to 1 in 1962, with a maximum of 6, in 1860 and again in 1866.

The counts look higher early on. A simple change-point model makes that precise: the disasters of each year are Poisson, at rate λ1 up to some unknown year and λ2 afterwards. With Gamma priors on the rates and a uniform prior on the change year, every full conditional is a standard distribution, so Gibbs sampling takes three easy draws per sweep. The model is also small enough to solve exactly: integrating the rates out leaves a posterior over 111 possible years that can simply be enumerated. That lets us grade the sampler against the truth, and then show what happens when the year is updated by a lazy local Metropolis step instead of its full conditional.

1 · The model and its three conditionals

\[ \begin{aligned} y_t &\sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_1), \quad t \le k \\ y_t &\sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_2), \quad t > k \\ \lambda_j &\sim \text{Gamma}(a, b) \\ k &\sim \text{Uniform}\{1, \dots, n-1\} \\ \lambda_1 \mid k &\sim \text{Gamma}(a + S_k,\ b + k) \\ \lambda_2 \mid k &\sim \text{Gamma}(a + S_n - S_k, \\ &\qquad\qquad b + n - k) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: k is the number of years at the old rate, so the first year at the new rate is 1851 + k; Sk = y1 + … + yk counts the disasters in the first k years; Gamma(a, b) has shape a and rate b. The Gamma prior is conjugate to the Poisson, so given k each rate’s conditional adds its segment’s disasters to the shape and its number of years to the rate.
Say it“Lambda-one given k is Gamma of a plus the first k counts, and b plus k.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \ln p(k \mid \lambda, y) &= S_k \ln \lambda_1 - k\lambda_1 \\ &\quad + (S_n - S_k) \ln \lambda_2 \\ &\quad - (n-k)\lambda_2 + c \\ p(k \mid y) &\propto \frac{\Gamma(a + S_k)}{(b + k)^{a + S_k}} \\ &\quad \times \frac{\Gamma(a + S_n - S_k)}{(b + n - k)^{a + S_n - S_k}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the change year’s conditional is the Poisson log-likelihood of the split, evaluated for all 111 candidate years and normalised; Gibbs draws from it by the inverse CDF. The second line integrates both rates out analytically, giving the exact posterior of k that the sampler should reproduce.
Say it“The year’s log-probability is each segment’s Poisson log-likelihood, added up.”

The same computation in Python

The script reproduces the four chains with the page’s own random-number generator (mulberry32, with the same Box–Muller and Marsaglia–Tsang Gamma steps), so its numbers can be compared with the table above digit for digit at 2,000 sweeps and 10% burn-in. It computes the exact posterior with SciPy’s log-gamma function, and the same split R̂ and ESS formulas as the diagnostics section.

import json, sys, math
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import gammaln
S = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "./"
M32 = 0xFFFFFFFF
def rng(a):                                   # mulberry32, as on the page
    st = [a & M32]
    def r():
        st[0] = (st[0] + 0x6D2B79F5) & M32; a = st[0]
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & M32
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & M32)) & M32) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M32) / 4294967296
    return r
def gauss(r):                                 # Box-Muller, as on the page
    u = 0.0
    while u == 0.0: u = r()
    return math.sqrt(-2 * math.log(u)) * math.cos(2 * math.pi * r())
def rgamma(a, r):                             # Marsaglia-Tsang, shape a, rate 1
    if a < 1:
        g = rgamma(a + 1, r); u = 0.0
        while u == 0.0: u = r()
        return g * u ** (1 / a)
    d = a - 1 / 3; c = 1 / math.sqrt(9 * d)
    while True:
        while True:
            x = gauss(r); v = 1 + c * x
            if v > 0: break
        v = v * v * v; u = r()
        if u < 1 - 0.0331 * x * x * x * x: return d * v
        if math.log(u) < 0.5 * x * x + d * (1 - v + math.log(v)): return d * v

def exact(a, b):                                                # lambdas integrated out analytically
    S1 = C[K]; S2 = Sn - S1
    lw = gammaln(a + S1) - (a + S1) * np.log(b + K) + gammaln(a + S2) - (a + S2) * np.log(b + n - K)
    p = np.exp(lw - lw.max()); p /= p.sum()
    l1 = (p * (a + S1) / (b + K)).sum(); l2 = (p * (a + S2) / (b + n - K)).sum()
    return p, l1, l2

def sampler(a, b, k0, N, seed, local=False):                   # local=False: Gibbs draw of the year; True: Metropolis +-1 year
    r = rng(seed); k = k0; out = np.zeros((N, 3)); acc = 0
    for it in range(N):
        S1 = int(C[k]); l1 = rgamma(a + S1, r) / (b + k); l2 = rgamma(a + Sn - S1, r) / (b + n - k)
        lg1, lg2 = math.log(l1), math.log(l2)
        f = lambda j: int(C[j]) * lg1 - j * l1 + (Sn - int(C[j])) * lg2 - (n - j) * l2
        if local:
            ks = k + (1 if r() < 0.5 else -1)
            if 1 <= ks <= n - 1 and math.log(1 - r()) < f(ks) - f(k): k = ks; acc += 1
        else:
            lw = [f(j) for j in range(1, n)]; mx = max(lw); w = [math.exp(v - mx) for v in lw]
            u = r() * sum(w); c = 0.0; k = n - 1
            for j in range(1, n):
                c += w[j - 1]
                if c >= u: k = j; break
        out[it] = (l1, l2, k)
    return out, acc / N

def ess1(x):                                                    # Geyer initial positive sequence, one chain
    N = len(x); x = x - x.mean(); v = (x * x).sum() / N
    if v == 0: return float(N)
    rho = lambda t: (x[:N - t] * x[t:]).sum() / N / v
    s = 0.0; t = 1
    while t + 1 < N:
        pair = rho(t) + rho(t + 1)
        if pair < 0: break
        s += pair; t += 2
    return N / (1 + 2 * s)
def split_rhat(ch):                                             # ch: M chains x N draws, split in halves
    h = ch.shape[1] // 2; parts = np.vstack([ch[:, :h], ch[:, h:2 * h]])
    W = parts.var(axis=1, ddof=1).mean(); B = h * parts.mean(axis=1).var(ddof=1)
    return math.sqrt(((h - 1) / h * W + B / h) / W)

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • MCMC builds a Markov chain whose invariant distribution is the target. Detailed balance, p(z)T(z, z′) = p(z′)T(z′, z), makes the target invariant; ergodicity makes the chain forget its start, at a rate set by |λ2|.
  • Metropolis–Hastings proposes z⋆ and accepts with min(1, p̃(z⋆)q(z|z⋆) / p̃(z)q(z⋆|z)); on rejection it repeats the current state. The normalising constant cancels; dropping the Hastings correction for an asymmetric q silently samples the wrong distribution.
  • Random walks travel like √τ: the step must be about σmin, so a stretched target costs about (σmax/σmin)² steps per independent sample. Judge a sampler by its ESS, not its acceptance rate.
  • Gibbs sampling draws each variable from its full conditional (always accepted); in graphs that needs only the Markov blanket, latent variables turn hard posteriors into easy conditionals, and correlated variables call for blocking or over-relaxation.
  • Separated modes can trap any local sampler: tempering and learned representations help. Always run several chains from scattered starts, discard burn-in, and check split R̂ and ESS; a chain that looks converged on its own may not be.

Check your understanding

A Metropolis step is rejected. Why must the chain record its current state again, rather than just trying another proposal?

Because the repeats are what give high-density states their extra weight. Discarding rejected steps changes the transition kernel: the chain would then leave every state at the same rate, and its stationary distribution would no longer be the target. Detailed balance is a statement about the full kernel, including the probability of staying put.

Your Metropolis sampler accepts 95% of its proposals. Is that good?

Usually not. A very high acceptance rate means the steps are tiny, so the chain moves by a random walk of small steps and its samples are highly correlated: few effective samples per iteration. For random-walk proposals the efficient range is far lower; theory for high-dimensional Gaussian targets gives about 23%, and about 44% in one dimension. Look at the ESS, not the acceptance rate.

Why is a Gibbs update always accepted, and when is Gibbs sampling slow anyway?

Viewed as Metropolis–Hastings with the full conditional as the proposal, the target and proposal factors cancel exactly in the acceptance ratio, giving 1. It is slow when variables are strongly dependent, because each update can only move along one axis while the others are held fixed; for a Gaussian pair with correlation r the lag-one autocorrelation is r². Blocking, over-relaxation or a reparameterisation helps.

Four chains each have a smooth trace and a fast-decaying autocorrelation, but R̂ is 3. What is the likely story?

Each chain has settled into a different region, most likely a different mode, and is mixing well inside it. Within-chain diagnostics cannot see the other modes; only the comparison between chains reveals that their means disagree. The posterior summaries from any one chain would be wrong. Run longer, use a sampler that can cross between modes, such as parallel tempering, or rethink the model.

In the coal-mine project, why does the Metropolis ±1-year chain from 1956 get stuck while the Gibbs chain from the same start does not?

The Metropolis update can only move the change year one year at a time, and with the rates adapted to a change in the 1940s or 1950s every step towards 1890 at first lowers the likelihood, so the chain sits in a spurious local mode whose exact posterior mass is about 10−9. The Gibbs update evaluates the conditional for all 111 years and can jump straight to the 1890s in one sweep. Both chains have the correct stationary distribution; they differ in how quickly they reach it.
