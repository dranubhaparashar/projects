Reasoning & Planning

Look before you leap, then keep looking

An agent that only reacts takes whichever step looks best right now. An agent that reasons imagines the consequences of a step before taking it; an agent that plans searches many possible futures for a good one, then keeps checking the world as it goes, and changes course when the world disagrees. This chapter builds all of that on one real problem: getting around the London Underground, 302 stations joined by 406 track segments. Tap any two stations on the map: an A* search spreads out from the first, and the plan appears the moment the search reaches the second.

The idea

Acting without thinking ahead

Picture an agent that must get from Belsize Park to Finchley Road, 1.2 km apart as the crow flies. The simplest policy is greedy: from wherever you are, step to the neighbouring station that is closest to the goal. It sounds sensible and fails at once: the closest neighbour is Hampstead, the closest neighbour of Hampstead is Belsize Park again, and the agent rocks between the two for ever. The two stations are close on the ground but on different lines, and the only way between them runs through central London, away from the goal.

Reasoning, in its most basic form, is imagining before acting: try out sequences of steps in your head, judge where each would leave you, and only then commit to the first step of the best one. Here the walker imagines every route of up to d stops. That is the same trade every reasoning model makes: more thinking (more imagined futures) for better actions. Raise the lookahead d and watch both the outcome and the bill.

\[ \begin{aligned} a_t &= \text{first step of } p^\star \\ p^\star &= \arg\min_{p \,\in\, \mathcal{P}_d(s_t)} \big\lVert \operatorname{end}(p) - g \big\rVert \\ \big|\mathcal{P}_d(s_t)\big| &\approx b + b^2 + \dots + b^d \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: at station st the agent lists every route p of up to d stops that never revisits a station (the set Pd), scores each by the straight-line distance from where it ends to the goal g, and takes the first step at of the best one, p∗; then it thinks again from the new station. With about b ways onward from each station, the number of imagined routes grows like bd: lookahead gets expensive quickly.
Say it“The action is the first step of the imagined route whose end is nearest the goal; the number of imagined routes grows like b to the d.”

Planner 1 · systematic search

Breadth-first search: the fewest stops

The greedy walker failed because it trusted a hunch (straight-line distance) and never looked back. Search replaces the hunch with bookkeeping. Treat the network as a graph: stations are nodes, track segments are edges, and a plan is a path. Breadth-first search (BFS) explores in rings: first every station one stop away, then every station two stops away, and so on, never visiting a station twice. The first ring that contains the goal tells you the fewest possible stops, and following each station back to the one it was reached from gives the route. BFS is complete (if a route exists, it finds one) and touches each station and segment at most once. Drag the slider to grow the rings one layer at a time.

\[ \begin{aligned} L_0 &= \{s\} \\ L_{k+1} &= \big\{\, v \notin L_0 \cup \dots \cup L_k \;: \\ &\qquad\;\; (u, v) \in E,\; u \in L_k \,\big\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: layer L0 is the start s. The next layer is every station that is joined by a segment to some station of the current layer and has not been reached before. A station in layer k is exactly k stops from the start, and the whole search costs time proportional to the number of stations plus the number of segments.
Say it“Layer k plus one is every new station next to a station in layer k.”

Planner 2 · cheapest first

Dijkstra: the quickest plan

Stops are the wrong thing to count: the Metropolitan line’s run from Finchley Road to Wembley Park takes 7 minutes without a stop, while a hop in Zone 1 often takes one or two. And changing trains costs a walk and a wait. So plan over states that remember which line you are on, (station, line) pairs, 413 of them on this network, with two kinds of move: ride one segment (its timetable minutes) or change line at the same station (a penalty of P minutes). Dijkstra’s algorithm (1959) grows a set of settled states, always settling next the unsettled state with the smallest cost so far. Because every move costs at least zero, a state’s cost is final when it is settled, and the first time a state at the goal station is settled, its cost is the quickest journey. Raise the change penalty P and watch the plan trade riding time for fewer changes.

\[ \begin{aligned} g(v) &\leftarrow \min\big(g(v),\; g(u) + c(u, v)\big) \\ c(u, v) &= \begin{cases} t_{uv} & \text{ride a segment} \\ P & \text{change line} \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: g(v) is the cheapest known cost of reaching state v. When state u is settled, each move out of it offers v a new price, g(u) plus the move’s cost c(u, v): the segment’s minutes tuv for a ride, or the penalty P for a change. If the offer is cheaper, it replaces g(v). The cost of a plan is its riding minutes plus P for every change.
Say it“The cost of v becomes the smaller of its old cost and the cost of u plus the cost of the move from u to v.”

    Planner 3 · informed search

    A*: search with a sense of direction

    Dijkstra spreads out evenly in every direction, so a trip from Brixton to Wembley Park also explores Upminster. A* (Hart, Nilsson and Raphael, 1968) puts the greedy walker’s hunch back, but safely: it settles states in order of f = g + h, the cost so far plus an estimate h of the cost still to come. If h never overestimates (it is admissible), A* still returns the quickest plan, and it skips states that cannot be on it. Here h is the straight-line distance to the goal divided by the fastest speed anywhere on the network, 76.6 km/h (Epping to Theydon Bois, 2.55 km in 2 minutes), so no real journey can beat it. Multiplying h by a weight w > 1 (weighted A*) makes the search bolder: fewer states, but the plan may cost up to w times the best. Slide w from 0 (Dijkstra) through 1 (A*) to 4.

    \[ \begin{aligned} f(n) &= g(n) + w\, h(n) \\ h(n) &= \frac{\lVert x_n - x_{\text{goal}} \rVert}{v_{\max}} \\ &\le \text{true cost to go} \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: f(n) is the priority of state n: what it cost to get there, g(n), plus w times the estimate h(n) of what remains, the crow-flies distance from the station’s position xn to the goal’s, divided by the network’s top speed. Because no train is faster than vmax, h never exceeds the true remaining cost; with w = 1 the first plan found is optimal, with w = 0 A* is exactly Dijkstra.
    Say it“f of n is g of n plus w times h of n; h is the straight-line distance over the top speed.”

    Breaking a goal down

    Task decomposition: errands into legs

    Real tasks are rarely one search. “Visit the museums, the Tower, Camden Market and Greenwich today, then go home” is a goal that an agent first breaks into sub-goals (the stops), then decides their order, then plans each leg with a search like the ones above. That is hierarchical planning, and it is how LLM agents are usually prompted to work (“first list the steps, then do each one”; Wei et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023). The top level is where the big wins and the big costs are: with n stops there are n! orders, so 5 stops give 120 tours and 10 stops 3.6 million. Pick the stops and compare the order you listed, the “nearest next” habit, and the best of every order.

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{cost}(\pi) &= \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} D\big(s_{\pi(i)},\, s_{\pi(i+1)}\big) \\ &\quad \big[\, + D(s_{\pi(n)}, s_0) \,\big] \\ \#\text{orders} &= n! \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: s0 is home and s1 … sn the stops; an order π visits them as sπ(1), sπ(2), … with π(0) = 0 for home. D(a, b) is the quickest journey between two places (Dijkstra, with a 5-minute change penalty), so the low level is solved once per pair of places and the high level is a search over orders. The bracket is added when the tour returns home.
    Say it“The cost of an order is the sum of the quickest journeys between consecutive stops; there are n factorial orders.”

    Reasoning with tools

    ReAct: think, act, observe

    Everything so far planned from a map the agent already held. A language model’s “map” is what it absorbed in training, and the world has moved on since: today, say, the Victoria line is suspended through central London. ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) interleaves reasoning with acting: the model writes a thought, chooses an action (a tool call), reads the observation the tool returns, and repeats, so each thought is grounded in what it has just seen. In the paper, reasoning alone (chain of thought) often hallucinated facts, acting alone lost track of what it was doing, and the interleaved loop hallucinated less than the first and clearly beat the second on interactive tasks in simulated worlds (ALFWorld, WebShop). The agent below is transparent: its thoughts come from a few hand-written rules standing in for the model, but its tools are real: status() reads today’s closures, plan_route() runs the A* search above, and ride() moves through the network as it is today. Step through it, then switch to “Reason only”.

    \[ \begin{aligned} c_t &= \big(x,\; \tau_1, a_1, o_1,\; \dots, \\ &\qquad\; \tau_{t-1}, a_{t-1}, o_{t-1}\big) \\ (\tau_t, a_t) &\sim \pi(\,\cdot \mid c_t) \\ o_t &= \text{env}(a_t) \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: x is the task, τ a thought, a an action and o the observation it produced. At turn t the model sees the whole context ct (the task and every earlier thought, action and observation) and produces the next thought and action; the environment, not the model, produces the observation. Each turn adds to c, so long tasks fill the context window: one reason the next chapter is about memory.
    Say it“The context is the task plus every thought, action and observation so far; the policy picks the next thought and action from it; the world returns the observation.”

      When a step fails

      Plan, execute, re-plan

      ReAct thinks before every action. That is flexible but expensive: each thought is a model call, with the whole growing context as input. The plan-and-execute pattern (Wang et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023) separates the two: a planner writes the whole plan once, a cheap executor carries it out step by step, and the planner is called again only when a step fails. Which is better depends on how often the world surprises you. Below, each segment of the network is closed independently with probability p, and nobody announces it: an agent finds out only when it reaches the platform. Three agents make the same 300 random journeys. Raise p, and step through single journeys to see each agent’s choices.

      \[ \begin{gathered} \Pr[\text{plan survives}] = (1 - p)^{L} \\ \text{calls} \approx \begin{cases} 1 & \text{plan once} \\ 1 + F & \text{re-plan} \\ L & \text{every stop} \end{cases} \end{gathered} \]
      Read it as: if each of the L segments of a plan is closed with probability p, independently, the whole plan works only if every one is open. F is the number of failures met on the way; when re-planning, each costs one more call to the planner. An agent that thinks again at every station pays one call per segment it rides, whether or not anything has changed.
      Say it“A plan of L steps survives with probability one minus p, to the power L.”

      Trying again, wiser

      Reflection: learning from a failed attempt

      Some agents cannot recover in the middle of a task: a failed step ends the attempt, and all they can do is try again. Retrying blindly helps only if the next attempt is different. Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023) adds a step between attempts: the agent looks at what went wrong, writes a short lesson in plain words (“the Central line is not running between Bank and St. Paul’s; avoid it”), and keeps the lessons in a memory that goes into the prompt of the next attempt. No weights change; the agent improves by remembering its own feedback. Here the closures stay put from one attempt to the next, and the planner is noisy, like a model sampling at a temperature T: each attempt, every segment’s time is multiplied by a random factor between 1 and 1 + T. Set T to 0 and see why blind retries are only worth something when attempts differ.

      \[ \begin{aligned} P_k &= 1 - (1 - q)^{k} \\ M_{k+1} &= M_k \cup \{\ell_k\} \end{aligned} \]
      Read it as: if every attempt succeeded independently with probability q, the chance Pk of at least one success in k attempts would be one minus the chance that all k fail. Real retries are not independent: the same agent makes the same mistake again. Reflection breaks the pattern: after failed attempt k, the lesson ℓk is added to the memory M, and every later plan must respect all the lessons so far.
      Say it“With independent tries, P-k, the chance of success within k tries, is one minus one-minus-q to the k; with reflection the memory grows by one lesson per failure.”

      One journey, attempt by attempt

      Real-world project

      Project: a journey-planning agent for the London Underground

      Every lab on this page runs on a real network: the station and track tables behind Wikimedia Commons’ London Underground geographic maps (station positions from public-domain GPS data), with a running time in whole minutes for each segment, as packaged in Nicola Greco’s open tubemaps dataset (2014). It covers 302 stations, 406 segments and 13 lines: the Underground, the Docklands Light Railway and the old East London line, as the network stood in the mid-2000s, before the Overground, Heathrow Terminal 5 and the Elizabeth line. Two misspelt station names in the source (“Picadilly Circus”, “Harrow & Wealdston”) are corrected; nothing else is changed. Running times are the dataset’s, not today’s timetable, and changes of line are a flat penalty rather than real walking times. The disruptions are invented.

      The agent puts the chapter together: it checks the status board (ReAct), plans with A* over (station, line) states, executes leg by leg, and re-plans when a segment it meets turns out to be closed.

      import json, heapq, math, itertools
import numpy as np
from scipy.sparse import coo_matrix
from scipy.sparse.csgraph import dijkstra, shortest_path

D = json.load(open("rp-data.json", encoding="utf8"))
ST, ED = D["st"], D["ed"]; NS, NE = len(ST), len(ED)
lines = [set() for _ in ST]
for a, b, l, t in ED: lines[a].add(l); lines[b].add(l)
sSt, sLn, stS = [], [], []                        # states = (station, line) pairs
for i in range(NS):
    m = {}
    for l in sorted(lines[i]): m[l] = len(sSt); sSt.append(i); sLn.append(l)
    stS.append(m)
sAdj = [[] for _ in sSt]
for e, (a, b, l, t) in enumerate(ED):
    u, v = stS[a][l], stS[b][l]; sAdj[u].append((v, t, e)); sAdj[v].append((u, t, e))
for L in sAdj: L.sort(key=lambda x: (x[0], x[2]))
def dist(i, j):                                 # the same float operations as the page
    dx = ST[i][1] - ST[j][1]; dy = ST[i][2] - ST[j][2]; return math.sqrt(dx * dx + dy * dy)
VMAX = max(dist(a, b) / t for a, b, l, t in ED); HK = 1 / VMAX
starts_at = lambda u, line, P: [(s, 0 if line is None or l == line else P) for l, s in sorted(stS[u].items())]

def search(starts, goal, P, w=0.0, avoid=None, noise=None):
    """A* over (station, line) states; w = 0 is Dijkstra. Ties broken by state id."""
    S = len(sSt); g = [math.inf] * S; par = [-1] * S; pe = [-2] * S; done = [False] * S; H = []
    h = (lambda s: w * (dist(sSt[s], goal) * HK)) if w else (lambda s: 0)
    for s, g0 in starts:
        if g0 < g[s]: g[s] = g0; pe[s] = -1; heapq.heappush(H, (g0 + h(s), s))
    exp, end = 0, -1
    while H:
        _, u = heapq.heappop(H)
        if done[u]: continue
        done[u] = True; exp += 1
        if sSt[u] == goal: end = u; break
        for v, t, e in sAdj[u]:
            if avoid is not None and avoid[e]: continue
            gv = g[u] + (t * noise[e] if noise is not None else t)
            if gv < g[v]: g[v] = gv; par[v] = u; pe[v] = e; heapq.heappush(H, (gv + h(v), v))
        for l, v in sorted(stS[sSt[u]].items()):
            if v != u and g[u] + P < g[v]: g[v] = g[u] + P; par[v] = u; pe[v] = -1; heapq.heappush(H, (g[v] + h(v), v))
    if end < 0: return dict(cost=math.inf, path=[], edges=[], exp=exp, g0=0)
    path, edges, s = [], [], end
    while s >= 0: path.append(s); edges.append(pe[s]); s = par[s]
    path.reverse(); edges.reverse()
    return dict(cost=g[end], path=path, edges=edges[1:], exp=exp, g0=g[path[0]])
plan = lambda a, b, P, w=0.0, avoid=None: search(starts_at(a, None, P), b, P, w, avoid)

def execute(r, closed, P):
    m = r["g0"]
    for k, e in enumerate(r["edges"]):
        if e >= 0 and closed[e]: return dict(ok=False, at=r["path"][k], e=e, min=m)
        m += P if e < 0 else ED[e][3]
    return dict(ok=True, at=r["path"][-1], min=m)

def run_strategy(kind, a, b, closed, P):
    known = [0] * NE; calls = mins = fails = 0; starts = starts_at(a, None, P)
    if kind == "once":
        x = execute(search(starts, b, P), closed, P); return dict(ok=x["ok"], min=x["min"], calls=1, gave_up=False)
    for _ in range(400):
        u = sSt[next(s for s, g0 in starts if g0 == 0)]
        if kind == "every":                         # read the departure boards at this station
            for s in stS[u].values():
                for _, _, e in sAdj[s]:
                    if closed[e]: known[e] = 1
        calls += 1; r = search(starts, b, P, avoid=known)
        if r["cost"] == math.inf: return dict(ok=False, min=mins, calls=calls, gave_up=True)
        if kind == "replan":
            x = execute(r, closed, P); mins += x["min"]
            if x["ok"]: return dict(ok=True, min=mins, calls=calls, gave_up=False)
            fails += 1; known[x["e"]] = 1; starts = starts_at(sSt[x["at"]], sLn[x["at"]], P)
        else:                                       # every: one ride, then think again
            mins += r["g0"]; k = 0
            while r["edges"][k] < 0: mins += P; k += 1
            e = r["edges"][k]
            if closed[e]: fails += 1; known[e] = 1; continue
            mins += ED[e][3]; s = r["path"][k + 1]
            if sSt[s] == b: return dict(ok=True, min=mins, calls=calls, gave_up=False)
            starts = starts_at(sSt[s], sLn[s], P)

def mulberry32(a):                                  # the page's random numbers, bit for bit
    st = [a & 0xFFFFFFFF]
    def r():
        st[0] = (st[0] + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF; t = st[0]
        t = ((t ^ (t >> 15)) * (1 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return r
def draw_task(r, p):
    a = math.floor(r() * NS); b = math.floor(r() * NS)
    while b == a: b = math.floor(r() * NS)
    return a, b, [1 if r() < p else 0 for _ in range(NE)]

P = 5
# 1. every pair of stations: scipy on the station graph (P = 0) against our search over states
W = {}
for a, b, l, t in ED: W[(a, b)] = min(t, W.get((a, b), 99)); W[(b, a)] = W[(a, b)]
A = coo_matrix(([t for t in W.values()], ([k[0] for k in W], [k[1] for k in W])), shape=(NS, NS)).tocsr()
T0 = dijkstra(A, directed=True); hops = shortest_path(A, unweighted=True)
off = ~np.eye(NS, dtype=bool)
print(f"stations {NS}, segments {NE}, states {len(sSt)}, v_max {VMAX * 60:.4f} km/h")
print(f"P = 0 (scipy): mean quickest {T0[off].mean():.4f} min, longest {T0.max():.0f} min; mean fewest stops {hops[off].mean():.4f}, most {hops.max():.0f}")
def all_from(a, P):
    g = [math.inf] * len(sSt); H = [(0, s) for s, _ in starts_at(a, None, P)]; done = [False] * len(sSt)
    for _, s in H: g[s] = 0
    heapq.heapify(H)
    while H:
        _, u = heapq.heappop(H)
        if done[u]: continue
        done[u] = True
        for v, t, e in sAdj[u]:
            if g[u] + t < g[v]: g[v] = g[u] + t; heapq.heappush(H, (g[v], v))
        for v in stS[sSt[u]].values():
            if v != u and g[u] + P < g[v]: g[v] = g[u] + P; heapq.heappush(H, (g[v], v))
    best = [math.inf] * NS
    for s in range(len(sSt)): best[sSt[s]] = min(best[sSt[s]], g[s])
    return best
T5 = np.array([all_from(a, P) for a in range(NS)]); T0s = np.array([all_from(a, 0) for a in range(NS)])
i, j = np.unravel_index(T5.argmax(), T5.shape)
print(f"P = 0 (our search) equals scipy for every pair: {np.array_equal(T0s, T0)}")
print(f"P = 5: mean quickest {T5[off].mean():.4f} min; longest {T5.max():.0f} min, {ST[i][0]} to {ST[j][0]}")

for _ in range(2000):

# 4. three agents on the same 300 journeys, segments closed with p = 0.03
r = mulberry32(2026); rows = {k: [] for k in ("once", "replan", "every")}; oracle = []

# 5. retries with and without reflection, temperature 0.3, up to 8 attempts
r = mulberry32(11); solved = {"none": [], "mem": []}
for _ in range(300):
    a, b, closed = draw_task(r, 0.03); noise = [[1 + 0.3 * r() for _ in range(NE)] for _ in range(8)]
    if search(starts_at(a, None, P), b, P, avoid=closed)["cost"] == math.inf: continue
    for kind in solved:
        memo = [0] * NE; s = 0
        for k in range(8):

      Wrap-up

      Five lines to remember

      • Reasoning is imagining before acting: a greedy step that looks good now can trap an agent for ever, and looking further ahead costs about bd imagined futures.
      • Planning is search over states: BFS finds the fewest steps, Dijkstra the cheapest plan, and A* the cheapest plan faster, as long as its estimate of the cost to go never overestimates.
      • Decompose big goals: choose the order of sub-goals at the top level (n! orders, so search or heuristics), and solve each leg with an ordinary planner.
      • ReAct grounds each thought in a fresh observation; reasoning from memory alone is fluent, confident and can be wrong about today’s world.
      • Plan once and re-plan on failure to save model calls; think at every step only when every step brings news; and keep lessons from failures so the next attempt is not a repeat of the last.

      Check your understanding

      Why does the greedy walker from Belsize Park need a lookahead of 8 stops before it ever reaches Finchley Road?

      Every route of fewer than 8 stops that it can imagine ends further from Finchley Road than Hampstead does, so each step it takes heads to Hampstead, and from Hampstead back. Only when the lookahead is long enough to contain the goal itself (8 stops, via King’s Cross and Baker Street) does an imagined route score better. A hunch that ignores the shape of the network is not a plan.

      BFS and Dijkstra both find “shortest” routes. When do they disagree, and why?

      BFS minimises the number of stops; Dijkstra minimises total cost (riding minutes plus change penalties). They disagree whenever a route with more stops is quicker, for example several short central hops against a few long suburban ones. On a graph where every move costs the same, Dijkstra settles states in the same order as BFS.

      Why must A*’s heuristic divide the distance by the network’s fastest speed, not its average speed?

      To be admissible it must never overestimate the remaining cost. Dividing by the average speed would overestimate on fast stretches (an express run out to Epping), and A* could then settle the goal before finding a quicker route. Dividing by the maximum speed guarantees an underestimate, at the price of a weaker hint and less pruning.

      A plan has 20 steps and each fails independently with probability 0.05. How likely is it to run without a hitch, and what does that suggest?

      (1 − 0.05)20 ≈ 0.36, so about two plans in three meet at least one failure. Long plans in an uncertain world need monitoring and re-planning (or shorter horizons), not just a good initial plan.

      With temperature 0, retrying without memory never helps. Why does reflection still help?

      At temperature 0 the planner is deterministic, so every retry produces the identical plan and fails at the identical place. Reflection changes the input of the next attempt (the lesson “avoid this segment” goes into memory), so the next plan is different, even though the planner itself has not changed.
