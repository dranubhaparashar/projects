Probability

Degrees of belief, two rules, and inference by counting

An agent never sees the whole world. A doctor cannot see the virus, a robot cannot see round the corner, and a spam filter cannot read the sender’s mind. Probability is how a rational agent keeps its books on what it does not know: it gives every possible world a weight, and every question becomes a sum of weights. The idea

Why probability? Acting when you can’t be sure

Try to write a medical rule in logic. “Fever implies flu” is false: most fevers have other causes. “Flu implies fever” is false too: plenty of people with flu never run a temperature. To fix the rule you would have to list every exception, and that fails for three reasons. Laziness: the list is far too long to write and use. Theoretical ignorance: medicine does not know every cause. Practical ignorance: even with a perfect theory, we have not run every test on this patient.

So the agent keeps a degree of belief instead, a number between 0 (certainly false) and 1 (certainly true). Goodfellow and colleagues name three sources of the uncertainty it summarises: the world may be truly random (a shuffled deck), we may not observe everything (the virus is invisible), or our model is incomplete (we drop details on purpose, as in “most birds fly”). For repeatable events, like dice, a probability can be read as a long-run frequency. For one-off events, like “this patient has flu”, it is a belief held by an agent. Remarkably, both readings obey the same rules, and this chapter is about those rules.

A belief is about the agent’s knowledge, not about the world. Before any test, P(flu) = 0.1 for a new patient; after a fever is found, P(flu | fever) ≈ 0.44. Both are correct: the patient did not change, the evidence did. And beliefs alone do not choose actions. You also need to know what you want. Decision theory = probability theory + utility theory: pick the action with the highest expected utility. Here is the classic example. You must decide how early to leave for the airport. The journey time is uncertain; arrive late and you miss the plane, arrive early and you wait. Move your leaving time, then change how much missing the plane would hurt.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{EU}(a) &= \sum_{s} P(s \mid a)\, U(s) \\ \text{cost}(t) &= t + M\, P(\text{late} \mid t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the expected utility of an action a is the utility of each outcome s, weighted by how probable that outcome is if you take a. Here the action is “leave t minutes before the flight”, and P(late | t) is the chance of missing the plane if you do. Every minute spent travelling or waiting costs 1, and missing the plane costs M minutes’ worth of misery. Minimising expected cost is the same as maximising expected utility.
Say it“The expected utility of a is the sum over outcomes s of P of s given a, times U of s. The cost of leaving t minutes early is t plus M times the probability of being late.”

The language

Possible worlds, events and random variables

Start with the sample space Ω: the set of every way the world could be. Roll two dice and Ω has 36 worlds, from (1, 1) to (6, 6). The worlds are mutually exclusive (only one happens) and exhaustive (one of them must). A probability model gives each world ω a weight P(ω). A proposition such as “the total is 7” is true in some worlds and false in others, so it picks out a set of worlds, called an event, and its probability is the total weight of that set. Logic becomes set algebra: and (∧) is the overlap, or (∨) the union, not (¬) the complement.

A random variable is a function that reads a number or label off each world. Total = die 1 + die 2 is one: its domain is {2, …, 12}, and “Total = 7” is an event. We write variables with capitals and their values in lower case, and the bold P(Total) means the whole distribution: the list of 11 probabilities, one per value. Variables can also be continuous, like journey time above; then they have a density instead of a list, which chapter 0.5 takes up. Build an event from the buttons, then load the first die and watch counting stop working.

\[ 0 \le P(\omega) \le 1, \qquad \sum_{\omega \in \Omega} P(\omega) = 1 \]
Read it as: every world gets a weight between 0 and 1, and the weights of all the worlds add up to exactly 1. Something must happen.
Say it“P of omega is between zero and one, and the sum of P of omega over all of Omega is one.”
\[ P(\phi) = \sum_{\omega \in \phi} P(\omega) \]
Read it as: the probability of a proposition φ is the sum of the weights of the worlds where it is true. When all worlds weigh the same, this is just counting: worlds in the event ÷ worlds in total.
Say it“P of phi is the sum of P of omega over the worlds omega in phi.”

The rules of the game

The axioms, and why you should accept them

The two facts above, weights between 0 and 1 that add up to 1, together with the rule that an event’s probability is the sum of its worlds, are essentially the axioms Andrey Kolmogorov wrote down in 1933. Everything else follows from them. A proposition and its negation split Ω in two, so their probabilities add to 1. And when you add P(a) and P(b), the worlds where both are true get counted twice, so you take them off once: that is inclusion–exclusion.

Why should an agent’s beliefs obey these rules? Bruno de Finetti’s answer is a betting argument. Suppose you believe a proposition φ to degree P(φ). Then a ticket that pays £10 if φ turns out true is worth £10×P(φ) to you, and you should be happy to buy or sell it at that price. De Finetti proved that if your beliefs break the axioms, a bookie can choose a handful of such tickets, each one fair by your own lights, that together lose you money in every possible world. This is a Dutch book. Richard Cox (1946) reached the same rules from a different direction: any sensible way of grading plausibility with numbers turns out to be probability. Set your four beliefs about tomorrow’s weather and see whether the bookie can trap you.

\[ \begin{aligned} P(\lnot a) &= 1 - P(a) \\ P(a \lor b) &= P(a) + P(b) - P(a \land b) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the worlds where a is false are all the others, so their weight is what is left of the total of 1. For “a or b”, add the two, then take away the overlap that you counted twice.
Say it“P of not a is one minus P of a. P of a or b is P of a plus P of b minus P of a and b.”
\[ G(\omega) = 10 \sum_{k} s_k \big( [\phi_k]_\omega - P(\phi_k) \big) \]
Read it as: your net gain in world ω from a set of £10 tickets. sk = +1 if you bought ticket k and −1 if you sold it; [φk]ω is 1 when φk is true in world ω and 0 when it is false. A Dutch book is a choice of tickets that makes G(ω) negative for every ω.
Say it“G of omega is ten times the sum over tickets of s-k times, the truth of phi-k in omega, minus P of phi-k.”

Two rules

The sum rule and the product rule

Take two variables: today’s weather X (sun, cloud or rain) and how you get to work Y (walk, bike, bus or car). Keep a diary for N days and write nij for the number of days with weather i and travel j. Following Bishop, read probabilities as fractions of days. The joint probability p(X = i, Y = j) is nij/N: one cell. The marginal p(X = i) ignores Y: it is the column total ci over N, so you get it by adding the joint across Y. That is the sum rule. The conditional p(Y = j | X = i) looks only inside column i: it is nij/ci. Multiply it by ci/N and you are back at the joint. That is the product rule, and with counts it holds exactly, not just in the limit.

As N grows, the fractions settle on the true probabilities. Two lines of algebra on these rules give everything else in this chapter, including Bayes’ rule. For a continuous variable the sum becomes an integral, p(x) = ∫ p(x, y) dy. Record more days, then pick a weather and a way to travel.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(X) &= \sum_{Y} p(X, Y) \\ p(X, Y) &= p(Y \mid X)\, p(X) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first line is the sum rule: to forget a variable, add over its values. The second is the product rule: to build a joint, take the chance of the first, then the chance of the second given the first. Rearranged, the product rule defines conditional probability: p(Y | X) = p(X, Y) / p(X), whenever p(X) > 0.
Say it“p of X is the sum over Y of p of X and Y. p of X and Y is p of Y given X, times p of X.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &P(x_1, \dots, x_n) \\ &\ = P(x_1)\, P(x_2 \mid x_1) \\ &\quad \cdots P(x_n \mid x_1, \dots, x_{n-1}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the chain rule: apply the product rule again and again. Any joint distribution is a product of one-variable conditionals, each given everything before it. It is always true, in any order; later chapters get their savings by dropping terms from the “given” lists.
Say it“P of x-one to x-n is P of x-one, times P of x-two given x-one, and so on, up to P of x-n given all the earlier ones.”

Answering any question

Inference by enumeration: the full joint table

Write down a probability for every combination of every variable and you have the full joint distribution. Here is the one behind the hero: three Boolean variables, Flu, Fever and Cough, so 2³ = 8 entries that add up to 1. From this one table you can answer any question about the three by adding entries. To get P(fever), add the four cells where fever is true: that is marginalisation, the sum rule again. To get P(flu | fever), add the cells where flu and fever are both true and divide by P(fever).

Look at that division. Asking “flu or not, given fever?”, both answers divide by the same P(fever). So you can skip it: add up the matching cells for each value of the query variable, then scale the results so they add to 1. That scaling constant is written α, and the step is normalisation. Variables that are neither asked about nor observed are hidden, and they get summed out. Choose what to ask and what you’ve seen; the cells that get added light up.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{P}(Y) &= \sum_{z} \mathbf{P}(Y, z) \\ &= \sum_{z} \mathbf{P}(Y \mid z)\, P(z) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: marginalising, then conditioning: to get a distribution over Y, add the joint over every value z of the other variables, or, equivalently, average Y’s conditional distributions weighted by how likely each z is.
Say it“Bold P of Y is the sum over z of bold P of Y and z, which is the sum over z of bold P of Y given z, times P of z.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{P}(X \mid \mathbf{e}) &= \alpha\, \mathbf{P}(X, \mathbf{e}) \\ &= \alpha \sum_{\mathbf{y}} \mathbf{P}(X, \mathbf{e}, \mathbf{y}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the whole recipe. X is what you ask about, e the evidence you observed, y the hidden variables. Fix the evidence, sum out the hidden variables, and normalise with α = 1 / P(e) so the answer adds to 1.
Say it“Bold P of X given e is alpha times bold P of X and e, which is alpha times the sum over y of bold P of X, e and y.”

Shrinking the table

Independence and conditional independence

Add a fourth variable, tomorrow’s Weather with four values, and the table grows to 8 × 4 = 32 entries. But the weather has nothing to do with your flu: P(flu | sunny) = P(flu). When learning one variable never changes your belief about another, they are independent, the joint is just the product of the two marginals, and you can store 8 + 4 numbers instead of 32. Absolute independence like this is powerful but rare. Fever and cough are not independent: each is a sign of flu, so a fever makes a cough more likely.

Yet once you know whether the patient has flu, a fever tells you nothing more about a cough: both depend on the illness, not on each other. That is conditional independence, and it is the workhorse of probabilistic AI. It lets the joint factor into small pieces, P(Flu) P(Fever | Flu) P(Cough | Flu): 1 + 2 + 2 = 5 numbers instead of 7, and with n symptoms 2n + 1 instead of 2n+1 − 1. Switch between everyone, flu and no flu, then use the slider to make the symptoms interact.

\[ \begin{aligned} a \perp b \iff\ & P(a \mid b) = P(a) \\ \iff\ & P(a \land b) = P(a)\,P(b) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a and b are independent when knowing b leaves your belief in a where it was. Put the first line into the product rule and you get the second: the joint is the product of the marginals. For variables, this must hold for every pair of values.
Say it“a is independent of b if and only if P of a given b equals P of a, if and only if P of a and b equals P of a times P of b.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{P}(X, Y \mid Z) \\ &\quad = \mathbf{P}(X \mid Z)\, \mathbf{P}(Y \mid Z) \\[4pt] &\mathbf{P}(Z, X, Y) \\ &\quad = \mathbf{P}(Z)\, \mathbf{P}(X \mid Z)\, \mathbf{P}(Y \mid Z) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: X and Y are conditionally independent given Z when, inside each value of Z, their joint is the product of their conditionals. Then the full joint factors into a cause and its separate effects. Here Z = Flu, X = Fever, Y = Cough.
Say it“Bold P of X and Y given Z is bold P of X given Z times bold P of Y given Z.”

Diagnosis

Bayes’ rule: turning the evidence around

The product rule can be written both ways round: P(a, b) = P(a | b) P(b) = P(b | a) P(a). Divide by P(b) and you have Bayes’ rule. Why is it so useful? Because knowledge usually comes in the causal direction. A test’s maker can tell you P(positive | disease), and that number is the same in every clinic. What a doctor needs is the diagnostic direction, P(disease | positive), and that depends on how common the disease is where you are. Bayes’ rule converts one into the other. The prior P(disease) is the base rate; the answer is the posterior.

Picture it as 1,000 people (Gigerenzer’s “natural frequencies”, the easiest way to get this right). With a rare disease, the healthy majority produces more false alarms than the sick minority produces true positives, so most positive tests are wrong, even for a good test. A second positive from an independent test helps a lot: because the tests are conditionally independent given the disease, their likelihoods simply multiply. Lower the base rate and watch the posterior collapse; then add tests.

\[ \begin{aligned} P(c \mid e) &= \frac{P(e \mid c)\, P(c)}{P(e)} \\ P(e) &= \sum_{c'} P(e \mid c')\, P(c') \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the chance of the cause given the effect is the chance of the effect given the cause, times the prior of the cause, divided by the overall chance of the effect. The bottom line comes from the sum rule: add the same top line over every possible cause.
Say it“P of c given e is P of e given c times P of c, over P of e.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{P}(C \mid e_1, \dots, e_n) \\ &\quad = \alpha\, \mathbf{P}(C) \prod_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{P}(e_i \mid C) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: when pieces of evidence are conditionally independent given the cause, multiply the prior by each likelihood in turn and normalise at the end. Each test’s posterior becomes the next test’s prior. For the flu table: P(flu | fever, cough) = α × 0.1 × 0.7 × 0.8 versus α × 0.9 × 0.1 × 0.2, which gives 0.056 / (0.056 + 0.018) = 0.757.
Say it“Bold P of C given e-one to e-n is alpha times bold P of C times the product of bold P of e-i given C.”

An agent under uncertainty

The Wumpus world revisited

The Wumpus world is a 4 × 4 cave that a logical agent explores square by square. Each square other than the start [1,1] hides a bottomless pit with probability 0.2, independently of the others, and the agent feels a breeze in any square next to a pit. (We ignore the Wumpus and the gold here; the pits are hard enough.) In the classic position the agent has visited [1,1], [1,2] and [2,1] and felt a breeze in both [1,2] and [2,1]. Logic is stuck: none of the three squares it could step into next, [1,3], [2,2] and [3,1], is provably safe. Probability is not stuck.

The full joint has 215 = 32,768 pit layouts (213 = 8,192 once the three visited squares are known to be pit-free), but most squares are far from anything the agent has sensed. Split the unknown squares into the query, the frontier (unknown squares next to visited ones) and everything else. The breezes depend only on the frontier, and the other squares’ pits are independent of everything, so their sum is 1 and they drop out. What is left is a sum over a handful of frontier layouts, and it gives exactly the same answer. Click a frontier square to step into it, or start a new cave and play; the numbers are each square’s chance of a pit.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{P}(P_{1,1}, \dots, P_{4,4}) &= \prod_{i,j} \mathbf{P}(P_{i,j}) \\ P(p_{i,j}) &= 0.2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the prior over caves. Pits are placed independently, so a layout with k pits among the 15 squares has probability 0.2k × 0.815−k. The breezes are then fixed by the pits: a square is breezy exactly when a neighbour holds a pit.
Say it“The joint over all the pit variables is the product of each square’s own pit probability, which is 0.2.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{P}(P_q \mid k, b) \\ &\ = \alpha'\, \mathbf{P}(P_q) \sum_{f} P(b \mid k, P_q, f)\, P(f) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: k is what the agent knows (the visited squares have no pits), b the breezes it felt, and f a layout of pits on the frontier. To get the pit chance of query square q, run over the frontier layouts only. Each layout scores 1 if it explains every breeze and no-breeze the agent felt, and 0 if not, weighted by its prior. Multiply by q’s own prior and normalise.
Say it“Bold P of P-q given k and b is alpha-prime times bold P of P-q, times the sum over frontier layouts f of P of b given k, P-q and f, times P of f.”

Why nobody stores the full joint

Every answer above was exact, and with only 720 cells it was cheap. But even here of the cells hold nobody, and hold fewer than ten people, so narrow questions rest on very few people. Keep all nine categorical columns of the original data instead (counting “?”, the mark for a missing value, as a value), and the table has 76,204,800 cells for 48,842 people. Only 12,672 of them hold anybody: 99.98% of the table is empty, and almost every combination you could ask about has never been seen. This is the O(2n) wall of the full joint, met with real data, and it is why the rest of the book is about structure: independence and conditional independence that let a model store a few small tables instead of one enormous one. The orange diamonds in “Ask the table” come from the simplest such model, and the naive Bayes chapter builds it properly.

V = ["age", "sex", "edu", "marital", "hours", "income"]

J = df.groupby(V, observed=False).size()          # the full joint table, as counts
print(f"{len(J)} cells, {(J == 0).sum()} empty, N = {J.sum()}")

def P(query, given={}):                            # P(query | given), by counting rows of the joint
    keep = pd.Series(True, J.index)
    for v, x in given.items(): keep &= J.index.get_level_values(v) == x
    hit = keep & (J.index.get_level_values(query[0]) == query[1])
    return J[hit].sum() / J[keep].sum(), J[hit].sum(), J[keep].sum()

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Probability summarises what an agent does not know: a weight on every possible world, adding to 1. Combined with utilities it tells the agent what to do: maximise expected utility.
  • An event is a set of worlds and its probability is their total weight. Beliefs that break the axioms can be Dutch-booked: a set of bets each fair to you that loses in every world.
  • Two rules do all the work. Sum rule: p(X) = ΣY p(X, Y). Product rule: p(X, Y) = p(Y | X) p(X). Conditional probability is their ratio.
  • The full joint answers any query by selecting, summing out the hidden variables and normalising, but it grows as 2n. Independence and conditional independence factor it into small pieces.
  • Bayes’ rule turns causal knowledge P(e | c) into diagnosis P(c | e), and the base rate matters: a 90% test for a 1-in-100 disease is right only about 15% of the time it says yes.

Check your understanding

Two fair dice. What is P(Total = 7 | a six shows)?

“A six shows” holds in 11 of the 36 worlds. Of those, two have total 7: (1, 6) and (6, 1). So the answer is 2/11 ≈ 0.18, a little more than P(Total = 7) = 6/36 ≈ 0.17.

You believe P(a) = 0.6 and P(¬a) = 0.5. Which tickets should a bookie sell you to guarantee a profit?

Sell you both £10 tickets, on a and on ¬a, for £6 and £5. Exactly one of them pays £10 whatever happens, so you pay £11 for a sure £10: a loss of £1 in every world.

Using the flu table, why can you find P(flu | fever) without first working out P(fever)?

Because P(fever) divides both P(flu, fever) = 0.070 and P(¬flu, fever) = 0.090. Normalising the pair (0.070, 0.090) to add to 1 gives the same answer, 0.4375, and the sum 0.16 you divided by is P(fever).

Fever and cough are conditionally independent given flu. Are they independent?

No. Overall P(cough) = 0.26 but P(cough | fever) = 0.4625: a fever raises the chance of flu, and flu raises the chance of a cough. Conditional independence and independence are different properties, and neither implies the other.

In the classic Wumpus position, why are [1,3] and [3,1] safer than [2,2]?

A pit in [2,2] would explain both breezes at once. Without one, you need pits in both [1,3] and [3,1], which is a priori much less likely (0.2 × 0.2). So the layouts consistent with the breezes mostly contain a pit in [2,2]: P = 0.86 there and 0.31 in each of the others.
