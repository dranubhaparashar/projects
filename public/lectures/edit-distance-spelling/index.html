Edit Distance & Spelling Correction

The cheapest way to turn one string into another, and what a typist most probably meant

How similar are graffe and giraffe? Count the smallest number of single-letter insertions, deletions and substitutions that turns one into the other: that number is the minimum edit distance, and the cheapest sequence of edits is an alignment. Finding it by trying every sequence would take exponential time; a table of small sub-answers, filled once, takes a few dozen multiplications. The same table, with probabilities instead of unit costs and a language model to say which words are likely, is a spell checker: Bayes’ rule applied to a noisy typist. Edit the two words and watch the table and the alignment follow.

The problem

Edit distance as a search problem

Much of language processing starts with the question how similar are these two strings? A spell checker wants to know that graffe is closer to giraffe than to grail. A machine-translation evaluator wants to know how far a system’s output is from a human reference. A biologist wants to line up two DNA sequences. The answer that Vladimir Levenshtein gave in 1966 is to count edits. Allow three operations, each applied to one character: insert a character, delete one, or substitute one for another. The minimum edit distance between two strings is the smallest total cost of a sequence of operations that turns the first into the second. With every operation costing 1 it is the Levenshtein distance; a common alternative, used in the first edition of Jurafsky and Martin’s textbook, charges 2 for a substitution, because a substitution is a deletion followed by an insertion.

Phrased that way, this is a search problem. The start state is the source string, the goal is the target, each operation is a move, and we want the cheapest path. A naive search is hopeless: the space of strings is infinite and even restricting ourselves to paths that never undo their own work leaves an exponential number of them. The figure below shows the search space in its useful form. Lay the source down the side and the target along the top. A path from the top-left corner to the bottom-right corner that moves down (delete a source letter), right (insert a target letter) or diagonally (copy or substitute) is exactly one way of transforming the source into the target: an alignment. The number of such paths is a Delannoy number, and it grows exponentially with the lengths. The number of distinct corners a path can pass through, however, is only (n+1)(m+1). That gap is the whole story of the next section. Type two words and ask for random paths; the cheapest one is drawn underneath.

\[ d(s, t) \;=\; \min_{\text{edit sequences } e:\ s \to t}\ \sum_{k} c(e_k), \qquad c(\text{ins}) = c(\text{del}) = 1,\quad c(\text{sub}) \in \{1, 2\} \]
Read it as: s is the source string, t the target. An edit sequence e is a list of operations that turns s into t; its cost is the sum of the costs c of its operations. The distance d(s, t) is the cost of the cheapest sequence. Copying a letter that already matches costs nothing. With all three costs equal to 1 the distance between graffe and giraffe is 1 (insert an i); with substitutions at 2 it is still 1, because no substitution is needed.

The algorithm

The dynamic-programming table

Every path through the lattice passes through corners, and the key observation is that the cheapest way to reach a corner does not depend on where the path goes afterwards. So instead of remembering millions of paths we remember one number per corner: D[i][j], the minimum cost of turning the first i letters of the source into the first j letters of the target. Each corner has only three possible predecessors, so D[i][j] is the smallest of three candidates: come from above and delete the source letter si; come from the left and insert the target letter tj; or come from the diagonal and either copy (if si = tj, for free) or substitute. The first row and column are the base cases: turning i letters into nothing takes i deletions, turning nothing into j letters takes j insertions. Fill the table row by row, and the bottom-right cell is the answer. This is dynamic programming, Richard Bellman’s name for solving a problem by tabulating its overlapping sub-problems; the string version was written down independently several times and is usually credited to Wagner and Fischer (1974). The cost is (n+1)(m+1) cells, each a constant amount of work: quadratic, not exponential.

\[ \begin{aligned} D[i][0] &= i\cdot c_{\text{del}}, \qquad D[0][j] = j\cdot c_{\text{ins}} \\ D[i][j] &= \min \begin{cases} D[i-1][j] + c_{\text{del}} & \text{delete } s_i \\ D[i][j-1] + c_{\text{ins}} & \text{insert } t_j \\ D[i-1][j-1] + \begin{cases} 0 & s_i = t_j \\ c_{\text{sub}} & s_i \ne t_j \end{cases} & \text{copy or substitute} \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: D[i][j] is the distance between the prefix s1..i and the prefix t1..j. The cell above has already dealt with one more source letter, so from there we delete si. The cell to the left has produced one target letter fewer, so from there we insert tj. The diagonal cell has one fewer of each, so from there we line si up with tj: free if they are equal, csub if not. Taking the minimum over the three is what makes the answer optimal: if a cheaper route to (i, j) existed it would come through one of these three cells, and each of them already holds its own cheapest cost.
Say it“D of i, j is the minimum of: the cell above plus a deletion, the cell to the left plus an insertion, and the diagonal cell plus zero or a substitution.”

Two refinements the real code uses. First, only the previous row is needed to compute the current one, so if you want the distance alone you can keep two rows and use O(min(n, m)) memory; you only need the whole table to recover the alignment. Second, if you only care whether the distance is below some bound k (a spell checker usually does), you can skip every cell farther than k from the diagonal, since |i − j| is a lower bound on D[i][j]: that is Ukkonen’s banded algorithm, O(kn) rather than O(nm). Neither changes the recurrence.

Reading the table backwards

Backtrace and the alignment it yields

The number in the last cell says how far; it does not say which edits. For that we remember, in each cell, which of the three candidates won, or we recompute it on the way back. Starting at the bottom-right corner, step to a predecessor whose value plus the step cost equals the current value, and keep going until the top-left corner. Read forwards, the steps are the alignment: a diagonal step pairs a source letter with a target letter (a copy or a substitution), a vertical step pairs a source letter with a gap (a deletion), a horizontal step pairs a gap with a target letter (an insertion). Jurafsky and Martin write the result as three rows: the source with gaps, the operations, the target with gaps.

Often several cells tie, and then several alignments are optimal. intention → execution has distance 5 and seven different optimal alignments: the one the textbook shows (delete i, substitute n→e, t→x, insert c, substitute n→u) and the one most implementations return (substitute the first five letters, copy tion). Both cost 5; the table cannot prefer one, and a tie-breaking rule decides. Switch the rule and watch the path move; hover a cell to see its pointers. The counter runs a second table that adds up, for each cell, how many optimal paths reach it.

\[ \text{ptr}[i][j] \in \{\searrow,\ \downarrow,\ \rightarrow\}, \qquad \text{alignment} = \text{reverse of the pointer walk from } (n, m) \text{ to } (0, 0), \qquad N_{\text{opt}}[i][j] = \sum_{\substack{(i',j') \to (i,j)\\ D[i'][j'] + c = D[i][j]}} N_{\text{opt}}[i'][j'] \]
Read it as: each cell stores one or more pointers to the predecessors that achieve its minimum. Walking the pointers from the last cell to the first and reversing the walk gives the sequence of operations. Nopt counts optimal paths: a cell inherits the counts of every predecessor that ties for its minimum, with Nopt[0][0] = 1. Its bottom-right value is the number of distinct optimal alignments.

A fourth operation

Transpositions: Damerau’s distance

Look at real typing errors and one pattern jumps out: teh, recieve, thier. Two adjacent letters have swapped places. Levenshtein charges 2 for that (a substitution each, or a deletion plus an insertion), which makes teh as far from the as tea is from the. Fred Damerau, studying the spelling errors in a 1964 corpus of typed text, found that over 80% of misspellings were exactly one of four things: a single insertion, deletion, substitution or transposition of adjacent letters. Adding the fourth operation at cost 1 gives the Damerau–Levenshtein distance, and the table needs one more candidate: if si−1si equals tjtj−1, the cell two steps back along the diagonal, plus 1.

There is a subtlety. The simple extension, called optimal string alignment (OSA), forbids editing a pair after it has been swapped, because the table only ever looks two cells back. The unrestricted distance allows it, and the two can differ: from abc to ca, OSA says 3 but the true Damerau–Levenshtein distance is 2 (delete b, then swap). The unrestricted version needs a slightly more elaborate table that remembers, for each letter, where it last occurred. For spelling correction the difference almost never matters, and the pages here use OSA. Try the examples; the lower chart counts which single edits appear in the chapter’s own list of 114 common misspellings, aligned by this very algorithm.

\[ D[i][j] = \min \begin{cases} D[i-1][j] + 1 & \text{delete} \\ D[i][j-1] + 1 & \text{insert} \\ D[i-1][j-1] + [\,s_i \ne t_j\,] & \text{copy / substitute} \\ D[i-2][j-2] + 1 & \text{if } s_{i-1}s_i = t_j t_{j-1}:\ \text{transpose} \end{cases} \]

Not all edits are equal

Weighted edit distance and confusion matrices

Unit costs treat every substitution alike, but typists do not. On a QWERTY keyboard e is next to r and far from p; in English spelling a and e swap constantly in unstressed syllables (seperate, definately) while a and k never do. A weighted edit distance lets each operation have its own cost: csub(a, b) for substituting b for a, cdel(a) or, better, cdel(a | previous letter), cins(b | previous letter), and ctrans(ab). The recurrence is untouched; only the constants change, and they can come from data. Kernighan, Church and Gale (1990) estimated them from a corpus of real errors as four confusion matrices: sub[x, y], how often y was typed as x; del[x, y], how often the pair xy was typed as x; ins[x, y], how often x was typed as xy; and trans[x, y], how often xy was typed as yx. Dividing each count by how often the intended letter (or pair) occurs turns the counts into probabilities, and the natural cost of an edit is then −log of its probability: common slips are cheap, rare ones dear.

This page learns its matrices from the 114 misspelling pairs listed in the project’s Python code, by aligning each pair with the OSA backtrace and tallying the edits; with so few pairs most cells are zero, so every count has λ = 0.5 added (add-λ smoothing). The denominators are letter and letter-pair counts from the chapter’s own corpus. That is a small error model, and the lab lets you see what a bigger one would look like: the seeded typos button generates several hundred synthetic errors from corpus words with a keyboard-neighbour model and the same seeded generator the project uses, and rebuilds the matrices from the combined data. Hover the matrix to read a count, then compare the two candidate corrections of recieve below: equally far under unit costs, very different once ie↔ei is known to be common.

\[ \begin{aligned} P(\text{sub: } y \to x) &= \frac{\text{sub}[x, y] + \lambda}{\text{count}(y) + 27\lambda} & P(\text{del: } wy \to w) &= \frac{\text{del}[w, y] + \lambda}{\text{count}(wy) + 27\lambda} \\ P(\text{ins: } w \to wx) &= \frac{\text{ins}[w, x] + \lambda}{\text{count}(w) + 27\lambda} & P(\text{trans: } yz \to zy) &= \frac{\text{trans}[y, z] + \lambda}{\text{count}(yz) + 27\lambda} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: x is what was typed, y (and z) what was meant, w the letter before the edit (a word-start marker # at position 0). count(·) is how often the intended letter or pair appears in the corpus. The 27 is the alphabet plus the marker, so that adding λ to every cell of a row still gives probabilities that sum to 1. Deletions and insertions are conditioned on the preceding letter because dropping a doubled letter (tommorow, begining) is far more common than dropping a letter at random.
Say it“Each edit’s probability is its smoothed count over the smoothed count of what was intended.”

Bayes for typists

The noisy-channel model of spelling correction

Picture the writer as a clean source and the keyboard as a noisy channel that garbles what passes through it, the way Shannon described a telephone line. We receive the garbled word x and want the word w that went in. Bayes’ rule turns the question round: the best guess ŵ is the word that maximises P(x | w) P(w), the probability that this intended word would be corrupted into this observation, times the probability of the intended word in the first place. The first factor is the channel model (or error model): it is built from the confusion matrices of the last section, multiplied along the edits that turn w into x. The second is a language model: for a single word, how common the word is. Kernighan, Church and Gale (1990) built exactly this and reported that it agreed with human judges on the great majority of the typos in a large corpus of newswire text.

Two practical questions remain. Which w are worth considering? Only words close to x: Damerau’s finding says almost every misspelling is within one edit of its correction, and nearly all of the rest within two. Peter Norvig’s short essay How to Write a Spelling Corrector makes the whole thing fit on one screen: generate every string one edit from x (there are at most 54n + 25 of them for an n-letter word: n deletions, n − 1 transpositions, 26n replacements and 26(n + 1) insertions), keep the ones that are real words, go to two edits only if nothing is found at one, and pick the most frequent survivor. Here the dictionary is small, so the page instead scans its 503 known words and keeps those within distance 2. Where does P(w) come from? From counting words in a corpus; this page’s corpus is the seventeen paragraphs embedded in the project section, written for this chapter, 1,775 words long. Add-one smoothing stops any word having probability zero. Type a misspelling and watch the two factors argue: the channel favours words a cheap edit away, the language model favours common words, and the product decides.

\[ \hat w = \arg\max_{w \in C(x)} \underbrace{P(x \mid w)}_{\text{channel}}\ \underbrace{P(w)}_{\text{language model}} \]
Read it as: C(x) is the candidate set: known words within a small edit distance of the observed x. P(x | w) is the probability that an intended w would be typed as x: the product of the probabilities of the edits that take w to x, read off the confusion matrices. P(w) is how likely the word is a priori. Bayes’ rule has a denominator P(x), but it is the same for every candidate and drops out of the argmax.
Say it“The correction is the candidate that maximises the channel probability times the prior.”
\[ P(x \mid w) = \prod_{e \in \text{edits}(w \to x)} P(e), \qquad P(w) = \frac{\text{count}(w) + 1}{N + V + 1}, \qquad |\text{edits}_1(x)| \le 54n + 25 \]
Read it as: the channel probability multiplies one factor per edit in the cheapest alignment of w to x (one factor for a single-edit candidate, two for a double). The prior is a unigram probability with add-one smoothing: N is the number of word tokens in the corpus, V the number of types, and the extra 1 makes room for unseen words. The last term bounds how many strings are one edit away from an n-letter word; duplicates make the true number a little smaller (336 of a possible 349 for kitten).

When the typo is a word

Real-word spelling errors: context decides

Everything so far assumes the misspelling is not a word, so it can be spotted by a dictionary. But between a quarter and two fifths of spelling errors in typed text produce another real word: their for there, by for buy, form for from, minuets for minutes. No dictionary catches them. Only context can: to go their is a strange sequence of words and to go there is an ordinary one. So we extend the model to whole sentences. Every word xi of the sentence gets a candidate set: the word itself, every known word within one edit of it, and its homophones. The channel probability of the word being typed correctly is a constant α, near 1 (we assume most words are right), and the remaining mass goes to the alternatives through the confusion matrices as before. The language model becomes a bigram model, P(wi | wi−1) estimated from counts of adjacent word pairs in the corpus and interpolated with the unigram so that unseen pairs are not impossible.

Jurafsky and Martin simplify by assuming at most one error per sentence and scoring each one-word change separately. This page does the general thing: the candidates form a lattice, one column per word, and the best sentence is the highest-scoring path through it, which the Viterbi algorithm finds in one left-to-right pass over the columns. Note how everything hinges on α. The typed word is defended by α/P(x | w), a factor around fifty when α = 0.95 and the alternative is a homophone; the context has to prefer the alternative by more than that to overturn it. For go their the bigram evidence is overwhelming and the correction happens; for to by three apples the corpus prefers to buy by about eighteen to one, not enough, and the page leaves by alone until you lower α. That trade-off, missed corrections against false alarms, is the real design decision in every spell checker.

\[ \hat W = \arg\max_{w_1 \dots w_n \in C(x_1)\times\dots\times C(x_n)} \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(x_i \mid w_i)\, P(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) \cdot P(\langle/s\rangle \mid w_n) \]
Read it as: choose one candidate per position; the score of the whole sentence is the product, over positions, of the channel probability of the typed word given the chosen word and the bigram probability of the chosen word given the previous chosen word (w0 is the start symbol). The final factor scores the sentence ending. Searching all combinations would be exponential; Viterbi keeps, for each candidate in column i, only the best path reaching it, because the rest of the path depends on that candidate alone.
Say it“The best sentence maximises the product of channel times bigram at every position.”
\[ P(x \mid w) = \begin{cases} \alpha & x = w \\ \prod_e P(e) & x \ne w,\ \text{one edit} \\ p_{\text{hom}} & x \text{ a homophone of } w \end{cases} \qquad P(w \mid v) = \lambda\,\frac{\text{count}(v, w)}{\text{count}(v)} + (1 - \lambda)\,\frac{\text{count}(w) + 1}{N + V + 1} \]
Read it as: α is the probability a word is typed as intended; the alternatives split the rest through the edit probabilities, or through a fixed phom for sound-alike pairs that are not one edit apart (their/there). The bigram is interpolated: a share λ of the maximum-likelihood estimate (pair count over the count of the previous word) plus 1 − λ of the smoothed unigram, so a pair never seen in the corpus still has a small probability. The same table elsewhere

Relatives: sequence alignment, word error rate, data cleaning

The edit-distance table is one of the most reused ideas in computing, and three of its relatives are worth meeting. Needleman and Wunsch (1970) wrote it down for biology four years before Wagner and Fischer did for strings. Their version flips the sign: instead of minimising a cost it maximises a score, with a positive reward for aligning identical nucleotides or amino acids, a penalty for a mismatch, and a gap penalty for an insertion or deletion. The table, the three candidates and the backtrace are identical; only max replaces min. (Smith and Waterman’s 1981 variant floors every cell at zero and starts the backtrace from the largest cell, which finds the best local alignment between two long sequences; it is what BLAST approximates.)

Word error rate is edit distance over words instead of letters. To score a speech recogniser, align its transcript against what was actually said with unit costs and count the substitutions S, deletions D and insertions I; WER = (S + D + I) / N, with N the number of reference words. It can exceed 100% when the hypothesis is much longer than the reference, and it remains the standard figure for speech recognition because, unlike a fuzzy similarity, each error has a name a developer can act on. Data cleaning uses the same distance to match records that were typed by different people: “Birmingam” against a list of towns, two customer names that differ by one letter. A common practice is to normalise the distance by the longer string’s length, giving a similarity between 0 and 1, and to set a threshold; the lab shows how much the threshold matters.

\[ F[i][j] = \max \begin{cases} F[i-1][j-1] + s(a_i, b_j) \\ F[i-1][j] + g \\ F[i][j-1] + g \end{cases}, \qquad F[i][0] = i g,\ F[0][j] = j g \]
Read it as: Needleman–Wunsch. s(a, b) is the substitution score, positive for a match and negative for a mismatch; g is the (negative) gap penalty. Every cell is the best score of aligning the prefixes, and the bottom-right cell is the global alignment score. Set s(match) = 0, s(mismatch) = −1, g = −1 and negate everything: you have Levenshtein distance again.
Say it“F of i, j is the maximum of the diagonal plus the match score, or either neighbour plus the gap penalty.”
\[ \text{WER} = \frac{S + D + I}{N}, \qquad \text{sim}(a, b) = 1 - \frac{d(a, b)}{\max(|a|, |b|)} \]
Read it as: S, D and I come from the minimum-cost word alignment of the hypothesis against the reference (there can be ties; the counts of each kind may change but the total does not). N is the number of words in the reference, so WER is the fraction of reference words that were got wrong, roughly speaking. The normalised similarity divides a character-level distance by the length of the longer string: 1 means identical, 0 means nothing in common.

# ---------- 2. edit distance: the table, the backtrace, the alignment ----------
def edit_table(src, tgt, costs=(1, 1, 1), trans=False):
    """D[i][j] = minimum cost of turning src[:i] into tgt[:j]. costs = (insert, delete, substitute)."""
    ci, cd, cs = costs
    D = [[0] * (len(tgt) + 1) for _ in range(len(src) + 1)]
    for i in range(1, len(src) + 1): D[i][0] = i * cd                    # delete every source letter
    for j in range(1, len(tgt) + 1): D[0][j] = j * ci                    # insert every target letter
    for i in range(1, len(src) + 1):
        for j in range(1, len(tgt) + 1):
            best = min(D[i-1][j-1] + (0 if src[i-1] == tgt[j-1] else cs),  # match or substitute
                       D[i-1][j] + cd,                                       # delete src[i-1]
                       D[i][j-1] + ci)                                       # insert tgt[j-1]
            if trans and i > 1 and j > 1 and src[i-1] == tgt[j-2] and src[i-2] == tgt[j-1] and src[i-1] != src[i-2]:
                best = min(best, D[i-2][j-2] + 1)                            # swap two adjacent letters
            D[i][j] = best
    return D

def backtrace(src, tgt, D, costs=(1, 1, 1), trans=False):
    """Walk back from the last cell. Ties are broken in the order diagonal, transposition, deletion, insertion.
    Each op is (kind, source_part, target_part, i) with i the source position it acts at."""
    ci, cd, cs = costs
    i, j, ops = len(src), len(tgt), []
    while i > 0 or j > 0:
        if i > 0 and j > 0 and D[i][j] == D[i-1][j-1] + (0 if src[i-1] == tgt[j-1] else cs):
            ops.append(("match" if src[i-1] == tgt[j-1] else "sub", src[i-1], tgt[j-1], i - 1)); i, j = i - 1, j - 1
        elif trans and i > 1 and j > 1 and src[i-1] == tgt[j-2] and src[i-2] == tgt[j-1] and src[i-1] != src[i-2] and D[i][j] == D[i-2][j-2] + 1:
            ops.append(("trans", src[i-2:i], tgt[j-2:j], i - 2)); i, j = i - 2, j - 2
        elif i > 0 and D[i][j] == D[i-1][j] + cd:
            ops.append(("del", src[i-1], "", i - 1)); i -= 1
        else:
            ops.append(("ins", "", tgt[j-1], i)); j -= 1
    return ops[::-1]

def distance(src, tgt, costs=(1, 1, 1), trans=False):
    return edit_table(src, tgt, costs, trans)[len(src)][len(tgt)]

def damerau_levenshtein(a, b):
    """Unrestricted Damerau-Levenshtein: a transposed pair may be edited again afterwards."""
    da, INF = {}, len(a) + len(b)
    H = [[INF] * (len(b) + 2) for _ in range(len(a) + 2)]
    H[1][1] = 0
    for i in range(1, len(a) + 1): H[i+1][1] = i
    for j in range(1, len(b) + 1): H[1][j+1] = j
    for i in range(1, len(a) + 1):
        db = 0
        for j in range(1, len(b) + 1):
            k, l, cost = da.get(b[j-1], 0), db, (0 if a[i-1] == b[j-1] else 1)
            if cost == 0: db = j
            H[i+1][j+1] = min(H[i][j] + cost, H[i+1][j] + 1, H[i][j+1] + 1, H[k][l] + (i - k - 1) + 1 + (j - l - 1))
        da[a[i-1]] = i
    return H[len(a)+1][len(b)+1]

def show_alignment(ops):
    rows = ["", "", ""]
    for kind, s, t, _ in ops:
        if kind == "trans": rows[0] += s; rows[1] += "><"; rows[2] += t
        elif kind == "del":  rows[0] += s;  rows[1] += "d";  rows[2] += "*"
        elif kind == "ins":  rows[0] += "*"; rows[1] += "i";  rows[2] += t
        else: rows[0] += s; rows[1] += (" " if kind == "match" else "s"); rows[2] += t
    return "\n".join(rows)

def edits1(word):
    """Every string one edit away (Norvig's enumeration): at most 54n + 25 of them."""
    splits = [(word[:i], word[i:]) for i in range(len(word) + 1)]
    dele = [a + b[1:] for a, b in splits if b]
    swap = [a + b[1] + b[0] + b[2:] for a, b in splits if len(b) > 1]
    repl = [a + c + b[1:] for a, b in splits if b for c in ALPHABET]
    inse = [a + c + b for a, b in splits for c in ALPHABET]
    return set(dele + swap + repl + inse)

def tokens(text): return re.findall(r"[a-z]+", text.lower())

UNI, BI = Counter(), Counter()
for s in SENTS:
    UNI.update(s + ["</s>"])
    for a, b in zip(["<s>"] + s, s + ["</s>"]): BI[(a, b)] += 1

def p_lm(w, prev, lam=0.6):
    """Interpolated bigram: lam * ML bigram + (1 - lam) * add-one unigram (unknown words share one slot)."""
    big = BI[(prev, w)] / CTX[prev] if CTX[prev] else 0.0
    uni = (UNI[w] + 1) / (N + V + 1)
    return lam * big + (1 - lam) * uni

# ---------- 4. the channel model: confusion counts from the error pairs ----------

    for a in s: C1[a] += n
    for a, b in zip(s, s[1:]): C2[a + b] += n

        elif kind == "ins":  INS[(prev, t)] += 1              # after intended prev, typed an extra t
        elif kind == "trans": TRA[s] += 1                     # meant s, typed it reversed

def p_edit(kind, s, t, prev, lam=0.5):
    """Probability of one edit, add-lambda smoothed, with Kernighan, Church & Gale's denominators."""
    if kind == "sub":   return (SUB[(t, s)] + lam) / (C1[s] + lam * SYMBOLS)
    if kind == "del":   return (DEL[prev + s] + lam) / (C2[prev + s] + lam * SYMBOLS)
    if kind == "ins":   return (INS[(prev, t)] + lam) / (C1[prev] + lam * SYMBOLS)
    if kind == "trans": return (TRA[s] + lam) / (C2[s] + lam * SYMBOLS)
    return 1.0

def p_channel(x, w, alpha=0.95, lam=0.5, hom=0.02):
    """P(typed x | intended w): alpha if x == w; else the product of the edits that turn w into x;
    for a homophone the fixed probability hom if that is larger."""
    if x == w: return alpha
    p = 1.0
    for kind, s, t, i in backtrace(w, x, edit_table(w, x, trans=True), trans=True):
        if kind != "match": p *= p_edit(kind, s, t, w[i-1] if i > 0 else "#", lam)
    return max(p, hom) if x in HOMO.get(w, ()) else p

# ---------- 5. candidates and the argmax ----------
def candidates(x, maxd=2):
    """Every vocabulary word within OSA edit distance maxd of x, plus x's homophones."""
    out = {v for v in VOCAB if abs(len(v) - len(x)) <= maxd and distance(x, v, trans=True) <= maxd}
    return sorted(out | (HOMO.get(x, set()) & VOCAB))

def correct_word(x, k=5, alpha=0.95, lam=0.5):
    """Non-word correction without context: rank candidates by P(x|w) P(w)."""
    rows = [(w, p_channel(x, w, alpha, lam), (UNI[w] + 1) / (N + V + 1)) for w in candidates(x) if w != x]
    rows.sort(key=lambda r: -r[1] * r[2])
    return rows[:k]

def correct_sentence(words, alpha=0.95, lam=0.5, lam_lm=0.6, hom=0.02):
    """Viterbi over per-word candidate sets, maximising sum of log P(c_i | c_i-1) + log P(x_i | c_i)."""
    cands = []
    for x in words:
        c = set(candidates(x, 1 if x in VOCAB else 2))
        if x in VOCAB or not c: c.add(x)                   # a known word is its own first candidate
        cands.append(sorted(c))
    ch = lambda x, c: math.log(p_channel(x, c, alpha, lam, hom))
    best = [{c: (math.log(p_lm(c, "<s>", lam_lm)) + ch(words[0], c), None) for c in cands[0]}]
    for i in range(1, len(words)):
        col = {}
        for c in cands[i]:
            prev, sc = max(((p, best[-1][p][0] + math.log(p_lm(c, p, lam_lm))) for p in cands[i-1]), key=lambda t: t[1])
            col[c] = (sc + ch(words[i], c), prev)
        best.append(col)
    last, _ = max(((c, best[-1][c][0] + math.log(p_lm("</s>", c, lam_lm))) for c in cands[-1]), key=lambda t: t[1])
    out = [last]
    for i in range(len(words) - 1, 0, -1): out.append(best[i][out[-1]][1])
    return out[::-1]

def wer(ref, hyp):
    """Word error rate: (substitutions + deletions + insertions) / reference length."""
    ops = backtrace(ref, hyp, edit_table(ref, hyp))
    s, d, i = (sum(1 for o in ops if o[0] == k) for k in ("sub", "del", "ins"))
    return (s + d + i) / len(ref), s, d, i

# ---------- 6. a seeded typo generator (the page's mulberry32, bit for bit) ----------
def mulberry32(a):
    def nxt():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return nxt

def neighbours(ch):
    for r, row in enumerate(KEYS):
        k = row.find(ch)
        if k >= 0:
            out = ([row[k-1]] if k > 0 else []) + ([row[k+1]] if k + 1 < len(row) else [])
            for rr in (r - 1, r + 1):
                if 0 <= rr < 3 and k < len(KEYS[rr]): out.append(KEYS[rr][k])
            return out
    return list(ALPHABET)

def corrupt(words, rnd, rate=0.3):
    out = []
    for w in words:
        if len(w) < 3 or rnd() >= rate: out.append(w); continue
        kind = int(rnd() * 4); k = int(rnd() * len(w))
        if kind == 0: nb = neighbours(w[k]); out.append(w[:k] + nb[int(rnd() * len(nb))] + w[k+1:])
        elif kind == 1: out.append(w[:k] + w[k+1:])
        elif kind == 2: out.append(w[:k] + ALPHABET[int(rnd() * 26)] + w[k:])
        else:
            k = min(k, len(w) - 2)
            out.append(w[:k] + w[k+1] + w[k] + w[k+2:] if w[k] != w[k+1] else w[:k] + w[k+1:])
    return out

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Minimum edit distance is the cheapest sequence of single-character insertions, deletions and substitutions turning one string into another. Searching edit sequences is exponential; the number of distinct sub-problems (prefix pairs) is only (n+1)(m+1).
  • The dynamic-programming table stores D[i][j], the distance between the prefixes, as the minimum of three candidates: above plus a deletion, left plus an insertion, diagonal plus zero or a substitution. Fill it in O(nm); the last cell is the answer.
  • Backtracing the winning candidates from the last cell gives an alignment; ties mean several equally good alignments. A fourth candidate two cells back along the diagonal adds Damerau’s transposition; replacing unit costs by −log probabilities from confusion matrices gives a weighted distance.
  • The noisy channel corrects spelling by Bayes’ rule: the best word maximises P(typed | meant) × P(meant) over candidates within one or two edits. The channel comes from edit confusion counts, the prior from word counts in a corpus.
  • Real-word errors need context: give every word a candidate set including itself (probability α), score sentences with a bigram model and decode with Viterbi. The same table scores DNA alignments (Needleman–Wunsch), speech recognisers (word error rate) and record matches in data cleaning.

Check your understanding

What is the Levenshtein distance between flaw and lawn, and what is the alignment?

2: delete f, then insert n (flaw → law → lawn). The three middle letters line up for free. With substitutions costing 2 the distance is still 2, since the cheapest alignment uses no substitution; a four-substitution alignment would cost 8.

Why does filling the table row by row give the optimum, when it never looks at whole paths?

Because the cheapest cost of reaching cell (i, j) depends only on the cheapest costs of its three predecessors, each of which is already final when (i, j) is computed. Any path to (i, j) must pass through one of them, so no path can beat the minimum over the three. This is Bellman’s principle of optimality applied to the lattice.

Two cells tie during the backtrace. Does the distance change depending on which you choose?

No. Every tied predecessor achieves the same value, so every path that follows ties reaches (0, 0) with the same total cost. What changes is the alignment: which letters are paired and where the gaps go. intention → execution has seven optimal alignments of cost 5.

Under the OSA distance, abc → ca costs 3, but the unrestricted Damerau–Levenshtein distance is 2. What is the two-edit route, and why can OSA not find it?

Delete b to get ac, then transpose to get ca. OSA’s transposition candidate only reaches back two cells along the diagonal, so a swapped pair cannot be touched again by another edit; the route above edits the pair (by removing the letter between its members) and then swaps it, which the restricted table cannot represent.

A typo x has two candidates: w1 with P(x | w1) = 0.3 and P(w1) = 0.001, and w2 with P(x | w2) = 0.002 and P(w2) = 0.05. Which wins, and why does P(x) not matter?

w1: 0.3 × 0.001 = 3 × 10−4 beats 0.002 × 0.05 = 1 × 10−4. A cheap edit beats a common word here, though a fifty-fold frequency advantage nearly closed the gap. P(x), the probability of the observed typo, divides both products equally and so cannot change their order.

In the real-word model, what happens to the number of corrections as α is raised towards 1, and what kind of error does each setting make?

Raising α makes the typed word harder to overturn, so fewer words are changed: missed real-word errors go up and false alarms go down. Lowering α does the reverse: more genuine errors are caught, but ordinary words whose neighbours happen to be rare in the corpus start being “corrected” into more common ones. The right value depends on how costly each kind of mistake is for the user.

A recogniser outputs 11 words for a 10-word reference, with 2 substitutions, 1 deletion and 2 insertions. What is the WER, and could it exceed 100%?

(2 + 1 + 2) / 10 = 50%. Yes: a hypothesis full of extra words can have more insertions than there are reference words, so WER is not bounded by 1.
