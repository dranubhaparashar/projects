Learning Undirected Models

Reading a network of direct links out of nothing but data

The last chapters drew the graph first and then reasoned with it. Here the graph is the thing we want to find. Give the computer a table of measurements and ask: which pairs of variables are linked directly, and which only look related because they both follow something else? For bell-shaped data the answer is hidden in the zeros of the inverse covariance matrix, and the graphical lasso digs them out. For yes/no data the same questions lead to the Ising model and to lasso logistic regressions. The figure shows 22 currencies over three years of trading days. Drag across the network to change the penalty λ and watch the direct links thin out, then switch to the strongest plain correlations to see which of them are direct links.

ESL 17 · 17.3

A zero in the precision matrix is a missing edge

Chapter 6.3 showed what an undirected graph promises: a missing edge between two nodes means they are conditionally independent given all the others. Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman devote the rest of their chapter on undirected models to the opposite job, learning such a graph from data. Two tasks hide in that phrase. If the edges are known, estimate the numbers that sit on them. If they are not known, estimate the edges themselves. This chapter does both, first for continuous variables and then for binary ones.

Continuous first. Suppose the p variables follow a multivariate Gaussian with covariance Σ. Its inverse Θ = Σ−1 is called the precision matrix (or concentration matrix), and it is the matrix that knows about the graph. If θjk = 0, then Xj and Xk are independent given everything else; if not, they are joined by an edge. The covariance matrix itself is usually full of non-zeros, because two variables can be strongly correlated without being directly linked: in a chain 1 – 2 – 3, variables 1 and 3 are correlated through 2.

The lab builds a four-variable Gaussian straight from its precision matrix: ones on the diagonal and minus the slider values off it, so each slider is exactly the partial correlation on its edge. Move the sliders and pick a pair, then look at both heat maps. The left one is what you would measure; the right one is what the graph says.

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x) &= \frac{(\det\Theta)^{1/2}}{(2\pi)^{p/2}} \\ &\quad\times e^{-\frac12 (x-\mu)^{\top}\Theta\,(x-\mu)} \\ \theta_{jk} = 0 &\iff X_j \perp\!\!\!\perp X_k \mid \text{rest} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the Gaussian density written with Θ = Σ−1 instead of Σ. In the exponent, the only terms that contain both xj and xk are θjk times (xj − μj)(xk − μk) (twice, by symmetry), so when θjk is zero the density splits into a part without xj and a part without xk once the other variables are fixed. That is conditional independence, and it is why the zeros of Θ are the missing edges.
Say it“The density is root det theta over two pi to the p over two, times e to minus a half x minus mu, theta, x minus mu; theta j k is zero exactly when X j and X k are independent given the rest.”
\[ \rho_{jk\,\cdot\,\text{rest}} = \frac{-\,\theta_{jk}}{\sqrt{\theta_{jj}\,\theta_{kk}}} \]
Read it as: the partial correlation of Xj and Xk: their correlation after the linear effect of all the other variables has been removed from both. Up to a sign and a rescaling it is the entry of Θ, so it is zero exactly when θjk is. The plain correlation Σjk/√(ΣjjΣkk) answers a different question: are they related by any route at all?
Say it“The partial correlation of j and k given the rest is minus theta j k over the square root of theta j j times theta k k.”

ESL 17.3 · the regression view

Every node is a regression on its neighbours

There is a second way to read Θ, and the fitting algorithms of this chapter are built on it. Pick one variable, call it Y = Xp, and call the others Z. In a Gaussian, the conditional distribution of Y given Z is again Gaussian, its mean is a linear function of Z, and its variance does not depend on Z at all. So predicting one node from the rest is plain linear regression, with no approximation.

Writing Σ and Θ in blocks (Z first, Y last) and using the formula for the inverse of a partitioned matrix, the regression coefficients turn out to be a column of Θ divided by its diagonal entry. A zero coefficient is therefore the same thing as a zero in Θ: Y’s best linear predictor uses its neighbours and only its neighbours. The lab draws 200 points from a six-node Gaussian (a ring with one chord) and regresses one node on the other five by least squares. Pick a node, then raise n: the coefficients on non-neighbours are zero in truth but never exactly zero in a sample, which is the problem the graphical lasso will solve.

\[ \begin{aligned} Y \mid Z = z &\sim N(m,\, \sigma^2) \\ m &= \mu_Y + (z-\mu_Z)^{\top}\beta \\ \sigma^2 &= 1/\theta_{YY} \\ \beta &= \Sigma_{ZZ}^{-1}\,\sigma_{ZY} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ΣZZ is the covariance among the predictors and σZY their covariances with Y, so β is the population least-squares coefficient vector. The residual variance σYY − σZY⊤ΣZZ−1σZY equals 1/θYY: a large diagonal entry of Θ means Y is well predicted by the rest.
Say it“Given Z, Y is normal with mean mu Y plus z minus mu Z times beta and variance one over theta Y Y; beta is sigma Z Z inverse times sigma Z Y.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \beta &= -\,\frac{\theta_{ZY}}{\theta_{YY}} \\ \text{so}\quad \beta_k = 0 &\iff \theta_{kY} = 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: θZY is Y’s column of Θ without the diagonal entry. Dividing it by −θYY gives the regression coefficients. The same identity holds for a sample: least squares on the data equals −(S−1)kY/(S−1)YY, with S the sample covariance.
Say it“Beta is minus theta Z Y over theta Y Y, so a coefficient is zero exactly when the matching entry of theta is.”

ESL 17.3.1

Fitting Θ when the graph is known

Suppose someone hands you the graph, and you must fit a Gaussian whose precision matrix is zero on every missing edge. With N observations and sample covariance S, the log-likelihood is, up to constants and a factor N/2, log det Θ − trace(SΘ). Without constraints the maximum is Θ = S−1, which has no zeros at all. With the zeros imposed, Dempster called the problem covariance selection.

Setting the gradient to zero gives a striking answer: the fitted covariance W = Θ−1 must agree with S on the diagonal and on every edge, while its entries on missing edges are free to be whatever makes Θ zero there. Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman solve it with a modified regression (their Algorithm 17.1): visit one column at a time, regress that variable on its neighbours only, using the current W in place of S for the predictors, and write the fitted covariances back into W. Step through the columns one by one, then change the graph and run it again. The data are the 200 points of the last lab.

\[ \begin{aligned} \max_{\Theta \succ 0}\;\; &\log\det\Theta - \operatorname{tr}(S\Theta) \\ &\text{with } \theta_{jk} = 0 \text{ for } (j,k) \notin E \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Θ ≻ 0 means positive definite. E is the set of edges. This is the Gaussian log-likelihood per observation (times 2, without constants), maximised over precision matrices with the required zeros. It is a concave problem, so there is one maximum and no local traps.
Say it“Maximise log det theta minus the trace of S theta, keeping theta zero on every missing edge.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &W - S - \Gamma = 0,\quad W = \Theta^{-1} \\ &\Rightarrow\; w_{jk} = s_{jk} \text{ for } j = k \\ &\qquad\text{and for } (j,k) \in E \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Γ holds one Lagrange multiplier for each missing edge and zeros elsewhere. So wherever the model is free (diagonal and edges), the fitted covariance must reproduce the sample covariance exactly. This is moment matching; the binary models of section 7 obey the same rule.
Say it“W minus S minus gamma is zero, so W equals S on the diagonal and on every edge.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &W^{*}_{11}\,\beta^{*} = s^{*}_{12} \\ &w_{12} \leftarrow W_{11}\,\beta \\ &\text{at the end:} \\ &\hat\theta_{22} = 1/(w_{22} - w_{12}^{\top}\beta) \\ &\hat\theta_{12} = -\hat\theta_{22}\,\beta \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for column j, W11 is W without row and column j and w12 is column j without its diagonal entry. The starred versions keep only the rows and columns of j’s neighbours, so β* is a small least-squares solve; β is β* padded with zeros for the non-neighbours. The update writes W11β into column j. Repeat over all columns until nothing changes, then read Θ̂ off the final βs.
Say it“Solve W star one one beta star equals s star one two, set w one two to W one one beta, and at the end theta two two is one over w two two minus w one two dot beta.”

ESL 17.3.2 · part 1

The graphical lasso

Usually nobody hands you the graph. The idea of the graphical lasso (Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani, 2008; with related work by Yuan and Lin and by Banerjee, El Ghaoui and d’Aspremont) is to add an L1 penalty to the log-likelihood. Just as the lasso sets regression coefficients exactly to zero, the penalty sets entries of Θ exactly to zero, and the zeros are the missing edges. One knob, λ, moves you from the full graph (λ = 0, Θ = S−1) to the empty one.

The algorithm reuses the column-by-column scheme of the last section. The gradient condition for one column is exactly the condition for a lasso regression, with the current W11 playing the part of the predictors’ covariance; each lasso is solved by cycling through its coefficients with the soft-threshold rule below. Here the truth is known: ten variables on a ring with two chords, twelve edges in all. Slide λ and compare the estimate with the truth, then try a smaller and a larger sample.

\[ \begin{aligned} \max_{\Theta \succ 0}\;\; &\log\det\Theta - \operatorname{tr}(S\Theta) \\ &- \lambda \textstyle\sum_{j \ne k} |\theta_{jk}| \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the log-likelihood of the last section minus λ times the sum of absolute off-diagonal entries. Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman write the penalty as λ‖Θ‖1, over all entries; penalising the diagonal too is the same as replacing S by S + λI, and the lab can do either. The problem is still concave.
Say it“Maximise log det theta minus trace S theta minus lambda times the sum of the absolute off-diagonal entries.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &W - S - \lambda\,\operatorname{Sign}(\Theta) = 0 \\ &\Rightarrow\; W_{11}\beta - s_{12} + \lambda\,\operatorname{Sign}(\beta) = 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Sign(θ) is +1 or −1 for a non-zero entry and any value in [−1, 1] for a zero one. So |wjk − sjk| = λ exactly on every edge that survives, and at most λ on every missing one. Column by column, with β = −θ12/θ22, this is the optimality condition of a lasso.
Say it“W minus S minus lambda sign theta is zero; for one column, W one one beta minus s one two plus lambda sign beta is zero: a lasso.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \beta_k &\leftarrow \mathcal{S}(r_k,\, \lambda)\,/\,V_{kk} \\ r_k &= s_{12,k} - \textstyle\sum_{c \ne k} V_{kc}\,\beta_c \\ \mathcal{S}(x, t) &= \operatorname{sign}(x)\,\big(|x| - t\big)_{+} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: one coordinate-descent step of the lasso inside column j. The numerator takes the covariance of variable k with the target, removes what the other coefficients already explain, and soft-thresholds it: anything smaller than λ in size becomes exactly zero. When every column has settled, Θ̂ is recovered as in Algorithm 17.1.
Say it“Beta k becomes the soft threshold of s one two k minus the sum over the other coefficients, at lambda, divided by V k k.”

ESL 17.3.2 · part 2

The λ path, a shortcut, and choosing λ

Running the graphical lasso over a grid of penalties traces a path: every partial correlation starts at its unpenalised value on the left and shrinks, one by one, to exactly zero on the right. Reading the order in which edges die is often more useful than any single graph. Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman show such a path for eleven proteins measured in thousands of cells.

Before the graphical lasso, Meinshausen and Bühlmann proposed a shortcut: run an ordinary lasso of each variable on all the others and call j and k neighbours if either coefficient is non-zero (the or rule) or if both are (the and rule). In the notation above, that is one pass of the inner lasso with W11 = S11 instead of the current W11, so it does not maximise any likelihood, but it is fast and often finds much the same graph. To pick λ, one honest recipe is cross-validation: fit on four fifths of the data and score the Gaussian log-likelihood on the fifth that was held out. Drag the vertical line along the path and switch between the three rules. The data are the sample of the last lab.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat b^{(j)} &= \arg\min_{b}\; \tfrac{1}{2N}\big\Vert x_j - X_{-j}\, b \big\Vert^2 \\ &\qquad + \lambda \Vert b \Vert_1 \\ \text{and: } & \hat b^{(j)}_k \ne 0 \text{ and } \hat b^{(k)}_j \ne 0 \\ \text{or: } & \hat b^{(j)}_k \ne 0 \text{ or } \hat b^{(k)}_j \ne 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: xj is the column of observations of variable j and X−j holds all the other columns. Each variable gets its own lasso; the two coefficients that concern the pair (j, k) may disagree, so a rule is needed to turn them into one edge.
Say it“Regress each variable on all the others with a lasso; join j and k if both coefficients survive, or if either does.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{CV}(\lambda) &= \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \ell_k(\lambda) \\ \ell_k(\lambda) &= \log\det\hat\Theta_{-k} - \operatorname{tr}\big(S_k\,\hat\Theta_{-k}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Θ̂−k(λ) is fitted without fold k; Sk is the covariance of fold k (centred at the training means). Each ℓk is the held-out Gaussian log-likelihood per observation, up to constants and a factor ½. Choose the λ with the largest average. Here K = 5 contiguous folds.
Say it“C V of lambda is the average over folds of log det theta hat minus the trace of the held-out S times theta hat.”

ESL 17.4

Binary nodes: the Ising model, or Boltzmann machine

Now let every variable be 0 or 1: a currency rose or it did not, a gene is on or off, a voter said yes or no. The pairwise undirected model for such data is the Ising model of statistical physics, known in machine learning as the Boltzmann machine. It gives each configuration a score that adds θjk for every edge whose two ends are both 1, plus a bias θj0 for every node that is 1, and normalises with the log partition function Φ. (Chapter 6.3 used ±1 spins; the two codings describe the same family, with re-arranged parameters.)

Two facts make the model easy to read. First, the conditional distribution of one node given all the others is a logistic regression on its neighbours, the binary twin of the Gaussian regression in section 2. Second, θjk is a conditional log odds ratio: how much more likely j and k are to agree, given the rest, than chance would make them. Move the couplings and the conditioning value of X3, and watch the log odds ratio stay pinned to θ12.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(x;\Theta) &= e^{-\Phi(\Theta)} \exp\Big( \sum_{E} \theta_{jk}\, x_j x_k \Big) \\ \Phi(\Theta) &= \log \sum_{x} \exp\Big( \sum_{E} \theta_{jk}\, x_j x_k \Big) \\ x_0 &\equiv 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the edge set E also contains a pair (j, 0) for every node, joining it to a constant node x0 = 1, so θj0 is node j’s bias. Φ is the log of the sum of all 2p unnormalised scores; exp(−Φ) is the 1/Z of chapter 6.3.
Say it“p of x is e to the sum over edges of theta j k x j x k, minus Phi; Phi is the log of that sum of exponentials over every x.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &P(X_j = 1 \mid x_{-j}) \\ &\quad= \sigma\Big(\theta_{j0} + \sum_{k \ne j} \theta_{jk}\, x_k\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: σ(t) = 1/(1 + e−t). The other nodes act through a weighted sum, and only the neighbours have non-zero weights. Everything that does not involve xj cancels between numerator and denominator, Φ included.
Say it“The chance that X j is one given the rest is the logistic of its bias plus the sum of theta j k x k.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \theta_{12} &= \log \frac{p_{11}\; p_{00}}{p_{10}\; p_{01}} \\ p_{ab} &= p(a, b \mid x_3) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: p(a, b | x3) is the probability that X1 = a and X2 = b given X3. The ratio compares agreeing pairs with disagreeing ones. Everything except the X1X2 term cancels, so the answer is θ12 whatever x3 is.
Say it“Theta one two is the log of p one one times p zero zero over p one zero times p zero one, given x three.”

ESL 17.4.1

Fitting with a known graph: match the moments

Fitting a Boltzmann machine to N observations follows the Gaussian story closely. The log-likelihood depends on the data only through the average products xjxk on the edges (and the node averages), and its gradient is the data’s average minus the model’s expectation of the same product. At the maximum the two agree on every edge: moment matching again. The function is concave, so plain gradient ascent gets there. Newton’s method and iterative proportional fitting are faster alternatives, and all of them need the model expectations EΘ(XjXk) at every step.

That is the expensive part. Exact expectations sum over all 2p configurations, which is easy for six nodes and hopeless for sixty; for sparse graphs the junction tree algorithm of chapter 6.6 can do better. Otherwise they are estimated, for instance by Gibbs sampling, or approximated by mean-field methods. The data here are real: whether each of six currencies rose against the US dollar on each of 749 trading days. Pick a graph, press Fit, then switch to Gibbs estimates to see what a noisy gradient does.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell(\Theta) &= \sum_{(j,k) \in E} \theta_{jk}\, \hat e_{jk} - \Phi(\Theta) \\ \hat e_{jk} &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_{ij}\, x_{ik} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the average log-likelihood per observation. As before, E includes the bias pairs (j, 0), for which êj0 is just the fraction of days on which node j is 1. Only these averages matter: two data sets with the same ês give the same fit.
Say it“l of theta is the sum over edges of theta j k times the data average of x j x k, minus Phi.”
\[ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta_{jk}} = \hat e_{jk} - E_{\Theta}(X_j X_k) \]
Read it as: the derivative of Φ is the model’s expectation of the matching product, so each step raises θjk when the data show the pair “both 1” more often than the model expects, and lowers it otherwise. At the maximum, every gap is zero.
Say it“The gradient for theta j k is the data average of x j x k minus the model’s expectation of it.”

ESL 17.4.2

Hidden nodes

A Boltzmann machine may contain nodes that are never observed. They buy flexibility: the visible variables can be tied together through a shared hidden cause instead of through edges of their own, and with enough hidden nodes the family can approximate any distribution on the visible ones. The price is that the log-likelihood is no longer concave and its gradient needs two expectations instead of one.

The first is the model’s expectation, exactly as before. The second is an average, over the observed rows, of the expectation given that row: the visible nodes are clamped to their data and only the hidden ones are averaged over. With sampling, that means two Gibbs runs per gradient step, one clamped and one free, which is why large Boltzmann machines were so slow to train and why the restricted version of chapter 6.23, with no edges inside a layer, was such a relief. The lab joins one hidden node H to four currencies (EUR, GBP, SEK and AUD) and gives them no edges among themselves. Train it and compare its fit with the models that have no hidden node.

\[ \ell(\Theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \sum_{x_{\mathcal H}} p\big(x_{i\mathcal V},\, x_{\mathcal H};\, \Theta\big) \]
Read it as: 𝒱 is the set of visible nodes and ℋ the hidden ones. The probability of an observed row is the joint probability summed over every value of the hidden nodes. The log of a sum does not split into simple terms, which is what costs us concavity.
Say it“l of theta is the average over rows of the log of the joint probability summed over the hidden nodes.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta_{jk}} &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} E_{\Theta}\big(X_j X_k \mid x_{i\mathcal V}\big) \\ &\quad - E_{\Theta}(X_j X_k) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: if both j and k are visible, the clamped expectation is just xijxik and we are back to the data average. If one of them is hidden, it is the observed value times the posterior probability that the hidden node is 1 for that row.
Say it“The gradient is the clamped expectation of x j x k averaged over rows, minus the free expectation.”

ESL 17.4.3

Learning the graph of a binary model

For the structure of a binary model, the natural first idea copies the graphical lasso: maximise ℓ(Θ) − λ‖Θ‖1. Lee, Ganapathi and Koller did exactly that, but every gradient step still needs EΘ(XjXk), and the junction tree or a sampler has to supply it. There is no column trick that hides Φ.

Wainwright, Ravikumar and Lafferty took the Meinshausen–Bühlmann route instead. The conditional of each node given the rest is a logistic regression on its neighbours, and a logistic regression needs no Φ. So fit one L1-penalised logistic regression per node and keep a pair if both its coefficients survive, or if either does. Each node gives its own estimate of θjk, and they can be averaged when numbers are wanted. Under conditions on the graph and with enough data this recovers the true edges with high probability; Höfling and Tibshirani later turned the same idea into a single joint pseudo-likelihood fit. The lab samples data exactly from a known eight-node model (ten edges, two of them negative). Change n and λ, and click a node to see its own lasso.

\[ \begin{aligned} \min_{\theta_{j\cdot}}\; &-\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log P\big(x_{ij} \mid x_{i,-j};\, \theta_{j\cdot}\big) \\ &+ \lambda \sum_{k \ne j} |\theta_{jk}| \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: θj· is node j’s bias and its p − 1 weights; the conditional P is the logistic model of section 6. The bias is not penalised. Each node is fitted separately, so p small convex problems replace one big hard one.
Say it“For each node, minimise the average negative log conditional likelihood plus lambda times the sum of the absolute weights.”
\[ \begin{aligned} w_k &\leftarrow \mathcal{S}\big(h_k w_k + g_k,\; \lambda\big)\,/\,h_k \\ g_k &= \tfrac1N \textstyle\sum_i (x_{ij} - \hat p_i)\, x_{ik} \\ h_k &= \tfrac{1}{4N} \textstyle\sum_i x_{ik}^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the coordinate update this page uses. gk is minus the gradient for weight k at the current fitted probabilities p̂i, and hk bounds the curvature, because p(1 − p) never exceeds ¼. Minimising that quadratic bound plus the penalty is a soft threshold, so every step can only lower the objective.
Say it“w k becomes the soft threshold of h k w k plus g k, at lambda, over h k.”

Project: a network of 22 currencies

The data are the Federal Reserve Board’s H.10 noon buying rates in New York for 22 currencies, downloaded from FRED (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis), which marks them Public Domain: Citation Requested. The series are listed below with their FRED identifiers and names; the three-letter labels are the ISO 4217 codes. From 3 January 2023 to 31 December 2025 there are 750 days on which all 22 rates were published. Each rate was turned into the value of one unit of the currency in US dollars, and then into 749 daily log returns. The project idea suggested stock prices; free daily stock series with a clear licence are hard to find, while these rates are public domain, so the network here links currencies instead.

Every currency is quoted against the same US dollar, so on a day when the dollar strengthens most of them fall together. The plain correlations are therefore large almost everywhere (the average absolute correlation is 0.39), and 147 of the 231 pairs have a correlation above 0.3 in size: a network of strong correlations is a hairball. The graphical lasso asks a sharper question: after accounting for all the other 20 currencies, which two still move together? The returns are standardised, so S is the correlation matrix. Move λ, switch the view, and compare each number with the Python column.

\[ \begin{aligned} r_{tj} &= \log v_{tj} - \log v_{t-1,j} \\ z_{tj} &= (r_{tj} - \bar r_j)\,/\,\mathrm{sd}(r_j) \\ S &= \tfrac{1}{N} Z^{\top} Z \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: vtj is the dollar value of currency j on day t, rtj its log return (about the percentage change, divided by 100), ztj the return standardised to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 (with divisor N), and S, with N = 749, the 22 × 22 correlation matrix that the graphical lasso receives.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • In a Gaussian graphical model the graph lives in the precision matrix Θ = Σ−1: θjk = 0 means no edge, the partial correlation is −θjk/√(θjjθkk), and regressing any node on the rest gives coefficients −θkY/θYY, zero off its neighbours.
  • With the graph known, maximise log det Θ − tr(SΘ) with zeros on the missing edges. The answer matches S on the diagonal and every edge, and the modified regression (Algorithm 17.1) finds it column by column.
  • With the graph unknown, the graphical lasso adds λ‖Θ‖1; each column becomes a lasso solved by soft-thresholding. Meinshausen–Bühlmann regressions are a fast approximation with an and/or rule; cross-validation chooses λ for prediction and tends to keep many edges.
  • Binary pairwise models (Ising, Boltzmann machines) have logistic conditionals and log odds ratios θjk. Their likelihood gradient is data average minus model expectation; the expectations need 2p terms, a junction tree, Gibbs sampling or mean field. Hidden nodes add a clamped expectation and lose concavity.
  • On 22 currencies the graphical lasso turned a dense correlation matrix into a readable network (53 edges at λ = 0.509, strongest EUR–DKK and AUD–NZD), matching scikit-learn; lasso logistic regressions on up/down days found a graph that shares 42 of its 62 edges with the Gaussian one.

Check your understanding

Two variables have correlation 0.4 but partial correlation 0. What does the graph look like between them?

There is no edge between them. They are related only through other variables, for example as the two ends of a chain j – m – k, so once the rest is known neither tells you anything more about the other. Section 1’s chain with partial correlations 0.5 gives exactly this: corr(X1, X3) = 0.41, partial correlation 0.

Why does the fitted covariance W agree with S on the edges but not on the missing edges?

The gradient of log det Θ − tr(SΘ) with respect to θjk is wjk − sjk. Where θjk is free (diagonal and edges) the maximum sets that gradient to zero. Where it is forced to zero, a Lagrange multiplier absorbs the gradient, and wjk takes whatever value the zero in Θ implies.

What exactly does the Meinshausen–Bühlmann approach skip, compared with the graphical lasso?

It solves each column’s lasso once with W11 = S11, the sample covariance of the predictors. The graphical lasso solves the same lassos with the current fitted W11 and repeats until the columns agree, which yields a single positive definite Θ̂ that maximises a penalised likelihood. The shortcut gives two possibly different answers per pair, hence the and/or rules.

Why is fitting a Boltzmann machine with p = 60 nodes hard even when the graph is known?

The gradient needs EΘ(XjXk), a sum over 260 configurations. Unless the graph is thin enough for the junction tree, those expectations have to be estimated by Gibbs sampling or approximated (mean field), so the gradient is noisy or biased. The Gaussian case avoids this because the normalising constant has the closed form (det Θ)1/2.

Why can a single hidden node explain most of the dependence among four currencies?

All four are quoted against the US dollar, so much of their day-to-day co-movement is shared. A hidden node joined to all of them can switch on for the days when they move up together and off when they move down, and that alone lifts the fit from −2.772 (independence) to −2.224 per day, close to the −2.204 of the model with all six direct edges. The data cannot say what the hidden node “is”; it is only a shared cause that fits.
