Stable Off‑policy Learning

Gradient-TD, Emphatic-TD and the geometry of the Bellman error: learning about one policy from another’s data without blowing up

You would like to learn how good a policy is from data that a different policy collected: yesterday’s logs, a cautious exploration policy, a human demonstrator. With a lookup table that is safe. With a function approximator and bootstrapping, the obvious algorithm can run off to infinity even when the right answer is easy to represent. The figure is the classic proof: seven states, eight weights, true value zero everywhere, and plain semi-gradient TD diverges. Pick an algorithm under the figure and watch the weights either explode or settle.

The setting · 11.1

Off-policy TD with a function approximator

Two policies are in play. The target policy π is the one we want to evaluate; the behaviour policy b is the one that actually chose the actions in the data. In the tabular chapters we fixed the mismatch with an importance-sampling ratio ρt: how much more (or less) likely π was to take the logged action than b was. Carrying that over to a linear approximator is a one-line change: multiply the usual semi-gradient TD update by ρt. An action the target would never take gets ρ = 0 and teaches nothing; an action the target likes more than the behaviour gets a bigger step.

That fixes the target of each update, but not the distribution of updates. On-policy, states are updated as often as the policy visits them, and that is what keeps semi-gradient TD stable. Off-policy, the states are visited as b visits them, and the ratios alone do not undo that. The smallest example shows why: two states whose estimated values are w and 2w (features 1 and 2, one shared weight), with a zero-reward transition from the first to the second. Push the discount past one half and compare updating only that transition, as an off-policy learner might, with updating every transition of the episode.

\[ \begin{aligned} \rho_t &\doteq \frac{\pi(A_t\mid S_t)}{b(A_t\mid S_t)} \\ \mathbf w_{t+1} &= \mathbf w_t + \alpha\,\rho_t\,\delta_t\,\nabla\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w_t) \\ \delta_t &= R_{t+1} + \gamma\,\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf w_t) - \hat v(S_t,\mathbf w_t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ρt re-weights the step so that, on average, it points where an on-policy update for π would. δt is the ordinary TD error and ∇v̂ is the feature vector x(St) for a linear approximator. It is called semi-gradient because the bootstrapped target v̂(St+1, w) is treated as a constant when differentiating. For action values, one-step semi-gradient Expected Sarsa needs no ratio at all: its target averages q̂(St+1, a) over the target’s actions, and the action At being judged was given. Multi-step versions do need ratios, one per step after the first (n-step Sarsa) or per step (n-step TD); n-step tree backup avoids them by averaging over the target policy at every step.
Say it“Rho is pi over b of the action taken. The weights move by alpha times rho times the TD error times the gradient of the estimate.”
\[ \begin{aligned} w_{k+1} &= w_k + \alpha\big(0 + \gamma\, 2w_k - w_k\big)\cdot 1 \\ &= \big(1 + \alpha(2\gamma - 1)\big)\, w_k \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the update for the transition from the w-state to the 2w-state. The TD error is γ·2w − w and the gradient (the feature of the first state) is 1. When γ > ½ the factor is above 1: raising w raises the target 2w even faster, so the learner chases its own tail. On-policy, the episode continues from the 2w-state to the end with reward 0, and that update multiplies w by (1 − 4α), which pulls it back.
Say it“Each off-policy update multiplies w by one plus alpha times two gamma minus one.”

The classic failure

Baird’s counterexample and the deadly triad

Leemon Baird’s 1995 example is the one every off-policy method has to survive. There are seven states and two actions. The dashed action jumps to one of the six upper states at random; the solid action goes to the seventh, lower state. The behaviour policy picks dashed with probability 6/7 and solid with 1/7, so the next state is uniform over all seven and every state is visited equally. The target policy always picks solid. Every reward is zero and γ = 0.99, so the true value is zero everywhere, and the linear approximator can represent it exactly (w = 0). Its feature vectors are even linearly independent. Nothing about the problem looks hard.

Yet semi-gradient TD diverges for any positive step size, and so does its expected version, the dynamic-programming sweep that replaces each sample by its exact average. So this is not noise: the expected update itself points the wrong way. Sutton and Barto name the cause the deadly triad: function approximation, bootstrapping and off-policy training. Any two are safe; all three together can diverge. Remove one leg of the triad at a time with the switches and watch the weights settle.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat v(i) &= 2w_i + w_8 \quad (i = 1,\dots,6) \\ \hat v(7) &= w_7 + 2w_8 \\ \mathbf w_{k+1} &= \mathbf w_k + \frac{\alpha}{|\mathcal S|}\sum_{s}\bar\delta_k(s)\,\nabla\hat v(s,\mathbf w_k) \\ \bar\delta_k(s) &= \mathbb E_\pi\big[R_{t+1} + \gamma\,\hat v(S_{t+1})\,\big|\,s\big] - \hat v(s) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the six upper states each own a weight and share w8; the lower state uses w7 and twice w8. The third line is semi-gradient DP: every state, weighted equally (the behaviour policy’s uniform distribution), moves toward its exact one-step target under π. Under π every state goes to state 7 with reward 0, so the target is just γv̂(7), and the fourth line is the expected TD error δ̄k(s) = γv̂(7) − v̂(s). The weights start at (1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 10, 1) as in the textbook.
Say it“Each sweep moves the weights by alpha over the number of states, times the sum over states of the expected TD error times the state’s gradient.”

Geometry · 11.4

Linear value-function geometry

To see what TD is really solving, stop thinking of a value function as a table and think of it as a point: with three states, a value function is a vector in three dimensions, one coordinate per state. A linear approximator with two features can only reach a plane through the origin, the span of the feature columns; each weight vector w names one point vw = Xw on it. The true value vπ usually lies off the plane. Distances are measured with a weighting μ(s) over states (how much we care about each), and the closest point on the plane to any v is its projection Πv.

The Bellman operator Bπ does one exact step of look-ahead. vπ is its fixed point, but applied to a point on the plane it usually lands off the plane; the gap is the Bellman error. That gives three different “best” weight vectors: the one closest to vπ (minimum VE), the one with the smallest Bellman error (minimum BE), and the one whose Bellman error projects to zero (the TD fixed point, where PBE = 0, which is where linear semi-gradient TD converges when it converges). Drag the weight point on the left, switch between the three error maps, then make μ more and more off-policy and iterate ΠB.

\[ \begin{aligned} \Vert v\Vert_\mu^2 &\doteq \sum_s \mu(s)\, v(s)^2 \\ \overline{\text{VE}}(\mathbf w) &= \Vert v_{\mathbf w} - v_\pi\Vert_\mu^2 \\ \Pi &= X\big(X^{\top} D X\big)^{-1} X^{\top} D \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: X is the |S| × d feature matrix (row s is x(s)T), D is diagonal with μ(s) on it. VE is the μ-weighted squared distance to the truth. Π maps any value vector to the closest representable one in the μ-norm: weighted least squares.
Say it“The value error is the mu-norm squared of v-w minus v-pi; pi-projection is X, X-transpose D X inverse, X-transpose D.”
\[ \begin{aligned} (B_\pi v)(s) &\doteq \sum_a \pi(a\mid s) \\ &\quad\times\sum_{s',r} p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r + \gamma v(s')\big] \\ \bar\delta_{\mathbf w} &= B_\pi v_{\mathbf w} - v_{\mathbf w} \\ \overline{\text{BE}}(\mathbf w) &= \Vert\bar\delta_{\mathbf w}\Vert_\mu^2 \\ \overline{\text{PBE}}(\mathbf w) &= \Vert\Pi\bar\delta_{\mathbf w}\Vert_\mu^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Bπv is the expected one-step return using v for the next state; δ̄w is the expected TD error in every state at once, the Bellman error vector. BE measures it whole; PBE measures only the part that lies in the plane, the part the approximator could do something about.
Say it“Delta-bar is B-pi of v-w minus v-w. BE is its squared mu-norm; PBE is the squared mu-norm of its projection.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \overline{\text{PBE}}(\mathbf w_{TD}) = 0 &\iff X^{\top} D\,\bar\delta_{\mathbf w_{TD}} = \mathbf 0 \\ &\iff \mathbf w_{TD} = A^{-1}\mathbf b \\ A = X^{\top} D (I - \gamma P_\pi) X&, \quad \mathbf b = X^{\top} D\, \mathbf r_\pi \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Pπ is the state-to-state transition matrix under π and rπ the expected reward from each state, so δ̄w = rπ + γPπXw − Xw. The TD fixed point makes the Bellman error orthogonal to every feature. Iterating v ← ΠBπv converges to it when ΠBπ is a contraction, which is guaranteed when μ is the on-policy distribution. Off-policy, it need not be.
Say it“The TD fixed point is X-transpose D times I minus gamma P times X, inverted, times X-transpose D r.”

Objectives · 11.5

Gradient descent in the Bellman error

Stochastic gradient descent is stable because it always goes downhill on some objective. Semi-gradient TD is not the gradient of anything, which is why it can wander off. So the natural move is to pick an objective built from TD errors and do true gradient descent on it. The first candidate is the mean squared TD error (TDE). Its gradient includes the next state’s gradient too, giving the naive residual-gradient algorithm. It is stable, and it converges to the wrong answer: it prefers values that make every individual TD error small, which is not the same as values that are right.

The textbook’s A-split example shows it: from A you go to B or C with equal chance (reward 0); B then ends with reward 1, C with reward 0; γ = 1. The true values are A = ½, B = 1, C = 0. Naive residual gradient settles at B = ¾, C = ¼: it shares A’s unavoidable surprise across both steps. The second candidate is the Bellman error itself. Its gradient multiplies two expectations over the next state, so an unbiased sample needs two independent next states from the same state (“double sampling”), which only a simulator or a deterministic world can give. That is the residual-gradient algorithm. Run all three learners on A-split, then switch to A-presplit, where A is secretly two states that the features cannot tell apart.

\[ \begin{aligned} \overline{\text{TDE}}(\mathbf w) &= \sum_s \mu(s)\,\mathbb E\big[\delta_t^2 \mid S_t = s, A_t \sim \pi\big] \\ &= \mathbb E_b\big[\rho_t\,\delta_t^2\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the average squared TD error you would see following π, re-weighted from the behaviour data by ρ. Because it is a plain expectation over observed data, it can be sampled one transition at a time.
Say it“The mean squared TD error is the expectation under b of rho times delta squared.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf w_{t+1} = \mathbf w_t &+ \alpha\,\rho_t\,\delta_t \\ &\times \big(\nabla\hat v(S_t) - \gamma\,\nabla\hat v(S_{t+1})\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the naive residual-gradient step, a true SGD step on TDE. Compared with TD, it also nudges the next state’s value against the error, so the target moves toward the estimate as well as the estimate toward the target.
Say it“w moves by alpha rho delta times the gradient here minus gamma times the gradient at the next state.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf w_{t+1} = \mathbf w_t &+ \alpha\big[\mathbb E_b[\rho_t(R_{t+1} + \gamma\hat v(S_{t+1}))] \\ &\qquad - \hat v(S_t)\big] \\ &\times \big[\nabla\hat v(S_t) \\ &\qquad - \gamma\,\mathbb E_b[\rho_t\nabla\hat v(S_{t+1})]\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the residual-gradient step on BE. Both brackets are expectations over the next state given St; using one sample for both would correlate them and bring back the naive algorithm. Two independent samples make the product unbiased.
Say it“The expected TD error, times the gradient here minus gamma times the expected gradient at the next state.”

What data can tell you · 11.6

The Bellman error is not learnable

A quantity is learnable if it can be worked out from the data an agent actually sees, the stream of feature vectors, actions and rewards, given as much of it as you like. It is not enough that it can be computed from the hidden state of the world. Here are two worlds that produce exactly the same data. In the first, one state repeats forever and each step pays 0 or 2 at random. In the second, two states A and B (which look identical to the agent: same feature) are visited at random; A always pays 0 and B always pays 2. Either way the agent sees one feature and a fair coin of 0s and 2s. Move the weight w and the discount: the value error VE differs between the worlds, but its minimum sits at the same w; the return error RE, which the data can estimate, is identical in both.

The A-split and A-presplit problems of the last section are the same trick for the Bellman error. They generate identical data, yet BE is minimised at B = 1, C = 0 in one and at B = ¾, C = ¼ in the other. So not even the minimiser of BE can be found from data, and residual gradient only managed it because double sampling peeks at the hidden state. That is why the rest of the chapter minimises the projected Bellman error.

\[ \begin{aligned} \overline{\text{RE}}(\mathbf w) &= \mathbb E\big[(G_t - \hat v(S_t,\mathbf w))^2\big] \\ &= \overline{\text{VE}}(\mathbf w) + \mathbb E\big[(G_t - v_\pi(S_t))^2\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the mean squared return error compares the estimate with the actual return Gt, which is observable. It equals VE plus a term that does not depend on w at all (the inherent noise of returns), so RE and VE have the same minimiser even though VE itself is hidden.
Say it“The return error is the value error plus the variance of the return around the true value.”

The fix, part 1 · 11.7

Gradient-TD: true SGD on the projected Bellman error

The projected Bellman error is learnable, and with linear features its zero is the TD fixed point, so doing true gradient descent on it would give TD’s answer with SGD’s stability. Written as expectations over the data, PBE is a product of three of them, and its gradient is a product too. The trick of Gradient-TD methods (Sutton, Maei and colleagues, 2008–2009) is to learn one of the factors separately: a second weight vector v that tracks the least-squares fit of the TD errors on the features, updated by the ordinary LMS rule. With v in hand, each step of w is cheap (O(d)) and unbiased. Two variants differ in how they use it: GTD2 and TDC, also called GTD(0); TDC is TD plus a correction term. Run them on Baird’s counterexample next to semi-gradient TD, and play with the two step sizes.

\[ \begin{aligned} \overline{\text{PBE}}(\mathbf w) &= \big(X^{\top}D\bar\delta_{\mathbf w}\big)^{\!\top}\big(X^{\top}DX\big)^{-1}\big(X^{\top}D\bar\delta_{\mathbf w}\big) \\ &= \mathbb E[\rho_t\delta_t\mathbf x_t]^{\top}\,\mathbb E[\mathbf x_t\mathbf x_t^{\top}]^{-1}\,\mathbb E[\rho_t\delta_t\mathbf x_t] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the projection only needs the correlation of the Bellman error with each feature, XTDδ̄, and that correlation is an expectation over data: E[ρδx]. The middle matrix is the features’ second moment. All three factors can be estimated from the behaviour data; none needs the hidden state.
Say it“PBE is E of rho delta x, transposed, times the inverse of E of x x-transpose, times E of rho delta x.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla\overline{\text{PBE}}(\mathbf w) &= 2\,\mathbb E\big[\rho_t(\gamma\mathbf x_{t+1} - \mathbf x_t)\mathbf x_t^{\top}\big]\;\mathbf v^\star \\ \mathbf v^\star &= \mathbb E[\mathbf x_t\mathbf x_t^{\top}]^{-1}\,\mathbb E[\rho_t\delta_t\mathbf x_t] \\ \mathbf v_{t+1} &= \mathbf v_t + \beta\,\rho_t\big(\delta_t - \mathbf v_t^{\top}\mathbf x_t\big)\mathbf x_t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: v☆ is the weight vector of the least-squares regression of ρδ on x, so the second line, LMS with its own step size β, learns it. Multiplying two expectations is what made BE need double sampling; here one of them is replaced by a learned vector, so a single sample is enough.
Say it“The gradient is twice E of rho times gamma x-next minus x, times x-transpose, times v-star; v learns v-star by least mean squares.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{GTD2:}\; \mathbf w_{t+1} &= \mathbf w_t + \alpha\rho_t\big(\mathbf x_t - \gamma\mathbf x_{t+1}\big)\mathbf x_t^{\top}\mathbf v_t \\ \text{TDC:}\; \mathbf w_{t+1} &= \mathbf w_t + \alpha\rho_t\delta_t\mathbf x_t \\ &\quad - \alpha\rho_t\gamma\,\mathbf x_{t+1}\big(\mathbf x_t^{\top}\mathbf v_t\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: both are −½α times a sample of the gradient above, with v standing in for v☆. TDC rearranges the algebra so that its first term is exactly the semi-gradient TD step, and the second is a correction that cancels the part that causes divergence. Both converge to the TD fixed point when v learns faster than w (β larger than α; formally, α/β → 0, two time scales).
Say it“TDC is the TD step minus alpha rho gamma times the next feature vector times x-transpose v.”

The fix, part 2 · 11.8

Emphatic-TD: re-weighting the updates

Gradient-TD changes the update. Emphatic-TD (Sutton, Mahmood and White, 2016) keeps the plain TD update and changes how much each one counts. Recall why on-policy TD is stable: the states are updated in proportion to how often π itself would pass through them, so whenever a state’s value is pushed up by bootstrapping from its successor, that successor is updated too and is held back by its successors. Off-policy, the chain is broken. Emphatic-TD repairs it with a scalar emphasis Mt that carries forward, discounted and importance-weighted, how much the states leading here matter. An interest It ≥ 0 says how much we care about the current state (here 1 everywhere). In expectation, the result is stable for any π and b. Compare the expected update with a single sampled run, and look at the emphasis trace.

\[ \begin{aligned} M_t &= \gamma\,\rho_{t-1}\,M_{t-1} + I_t, \qquad M_{-1} = 0 \\ \mathbf w_{t+1} &= \mathbf w_t + \alpha\,M_t\,\rho_t\,\delta_t\,\nabla\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w_t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the emphasis is a follow-on trace: it grows when the target policy would have kept following the path that led here (ρ > 0) and resets towards It when it would not have. States that π reaches via many routes get big emphasis. In Baird’s problem, every solid step has ρ = 7, so a run of k solid steps grows M roughly like (7γ)k.
Say it“M is gamma rho-previous M-previous plus the interest; w moves by alpha M rho delta times the gradient.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf m^{\top} &= \big(\mathbf d_b \circ \mathbf i\big)^{\top}\big(I - \gamma P_\pi\big)^{-1} \\ \mathbf w_{k+1} &= \mathbf w_k + \alpha\sum_s m(s)\,\bar\delta_{\mathbf w_k}(s)\,\mathbf x(s) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: in expectation, the emphasis re-weights the behaviour distribution db (times the interest i) into the emphatic weighting m, as if every state handed its weight on to the states π would visit next. The expected update then uses m instead of db, and the matrix XTM(I − γPπ)X it produces is positive definite whenever the features are linearly independent, the property that makes on-policy TD stable. In Baird’s problem each upper state keeps m = 1/7 and the lower state collects everyone’s.
Say it“m-transpose is d-b times i, times the inverse of I minus gamma P-pi.”

Taming variance · 11.9

Reducing variance

Stability in expectation is not the end of the story. Importance ratios always average to exactly 1 under the behaviour policy, but they get there by being 0 most of the time and occasionally very large, and multi-step methods multiply them together. A product of n ratios has the same mean, 1, and a variance that grows exponentially with n. For SGD this is poison: a rare huge ratio produces one enormous step. The textbook lists the remedies. Keep step sizes small or adapt them per component; use importance-weight-aware updates that never overshoot however big ρ is (the project uses one); average the iterates. Use weighted importance sampling, which divides by the sum of the ratios instead of the count: slightly biased, far steadier. Use tree backup, which never forms ratios at all, or let the target policy be defined relative to the behaviour policy so the ratios cannot get large. Lengthen the episodes and watch ordinary importance sampling fall apart while the weighted estimator holds on.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E_b[\rho] &= \sum_a b(a\mid s)\frac{\pi(a\mid s)}{b(a\mid s)} = 1 \\ \text{Var}_b\Big[\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\rho_k\Big] &= \frac{1}{p^{\,n}} - 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first line holds for any b that covers π. In the second, the target always takes one action and the behaviour takes it with probability p: the product of n ratios is p−n if the behaviour matched the target at every step (chance pn) and 0 otherwise. Its mean is 1; its variance explodes geometrically with the horizon.
Say it“Rho averages to one; the product of n of them has variance one over p to the n, minus one.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat v_{\text{OIS}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rho^{(i)} G^{(i)} \\ \hat v_{\text{WIS}} &= \frac{\sum_i \rho^{(i)} G^{(i)}}{\sum_i \rho^{(i)}} \\ \hat v_{\text{PD}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\rho^{(i)}_{0:k}\,R^{(i)}_{k+1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ρ(i) is the whole product of ratios for episode i and G(i) its return. Ordinary IS is unbiased but noisy; weighted IS is biased for small N, consistent, and its estimate is always a weighted average of real returns, so it cannot exceed the largest return seen. Per-decision IS (PD) weights each reward only by the ratios up to that reward, which removes needless noise.
Say it“Ordinary IS averages rho G; weighted IS divides the sum of rho G by the sum of rho; per-decision IS weights each reward by the ratios so far.”

Real-world project

Project: Baird reproduced, then a real recommender log

Part A re-runs Baird’s counterexample with the textbook’s settings and checks every number against an independent Python implementation that uses the same random-number generator bit for bit. Part B takes off-policy evaluation to real data: the Open Bandit Dataset (Saito, Aihara, Matsutani and Narita, 2021), released by the fashion retailer ZOZO under CC BY 4.0. During a week in November 2019, ZOZOTOWN showed each visitor three items out of 80, chosen either by a uniform-random policy or by Bernoulli Thompson sampling (BTS), and logged every impression, the probability with which the shown item was chosen, and whether it was clicked. That makes it a rare thing: a log from one policy plus the on-policy truth for another. The task: using only the BTS log, estimate the click rate the random policy would have earned, then compare with the random policy’s own log.

This is the one-step corner of the chapter. Each impression is an episode of length one, so γ = 0, the TD target is just the click, and TDC’s correction term (which is multiplied by γ) vanishes: TDC is off-policy TD(0) here. What remains is the part of the problem that never goes away, the importance ratio ρ = (1/80) / b(item), and section 11.9’s fight with its variance. The page uses two slices: the 10,000-row sample that ships with the authors’ Open Bandit Pipeline (embedded row by row, for the learners) and the first 2,121,418 rows (28 hours) of the full BTS log, fetched from the official archive by HTTP range requests (only the first 35 MB of the compressed file) and reduced to sufficient statistics per item and position.

import json, math, numpy as np

def mulberry32(a):                       # the page's random-number generator, bit for bit
    def nxt():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return nxt

# ---------- Part A: Baird's counterexample ----------
G, W0 = 0.99, [1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 10, 1]
val = lambda w, s: 2 * w[s] + w[7] if s < 6 else w[6] + 2 * w[7]      # x(s) . w
def add(g, s, c):                                                     # g += c * x(s)
    if s < 6: g[s] += 2 * c; g[7] += c
    else: g[6] += c; g[7] += 2 * c
VE = lambda w: sum(val(w, s) ** 2 for s in range(7)) / 7             # v_pi = 0 everywhere
BE = lambda w: sum((G * val(w, 6) - val(w, s)) ** 2 for s in range(7)) / 7   # = PBE here (X has rank 7)
M7 = (1 + 6 * G) / (7 * (1 - G))                                      # emphatic weighting of the lower state

def expected(kind, a, b=0.05, n=1000):
    w, u = list(map(float, W0)), [0.0] * 8
    for k in range(n):
        v = [val(w, s) for s in range(7)]; e = [0.0] * 8
        for s in range(7): add(e, s, (G * v[6] - v[s]) * ((M7 if s == 6 else 1 / 7) if kind == 'etd' else 1 / 7))
        if kind in ('tdc', 'gtd2'):
            xu = [val(u, s) for s in range(7)]; cu, corr = [0.0] * 8, [0.0] * 8
            for s in range(7): add(cu, s, xu[s] / 7)
            add(corr, 6, G * sum(xu) / 7)
            for i in range(8):
                w[i] += a * (e[i] - corr[i]) if kind == 'tdc' else a * (cu[i] - corr[i]); u[i] += b * (e[i] - cu[i])
        else:
            for i in range(8): w[i] += a * e[i]
    return w

def sampled(kind, a, b=0.05, n=1000, seed=1):
    rng = mulberry32(seed); w, u = list(map(float, W0)), [0.0] * 8
    s, M, rho_prev, Mmax = math.floor(rng() * 7), 0.0, 0.0, 0.0
    for t in range(n):
        if rng() < 1 / 7: s2, rho = 6, 7.0                           # solid: the only action the target takes
        else: s2, rho = math.floor(rng() * 6), 0.0                     # dashed: rho = 0
        M = G * rho_prev * M + 1; Mmax = max(Mmax, M)
        if rho > 0:
            d = G * val(w, s2) - val(w, s)
            if kind == 'td': add(w, s, a * rho * d)
            elif kind == 'etd': add(w, s, a * M * rho * d)
            else:                                                      # tdc / gtd2
                xu = val(u, s); add(w, s, a * rho * (d if kind == 'tdc' else xu)); add(w, s2, -a * rho * G * xu); add(u, s, b * rho * (d - xu))
        s, rho_prev = s2, rho
    return w, Mmax

Wrap-up · 11.10

Five lines to remember

  • Off-policy TD with function approximation multiplies each update by ρ = π/b. That fixes the target but not the distribution of updates, and with bootstrapping the combination (the deadly triad) can diverge, as Baird’s counterexample shows even for the exact expected update.
  • Think in value space: approximate value functions live on a subspace; vπ, the minimum-VE point, the minimum-BE point and the TD fixed point (PBE = 0) are generally four different places.
  • True SGD needs an objective. The TD error gives stable but wrong values; the Bellman error needs double sampling and is not even learnable from data. The projected Bellman error is learnable, and its zero is TD’s fixed point.
  • Gradient-TD (GTD2, TDC) does SGD on PBE by learning a second vector v with LMS; Emphatic-TD keeps the TD update but re-weights it with a follow-on emphasis so that, in expectation, it is as stable as on-policy TD.
  • Stable in expectation is not enough: importance ratios average 1 but are heavy-tailed. Weighted IS, importance-weight-aware steps, averaging, tree backup and target policies that stay close to the behaviour keep the variance in check.

Check your understanding

Baird’s problem diverges even with the expected (DP) update. Why does that rule out “it’s just noise”?

The expected update has no sampling at all: it uses the exact average of every TD error under the uniform state distribution. If it still diverges, the direction of the average step is itself wrong. The cause is the mismatch between the distribution of updates (uniform) and the on-policy distribution (all mass on state 7), not the variance of samples.

Why does one-step Expected Sarsa need no importance ratio, while one-step TD for state values does?

An action-value q(s, a) is about a given action, so the action actually taken needs no correction; the next action is averaged over π inside the target. A state value v(s) averages over the action at s under π, but the data contain b’s choice there, so the step must be re-weighted by π(a|s)/b(a|s).

Two problems produce identical streams of features and rewards but have different Bellman-error minimisers. What follows for an algorithm that sees only that stream?

No algorithm can find the BE minimiser from the stream, because the stream does not determine it. Residual gradient only gets there with double sampling, which uses access to the hidden state. Objectives whose minimiser is determined by the data distribution, such as PBE, TDE or RE, are the ones a learner can target.

In TDC, what does the second weight vector v estimate, and why must it learn faster than w?

v estimates E[xxT]−1E[ρδx], the least-squares fit of the TD errors on the features, which appears as a factor in the gradient of PBE. The w update treats v as if it were already that fit, so v must track it on a faster time scale (larger β); otherwise the correction term uses a stale estimate and the convergence argument fails.

The BTS log in the project has ρ up to 12,500. Why does weighted IS help, and what does it cost?

Dividing by the sum of the ratios instead of the count means one huge ratio cannot push the estimate above the largest observed reward; the estimate stays a weighted average of real outcomes, so its variance is far lower. The cost is a bias for finite samples (it is consistent, so the bias vanishes with more data) and, with heavy tails, a sum of ratios that can sit well away from its expectation for a long time.
