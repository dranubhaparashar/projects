Dissimilarities, K-medoids & Self-Organising Maps

Decide what “alike” means, then group things and map them by it

Every clustering method rests on one quiet decision: how far apart are two things? For points on a plane the ruler is obvious. For countries described by incomes, birth rates, a region and an income band, it is not, and the answer shapes every group you find. This chapter builds dissimilarities from raw attributes, searches for the best partition, swaps K-means’ averages for real representative members (medoids), and then asks the prototypes to arrange themselves on a sheet, so that neighbours on the sheet are neighbours in the data: a self-organising map. On the right, an 8 × 8 sheet of prototypes is unfolding over a cloud of points. Click the plot to drop a new cloud and watch the sheet stretch to cover it.

The raw material

Proximity matrices: when all you have is “how alike”

Clustering does not need coordinates. It needs, for every pair of objects i and i′, a number saying how different they are. Collect those numbers in an N × N proximity matrix. Sometimes they come straight from the world: people asked to rate how alike two wines taste, how often shoppers buy two products together, or how often one thing is mistaken for another.

Here is a real one. A simple classifier (nearest class centroid) read 897 handwritten digits. Row i of the matrix counts how often a true i was read as each digit. Confusion is a similarity: digits that get mixed up must look alike. But it is lopsided (a 5 is read as a 9 far more often than a 9 as a 5), and its diagonal is large rather than zero. Before clustering, we make it symmetric and turn it into a dissimilarity with any decreasing function. Switch between the three views, then ask K-medoids (section 6) to group the digits.

\[ \begin{aligned} &s_{ii'} = \tfrac12\big(P_{ii'} + P_{i'i}\big), \\ &P_{ii'} = \frac{\#\{\text{true } i \text{ read as } i'\}}{\#\{\text{true } i\}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: P is the confusion table with each row turned into rates, so digits with more examples don’t count more. Averaging P with its transpose makes the similarity symmetric: sii′ = si′i. Most algorithms assume symmetry, so this is the usual first step with judged or counted proximities.
Say it“s-i-i-prime is half of P-i-i-prime plus P-i-prime-i.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &d_{ii'} = f\big(s_{ii'}/s_{\max}\big),\quad d_{ii} = 0, \\ &f(u) = 1-u \ \text{ or } \ f(u) = \sqrt{1-u} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: any decreasing f turns “more alike” into “closer”. Dividing by the largest off-diagonal similarity smax puts u between 0 and 1. The result is non-negative with a zero diagonal, which is all most clustering methods ask. It need not obey the triangle inequality dik ≤ dij + djk, and judged proximities often don’t.
Say it“d-i-i-prime is f of s-i-i-prime over s-max, with f decreasing, and zero on the diagonal.”

Attributes

Dissimilarity, one attribute at a time

Far more often we have measurements: object i is a row xi1, …, xip of p attributes. Then we choose a dissimilarity dj for each attribute and add them up. The right dj depends on the attribute’s type. For quantitative attributes the usual choice is squared difference; absolute difference grows more slowly, so a single huge gap counts for less. A third option compares the shapes of two rows with their correlation, taken across the attributes rather than across objects.

Below, each line is a country’s profile across nine World Bank indicators, each put on a common scale (z-scores across 156 countries). Drag the orange points of country B up and down, or press the shift button: Euclidean distance cares about the level of the line, correlation only about its shape.

\[ \begin{aligned} D_{\text{sq}}(x_i,x_{i'}) &= \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{p} (x_{ij}-x_{i'j})^2 \\ D_{\text{abs}}(x_i,x_{i'}) &= \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{p} |x_{ij}-x_{i'j}| \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: line up the two rows attribute by attribute, measure each gap, and add. Squaring punishes one large gap far more than several small ones; absolute values are more forgiving of a single outlying attribute.
Say it“The squared distance is the sum over attributes of the squared gaps; the absolute distance sums the sizes of the gaps.”
\[ \begin{aligned} c_{ij} &= x_{ij}-\bar x_i \\ \rho(x_i,x_{i'}) &= \frac{\sum_j c_{ij}\,c_{i'j}}{\sqrt{\sum_j c_{ij}^2\,\sum_j c_{i'j}^2}} \\ \textstyle\sum_j (\tilde x_{ij}-\tilde x_{i'j})^2 &= 2p\,(1-\rho) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: x̄i is the average of row i over its attributes, and cij is the row centred on it. The correlation is +1 when the two profiles rise and fall together, whatever their levels and spreads. If each row is first standardised to its own mean 0 and variance 1 (that is x̃), squared distance and correlation say the same thing: small distance means high correlation.
Say it“Rho is the correlation between the two rows. For standardised rows, the squared distance equals two p times one minus rho.”

Not every attribute is a number. An ordinal attribute has ordered levels without a scale: the World Bank’s income band (low, lower-middle, upper-middle, high). Replace level i of M by the midpoint (i − ½)/M and treat it as quantitative. A categorical (nominal) attribute has unordered levels, like the region a country is in. Its dissimilarities are set by hand in an M × M loss matrix L, symmetric with a zero diagonal; the plainest choice charges 1 for any mismatch.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{level } i \text{ of } M \ \longmapsto\ \frac{i-\tfrac12}{M}, \\ &i = 1,\dots,M \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: spread the M levels evenly between 0 and 1, each at the centre of its own slice. Low income becomes 0.125, high income 0.875. The gaps between neighbouring levels are now equal, which is an assumption you are choosing to make.
Say it“Level i of M maps to i minus a half, over M.”
\[ \begin{aligned} d(x_{ij}, x_{i'j}) &= L_{rr'}, \\ L_{rr'} &= \begin{cases} 0 & r = r' \\ 1 & r \ne r' \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: when object i is in category r and object i′ in category r′, look the cost up in the table L. With the 0–1 table every mismatch costs the same; a domain expert can make some mismatches cheaper (Europe and Central Asia closer than Europe and the Pacific, say).
Say it“The dissimilarity is the entry L-r-r-prime of the loss matrix: zero for the same category, one otherwise.”
Combining

Object dissimilarity: how much should each attribute count?

Adding the per-attribute dissimilarities with weights gives one number per pair of objects. The weights look innocent, but an attribute’s real say in D is its weight times its typical size d̄j. An income in dollars would swamp a birth rate in units of one. So a common default is to give every attribute equal influence, which for squared differences means dividing each one by twice its variance: standardising. It is a choice, not a law. Switch the weighting below and see what two medoids make of the same 120 points.

\[ \begin{aligned} D(x_i,x_{i'}) &= \sum_{j=1}^{p} w_j\, d_j(x_{ij},x_{i'j}), \\ \sum_{j=1}^{p} w_j &= 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a weighted average of the attribute dissimilarities. Set wj = 0 to ignore an attribute; raise it to make that attribute matter more.
Say it“D between objects i and i-prime is the sum over attributes of w-j times d-j, with weights adding to one.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \bar d_j &= \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{i,\,i'} d_j(x_{ij},x_{i'j}) \\ &= 2\,\widehat{\operatorname{var}}_j \\ \text{influence}_j &= \frac{w_j\,\bar d_j}{\sum_l w_l\,\bar d_l} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: d̄j is the average dissimilarity on attribute j over all N² pairs. For squared differences it equals twice the variance (with 1/N). Attribute j’s share of the average D is its influence; choosing wj ∝ 1/d̄j makes every share equal to 1/p.
Say it“d-bar-j is the average of d-j over all pairs, twice the variance for squared error. The influence of attribute j is w-j d-bar-j over the total.”

Mixed data and missing values, on real countries

Real tables mix types and have holes. The recipe still works: compute each attribute’s own dj (squared gap for numbers and for the income band’s midpoints, 0–1 mismatch for region), divide by its d̄j so each has equal influence, and average over the attributes both objects actually have. A missing value just drops out of that one pair’s average. This is the dissimilarity the project uses. Pick two countries and follow the sum line by line.

\[ D(x_i,x_{i'}) = \frac{1}{|A_{ii'}|}\sum_{j\in A_{ii'}} \frac{d_j(x_{ij},x_{i'j})}{\bar d_j} \]
Read it as: Aii′ is the set of attributes recorded for both objects. Each term is an attribute’s dissimilarity in units of its own average, so a term of 1 means “as different as a typical pair”. With no missing values and numbers only, this is squared Euclidean distance between z-scores, divided by 2p.
Say it“D is the average, over the attributes both objects have, of d-j divided by d-bar-j.”

Clustering as search

Combinatorial clustering: the best partition, and why we can’t find it

Once D is fixed, a clustering is an encoder C(i) that gives each object a cluster number from 1 to K. A natural score is the within-cluster scatter W(C): half the sum of dissimilarities between objects that share a cluster. The total T of all pair dissimilarities doesn’t depend on C, so it splits into W plus the between-cluster part B: making groups tight is the same as making them far apart. Click a point to move it to the next cluster and watch W and B trade while T stays fixed.

\[ \begin{aligned} W(C) &= \tfrac12\sum_{k=1}^{K}\ \sum_{C(i)=C(i')=k} d_{ii'} \\ T &= \tfrac12\sum_{i,\,i'} d_{ii'} \\ &= W(C) + B(C) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: W adds up the dissimilarity of every pair inside the same cluster (the ½ counts each pair once). B adds up every pair split across clusters. Every pair is one or the other, so W + B = T always, and minimising W is maximising B. With squared Euclidean d, W(C) = Σk Nk ΣC(i)=k ‖xi − x̄k‖², the K-means criterion.
Say it“W of C is half the sum, over clusters, of all dissimilarities between members of the same cluster. T, the total, is W plus B.”
\[ S(N,K) = \tfrac{1}{K!}\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}(-1)^{K-k}\tbinom{K}{k}\,k^{N} \]
Read it as: the number of ways to split N objects into K non-empty groups (a Stirling number of the second kind). Count the kN labellings that use at most k labels, correct for over-counting with alternating signs, and divide by K! because the cluster names don’t matter. It grows roughly like KN/K!.
Say it“S of N and K is one over K factorial times the sum over k of minus one to the K minus k, times K choose k, times k to the N.”

Twelve points and four clusters already give 611,501 partitions; nineteen points give over ten billion. So every practical method is an iterative greedy descent: start from some partition and keep making changes that lower W until none helps. Such a search can only promise a local minimum, which is why the K-means chapter ran many random starts. K-means and K-medoids are two clever descents that change many labels at once.

Compression

Vector quantisation: K-means as a codebook

K-means was invented twice, once by statisticians and once by engineers squeezing signals down phone lines. The engineers call it vector quantisation. Cut a greyscale photo into 2 × 2 blocks of pixels; each block is a point in four dimensions. Run K-means on the blocks: the K centres are a codebook. To send the picture, send each block’s codeword number, which costs log2K bits per block instead of 4 × 8 = 32. The receiver looks the numbers up and rebuilds an approximation. Change K and drag the divider across the photo to compare.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{rate} &= \frac{\log_2 K}{4}\ \text{bits per pixel} \\ H &= -\sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \log_2 \pi_k \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: one codeword index per block of four pixels. Some codewords (flat sky) are used far more than others; πk is codeword k’s share of the blocks. Coding frequent indices with short codes gets the cost down towards the entropy H bits per block, a free, lossless second step.
Say it“The rate is log base two of K, over four, bits per pixel. H is minus the sum of pi-k log two pi-k.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{MSE} &= \frac{1}{4n}\sum_{b=1}^{n}\big\lVert x_b - c_{k(b)}\big\rVert^2 \\ \text{PSNR} &= 10\log_{10}\frac{255^2}{\text{MSE}} \ \text{dB} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the distortion is the average squared error per pixel between each block xb and the codeword ck(b) that replaces it, which is the K-means criterion divided by 4n. Engineers quote it as a peak signal-to-noise ratio: higher is better, and around 30 dB looks fine to most eyes.
Say it“MSE is one over four n times the sum of squared distances from each block to its codeword. PSNR is ten log ten of 255 squared over the MSE.”

Any dissimilarity

K-medoids: centres that are real members

K-means needs averages, so it needs numbers, and it needs squared Euclidean distance for its update to be optimal. K-medoids drops both requirements. Each cluster is represented by one of its own members, the medoid: the object with the smallest total dissimilarity to the rest of its cluster. Assign every object to its nearest medoid, re-pick each medoid, repeat. Only D is ever used, so it works on the digit confusions above as happily as on points. And because a medoid has to be a real member, one wild outlier can’t drag it off into empty space. Step the algorithm, then add the outlier and compare the medoids (stars) with K-means’ means (crosses).

\[ \min_{C,\ \{i_k\}}\ \sum_{k=1}^{K}\ \sum_{C(i)=k} D\big(x_i,\, x_{i_k}\big) \]
Read it as: choose K members i1, …, iK as centres and an assignment C, so that the total dissimilarity from each object to its own centre is as small as possible.
Say it“Minimise, over assignments and medoid choices, the sum over clusters of the dissimilarities from each member to its medoid.”
\[ \begin{aligned} i_k^{*} &= \operatorname*{argmin}_{\{i:\,C(i)=k\}} \ \sum_{C(i')=k} D(x_i, x_{i'}) \\ C(i) &= \operatorname*{argmin}_{1\le k\le K}\ D\big(x_i, x_{i_k^*}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: step 1, inside each cluster, try every member as the centre and keep the one with the smallest total; step 2, send every object to its nearest centre. Each step can only lower the criterion, so it stops. Step 1 costs about Nk² dissimilarities per cluster, against Nk for a mean: the price of the generality.
Say it“The new medoid of cluster k is the member with the smallest total dissimilarity to the others. Then each object joins the cluster of its closest medoid.”

The alternating scheme above is the one in Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman. Kaufman and Rousseeuw’s original PAM (partitioning around medoids) goes further: it tries swapping every medoid with every non-medoid and makes the swap that lowers the total most, until no swap helps. That costs more per pass but escapes some traps that alternation gets stuck in, as the countries project shows.

Maps

Self-organising maps: prototypes on a sheet

Teuvo Kohonen’s self-organising map (SOM) is K-means with one constraint added. The K prototypes mk live on a fixed grid, a chain or a lattice, and each has grid coordinates ℓk. Show the map one data point at a time. Find the prototype closest to it, the best-matching unit, and pull it towards the point; then also pull its grid neighbours, by less. Neighbours on the grid end up near each other in the data, so the grid folds itself into the data like a sheet of rubber. The pull α and the reach r both shrink as training goes on. Press Play, then try other shapes and a bigger starting reach.

\[ \begin{aligned} j &= \operatorname*{argmin}_{k}\ \lVert x_i - m_k\rVert \\ m_k &\leftarrow m_k + \alpha\, h_{jk}\,(x_i - m_k) \\ h_{jk} &= h(\lVert \ell_j-\ell_k\rVert) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: unit j wins point xi. Every unit k then moves a fraction of the way towards xi; the fraction is the learning rate α times h, which is 1 for the winner and fades with distance on the grid, not in the data. In the simplest version h = 1 for units within grid distance r and 0 beyond.
Say it“j is the unit closest to x-i. Each m-k moves alpha times h of the grid distance between j and k, of the way towards x-i.”
\[ \begin{aligned} h(\delta) &= \exp\!\big(-\delta^2 / 2r^2\big) \\ \alpha_t &= \alpha_0\,(1 - t/T) \\ r_t &= r_0 + (0.5 - r_0)\,t/T \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the smooth (Gaussian) neighbourhood: a unit at grid distance r feels about 61% of the winner’s pull. Over T passes through the data, α falls to 0 and r shrinks to half a grid step. Early on, big moves of whole regions unfold the sheet; late on, small local moves fine-tune it.
Say it“h of delta is e to the minus delta squared over two r squared. Alpha falls linearly to zero; r shrinks linearly to one half.”

Batch version

Batch SOM, and K-means as the special case

The one-point-at-a-time update has a batch cousin, which is faster, has no learning rate, and gives the same answer every run. Find every point’s best-matching unit, then move each prototype to a weighted average of all the points, each point weighted by how close its winner is to that prototype on the grid. Now shrink the reach until a unit’s neighbourhood is only itself: the weights become 1 for a unit’s own points and 0 for everyone else’s, and the update is exactly K-means. A SOM is K-means with a smoothness constraint along the grid, so it usually pays for its orderly grid with a higher squared error than K-means. Move the final reach and compare the two errors.

\[ \begin{aligned} m_k &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} h_{k,j(i)}\, x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} h_{k,j(i)}} \\ h_{k,j} &= h\big(\lVert \ell_j - \ell_k\rVert\big) \\ m_k &\xrightarrow{\ r\,\to\,0\ } \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{j(i)=k} x_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: j(i) is the winning unit for point i. Prototype k becomes the average of all points, weighted by h of the grid distance between their winner and k. As r shrinks, h(0) = 1 and h of any other grid distance tends to 0, so only the unit’s own points count: the K-means centroid.
Say it“m-k is the h-weighted average of all the points, the weight being h of the grid distance from the point’s winner to unit k. As r goes to zero it becomes the plain mean of k’s own points.”

The topographic error counts the points whose best and second-best units are not grid neighbours: a fold or tear in the sheet. K-means has no grid, so it has nothing to keep. SOMs earn their keep when the grid itself is the product: a picture of high-dimensional data that people can read. Kohonen’s group famously laid out millions of patent abstracts on a map this way (WEBSOM), so that nearby cells held related documents.

The same project in Python

scikit-learn has no K-medoids or SOM (they live in add-on packages with their own conventions), so the reference is a short NumPy implementation of exactly the algorithms above, checked against scikit-learn’s own pairwise distances where they overlap.

import json, numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import pairwise_distances, adjusted_rand_score

W = json.load(open("wdi.json"))                   # 156 countries, World Bank WDI (CC BY 4.0)
X = np.array([[np.nan if v is None else v for v in c[4]] for c in W["c"]], float)
for j, f in enumerate(W["ind"]):
    if f["tr"] == "log": X[:, j] = np.log10(X[:, j])
inc = np.array([(c[2] + 0.5) / 4 for c in W["c"]])     # ordinal: (i - 1/2) / M
reg = np.array([c[3] for c in W["c"]], float)          # nominal: 7 regions
N, names = len(X), [c[1] for c in W["c"]]

def dissim(mixed):                    # D = average over available attributes of d_j / dbar_j
    cols = [(X[:, j], "sq") for j in range(9)] + ([(inc, "sq"), (reg, "01")] if mixed else [])
    num, cnt = np.zeros((N, N)), np.zeros((N, N))
    for v, kind in cols:
        d = (v[:, None] - v[None, :]) ** 2 if kind == "sq" else (v[:, None] != v[None, :]) * 1.0
        ok = ~np.isnan(d)
        num += np.where(ok, d, 0) / d[ok].mean(); cnt += ok   # dbar_j: mean over all pairs
    return num / cnt

def kmedoids(D, K):
    med = [int(D.sum(1).argmin())]                       # BUILD: greedy start
    while len(med) < K:
        gain = np.maximum(D[:, med].min(1)[None, :] - D, 0).sum(1); gain[med] = -1
        med.append(int(gain.argmax()))
    while True:                                          # ESL Algorithm 14.2: alternate
        lab = D[:, med].argmin(1); new = []
        for k, m in enumerate(med):
            idx = np.flatnonzero(lab == k); c = D[np.ix_(idx, idx)].sum(1)
            new.append(int(idx[c.argmin()]) if c.min() < D[m, idx].sum() - 1e-12 else m)
        if new == med: break
        med = new
    alt = D[:, med].min(1).sum()
    while True:                                          # PAM swaps: polish
        cur, best = D[:, med].min(1).sum(), (-1e-12, None)
        for k in range(K):
            rest = D[:, [m for q, m in enumerate(med) if q != k]].min(1)
            for h in range(N):
                if h in med: continue
                delta = np.minimum(rest, D[:, h]).sum() - cur
                if delta < best[0]: best = (delta, (k, h))
        if best[1] is None: break
        med[best[1][0]] = best[1][1]
    return med, D[:, med].argmin(1), alt, D[:, med].min(1).sum()

Z = (X - np.nanmean(X, 0)) / np.nanstd(X, 0)
full = ~np.isnan(X).any(1)
Dn = dissim(False)
print("D (9 numbers) vs scikit-learn squared distances / 2p, complete rows: max diff",
      f"{np.abs(Dn[np.ix_(full, full)] - pairwise_distances(Z[full], metric='sqeuclidean') / 18).max():.1e}")
res = {}
for mixed in (False, True):
    D = dissim(mixed)
    for K in (3, 4, 5):
        med, lab, alt, cost = kmedoids(D, K); res[mixed, K] = lab
        print(f"{'mixed' if mixed else 'numbers'} K={K}: cost alternate {alt:.6f} -> swaps {cost:.6f}  medoids:",
              ", ".join(f"{names[m]} ({(lab == k).sum()})" for k, m in enumerate(med)))
print("agreement numbers vs mixed, K=4: ARI =", round(adjusted_rand_score(res[False, 4], res[True, 4]), 4))

R, C = 6, 8                                              # batch SOM on a 6 x 8 hexagonal grid
L = np.array([[c + 0.5 * (r % 2), r * np.sqrt(3) / 2] for r in range(R) for c in range(C)])
Z0 = np.nan_to_num(Z); lam, V = np.linalg.eigh(Z0.T @ Z0 / N); lam, V = lam[::-1], V[:, ::-1]
V = V * np.sign(V[np.abs(V).argmax(0), range(9)])        # sign rule: largest entry positive
a, b = np.tile(np.linspace(-1, 1, C), R), np.repeat(np.linspace(-1, 1, R), C)
M = np.outer(a, np.sqrt(lam[0]) * V[:, 0]) + np.outer(b, np.sqrt(lam[1]) * V[:, 1])
ok = ~np.isnan(Z)
def dist2(M): return ((np.where(ok, Z0, 0)[:, None, :] - M[None]) ** 2 * ok[:, None, :]).sum(2)
T, s0, s1 = 40, 3.0, 0.5
G2 = ((L[:, None] - L[None]) ** 2).sum(2)
for t in range(T):
    s = s0 + (s1 - s0) * t / (T - 1)
    bmu = dist2(M).argmin(1)
    H = np.exp(-G2[:, bmu] / (2 * s * s))                # units x countries
    M = (H @ (Z0 * ok)) / (H @ ok)
d2 = dist2(M); o = np.argsort(d2, 1); bmu = o[:, 0]
te = np.mean(np.sqrt(G2[bmu, o[:, 1]]) > 1.01)
print(f"SOM 6x8: mean squared error {d2[range(N), bmu].mean():.6f}, topographic error {te:.4f},",
      f"units used {len(set(bmu))} of {R * C}")
for iso in ("NOR", "IND", "NGA", "BRA", "JPN"):
    i = [c[0] for c in W["c"]].index(iso); print(f"  {names[i]:8s} -> unit (row {bmu[i] // C}, col {bmu[i] % C})")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Clustering only needs an N × N dissimilarity matrix: non-negative, zero on the diagonal, best made symmetric. It may come from judgments or confusions, and it need not obey the triangle inequality.
  • From attributes, build one dissimilarity per attribute (squared or absolute gap, correlation for shapes, (i − ½)/M for ordered levels, a loss matrix for categories) and average them with weights. An attribute’s influence is its weight times its average dissimilarity, so equal weights are not equal influence, and standardising can hide real structure.
  • The best partition minimises within-cluster scatter W, equivalently maximises B, since W + B = T is fixed. There are S(N, K) ≈ KN/K! partitions, so every practical method is a greedy descent to a local minimum.
  • K-medoids replaces each mean with the member of smallest total dissimilarity: it works for any D and resists outliers, at about Nk² work per update. Vector quantisation is K-means used as a codebook for compression.
  • A self-organising map is K-means with prototypes on a grid, updated together with their grid neighbours; shrink the neighbourhood to nothing and it becomes K-means. It trades a little squared error for a map people can read.

Check your understanding

A survey gives you, for 30 wines, how similar tasters judged every pair. Can you run K-means? K-medoids?

Not K-means: it needs coordinates to average, and there are none, only pairwise judgments. K-medoids needs nothing but the dissimilarities, so after symmetrising the judgments and turning similarity into dissimilarity with a decreasing function, it runs directly.

Income in dollars ranges over tens of thousands; births per woman range from 1 to 7. You sum squared differences with equal weights. What happens, and what is the usual fix?

Income’s average squared gap is millions of times larger, so it decides every distance and births are ignored. Dividing each attribute’s squared gap by its average d̄j = 2 varj (standardising) gives equal influence. Whether equal influence is right is a separate, subject-matter question: section 3’s lab shows it can blur a real split.

Why does lowering W(C) automatically raise B(C)?

Every pair of objects is either in the same cluster (counted in W) or split (counted in B), and T = W + B sums all pairs, which doesn’t depend on the clustering. Whatever W loses, B gains.

A vector quantiser uses 2 × 2 blocks and K = 64 codewords. What is the rate, and the compression against 8-bit pixels?

log264 = 6 bits per block of 4 pixels: 1.5 bits per pixel, 8/1.5 ≈ 5.3 times smaller, before entropy coding of the indices squeezes it further (and before counting the codebook itself).

You train a SOM with a neighbourhood that never shrinks below 3 grid steps. Compared with K-means on the same number of prototypes, what do you expect?

A visibly smoother, more ordered grid with higher squared error: each prototype is pulled towards points won by its neighbours, so it can’t sit at its own points’ centroid. Prototypes are drawn in from the edges of the data. Shrinking the neighbourhood to zero turns the update into K-means.
