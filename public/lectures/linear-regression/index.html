Linear Regression

One straight line, fitted so the misses are as small as possible

How much will this house sell for? How many units will we sell next month? How fast will this patient's disease progress? When the answer is a number, the first model anyone should try is a straight line. This lecture builds it three ways — by hand, by formula, and by rolling downhill — and then teaches it to bend and to behave.

Foundations

The oldest line in data science

In 1886 Francis Galton measured the heights of parents and their grown-up children. Tall parents had tall children — but less tall than themselves; short parents had children a little closer to average. He called it “regression towards mediocrity”, drew the best straight line through his scatter of heights, and the name regression stuck to the whole method.

The idea is the same today: we have pairs (x, y) — a feature and a number we want to predict — and we look for the straight line that predicts y from x with the smallest possible misses.

\[ \hat{y} = w_0 + w_1 x \]
Read it as: the prediction ŷ (“y-hat”) starts at w₀, the intercept (the prediction when x = 0), and rises by w₁, the slope, for every extra unit of x. Learning = choosing w₀ and w₁.
Say it“Y-hat equals w-zero plus w-one times x.”
\[ \hat{y} = w_0 + w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + \dots + w_dx_d = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} \]
Read it as: with many features, each one gets its own weight; the prediction is their weighted sum. Still “linear”: no feature is squared or multiplied by another — the model is a flat plane (or hyperplane) instead of a line. The project at the end uses ten features.
Say it“Y-hat is the dot product of the weight vector with the feature vector.”

How a biomarker responds to dose; how fast a disease progresses from BMI and blood tests (our project).

Science

Turning a sensor's voltage into a temperature, or absorbance into concentration. The lab's oldest tool.

Economics

Machine learning

The baseline

Before any neural net, fit a line. If the fancy model can't beat it, the fancy model isn't learning anything.

The objective

Why we square the misses

To let a computer choose the line, we need a single number that says how bad a line is. Adding the raw misses fails — positive and negative misses cancel, and a terrible line can score zero. So we square them (all become positive, and big misses are punished hard), add them up, and divide by n. That is the mean squared error, the number on your scoreboard.

\[ \text{MSE}(w_0, w_1) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\,y_i - (w_0 + w_1x_i)\,\big)^2 \]
Read it as: for each student i, take the real score yᵢ, subtract the line's prediction, square the difference, then average over all n students. Notice what's on the left: MSE is a function of the weights, not the data — the data is fixed; we are searching over lines.
Say it“MSE of w-zero, w-one is one over n times the sum of the squared residuals.”

Every line is a point in a bowl

Here is the big idea of this lecture. Each possible line is one pair (w₀, w₁) — one point on the right-hand map. Colour each point by that line's MSE and you get a landscape shaped like a bowl: darker = lower error. Click or drag anywhere on the bowl and the line on the left becomes that point. The star marks the bottom — the best line. (It's the same line as your sliders above; they move together.)

Squared vs absolute: the outlier test

Squaring has a price: a point that misses by 30 costs 900, as much as 36 points that miss by 5. So one bad point can drag the whole line. The alternative, absolute error ∑|yᵢ − ŷᵢ|, treats a big miss as just big, not huge. Add an outlier — then drag it — and compare the two lines.

So why square at all?

Squared error is smooth (it has a derivative everywhere, so calculus and gradient descent work), has a one-line closed-form solution, and is exactly right when the noise is bell-shaped (Gaussian). Absolute error is more robust but needs iterative solvers. Pick by the data you have.

Route 1 · the formula

Solving it in one shot

The bowl has exactly one bottom, and at the bottom the ground is flat — the slope of the MSE in every direction is zero. Setting both derivatives to zero and solving gives a formula you can compute with a pencil. Let's do it by hand on five students. The scores are editable: change any y and every number below recomputes.

x hoursy scorex − x̄y − ȳ(x−x̄)(y−ȳ)(x−x̄)²
\[ w_1 = \frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{\sum_i (x_i-\bar{x})^2}, \qquad w_0 = \bar{y} - w_1\,\bar{x} \]
Read it as: the slope is “how much x and y move together” (top) divided by “how much x moves on its own” (bottom). When x is above its average and y is too, the product is positive and pushes the slope up. Then the intercept is chosen so the line goes through the mean point.
Say it“Slope equals the sum of cross-deviations over the sum of squared x-deviations; intercept equals y-bar minus slope times x-bar.”
Where does this formula come from? (two derivatives, set to zero)

Start from MSE(w₀, w₁) = (1/n)∑(yᵢ − w₀ − w₁xᵢ)².

Step 1 — derivative in w₀: ∂MSE/∂w₀ = −(2/n)∑(yᵢ − w₀ − w₁xᵢ). Setting it to 0 gives ∑yᵢ = n·w₀ + w₁∑xᵢ, i.e. w₀ = ȳ − w₁x̄. The residuals of the best line always add up to zero.

Step 2 — derivative in w₁: ∂MSE/∂w₁ = −(2/n)∑xᵢ(yᵢ − w₀ − w₁xᵢ) = 0. Substitute w₀ from step 1 and rearrange: ∑xᵢ(yᵢ − ȳ) = w₁∑xᵢ(xᵢ − x̄), which equals the deviation form above because ∑(xᵢ − x̄) = 0.

Why it's a minimum, not a maximum: the second derivative in w₁ is (2/n)∑xᵢ² > 0 — the bowl curves upward.

The same thing with matrices: the normal equation

\[ \mathbf{w} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y} \]
Read it as: stack the data into a matrix X with a column of 1s (for the intercept) and a column per feature. Then one matrix formula gives all the weights at once — for 1 feature or 1,000. This is what LinearRegression().fit() computes (via a numerically safer route).
Say it“W equals X-transpose-X inverse, times X-transpose y.”
How exactly: building X, XᵀX and Xᵀy for these five rows
Route 2 · rolling downhill

Rolling downhill to the answer

The formula needs (XᵀX)⁻¹, and inverting a matrix with a million features is impossible. So the method that trains every neural network does something humbler: stand somewhere on the bowl, feel which way is downhill (the gradient), take a small step that way, repeat. The step size is the learning rate η. Press Run, then try a tiny and a huge learning rate. Click the bowl to choose where to start.

\[ w_j \leftarrow w_j - \eta\,\frac{\partial\,\text{MSE}}{\partial w_j}, \qquad \frac{\partial\,\text{MSE}}{\partial w_0} = \frac{2}{n}\sum_i(\hat{y}_i - y_i), \quad \frac{\partial\,\text{MSE}}{\partial w_1} = \frac{2}{n}\sum_i(\hat{y}_i - y_i)\,x_i \]
Read it as: each weight moves against its own slope, scaled by η. If the line predicts too high on average, (ŷ − y) is positive, so the intercept goes down. If it's too high mostly where x is big, the slope goes down too.
Say it“New weight equals old weight minus learning-rate times the partial derivative of the loss.”

Controls

Feature x

Raw hours make a long, tilted valley: slope and intercept fight each other and the ball zig-zags. Centring x makes the bowl round, and the same learning rate gets there in a fraction of the steps. This is why we scale features before gradient descent.

Status

Closed form (route 1)

  • Exact answer in one computation
  • No learning rate to tune
  • Cost grows like d³ in the number of features — fine up to a few thousand

Gradient descent (route 2)

  • Approximate, iterative, needs η and scaled features
  • Works for any number of features, and for data streaming in batches (SGD)
  • The same algorithm trains logistic regression and every neural net
Diagnosis

Grading the fit: R² and residuals

MSE has awkward units (“marks squared”), so we also report R²: the share of y's variation that the line explains. Start from the dumbest possible model — always predict the average ȳ — whose error is SST. The line's error is SSE. R² = how much of SST the line removed.

\[ R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_i (y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_i (y_i-\bar{y})^2} \]
Read it as: 1 means perfect predictions; 0 means no better than guessing the average; negative (possible on test data) means worse than the average. But a high R² does not prove the line is the right shape — the residual plot does that.
Say it“R-squared is one minus the residual sum of squares over the total sum of squares.”

Bar = SST, all the variation in y. Red = SSE, what the line could not explain. The rest is R².

The four checks (L-I-N-E)

  • LinearThe mean of y really changes in a straight line with x. Broken by Curved truth → the residuals smile.
  • IndependentOne point's miss tells you nothing about the next. Broken by time series, where today's error predicts tomorrow's.
  • NormalMisses are roughly bell-shaped. Needed for confidence intervals, not for the line itself.
  • Equal varianceThe spread of misses is the same everywhere. Broken by Fanning noise → the residuals form a cone.
Bending the line

Bending a straight line

A surprise: linear regression can fit curves. Make new features x², x³, … and fit a weight to each. The model is still linear in the weights — the same normal equation solves it — but the prediction now bends. The question is how much bending is too much. Fifteen noisy points from a smooth wave; slide the degree.

\[ \hat{y} = w_0 + w_1x + w_2x^2 + \dots + w_dx^d \]
Read it as: a degree-d polynomial; d + 1 weights. Degree 1 is the straight line. Each extra degree lets the curve change direction once more — and gives it one more way to chase noise.
Say it“Y-hat is a polynomial of degree d in x.”

Taming it

Ridge & lasso: a leash on the weights

The degree-12 curve overfits because its weights become enormous: huge positive and negative terms cancelling to thread every training point. Regularisation adds a second cost — a price on big weights — and lets you choose how hard to pull with λ (lambda). Two flavours: ridge charges for squared weights and shrinks them all smoothly; lasso charges for absolute weights and pushes some to exactly zero — it selects features for you.

\[ \text{Ridge:}\;\; \min_{\mathbf{w}} \frac{1}{n}\lVert\mathbf{y}-X\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \lambda\sum_{j\ge1} w_j^2 \]
Read it as: fit error plus λ times the sum of squared weights (the intercept is not charged). Still a closed form: w = (XᵀX + nλI′)⁻¹Xᵀy.
Say it“Minimise the mean squared error plus lambda times the squared length of w.”
\[ \text{Lasso:}\;\; \min_{\mathbf{w}} \frac{1}{2n}\lVert\mathbf{y}-X\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \lambda\sum_{j\ge1} |w_j| \]
Read it as: same idea, but the penalty is the sum of absolute weights. No closed form; solved by coordinate descent (one weight at a time, soft-thresholding it toward zero).
Say it“Minimise half the mean squared error plus lambda times the L1 norm of w.”

Why lasso makes exact zeros: the picture

Imagine only two weights. The loss is a bowl whose bottom (the red star) is the unpenalised answer. The penalty says “stay inside this region”: a circle for ridge, a diamond for lasso. The answer is where the smallest loss contour first touches the region. A diamond has corners sitting on the axes — and contours very often touch a corner first, where one weight is exactly zero. A circle has no corners, so ridge almost never gives exact zeros. Shrink the budget and watch.

Real-world project

Project: forecasting diabetes, a year ahead

442 real diabetes patients (Efron, Hastie, Johnstone & Tibshirani, 2004 — the paper that introduced the LARS algorithm for lasso). At the first visit, doctors recorded ten baseline measurements. One year later they measured how far the disease had progressed, as a score from 25 to 346. Can a straight-line model forecast that score from the first visit?

The feature race: lasso's path

Start with a huge λ (every weight forced to zero) and slowly relax it. Features enter the model one at a time, most useful first. This is how lasso ranks the ten measurements — and it agrees with the 2004 paper: BMI first, then triglycerides, then blood pressure.

The same project in scikit-learn

from sklearn.datasets import load_diabetes
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression, lasso_path
from sklearn.metrics import r2_score, mean_squared_error

d = load_diabetes(scaled=False)
X, y = d.data, d.target
idx = range(len(y))
tr, te = train_test_split(list(idx), test_size=0.25, random_state=42)

def grade(cols):
    sc = StandardScaler().fit(X[tr][:, cols])
    m = LinearRegression().fit(sc.transform(X[tr][:, cols]), y[tr])
    p = m.predict(sc.transform(X[te][:, cols]))
    return r2_score(y[te], p), mean_squared_error(y[te], p) ** 0.5

for name, cols in [("All ten", list(range(10))), ("BMI + BP + s5", [2, 3, 8]), ("BMI only", [2])]:
    r2, rmse = grade(cols)
    print(f"{name:16s} test R² = {r2:.3f}   RMSE = {rmse:.1f}")
All ten          test R² = 0.485   RMSE = 53.4
BMI + BP + s5    test R² = 0.502   RMSE = 52.5
BMI only         test R² = 0.317   RMSE = 61.5

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Linear regression predicts a number as a weighted sum of features: ŷ = w₀ + w₁x₁ + … + w_dx_d.
  • It picks the weights that minimise the mean squared error — a bowl in weight space with one bottom.
  • Reach the bottom by formula (w = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy) or by gradient descent (small steps downhill, scaled features, a sensible η).
  • Judge it by R² on test data and by the residual plot; a smile, a cone or a lone outlier each tell a different story.
  • Polynomial features bend the line; ridge and lasso stop it bending too far, and lasso also chooses features.

Check your understanding

Why can't we just add up the residuals instead of squaring them?

Positive and negative misses cancel. A flat line through the mean has residuals summing to exactly zero, however bad it is. Squaring (or taking absolute values) makes every miss count.

Gradient descent's MSE goes up every step. What happened, and what do you change?

The learning rate is too large: each step overshoots the valley and lands higher on the other side. Lower η, or scale the features so the bowl is rounder — try both in the lab.

A model has R² = 0.95 but the residual plot is a clear U shape. Is the model good?

It's the wrong shape. The line explains a lot of variation, but the misses are systematic: positive at the ends, negative in the middle. Add a curved feature (x²) or transform the data.

In the project, why did three features beat all ten on the test set?

Each extra feature adds a weight estimated from only 331 patients. Weak or redundant features (the six blood tests are strongly correlated) add estimation noise more than signal — variance. Lasso finds this automatically.

You need a model with only the important features, for a clinic form. Ridge or lasso?

Lasso: its L1 penalty sets unhelpful weights to exactly zero, so the remaining features are the ones to measure. Ridge shrinks all weights but keeps every feature.
