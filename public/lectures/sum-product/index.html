Factor Graphs & the Sum‑Product Algorithm

Every marginal on a tree, in two sweeps of messages

Summing a joint distribution over every variable but one costs a number of terms that grows exponentially with the number of variables. On a graph without loops you never have to do that: each node passes its neighbours a short message, a small table that summarises everything on its side of the graph, and two sweeps of messages give every node its exact marginal. The figure is a tree of ten binary variables (circles) joined by factors (squares) that prefer neighbours to agree. Each circle is filled to its probability of being 1. Touch a circle to observe it as 1, then 0, then free again: it becomes the root, and you can watch the messages run in to it and back out.

Bishop 8.4 · inference

Inference: the smallest message

Inference in a graphical model means this: some variables have been observed, and you want the distribution of one or more of the others given what you saw. The graph matters because it tells you how the joint distribution breaks into pieces, and the pieces decide how much work the answer costs. Bishop opens the topic with the smallest possible case: two variables and one arrow, x → y, so that the joint is p(x) p(y | x).

Observe y. Bayes’ theorem turns the arrow round: the same joint can be written p(y) p(x | y), a graph with the arrow pointing the other way, and p(x | y) is the posterior you want. Read it as a message. The observed y sends x a function of x, the likelihood p(y | x) evaluated at the value seen; x multiplies its prior by that message and rescales. Everything in this chapter is that step repeated across a larger graph. The running example is a single bit sent over a noisy channel: x is the bit sent, y the bit received, and the channel flips it with probability ε. Set the prior and the flip probability, then observe y.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(x, y) &= p(x)\,p(y \mid x) \\ &= p(y)\,p(x \mid y) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: one joint distribution, written two ways. The left form follows the arrow x → y; the right form is the reversed graph y → x. Inference is the job of getting from the first form to the second.
Say it“p of x and y is p of x times p of y given x, which is also p of y times p of x given y.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(x \mid y) &= \frac{p(y \mid x)\,p(x)}{p(y)} \\ p(y) &= \textstyle\sum_{x'} p(y \mid x')\,p(x') \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the prior p(x) times the message p(y | x) from the observed node, divided by its total so that it sums to 1. The total, p(y), is the probability of what was observed.
Say it“p of x given y is the likelihood times the prior, over the sum of the same over every x.”

Bishop 8.4.1 · inference on a chain

A chain: pass the sum along

Line up N variables, each with K states, and join neighbours with an undirected link. The joint is a product of one potential per link, divided by a normalising constant Z. (A directed chain is the same thing: the potentials are the conditionals p(xn | xn−1), with the first marginal folded into the first link, and Z = 1.) To get the marginal of one variable xn you must sum the joint over all the others. Done naively that is KN−1 products for each value of xn.

The rescue is the distributive law, ab + ac = a(b + c). The sum over x1 touches only the first potential, so do it first: what is left is a function of x2 alone, a table of K numbers. Call it the message μα(x2). Multiply it by the next potential and sum out x2, and you have μα(x3). Messages run in from the left end (α) and from the right end (β), and the marginal at xn is the product of the two that arrive there. Here every link uses ψ(a, b) = eJ when a = b and 1 otherwise, so J > 0 makes neighbours like to agree. Observed nodes are clamped: the sums simply skip every state except the one seen. Click a node to clamp it, then step through the messages.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{Z}\,&\psi_{1,2}(x_1,x_2)\,\psi_{2,3}(x_2,x_3) \\ &\cdots\,\psi_{N-1,N}(x_{N-1},x_N) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the chain’s joint distribution is the product of one potential per link, each a K × K table of positive numbers, rescaled by Z so that the total over all KN configurations is 1.
Say it“p of x is one over Z times psi-one-two, psi-two-three, and so on up to psi N-minus-one N.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_\alpha(x_n) &= \sum_{x_{n-1}} \psi_{n-1,n}(x_{n-1},x_n) \\ &\qquad \times \mu_\alpha(x_{n-1}) \\ \mu_\beta(x_n) &= \sum_{x_{n+1}} \psi_{n,n+1}(x_n,x_{n+1}) \\ &\qquad \times \mu_\beta(x_{n+1}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the message arriving at xn from the left is the previous message pushed through one more link and summed over the previous variable; μβ does the same from the right. The two ends start with a message of all ones. A clamped variable keeps only its observed state in the sum.
Say it“mu-alpha of x-n is the sum over x-n-minus-one of psi times the previous mu-alpha.”
\[ p(x_n) = \frac{1}{Z}\,\mu_\alpha(x_n)\,\mu_\beta(x_n) \]
Read it as: everything to the left of xn is summarised by one message and everything to the right by another, so the marginal is just their product, rescaled. Neither message needs to know which node you were interested in.
Say it“p of x-n is one over Z times mu-alpha times mu-beta.”

Bishop 8.4.1 · cost, Z and neighbouring pairs

Every marginal for the price of two passes

The message arriving at xn from the left does not depend on which node you want next. So run one pass from left to right, storing every μα, and one pass from right to left, storing every μβ: every marginal on the chain then costs one multiplication of two stored tables. Each message is a K × K table times a vector of K numbers, about K² operations, and there are 2(N − 1) of them. The work grows linearly in N, against KN for brute force.

Two more things fall out for free. The normaliser Z is the total of μαμβ at any node, so it comes out the same at all of them, which makes a handy check. And the joint distribution of two neighbours, p(xn−1, xn), uses the same messages with the link’s potential in between. You will need those pairwise marginals when the potentials are learned from data, later in the book (the EM algorithm for hidden Markov models uses exactly these quantities). Raise N and K and watch the gap open; then pick a link of the chain above.

\[ Z = \sum_{x_n} \mu_\alpha(x_n)\,\mu_\beta(x_n) \]
Read it as: the normalising constant can be read off at whichever node n you like. If the potentials are conditionals of a directed chain and nothing is clamped, Z is 1; with clamped nodes it is the probability of the observed values.
Say it“Z is the sum over x-n of mu-alpha times mu-beta, at any n.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(x_{n-1}, x_n) = \tfrac{1}{Z}\,&\mu_\alpha(x_{n-1}) \\ &\times \psi_{n-1,n}(x_{n-1},x_n) \\ &\times \mu_\beta(x_n) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the joint of two neighbours is the message from the far left, the link between them and the message from the far right, multiplied: a K × K table that sums to 1.
Say it“p of x-n-minus-one and x-n is mu-alpha, times psi, times mu-beta, over Z.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{brute force:}&\quad N\,K^{N} \\ \text{two passes:}&\quad 2(N-1)\,K^{2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: rough operation counts for all N marginals. Brute force visits all KN configurations and adds each one into N marginals; message passing does 2(N − 1) small matrix–vector products. The first is exponential in N, the second linear.
Say it“N K to the N against two N-minus-one K squared.”

Bishop 8.4.2 · trees

Trees: exactly one path between any two nodes

Nothing in the chain argument needed the nodes to be in a line. It needed each sum to cut the graph cleanly in two, and that works whenever the graph is a tree: there is exactly one path between any two nodes, so there are no loops. For an undirected graph that is the whole definition. For a directed graph Bishop distinguishes two kinds:

A directed tree has one node with no parent, the root, and every other node has exactly one parent. Converting it to an undirected graph adds no links, because moralisation (joining the parents of each node) has nothing to join; the result is an undirected tree. A polytree lets a node have several parents, while still having only one path between any two nodes if you ignore the arrows. Moralise a polytree and the married parents form loops, so the undirected graph is no longer a tree. The fix is in the next section: draw the model as a factor graph, and a polytree stays a tree. Pick a graph, then switch between the three views.

\[ \text{independent loops} = E - V + C \]
Read it as: E is the number of links, V the number of nodes and C the number of separate pieces. A tree with V nodes has exactly V − 1 links, so the count is 0; each extra link closes one more loop. In a factor graph, count both circles and squares as nodes.
Say it“The number of loops is edges minus vertices plus components.”

Bishop 8.4.3 · Goodfellow et al. 16.2.7 · factor graphs

Factor graphs: draw the factors too

Directed and undirected graphs both say the same kind of thing: the joint distribution is a product of functions, each of a few variables. A factor graph draws those functions explicitly. Every variable gets a circle, every factor gets a square, and each square is linked to the circles of the variables it uses. Links only ever join a circle to a square, so the graph is bipartite. For a directed model the factors are the conditionals (one per node, including the root’s plain marginal); for an undirected model they are the clique potentials, with 1/Z as a factor of no variables.

Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville give the reason to bother. An undirected triangle a – b – c does not say whether the model has one factor over all three variables (K³ numbers) or three pairwise factors (3K² numbers). The graph is the same; the models are not. A three-way factor can say things pairwise ones cannot: with binary variables, “a, b and c contain an even number of 1s” has no pairwise form, because any pairwise factor that ruled out the odd triple (1, 0, 0) would also rule out one of the even triples (1, 0, 1), (0, 0, 0) or (1, 1, 0). The same directed or undirected graph can therefore have several factor graphs. Grouping factors can also remove a loop, as it does for the triangle, at the price of a bigger table. Pick a graph and a factorisation, then raise K.

\[ p(\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{s} f_s(\mathbf{x}_s) \]
Read it as: the joint is a product of factors fs, each a function of its own subset xs of the variables. A factor graph is a picture of exactly this sentence: one square per fs, linked to the variables in xs.
Say it“p of x is the product over s of f-s of x-s.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p_{\text{one}} &\propto f(a,b,c) \\ p_{\text{pair}} &\propto f_1(a,b)\,f_2(b,c)\,f_3(a,c) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: two different models with the same undirected triangle. The first needs a table over every triple; the second three smaller tables, but it cannot express interactions that involve all three variables at once.
Say it“Either one factor of a, b and c, or three factors, one for each pair.”

Bishop 8.4.4 · the two messages

Sum-product: two kinds of message

Take a tree factor graph and one variable x. Cut each link at x and the graph falls into separate subtrees, one hanging off each factor that touches x. Because they share nothing, the sum over all the other variables splits into one sum per subtree, and each of those is a function of x alone: a message from that factor to x. Following the same argument one level down gives the whole algorithm, which needs only two rules.

A factor sends to one of its variables: multiply the factor by the messages arriving from all its other variables, then sum those variables out. That is the “sum” and the “product” in the name. A variable sends to one of its factors: multiply the messages arriving from all its other factors (no sum, because the variable is not being removed). The leaves start things off: a variable at a leaf sends all ones, and a factor at a leaf sends its own table. On a chain each variable has only one other factor, so the variable rule just passes the message through, which is why the last section needed only one kind. Two tables matter later: the equality factor (all three variables equal) is the repetition code of the project, and the parity factor is the building block of modern error-correcting codes. Choose the factor’s table, then drag the incoming messages.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_{f \to x}(x) &= \sum_{x_1}\cdots\sum_{x_M} \\ &\quad f(x, x_1, \ldots, x_M) \\ &\quad \times \prod_{m=1}^{M} \mu_{x_m \to f}(x_m) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for each value of x, go through every combination of the factor’s other variables, multiply the factor’s entry by the messages those variables sent in, and add it all up. The cost is one pass over the factor’s table.
Say it“The message from f to x is the sum over the other variables of f times the product of their messages.”
\[ \mu_{x \to f}(x) = \prod_{l \in \mathrm{ne}(x) \setminus f} \mu_{f_l \to x}(x) \]
Read it as: ne(x) is the set of factors linked to x. The message x sends to f is the product of the messages from every other neighbouring factor, value by value. Leaving out f’s own message is what stops evidence from echoing back to where it came from.
Say it“The message from x to f is the product of the messages from x’s other factors.”
\[ \mu_{x \to f}(x) = 1, \qquad \mu_{f \to x}(x) = f(x) \]
Read it as: how the leaves start. A variable with no other factor has nothing to multiply, so it sends a message of ones; a factor with no other variable has nothing to sum, so it sends its own table.
Say it“A leaf variable sends one; a leaf factor sends itself.”

Bishop 8.4.4 · all marginals

Two passes, every marginal

A node may send a message along a link once it has heard from all its other links. Leaves can send at once. To get every marginal, pick any variable as the root. In the first pass messages flow inward from the leaves: each node waits for its subtrees, then sends towards the root. When the root has heard from all its neighbours, its marginal is ready. In the second pass the root sends outward, and the messages spread back to the leaves. Now every node has heard from every neighbour, so every marginal is available, and so is the joint marginal of the variables of each factor. Each link has carried one message in each direction: twice as many messages as links, whichever root you chose.

The tree below has six binary variables and five factors, one of them a three-way factor, with small whole-number tables so that every message can be checked by hand (hover any number). The tables are not normalised, so the products are unnormalised too; Z, the sum of any variable’s unnormalised marginal, is the same at every node. Click a circle to make it the root, then step through the messages.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(x) &= \frac{1}{Z} \prod_{s \in \mathrm{ne}(x)} \mu_{f_s \to x}(x) \\ Z &= \sum_{x} \prod_{s \in \mathrm{ne}(x)} \mu_{f_s \to x}(x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: once every factor next to x has sent its message, the marginal of x is their product, rescaled. The total of that product is the normaliser Z, and every variable gives the same total.
Say it“p of x is the product of the messages from x’s factors, over Z.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{x}_s) = \frac{1}{Z}\, &f_s(\mathbf{x}_s) \\ \times &\prod_{i \in \mathrm{ne}(f_s)} \mu_{x_i \to f_s}(x_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the joint marginal of a factor’s own variables is the factor’s table multiplied by the messages its variables sent to it: the factor’s view of the rest of the graph.
Say it“p of x-s is f-s times the messages from its variables, over Z.”

Bishop 8.4.4 · observed variables

Evidence: clamp, then send the same messages

So far nothing was observed. Usually some variables v have been seen with values v̂, and you want the others, h, given them. Multiply the joint by an indicator for each observation, I(vi, v̂i), which is 1 when vi takes its observed value and 0 otherwise. In message terms an observed variable sends out messages that are zero for every state but the one seen. Then run exactly the same two passes. Every hidden node ends up with the unnormalised p(x, v̂), its total is p(v̂), the probability of the evidence, and dividing gives the posterior p(x | v̂).

The example is a bit broadcast down a tree. The root bit goes to two relays, each relay passes it on to two receivers, and every hop flips the bit with probability ε. As a directed model it is a directed tree, and its factor graph is a tree. Observe some receivers (or relays) and ask what the root sent. The posterior is not a head count: two receivers that share a relay are not independent witnesses, so together they are worth less than two separate votes. Click receivers to set what they heard, then change ε.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{h}, \hat{\mathbf{v}}) &= p(\mathbf{x}) \prod_i I(v_i, \hat v_i) \\ p(x \mid \hat{\mathbf{v}}) &= \frac{p(x, \hat{\mathbf{v}})}{p(\hat{\mathbf{v}})} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: clamping multiplies the joint by indicators, so summing over every variable still gives the right total, but only configurations that agree with the evidence survive. The sum-product messages then deliver p(x, v̂); dividing by its total, p(v̂), gives the posterior.
Say it“Clamp with indicators, run the same messages, divide by p of v-hat.”
\[ f(a, b) = p(b \mid a) = \begin{cases} 1 - \varepsilon & b = a \\ \varepsilon & b \neq a \end{cases} \]
Read it as: each hop of the broadcast is one factor joining the sender a and the receiver b: the bit arrives intact with probability 1 − ε and flipped with probability ε. The root has its own prior factor, p(r).
Say it“f of a and b is one minus epsilon if they match, epsilon if they don’t.”

Real-world project

Project: decoding one aircraft reply that was sent 49 times

The data is the test recording that ships with dump1090, Salvatore Sanfilippo’s Mode S decoder for cheap RTL-SDR radio dongles: testfiles/modes1.bin, 356,868 raw 8-bit I/Q samples taken at 2 million samples a second (0.178 s of radio), tuned to 1090 MHz, the channel aircraft transponders reply on. The repository is licensed BSD 3-Clause (copyright 2012–present, Salvatore Sanfilippo); this page embeds 49 slices of 128 samples from the file, unchanged, plus a coarse envelope of the whole recording. The file was downloaded from github.com/antirez/dump1090.

Mode S sends each bit in 1 µs, split into two half-microsecond chips: a pulse in the first chip means 1, in the second chip 0. At 2 MHz that is exactly one sample per chip. Each transmission starts with an 8 µs preamble of four pulses. A short reply has 56 bits: a 5-bit format number, 3 bits of capability, the transponder’s 24-bit address and a 24-bit parity check (a CRC). One transponder in this recording (address 4D2023 in the data) sends the same 56-bit all-call reply, format 11, again and again. A decoder like dump1090’s finds 49 receptions of it: 45 identical copies that pass the parity check, and 4 that differ from them in 1 to 4 bits and fail it. (Replies with the same address but a different parity field, which all-call replies use to carry an interrogator code, are different messages and are left out.)

Sending the same bits R times is a repetition code, and decoding it is inference on a tree factor graph: one variable per bit, linked to one factor per reception, each factor carrying that reception’s evidence about the bit. The recording is clean (the aircraft was close), so the lab adds complex Gaussian noise of standard deviation σ to every I and Q sample (seeded, so every number here can be reproduced) and asks three decoders to recover the word: the first copy alone, a majority vote of the copies’ hard decisions, and sum-product on the factor graph. The frame timing is taken from the clean recording; only the bit decisions are redone.

2 · The factor graph of a repetition code

For one bit xi, reception k supplies two magnitudes, mfirst and msecond, one per chip. Model each as the pulse level Ak or the gap level Bk (both measured on that reception’s preamble) plus Gaussian noise of variance σ0² + σ², where σ0 = 2.48 is the recording’s own noise level, estimated from the median magnitude of the whole file. The factor for reception k is then the likelihood of its two chips, and its message to xi is summarised by one number, a log-likelihood ratio. The factor graph for each bit is a star, a tree, so sum-product is exact: the posterior is the product of the R messages, which in logs is a sum. Each bit has its own star, so the 56 stars are solved separately. (The parity check ties all 56 bits together and would create loops; here it is used only to check the result.)

\[ \begin{aligned} L_{k,i} &= \log \frac{\mu_{f_k \to x_i}(1)}{\mu_{f_k \to x_i}(0)} \\ &= \frac{(A_k - B_k)\,(m_{\text{first}} - m_{\text{second}})}{\sigma_0^2 + \sigma^2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: under the Gaussian model, reception k’s evidence about bit i is the difference between its two chips, weighted by how strong that reception’s pulses are and divided by the noise variance. Positive favours 1, negative 0, near 0 means “can’t tell”. (The weight Ak − Bk is floored at 1.)
Say it“L-k-i is A minus B times the chip difference, over the noise variance.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(x_i = 1 \mid \text{all } R) &= \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\Lambda_i}} \\ \Lambda_i &= \sum_{k=1}^{R} L_{k,i} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with a flat prior, the variable node multiplies the R factor messages, so their log-ratios add. The decoded bit is 1 when the total Λi is positive. A strong, clean copy outweighs a faint, doubtful one; a majority vote counts them equally and ignores the doubt.
Say it“Add the R log-likelihood ratios, then squash the total with the sigmoid.”

4 · The scoreboard

The same experiment in Python

The script below rebuilds the data file from the recording, then repeats every decoder on this page with the same random-number generator (mulberry32 and the same Box–Muller draws), so its table can be compared line by line with the browser’s. It also checks the tree labs above against brute-force enumeration.

import json, sys, math, itertools
import numpy as np
S = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "./"
M32 = 0xFFFFFFFF
def rng(a):                                   # mulberry32, the generator the page uses
    st = [a & M32]
    def r():
        st[0] = (st[0] + 0x6D2B79F5) & M32; a = st[0]
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & M32
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & M32)) & M32) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M32) / 4294967296
    return r
def gauss(r):                                 # Box-Muller, cosine branch, as on the page
    u = 0
    while u == 0: u = r()
    return math.sqrt(-2 * math.log(u)) * math.cos(2 * math.pi * r())

# ---------- brute-force checks of the tree labs ----------
def brute(K, F, ev=None):
    """K: states per variable; F: list of (vars, table, last variable fastest); ev: {var: state}."""
    mg = [np.zeros(k) for k in K]; Z = 0.0
    for x in itertools.product(*[range(k) for k in K]):
        if ev and any(x[v] != s for v, s in ev.items()): continue
        p = 1.0
        for vs, t in F:
            i = 0
            for v in vs: i = i * K[v] + x[v]
            p *= t[i]
        Z += p
        for v in range(len(K)): mg[v][x[v]] += p
    return mg, Z

psi = [math.e if a == b else 1.0 for a in range(3) for b in range(3)]
mg, Z = brute([3] * 6, [((n, n + 1), psi) for n in range(5)], {0: 0, 5: 2})
print("chain lab, N=6, J=1, x1=1, x6=3: Z = %.6f" % Z)
print("  p(x3) =", [round(float(v), 6) for v in mg[2] / Z], "  p(x4) =", [round(float(v), 6) for v in mg[3] / Z])
# the two-pass lab's default tables: mulberry32(1), entries 1 + floor(9 r)
r = rng(1); V = [(0, 1), (1, 2, 3), (3, 4), (3, 5), (0,)]
F = [(v, [1 + math.floor(r() * 9) for _ in range(2 ** len(v))]) for v in V]
mg, Z = brute([2] * 6, F)
print("two-pass lab, tables", [t for _, t in F])
print("  Z = %d; p(x=1) for x1..x6:" % Z, [round(float(m[1] / Z), 6) for m in mg])

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Inference means summing the joint over the variables you don’t care about. Pushing each sum inside the product, as far as it will go, turns an exponential sum into a sequence of small local ones.
  • On a chain, a forward pass of μα messages and a backward pass of μβ give every marginal, the normaliser Z and every neighbouring pair’s joint, in O(NK²) work instead of O(KN).
  • A factor graph draws each factor as a square linked to its variables. It removes the ambiguity of undirected graphs, and it turns a polytree into a tree.
  • Sum-product needs two rules: a factor multiplies in the messages from its other variables and sums them out; a variable multiplies the messages from its other factors. Leaves start with ones (variables) or their own table (factors).
  • Two passes through any root give every marginal and every factor’s joint marginal on a tree. Evidence is just clamping, and the total of a clamped marginal is the probability of the evidence.

Check your understanding

A chain has N = 20 binary variables. Roughly how many products does brute force need for one marginal, and how many does message passing need for all twenty?

Brute force sums over the 219 ≈ 524,000 settings of the other variables for each of the two values of the target, about a million products of 19 factors each. Message passing sends 2 × 19 = 38 messages, each about K² = 4 multiply–adds, so roughly 150 operations, plus 20 small products for the marginals.

Why does the message from x to f leave out the message that f sent to x?

That message carries the evidence from f’s side of the tree. If x included it in what it sends back to f, that evidence would be counted twice when f combines it with its own side. Leaving it out means each piece of evidence reaches every node exactly once, which is why the result is exact on a tree.

A node has two parents, and the graph has no other loops. Is the moralised graph a tree? Is the factor graph?

The moralised graph is not a tree: joining the two parents closes a triangle with the child. The factor graph is a tree: the conditional p(child | both parents) becomes one square linked to three circles, and no loop is formed. That is why sum-product on a factor graph handles polytrees exactly.

After both passes, how do you get the probability of the evidence?

Take any variable’s unnormalised marginal, the product of its incoming messages with the evidence indicators applied, and add up its entries. For a directed model, where the factors are normalised conditionals, that total is exactly p(v̂). Every variable gives the same total.

In the project, why can sum-product beat a majority vote with the same copies?

A majority vote throws away how sure each copy was. Sum-product adds log-likelihood ratios, so a copy whose two chips are almost equal (a doubtful bit) counts for little, and a strong, clean copy counts for a lot. The vote only matches it when every copy is equally reliable.
