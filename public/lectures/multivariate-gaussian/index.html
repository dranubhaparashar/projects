The Multivariate Gaussian

Geometry, conditionals and marginals

One distribution turns up in almost every chapter of this book: in linear regression’s noise, in Gaussian mixtures, in Kalman filters, in the latent space of a VAE. In many dimensions it is a cloud shaped like an ellipse, and two things make it so useful: slice the cloud along any line and the slice is Gaussian; project it onto any axis and the shadow is Gaussian too. On the right, move along the cloud to slice it, and drag the white handle to stretch and turn it.

Start small

The bell curve in one dimension

A Gaussian (or normal) distribution over one number x has two settings: the mean μ, where the bell is centred, and the variance σ², how wide it spreads. Its standard deviation σ is the natural ruler: about 68% of the probability lies within one σ of the mean, and about 95% within two. The curve is a density, not a probability: areas under it are probabilities, and its height can be larger than 1 when the bell is narrow. Move the marker x, then squeeze σ below 0.4 and watch the peak rise above 1.

\[ \mathcal{N}(x\mid\mu,\sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big) \]
Read it as: measure how far x is from the mean in units of σ (that is z = (x − μ)/σ), and the height of the bell falls off as e−z²/2. The factor in front makes the total area exactly 1, so a wider bell must be lower.
Say it“The normal density of x with mean mu and variance sigma squared is one over root two pi sigma squared, times e to the minus x minus mu squared over two sigma squared.”
density p(x)
z = (x − μ)/σ
chance of landing closer to μ

Four facts summarise the bell, and they are the one-dimensional case of the table of properties that every probability textbook keeps for the Gaussian. The inverse of the variance has its own name, the precision β: a large precision means a narrow, confident bell. The entropy H measures how spread out, how unpredictable, the distribution is; it grows with the logarithm of σ and we will meet it again at the end of the chapter.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}[x]=\operatorname{mode}[x]=\mu,\qquad \operatorname{var}[x]=\sigma^2 \\ &\beta = \frac{1}{\sigma^2},\qquad \mathrm{H}[x] = \tfrac12\ln\!\big(2\pi e\,\sigma^2\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the average, the most likely value and the centre of symmetry are all the same point, μ. The spread is σ²; its reciprocal β is the precision. The entropy (in nats, because of the natural log) depends only on σ, never on where the bell sits.
Say it“The mean and the mode are mu, the variance is sigma squared, the precision beta is one over sigma squared, and the entropy is a half log of two pi e sigma squared.”
Many dimensions

The density in D dimensions

Now let x be a vector of D numbers: two heights, or a pixel’s red, green and blue. The mean becomes a vector μ, and the variance becomes a D×D covariance matrix Σ. Its diagonal holds each coordinate’s variance; its off-diagonal entries say how pairs of coordinates move together. In 2-D, with standard deviations σ1, σ2 and correlation ρ, the off-diagonal entry is ρσ1σ2.

The exponent no longer measures plain distance. It uses the Mahalanobis distance Δ, which stretches the ruler along the directions where the cloud is wide and shrinks it where the cloud is narrow. The lines of equal density are ellipses of equal Δ. Drag the two points P and Q: they start the same ordinary distance from the centre, yet one of them is far more surprising.

\[ \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{D/2}\,|\boldsymbol\Sigma|^{1/2}}\,\exp\!\big(-\tfrac12\Delta^2\big) \]
Read it as: the same bell as before, with the squared z-score replaced by Δ². |Σ| is the determinant of the covariance, a measure of the cloud’s volume; dividing by its square root keeps the total probability at 1, just as dividing by σ did in one dimension.
Say it“The Gaussian density of x is one over two pi to the D over two times root det Sigma, times e to the minus a half Delta squared.”
\[ \Delta^2 = (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)^{\mathsf T}\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\,(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu) \]
Read it as: take the offset from the mean, and weigh it by the inverse covariance. An offset along a wide direction of the cloud costs little; the same offset across a narrow direction costs a lot. With Σ = I this is the ordinary squared distance.
Say it“Delta squared is x minus mu, transposed, times Sigma inverse, times x minus mu.”

Geometry

Eigenvectors: the cloud’s own axes

Every covariance matrix is symmetric, so it has D perpendicular eigenvectors ui, with eigenvalues λi. They are the ellipse’s axes: ui points along an axis and √λi is how far the Δ = 1 ellipse reaches along it. All the eigenvalues must be positive, otherwise the cloud would be flat in some direction and the density would not exist.

Measure each point in these axes, yi = uiT(x − μ), and something remarkable happens: the coordinates stop talking to each other. The D-dimensional Gaussian becomes D separate one-dimensional bells, one per axis, multiplied together. Build the cloud from its axes, then slide from x to y to z: first shift and rotate, then divide each axis by its √λ. The correlation vanishes and a round cloud is left.

\[ \boldsymbol\Sigma = \sum_{i=1}^{D}\lambda_i\,\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{u}_i^{\mathsf T}, \qquad \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} = \sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{1}{\lambda_i}\,\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{u}_i^{\mathsf T} \]
Read it as: the covariance is built from its axes, each direction ui weighted by its variance λi. Inverting it just flips each weight to 1/λi: that is why a wide direction is cheap in Δ².
Say it“Sigma is the sum of lambda-i u-i u-i transpose; its inverse is the sum of one over lambda-i u-i u-i transpose.”
\[ \begin{aligned} y_i &= \mathbf{u}_i^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu), \qquad \Delta^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i} \\ p(\mathbf{y}) &= \prod_{i=1}^{D}\mathcal{N}(y_i\mid 0,\lambda_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: in the eigen-coordinates y the Mahalanobis distance is a sum of squared z-scores, one per axis, so the density splits into a product of independent bells with variances λi. The change of variables is a rotation, which does not stretch volume, so no extra factor appears.
Say it“y-i is u-i transpose times x minus mu; Delta squared is the sum of y-i squared over lambda-i; and p of y is a product of D one-dimensional Gaussians.”
covariance Σ
correlation of the 600 dots

The eigenvalues also give the cloud’s volume and its entropy: the determinant is their product, and the entropy is the D-dimensional version of the one we saw for the bell. And they show why a full covariance is expensive. A D-dimensional Gaussian has D means and D(D+1)/2 distinct covariance entries. Forcing Σ to be diagonal (axis-aligned ellipses) or isotropic, σ²I (circles), saves parameters at the cost of shape. Turn D up to see how fast the full count grows.

\[ \begin{aligned} |\boldsymbol\Sigma| &= \prod_{i=1}^{D}\lambda_i \\ \mathrm{H}[\mathbf{x}] &= \tfrac12\ln|\boldsymbol\Sigma| + \tfrac D2\big(1+\ln 2\pi\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the volume of the cloud is the product of its axes’ variances. The entropy grows with the log of that volume; for D = 1 it is the ½ln(2πeσ²) from before.
Say it“The determinant of Sigma is the product of the eigenvalues; the entropy is a half log det Sigma plus D over two times one plus log two pi.”
\[ \underbrace{\tfrac12 D(D+3)}_{\text{full }\boldsymbol\Sigma} \qquad \underbrace{2D}_{\text{diagonal}} \qquad \underbrace{D+1}_{\text{isotropic}} \]
Read it as: the number of free parameters (means plus covariance entries) under each choice. The full count grows like D², which is why high-dimensional models often restrict the covariance, or use the structured forms of later chapters (mixtures, factor analysis, PCA).
Say it“A full Gaussian has D times D plus three over two parameters; a diagonal one two D; an isotropic one D plus one.”

From data

Fitting a Gaussian, and the biased variance

Given N data points, which μ and σ² should we choose? Maximum likelihood picks the values that make the observed data most probable. Because the points are independent, their joint density is a product, and its logarithm is a sum that is easy to maximise. Set the derivatives to zero and the answers are the ones you would guess: the sample mean, and the average squared deviation from it.

There is a catch. The deviations are measured from the sample mean, which sits in the middle of this particular sample, closer to its points than the true mean is. So the spread is underestimated, on average by a factor (N − 1)/N. With five points that is 20% too small. Draw thousands of samples and watch the average of σ²ML settle below the truth.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mu,\sigma^2) = &-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{n=1}^N (x_n-\mu)^2 \\ &- \frac N2\ln\sigma^2 - \frac N2\ln(2\pi) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the log of the product of N bells. The first term rewards a mean close to the points; the second penalises a wide bell, so σ² cannot simply grow to swallow every point. Maximising over μ does not involve σ², so we can find μ first.
Say it“The log-likelihood is minus one over two sigma squared times the sum of squared deviations, minus N over two log sigma squared, minus N over two log two pi.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_{\mathrm{ML}} &= \frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N x_n \\ \sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}} &= \frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N (x_n-\mu_{\mathrm{ML}})^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the best-fitting mean is the sample average; the best-fitting variance is the mean squared distance from that average. They depend on the data only through two sums, Σxn and Σxn²: these are the sufficient statistics.
Say it“Mu M-L is the average of the x's; sigma squared M-L is the average squared deviation from mu M-L.”
\[ \mathbb{E}[\mu_{\mathrm{ML}}] = \mu, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}}] = \frac{N-1}{N}\,\sigma^2 \]
Read it as: averaged over many samples the mean estimate is right on target (unbiased), but the variance estimate is too small by (N − 1)/N. Dividing by N − 1 instead of N removes the bias. The gap fades as N grows, which is why it rarely matters for big data and matters a lot for small samples.
Say it“The expected mu M-L is mu; the expected sigma squared M-L is N minus one over N times sigma squared.”

Everything carries over to D dimensions. The maximum-likelihood mean is the average vector, and the maximum-likelihood covariance is the average of the outer products of the deviations. It is biased by the same factor (N − 1)/N, and its sufficient statistics are Σxn and ΣxnxnT. The project at the end fits exactly this to Galton’s families.

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol\mu_{\mathrm{ML}} &= \frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N \mathbf{x}_n, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol\Sigma_{\mathrm{ML}}] = \frac{N-1}{N}\,\boldsymbol\Sigma \\ \boldsymbol\Sigma_{\mathrm{ML}} &= \frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N (\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_{\mathrm{ML}})(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_{\mathrm{ML}})^{\mathsf T} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each entry of ΣML is an average product of deviations: on the diagonal, a variance; off the diagonal, how two coordinates co-vary. numpy’s np.cov(X.T, bias=True) computes this; the default bias=False divides by N − 1.
Say it“Mu M-L is the average vector; Sigma M-L is the average of x-n minus mu M-L times its transpose; its expectation is N minus one over N times Sigma.”
Slicing

Conditional Gaussians: slicing a cloud

Split the vector into two parts, xa and xb, and split μ and Σ to match. Now suppose we learn xb. What do we believe about xa? Geometrically we take a slice through the cloud at the known xb, and renormalise it. The slice of a Gaussian is again a Gaussian, with a mean that moves in a straight line as xb changes and a variance that does not depend on xb at all.

Here the cloud is 2-D, xb is the horizontal coordinate and xa the vertical one. The page has drawn 6,000 points from the Gaussian; the ones inside a thin strip around the slice give a check by counting. Drag the slice across the cloud and turn the correlation.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{x} &= \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}_a\\ \mathbf{x}_b\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \boldsymbol\mu = \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol\mu_a\\ \boldsymbol\mu_b\end{pmatrix} \\ \boldsymbol\Sigma &= \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{aa} & \boldsymbol\Sigma_{ab}\\ \boldsymbol\Sigma_{ba} & \boldsymbol\Sigma_{bb}\end{pmatrix} \\ \boldsymbol\Lambda &=\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol\Lambda_{aa} & \boldsymbol\Lambda_{ab}\\ \boldsymbol\Lambda_{ba} & \boldsymbol\Lambda_{bb}\end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: bookkeeping. Σaa holds the variances within part a, Σab the covariances between the parts, and Σba = ΣabT. Λ, the inverse covariance, is the precision matrix, split the same way. Careful: Λaa is not the inverse of Σaa.
Say it“Split x into a and b; split mu and Sigma into matching blocks; Lambda, the precision, is Sigma inverse, split the same way.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol\mu_{a\mid b} &= \boldsymbol\mu_a + \boldsymbol\Sigma_{ab}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_b-\boldsymbol\mu_b) \\ \boldsymbol\Sigma_{a\mid b} &= \boldsymbol\Sigma_{aa} - \boldsymbol\Sigma_{ab}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{ba} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: start from a’s own mean and correct it by how surprising xb is, scaled by how strongly a and b co-vary. The correction is linear in xb. The variance shrinks by a fixed amount, whatever value xb takes: knowing b always helps (or at worst, when Σab = 0, changes nothing).
Say it“Mu a given b is mu a plus Sigma a b, Sigma b b inverse, times x b minus mu b. Sigma a given b is Sigma a a minus Sigma a b, Sigma b b inverse, Sigma b a.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol\Sigma_{a\mid b} &= \boldsymbol\Lambda_{aa}^{-1} \\ \boldsymbol\mu_{a\mid b} &= \boldsymbol\mu_a - \boldsymbol\Lambda_{aa}^{-1}\boldsymbol\Lambda_{ab}(\mathbf{x}_b-\boldsymbol\mu_b) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same answer in the precision’s language, and it is simpler: the conditional covariance is just the inverse of one block. It comes from completing the square: write out the exponent −½Δ², keep only the terms that involve xa, and read off the quadratic part (the precision) and the linear part (which fixes the mean).
Say it“Sigma a given b is Lambda a a inverse; mu a given b is mu a minus Lambda a a inverse Lambda a b times x b minus mu b.”

Projecting

Marginal Gaussians: projecting a cloud

The other move is to forget part of the vector: to integrate xb out and ask only about xa. Geometrically, we shine a light along the b-direction and look at the cloud’s shadow. For a Gaussian the answer needs no work at all: the marginal is Gaussian, and its mean and covariance are simply the a-blocks of μ and Σ.

The same is true for any direction w: the projected value wTx is Gaussian, with variance wTΣw. Turn the direction w and compare the histogram of the 6,000 projected points with the formula. Notice that a shadow is always at least as wide as a slice in the same direction: forgetting xb leaves you less certain than knowing it.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{x}_a) &= \int p(\mathbf{x}_a,\mathbf{x}_b)\,d\mathbf{x}_b = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_a\mid\boldsymbol\mu_a,\boldsymbol\Sigma_{aa}) \\ \boldsymbol\Sigma_{aa} &= \big(\boldsymbol\Lambda_{aa}-\boldsymbol\Lambda_{ab}\boldsymbol\Lambda_{bb}^{-1}\boldsymbol\Lambda_{ba}\big)^{-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: to marginalise, just read off the blocks of the covariance. In the precision’s language it is the other way round: marginalising is the awkward one, while conditioning was easy. That symmetry is worth remembering: covariance form for marginals, precision form for conditionals.
Say it“The marginal of x a is the Gaussian with mean mu a and covariance Sigma a a, which equals Lambda a a minus Lambda a b, Lambda b b inverse, Lambda b a, all inverted.”
\[ \mathbf{w}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x} \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\mu,\ \mathbf{w}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\Sigma\,\mathbf{w}\big) \]
Read it as: any linear combination of jointly Gaussian variables is Gaussian. Its mean is the same combination of the means; its variance is the quadratic form wTΣw. With w = (1, 0) it is the marginal of x1; with w = u1 the variance is the largest eigenvalue.
Say it“w transpose x is Gaussian with mean w transpose mu and variance w transpose Sigma w.”

Putting it together

Bayes’ theorem for linear-Gaussian models

A pattern that runs through this book: an unknown x has a Gaussian prior, and we observe y, a linear function of x plus Gaussian noise. A robot’s position and a GPS fix; a true signal and a noisy sensor; the weights of a linear regression and the data. Joining the two Gaussians gives one big Gaussian over (x, y), and then the two moves we just learned do all the work: marginalise to predict y, condition to learn about x. Every answer is Gaussian and has a closed form.

Below, x is a position on a map. The prior (grey) is tilted: we believe east and north errors go together. Drag the measurement y and switch sensors. With the east-only sensor we measure one coordinate, yet the posterior moves in both, because the prior links them.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{x}) &= \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu,\ \boldsymbol\Lambda^{-1}\big) \\ p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}) &= \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b},\ \mathbf{L}^{-1}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the prior over x has mean μ and precision Λ. The measurement is a linear map of x (matrix A, offset b) plus noise with precision L. y can have fewer dimensions than x: the east-only sensor has A = (1 0).
Say it“x is Gaussian with mean mu and covariance Lambda inverse; y given x is Gaussian with mean A x plus b and covariance L inverse.”
\[ p(\mathbf{y}) = \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\mu+\mathbf{b},\ \mathbf{L}^{-1}+\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\Lambda^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}\big) \]
Read it as: before measuring, the reading we expect is the prior mean pushed through the sensor. Its uncertainty adds two sources: the sensor’s own noise L−1, and our uncertainty about x mapped through A.
Say it“p of y is Gaussian with mean A mu plus b and covariance L inverse plus A Lambda inverse A transpose.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{y}) &= \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{m},\ \boldsymbol\Sigma\big) \\ \boldsymbol\Sigma &= \big(\boldsymbol\Lambda+\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{A}\big)^{-1} \\ \mathbf{m} &= \boldsymbol\Sigma\big\{\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{b})+\boldsymbol\Lambda\boldsymbol\mu\big\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: precisions add: the posterior precision is the prior’s plus what the measurement contributes. The posterior mean is a precision-weighted blend of the prior mean and the measured evidence. A precise sensor pulls hard; a vague one barely moves the prior.
Say it“x given y is Gaussian with covariance Lambda plus A transpose L A, inverted, and mean Sigma times A transpose L times y minus b, plus Lambda mu.”

Why this one?

Why the Gaussian is everywhere

Two deep reasons. The first is the central limit theorem: add up many independent random quantities, each with a finite variance, and the total tends to a Gaussian, whatever the shape of the pieces. A measurement error made of many small disturbances, a height shaped by many genes, an average over many customers: all drift towards the bell. Pick a lopsided or lumpy distribution and raise N, the number of draws averaged.

\[ z_N = \frac{\bar x_N - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt N} \;\longrightarrow\; \mathcal{N}(0,1) \quad\text{as } N\to\infty \]
Read it as: standardise the average of N draws (subtract the true mean, divide by its standard error σ/√N) and its distribution approaches the standard bell. How fast depends on the pieces: the skewness of the average falls like 1/√N, so symmetric pieces converge quickly and lopsided ones slowly.
Say it“x-bar N minus mu, over sigma over root N, tends to a standard normal as N goes to infinity.”

The second reason is maximum entropy. Suppose all you know about a quantity is its mean and variance. Which distribution should you assume? The honest choice is the one that adds no other information: the one with the largest entropy. Among all densities with a given variance, that is the Gaussian. Choosing it is the least committal thing you can do. Change σ: the three shapes below share the same variance, and the Gaussian always has the most entropy, by the same margins.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{p}\ \mathrm{H}[p] = -\!\int p(x)\ln p(x)\,dx \\ &\text{with mean } \mu \text{ and variance } \sigma^2 \text{ fixed} \\ &\Rightarrow\ p(x) = \mathcal{N}(x\mid\mu,\sigma^2) \\ &\phantom{\Rightarrow}\ \mathrm{H} = \tfrac12\ln\!\big(2\pi e\,\sigma^2\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: maximise the spread-out-ness of p, using Lagrange multipliers for the constraints (total probability 1, the mean, the variance). The solution has the form exp(quadratic in x), which is a Gaussian. In D dimensions, fixing the mean vector and covariance gives the multivariate Gaussian in the same way.
Say it“Of all densities with this mean and variance, the one with the most entropy is the Gaussian, with entropy a half log two pi e sigma squared.”

The Gaussian also has limits. It has a single peak, so it cannot describe data that clusters into groups; its tails are thin, so a few outliers can drag its mean and inflate its covariance; and a full covariance in high dimensions has too many parameters to fit from little data. Mixtures of Gaussians, Student’s t and the other distributions in chapter 0.10 are the fixes.

Real-world project

Project: predicting a child’s height from the parents’

In the 1880s Francis Galton collected the heights of 205 families: father, mother and their 934 adult children (481 sons, 453 daughters), in inches. Looking at this data he discovered regression to the mean: tall parents have tall children, but on average less tall than themselves. He summarised each couple by a mid-parent height, (father + 1.08 × mother)/2, scaling the mother up to put her on the men’s scale. The data comes from his notebook, transcribed by Hanley (2004).

The model is the simplest one this chapter can offer: fit a 2-D Gaussian to (mid-parent, child) by maximum likelihood, separately for sons and daughters, and predict by conditioning on the parents. We hold out 50 whole families (239 children) for testing, so no family is split between training and test. Drag the slice to a mid-parent height.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[c\mid p] &= \mu_c + \frac{\Sigma_{cp}}{\Sigma_{pp}}\,(p-\mu_p) \\ \operatorname{var}[c\mid p] &= \Sigma_{cc} - \frac{\Sigma_{cp}^2}{\Sigma_{pp}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the conditional Gaussian with a = c, the child’s height, and b = p, the mid-parent height. The slope Σcp/Σpp is below 1, so the prediction moves less than the parents do: that is regression to the mean, falling straight out of the formula.
Say it“The expected height given the parents is mu c plus Sigma c p over Sigma p p times p minus mu p; the variance is Sigma c c minus Sigma c p squared over Sigma p p.”

Scoring on the 50 held-out families

Three models, each a Gaussian fitted to the training families: the child’s height alone (ignore the parents), the 2-D Gaussian given the mid-parent, and a 3-D Gaussian over (father, mother, child) conditioned on both parents separately. A good probabilistic prediction is both close (low RMSE) and honest: its 90% intervals should contain about 90% of the test children, and the average log density it gives them should be high.

Your own family

The 3-D model conditions on two numbers at once: xb = (father, mother) is now a vector and ΣabΣbb−1 is a pair of weights. Set the parents’ heights.

The same project in numpy and scikit-learn

import json, numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

D = json.load(open("x006-data.json"))                  # Galton (1886): 934 children, 205 families
f, m, c = (np.array(D[k]) for k in "fmc")
son, test = np.array(D["g"]) == 1, np.array(D["te"]) == 1   # 50 whole families held out
mid = (f + 1.08 * m) / 2                               # Galton's "mid-parent" height

def fit(X):                                            # maximum likelihood: mean and 1/N covariance
    mu = X.mean(0); return mu, (X - mu).T @ (X - mu) / len(X)

def condition(mu, S, xb):                              # p(last | others = xb)
    a, b = -1, slice(0, -1)
    K = S[a, b] @ np.linalg.inv(S[b, b])               # Sigma_ab Sigma_bb^-1
    return mu[a] + (xb - mu[b]) @ K, np.sqrt(S[a, a] - K @ S[b, a]), K

for name, g in [("sons", son), ("daughters", ~son)]:
    tr, te = g & ~test, g & test
    print(f"{name}: {tr.sum()} training children, {te.sum()} test children")
    for label, cols in [("marginal (no parents)", []), ("given mid-parent", [mid]), ("given father & mother", [f, m])]:
        X = np.column_stack(cols + [c])
        mu, S = fit(X[tr])
        mean, sd, K = condition(mu, S, X[te][:, :-1]) if cols else (mu[-1], np.sqrt(S[-1, -1]), None)
        y = c[te]; z = (y - mean) / sd
        print(f"  {label:22s} RMSE {np.sqrt(np.mean((y - mean)**2)):.4f}  sd {sd:.4f}"
              f"  90% cover {np.mean(np.abs(z) < norm.ppf(0.95)):.4f}  log p {np.mean(norm.logpdf(y, mean, sd)):.4f}")
        if cols:
            lr = LinearRegression().fit(X[tr][:, :-1], c[tr])
            print(f"    weights {np.round(K, 6)}  (least squares: {np.round(lr.coef_, 6)})")
    mu, S = fit(np.column_stack([f, m, c])[tr])
    mean, sd, _ = condition(mu, S, np.array([70.0, 64.0]))
    print(f"  father 70in, mother 64in -> {mean:.3f} in, 90% interval {mean - 1.6449*sd:.2f} to {mean + 1.6449*sd:.2f}")

# is the 90% interval honest?  re-split the 205 families 300 ways (50 held out each time)
fam = np.array(D["fam"]); cover = {True: [], False: []}
for seed in range(300):
    held = np.isin(fam, np.random.RandomState(seed).permutation(205)[:50])
    for g in (True, False):
        tr, te = (son == g) & ~held, (son == g) & held
        mu, S = fit(np.column_stack([f, m, c])[tr])
        mean, sd, _ = condition(mu, S, np.column_stack([f, m])[te])
        cover[g].append(np.mean(np.abs(c[te] - mean) < norm.ppf(0.95) * sd))
print(f"300 splits: 90% intervals cover {np.mean(cover[True]):.4f} of sons, {np.mean(cover[False]):.4f} of daughters")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A Gaussian is set by a mean and a covariance. Its density falls off with the Mahalanobis distance Δ² = (x − μ)TΣ−1(x − μ), so its contours are ellipses.
  • The eigenvectors of Σ are the ellipse’s axes and the eigenvalues their variances; in those coordinates the Gaussian splits into independent one-dimensional bells.
  • Maximum likelihood gives the sample mean and the 1/N sample covariance, and that covariance is too small on average by a factor (N − 1)/N.
  • Slice a Gaussian (condition) or project it (marginalise) and you get a Gaussian back: the conditional mean is linear in what you know and its variance is smaller; the marginal is just a block of μ and Σ.
  • With a Gaussian prior and a linear-Gaussian measurement, the posterior is Gaussian and precisions add. The bell is everywhere because sums of many small effects tend to it, and because it assumes the least beyond a mean and a variance.

Check your understanding

A density value of 2.4 appears for a one-dimensional Gaussian. Is something wrong?

No. A density is not a probability; only areas under it are. A bell with σ = 1/6 has a peak height of 1/(σ√2π) ≈ 2.39, and its total area is still 1.

Two points are both 2 units from the mean. Why might one have a much lower density than the other?

The density depends on the Mahalanobis distance, not the ordinary one. A point 2 units along the cloud’s wide axis is unsurprising; a point 2 units across its narrow axis can be many “standard deviations” out.

With N = 2 data points, what is the expected value of σ²ML if the true variance is 9?

(N − 1)/N × 9 = ½ × 9 = 4.5. Dividing the sum of squared deviations by N − 1 = 1 instead would give an unbiased 9 on average.

For a 2-D Gaussian with correlation ρ, by how much does learning xb shrink the variance of xa? Does it depend on the value of xb?

The conditional variance is σa²(1 − ρ²), so it shrinks by the factor 1 − ρ² whatever value xb takes. Only the conditional mean depends on xb, and it does so linearly.

Why does Galton’s regression to the mean appear in the conditional Gaussian?

The slope of the conditional mean is Σcp/Σpp = ρσc/σp. When parents and children spread about equally, it is about ρ, which is below 1 unless the relationship is perfect, so predictions sit closer to the average than the parents do.
