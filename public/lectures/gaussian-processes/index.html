Gaussian Processes for Regression

A prior over functions, not over weights

Bayesian linear regression put a Gaussian belief on a handful of weights and let the curve follow. A Gaussian process skips the weights. It puts the belief straight onto the function: any finite set of its values is jointly Gaussian, and a single function, the kernel, says how strongly two values move together. Conditioning on data is then ordinary Gaussian algebra, and the answer comes with error bars that open up wherever the data run out. On the right, twelve functions drawn from the posterior drift through the points. Click anywhere to add a point and watch them all bend to pass through it.

The idea

Linear regression, seen as functions

Start from chapter 1.2d's model, y(x) = w⊤φ(x), with M fixed basis functions and a Gaussian prior on the weights. Every weight vector drawn from the prior is a whole function, so the prior over w is secretly a prior over functions. Now ask a different question: not “what are the weights?” but “what does the prior say about the function's values at the inputs x1, …, xN?”

Stack those values into a vector y = Φw. A fixed matrix times a Gaussian vector is Gaussian, so y is Gaussian, with mean 0 and a covariance that depends only on the inputs. Each entry of that covariance is a function of two inputs, the kernel. The weights have disappeared from the answer. Here the basis is M Gaussian bumps of width 0.5, packed into [−3, 3], with each weight's prior variance scaled so the total stays fixed. Slide M upwards and watch the covariance with the probe settle onto one smooth curve.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{y} &= \boldsymbol\Phi \mathbf{w}, \quad \mathbf{w} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \alpha^{-1}\mathbf{I}) \\ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}] &= \boldsymbol\Phi\, \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}] = \mathbf{0} \\ \operatorname{cov}[\mathbf{y}] &= \boldsymbol\Phi\, \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\top}]\, \boldsymbol\Phi^{\top} \\ &= \tfrac{1}{\alpha}\boldsymbol\Phi\boldsymbol\Phi^{\top} = \mathbf{K} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Φ is the design matrix, one row φ(xn)⊤ per input. y lists the function's values at the N inputs. Its mean is zero because the weights' mean is zero, and its covariance is the Gram matrix K, which needs only the inputs. So y ~ N(0, K): a Gaussian over function values, with no w in sight.
Say it“y is Phi w, with w Gaussian, mean zero, covariance alpha-inverse I. Then y has mean zero and covariance one over alpha, Phi Phi-transpose, which we call K.”
\[ \begin{aligned} K_{nm} &= k(x_n, x_m) \\ &= \tfrac{1}{\alpha}\, \boldsymbol\phi(x_n)^{\top} \boldsymbol\phi(x_m) \\ &\xrightarrow{M \to \infty} \sigma_f^2 \exp\!\big(\!-\tfrac{(x_n - x_m)^2}{4 s^2}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the kernel is an inner product of feature vectors, divided by α. Pack the bumps of width s ever more densely, shrink each weight's variance in proportion, and the sum over bumps becomes an integral that can be done exactly: a smooth Gaussian of the distance x − x′. That limit uses infinitely many basis functions, yet it costs one exponential to evaluate.
Say it“K n m is k of x-n and x-m, one over alpha times phi of x-n dotted with phi of x-m. As M goes to infinity it becomes sigma-f squared times e to the minus the squared distance over four s squared.”

Before any data

The GP prior: a kernel is a covariance

Turn the previous section around. Instead of choosing basis functions and deriving a kernel, choose the kernel and never mention weights at all. A Gaussian process is a probability distribution over functions y(x) such that the values at any finite set of inputs are jointly Gaussian. Because a Gaussian is pinned down by its mean and covariance, a GP is pinned down by a mean function (we take it to be zero, for lack of better knowledge) and a covariance function, the kernel. Any kernel that always yields a valid covariance matrix will do.

To draw a function from the prior, pick a grid of inputs, build K entry by entry from the kernel, and sample N(0, K): that is all the five curves below are. The kernel Bishop uses throughout has four knobs: θ0 scales a smooth bump, θ1 sets how fast it decays (the length-scale is 1/√θ1), θ2 adds a random constant and θ3 a random straight line. Click through the six settings of Bishop's figure, then switch the bump to an exponential.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{y}) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{0}, \mathbf{K}) \\ K_{nm} &= k(x_n, x_m) \\ k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') &= \theta_0\, e^{-\frac{\theta_1}{2}\lVert \mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\rVert^2} \\ &\quad + \theta_2 + \theta_3\, \mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{x}' \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the GP's values at the inputs are Gaussian with covariance matrix K. Bishop's kernel is a sum of three valid kernels: a Gaussian bump of height θ0, a constant θ2, and a linear kernel θ3x⊤x′. Each term, sampled alone, draws one kind of function: a smooth wiggle, a flat offset, a line through the origin. Their sum draws their sum.
Say it“y is Gaussian with mean zero and covariance K. The kernel is theta-nought e to the minus theta-one over two times the squared distance, plus theta-two, plus theta-three x-transpose x-prime.”
\[ \begin{aligned} k_{\text{exp}}(x, x') &= \theta_0 \exp\!\Big(\!-\tfrac{\theta_1}{2}\,\lvert x - x'\rvert\Big) \\ \rho(x, x') &= \frac{k(x, x')}{\sqrt{k(x,x)\,k(x',x')}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the exponential kernel decays with the distance itself rather than its square. Its graph has a corner at x = x′, and that corner makes the functions rough: this is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, which Uhlenbeck and Ornstein used for a particle in Brownian motion. The correlation ρ says how to read any kernel: divide by the two standard deviations and you get how tightly y(x) and y(x′) move together.
Say it“The exponential kernel is theta-nought e to the minus theta-one over two times the absolute distance. The correlation rho of y at x and y at x-prime is k over the square root of k at x, x times k at x-prime, x-prime.”

The engine

Conditioning: how one reading informs another

Everything a GP does with data comes from one fact about Gaussians: if two quantities are jointly Gaussian and you learn one of them, the other is still Gaussian, with a shifted mean and a smaller variance. Take the simplest case. We read a noisy value t1 = y(0) + noise at x1 = 0, and ask about y(x2) somewhere else. The kernel (here a squared exponential with length-scale 1) says how correlated they are; the correlation decides how far the reading pulls y(x2), and how much it narrows it.

On the left is the joint Gaussian of the pair; the vertical line is “what we saw”, and the purple slice through the ellipse is the conditional distribution. On the right is the same computation done for every x at once, which is already GP regression with a single point. Slide the distance and the observed value, then add noise.

\[ \begin{aligned} \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{a} \\ \mathbf{b} \end{pmatrix} &\sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol\mu_a \\ \boldsymbol\mu_b \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol\Sigma_{aa} & \boldsymbol\Sigma_{ab} \\ \boldsymbol\Sigma_{ba} & \boldsymbol\Sigma_{bb} \end{pmatrix}\right) \\ \boldsymbol\mu_{a \mid b} &= \boldsymbol\mu_a + \boldsymbol\Sigma_{ab}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}(\mathbf{b} - \boldsymbol\mu_b) \\ \boldsymbol\Sigma_{a \mid b} &= \boldsymbol\Sigma_{aa} - \boldsymbol\Sigma_{ab}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\boldsymbol\Sigma_{ba} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: split a Gaussian vector into a part we will learn (b) and a part we want (a). Seeing b moves a's mean by the surprise b − μb, scaled by how strongly a and b co-vary relative to b's own variance. The covariance shrinks by an amount that does not depend on the value of b at all, only on where b was measured. This is the result from chapter 0.6, and the rest of the chapter is this formula with kernel entries plugged in.
Say it“The mean of a given b is mu-a plus Sigma-a-b, Sigma-b-b inverse, times b minus mu-b. The covariance of a given b is Sigma-a-a minus Sigma-a-b, Sigma-b-b inverse, Sigma-b-a.”

The payoff

GP regression: the predictive mean and variance

Now with N readings. Each target is the function plus independent Gaussian noise, tn = y(xn) + εn, with noise precision β (variance σn² = 1/β). The function values are N(0, K) and the noise adds β−1 to the diagonal, so the targets are N(0, CN) with CN = K + β−1I. To predict at a new input x, append it: the N + 1 values (t, tN+1) are jointly Gaussian with a bordered covariance, and the previous section's formula gives the answer in one line.

Two things to notice. The mean is a weighted sum of the targets, and the variance does not depend on the targets at all, only on where they were taken. And there is a second kernel here: switch to “9 Gaussian bumps” and the kernel is built from chapter 1.2d's basis, so the GP reproduces that chapter's Bayesian regression exactly, trap included. Click to add points, drag them, and move the probe outside [0, 1].

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{t} \mid \mathbf{y}) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{t} \mid \mathbf{y}, \beta^{-1}\mathbf{I}) \\ p(\mathbf{t}) &= \textstyle\int p(\mathbf{t}\mid\mathbf{y})\, p(\mathbf{y})\, d\mathbf{y} \\ &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{t} \mid \mathbf{0}, \mathbf{C}_N) \\ C(x_n, x_m) &= k(x_n, x_m) + \beta^{-1}\delta_{nm} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: given the function values, targets scatter around them with variance 1/β. Integrating the function out adds the two independent Gaussians: covariances add, so the targets' covariance is the kernel matrix plus noise on the diagonal (δnm is 1 when n = m and 0 otherwise). This p(t) is also the marginal likelihood we will maximise later.
Say it“t given y is Gaussian around y with variance one over beta. The marginal of t is Gaussian with mean zero and covariance C-N, whose entries are k of x-n, x-m plus beta-inverse delta-n-m.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{C}_{N+1} &= \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{C}_N & \mathbf{k} \\ \mathbf{k}^{\top} & c \end{pmatrix} \\ c &= k(x, x) + \beta^{-1} \\ m(x) &= \mathbf{k}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_N^{-1}\mathbf{t} \\ \sigma^2(x) &= c - \mathbf{k}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_N^{-1}\mathbf{k} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: k lists k(xn, x), the new input's covariance with each training input. Conditioning tN+1 on t (a = new point, b = training targets) gives a Gaussian predictive with this mean and variance. The variance starts at c, the prior's, and k⊤CN−1k is how much the data explain away; it is large only when x is close to training inputs.
Say it“C-N-plus-one is C-N bordered by k and c. The predictive mean is k-transpose C-N-inverse t; the variance is c minus k-transpose C-N-inverse k.”

Another view

The mean is a sum of kernel bumps

Read the mean formula from the right. a = CN−1t is a fixed vector once the data are in, so m(x) = Σn ank(xn, x): one copy of the kernel centred on each training input, scaled by an, added up. Read it from the left instead, and m(x) = Σn wn(x) tn, a weighted sum of the targets: the GP's version of chapter 1.2d's equivalent kernel. Same number, two stories.

Both stories need CN to be invertible, and the variance needs more: k must make K positive semidefinite for every set of inputs, or some combination of function values would have negative variance. Plenty of innocent-looking similarities fail. exp(−|r|p) is a valid kernel for powers up to 2 and invalid beyond. Switch to the cube, lower the noise, and watch the arithmetic break.

\[ \begin{aligned} m(x) &= \textstyle\sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n\, k(x_n, x) \\ &= \textstyle\sum_{n=1}^{N} w_n(x)\, t_n \\ \mathbf{a} &= \mathbf{C}_N^{-1}\mathbf{t}, \quad \mathbf{w}(x) = \mathbf{C}_N^{-1}\mathbf{k}(x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first line is a basis-function model with one basis function per data point, k(xn, ·). The second line is a linear smoother: the weights wn(x) depend on the inputs and on x, never on the targets. With a constant kernel term and little noise, the weights add to about 1.
Say it“m of x is the sum over n of a-n times k of x-n and x, where a is C-N-inverse t. Equivalently it is the sum of w-n of x times t-n, where w of x is C-N-inverse k of x.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\,\mathbf{u} &= \operatorname{var}\Big[\textstyle\sum_n u_n\, y(x_n)\Big] \\ &\ge 0 \ \ \text{for every } \mathbf{u} \\ \mathbf{C}_N &= \mathbf{K} + \beta^{-1}\mathbf{I} \\ \lambda_i(\mathbf{C}_N) &= \lambda_i(\mathbf{K}) + \beta^{-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: any weighted sum of function values is itself a random number, and its variance is u⊤Ku, which cannot be negative. So K must be positive semidefinite: all its eigenvalues λi ≥ 0. Noise shifts every eigenvalue up by 1/β, so CN is safely positive definite whenever K is semidefinite and the noise is positive. Chapter 1.9a's rules (sums, products, positive scalings of valid kernels) build valid kernels safely.
Say it“u-transpose K u is the variance of the sum of u-n y of x-n, so it is never negative. The eigenvalues of C-N are those of K plus beta-inverse.”

Fitting the kernel

Learning the hyperparameters

The kernel's knobs, the length-scale ℓ, the signal sd σf and the noise sd σn, are hyperparameters: they shape the prior rather than the fitted curve. A GP comes with its own way to set them, the one chapter 1.2e used for α and β. The probability of the data with the function integrated out, p(t | θ) = N(t | 0, CN), is available in closed form, so choose the θ that makes the observed targets most probable. This is type-II maximum likelihood, or empirical Bayes.

Its logarithm splits into a data-fit term, which rewards kernels under which the targets are unsurprising, and a complexity term, −½ln|CN|, which penalises kernels that spread their probability over too many possible data sets. The surface is not concave, and it often has several peaks, each a different story about the same points. The map below is ln p(t) over ℓ and σn for twelve points, with σf fixed at 1. Click the map to pick hyperparameters, then press Climb.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\ln p(\mathbf{t} \mid \boldsymbol\theta) = -\tfrac12 \mathbf{t}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_N^{-1}\mathbf{t} \\ &\qquad - \tfrac12 \ln\lvert\mathbf{C}_N\rvert - \tfrac{N}{2}\ln(2\pi) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the log density of a zero-mean Gaussian with covariance CN(θ), evaluated at the observed targets. The first term is the data fit; the second, the log-determinant, measures the volume of data sets the model finds plausible, so it falls as the model becomes more flexible; the third is a constant. With a Cholesky factor CN = LL⊤, ln|CN| = 2Σ ln Lii.
Say it“The log marginal likelihood is minus a half t-transpose C-N-inverse t, minus a half log det C-N, minus N over two log two pi.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial \ln p(\mathbf{t}\mid\boldsymbol\theta)}{\partial\theta_i} = -\tfrac12 \operatorname{Tr}\Big(\mathbf{C}_N^{-1}\frac{\partial \mathbf{C}_N}{\partial\theta_i}\Big) \\ &\qquad + \tfrac12\, \mathbf{t}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_N^{-1}\frac{\partial \mathbf{C}_N}{\partial\theta_i}\mathbf{C}_N^{-1}\mathbf{t} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the gradient needs only the derivative of each kernel entry with respect to the hyperparameter, so any gradient optimiser can climb the surface. The trace term pulls towards simpler kernels, the quadratic term towards a better fit. Once CN−1 is known (cost N³), each extra hyperparameter costs only N². We work with ln ℓ and ln σn so that they stay positive.
Say it“The derivative is minus a half the trace of C-N-inverse times dC by d-theta-i, plus a half t-transpose C-N-inverse, dC by d-theta-i, C-N-inverse t.”

Three refinements, all in Bishop's section. Priors on θ. Add ln p(θ) to the objective and maximise the log posterior instead; a gentle prior keeps ℓ away from absurd values when the data are few. Being fully Bayesian. Strictly, one should average predictions over p(θ | t) rather than pick one θ; that integral has no closed form, and needs sampling or approximations (chapter 1.2e met the same trade-off). Noise that varies. The model above assumes one noise level everywhere. For heteroscedastic data, model ln β(x) itself with a second Gaussian process, so the noise can be larger in some regions than others.

Building kernels

Composite kernels: trend, seasons and noise

Valid kernels can be combined into new valid kernels (chapter 1.9a): add two and you get the covariance of the sum of two independent GPs; multiply two and each modulates the other; scale one by a positive constant and you rescale its functions. This turns kernel design into describing the data in words. Rasmussen and Williams' classic model for atmospheric CO₂ reads: a smooth long-term trend, plus a yearly seasonal cycle whose shape slowly drifts, plus medium-term irregularities of many sizes, plus noise.

The seasonal term is the interesting one. A periodic kernel says “dates exactly a year apart behave alike”; multiplying it by a squared exponential with a long decay length relaxes that to “nearby years behave alike”. Switch the components on and off and change the seasonal decay.

\[ \begin{aligned} k &= k_{\text{trend}} + k_{\text{seas}} \\ &\quad + k_{\text{med}} + k_{\text{noise}} \\ k_{\text{trend}} &= \theta_1^2 \exp\!\big(\!-\tfrac{r^2}{2\theta_2^2}\big) \\ k_{\text{seas}} &= \theta_3^2 \exp\!\Big(\!-\tfrac{r^2}{2\theta_4^2} - \tfrac{2\sin^2(\pi r)}{\theta_5^2}\Big) \\ k_{\text{med}} &= \theta_6^2 \Big(1 + \tfrac{r^2}{2\theta_8\theta_7^2}\Big)^{-\theta_8} \\ k_{\text{noise}} &= \theta_9^2 \exp\!\big(\!-\tfrac{r^2}{2\theta_{10}^2}\big) + \theta_{11}^2\,\delta_{tt'} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: r = |t − t′| in years. Each θ with an even-looking role is an amplitude (in ppm for the project) and the others are length-scales in years. The seasonal term is a periodic kernel with period 1 year and smoothness θ5, times a decay with length θ4. The rational quadratic is a mixture of squared exponentials of many length-scales, with θ8 setting the mix. The last line models measurement noise that is slightly correlated over a month or two, plus white noise.
Say it“k is a squared-exponential trend, plus a periodic kernel times a squared-exponential decay for the seasons, plus a rational quadratic for medium-term wiggles, plus a short squared exponential and white noise.”

Choosing

The price of exactness, and when to pay it

The weight-space and function-space views give the same predictions whenever the kernel comes from M basis functions, but they cost different things. Bayesian linear regression inverts an M × M matrix; a GP inverts the N × N matrix CN, which takes time proportional to N³ and memory proportional to N². After that, each prediction costs N for the mean and N² for the variance. So a GP wins when the natural basis is large or infinite and the data are modest (hundreds to a few thousand points), and loses when N is in the millions. Press the button to measure the cube in your own browser.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{GP: } & O(N^3) \text{ train} \\ & O(N) \text{ mean}, \ O(N^2) \text{ var} \\ \text{basis: } & O(M^3) \text{ train} \\ & O(M) \text{ mean}, \ O(M^2) \text{ var} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the two views trade the number of data points for the number of basis functions. Factorising (Cholesky) is the expensive step and is done once per set of hyperparameters, so learning θ by gradient ascent pays it at every step. For large N, practical GPs use approximations: a subset of the data, or a few hundred inducing points that summarise it (sparse GPs), or kernels with special structure.
Say it“A GP costs N cubed to train and N or N squared per prediction; a basis-function model costs M cubed and M or M squared.”

Real-world project

Project: forecasting Mauna Loa CO₂

Since March 1958 the observatory on Mauna Loa, Hawaiʻi, has measured the carbon dioxide in the air: first Charles David Keeling of Scripps, now NOAA's Global Monitoring Laboratory, which publishes the monthly means freely. There are of them, through . Chapter 1.2d forecast them with hinge basis functions; here we use the composite kernel of the previous section, exactly as Rasmussen and Williams did, with every hyperparameter learned by maximising the marginal likelihood in scikit-learn. The mean function is the constant average of the training months.

The classic kernel has a weakness that only shows when you extrapolate. Its trend is a squared exponential, so beyond the data it can only carry the recent slope for about one length-scale before it drifts back towards the mean. So we also try a second kernel that replaces part of the trend with a Bayesian quadratic, written as a kernel exactly as in the first section: (σ0² + ττ′)² = σ0⁴ + 2σ0²ττ′ + τ²τ′² is φ(τ)⊤φ(τ′) for the basis φ(τ) = (σ0², √2σ0τ, τ²). Move the cut-off and switch kernels: the shaded region is data the model never saw.

\[ \begin{aligned} y(t) &= \mu_0 + f(t) + \varepsilon(t) \\ f &\sim \mathcal{GP}(0, k) \\ k_{\text{quad}}(t, t') &= c\,(\sigma_0^2 + \tau\tau')^2 \\ \tau &= t - 1990 \\ k &= k_{\text{quad}} + k_{\text{trend}} + k_{\text{seas}} \\ &\quad + k_{\text{med}} + k_{\text{noise}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each month's reading is a constant μ0 plus a GP plus noise, and the GP's kernel is the sum from the composite-kernel section. The second kernel adds kquad, a quadratic in the years since 1990 whose three coefficients have Gaussian priors with sds set by c and σ0; with it, the squared-exponential “trend” only has to describe smooth deviations from a quadratic. That gives 13 hyperparameters instead of 11, all learned.
Say it“The reading is mu-nought plus a Gaussian process plus noise. The quadratic kernel is c times sigma-nought squared plus tau tau-prime, all squared, with tau the years since 1990; the full kernel adds it to the trend, seasonal, medium-term and noise kernels.”

Backtest: two kernels, four cut-offs

One cut-off can be lucky. Both kernels, with their own learned hyperparameters, are scored on every held-out month and on the first ten years after each cut-off (6.75 years for 2020). Computed in your browser.

The same project in scikit-learn

import numpy as np, json
from sklearn.gaussian_process import GaussianProcessRegressor
from sklearn.gaussian_process.kernels import (RBF, ExpSineSquared, RationalQuadratic,
                                              WhiteKernel, DotProduct, ConstantKernel as C)

J = json.load(open("co2_mm_mlo.json"))                 # NOAA GML monthly means, Mauna Loa
y = np.array(J["co2"]); k = np.arange(len(y))
t = J["y0"] + (J["m0"] - 1 + k + 0.5) / 12             # mid-month, in years
X = (t - 1990)[:, None]                                # years since 1990

seasons = 2.0**2 * RBF(100.0) * ExpSineSquared(1.0, 1.0, periodicity_bounds="fixed")
medium = 0.5**2 * RationalQuadratic(length_scale=1.0, alpha=1.0)
noise = 0.1**2 * RBF(0.1) + WhiteKernel(0.1**2, noise_level_bounds=(1e-5, 1e5))
kernels = {"R&W": 50.0**2 * RBF(50.0) + seasons + medium + noise,             # Rasmussen & Williams 5.4.3
           "R&W+quad": C(1e-3) * DotProduct(30.0)**2 + 2.0**2 * RBF(10.0)    # Bayesian quadratic trend
                       + seasons + medium + noise}

def fit(name, cut):
    tr = t < cut; mu0 = y[tr].mean()                   # constant mean = training average
    gp = GaussianProcessRegressor(kernels[name]).fit(X[tr], y[tr] - mu0)   # L-BFGS on log-theta
    return gp, mu0, tr

out = {}
for name in kernels:
    for cut in (1990, 2000, 2010, 2020, 2100):
        gp, mu0, tr = fit(name, cut)
        r = dict(theta=np.exp(gp.kernel_.theta).tolist(), mu0=mu0, lml=gp.log_marginal_likelihood_value_)
        line = f"{name:8s} cut {cut}: N {tr.sum()}  log ML {r['lml']:9.3f}"
        if cut < 2100:
            for tag, h in (("all", ~tr), ("10y", ~tr & (t < cut + 10))):
                m, s = gp.predict(X[h], return_std=True); e = y[h] - (m + mu0)
                r[tag] = [int(h.sum()), np.sqrt(np.mean(e**2)), np.mean(np.abs(e) < 1.96 * s)]
                line += f"  {tag}: RMSE {r[tag][1]:.3f} in95 {r[tag][2]:.3f}"
        q = np.array([2030 + 1/24, 2040 + 1/24, min(cut, 2026) + 0.5]); m, s = gp.predict((q - 1990)[:, None], return_std=True)

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A Gaussian prior on the weights of a linear model is a Gaussian prior on its function values, with covariance K = α−1ΦΦ⊤; a Gaussian process takes the kernel k(x, x′) as the starting point and drops the weights.
  • A GP is a distribution over functions whose values at any finite set of inputs are jointly Gaussian; mean zero and a kernel define it, and the kernel must make every K positive semidefinite.
  • With Gaussian noise, the targets are N(0, CN), CN = K + β−1I, and conditioning gives the predictive mean k⊤CN−1t and variance c − k⊤CN−1k, which reverts to the prior far from the data.
  • Hyperparameters are learned by maximising ln p(t | θ) = −½t⊤CN−1t − ½ln|CN| − (N/2)ln 2π, a data fit plus a built-in complexity penalty; the surface can have several peaks.
  • Kernels add and multiply, so a model can be described in words (trend + drifting seasons + wiggles + noise); the price of exactness is O(N³) time.

Check your understanding

A model has one basis function, φ(x) = x, and prior w ~ N(0, 1/α) with α = 4. What kernel does it define, and what do draws from the GP look like?

k(x, x′) = α−1φ(x)φ(x′) = xx′/4. Every draw is a straight line through the origin with a random slope of sd 0.5; the prior sd at x is |x|/2, so it is zero at the origin. It is Bishop's θ3 term on its own.

You have one noisy reading t1 = 2 at x = 0, squared-exponential kernel with σf = 1 and ℓ = 1, noise variance 0.25. What is the predictive mean of y at x = 1?

k(0, 1) = e−1/2 ≈ 0.607 and C1 = 1 + 0.25 = 1.25, so m = 0.607 × 2 / 1.25 ≈ 0.970. The variance of y(1) is 1 − 0.607²/1.25 ≈ 0.706.

Why does the predictive variance not depend on the observed targets, and when is that a weakness?

For jointly Gaussian variables the conditional covariance Σaa − ΣabΣbb−1Σba involves only covariances, which depend on the inputs. The targets enter only through the hyperparameters, if they are learned. So a GP with fixed θ cannot become less certain when the data surprise it, and a single noise level cannot say that some regions are noisier than others (heteroscedastic noise needs a second GP).

The marginal likelihood surface has two peaks: a short length-scale with small noise, and a long length-scale with large noise. What does each say, and how would you choose?

The first says the wiggles are real signal; the second says they are noise around a smooth trend. The higher peak is preferred by the data, and the ratio of their marginal likelihoods is the Bayes factor between the two readings. With few points the difference can be small; then restart the optimiser from several points, compare the peaks, and consider a prior on θ or held-out data.

Why can a GP use “infinitely many basis functions” while Bayesian linear regression cannot?

Predictions need only the kernel's values at the training and test inputs: an N × N matrix and an N-vector. The basis can be infinite as long as the inner products φ(x)⊤φ(x′) have a closed form, as the squared exponential does. The cost then scales with N rather than with the number of basis functions.
