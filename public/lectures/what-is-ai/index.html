What Is AI?

Four definitions and seventy years of history

Artificial intelligence is the project of building machines that do things we would call intelligent. Nobody agrees on one definition. There are four serious ones, and the field’s history is partly a long argument about which to chase. The question

Four ways to define AI

Ask ten researchers what AI is and you will hear answers that differ along two lines. The first is thought versus behaviour: is intelligence about the reasoning going on inside, or about what the system does? The second is people versus an ideal: do we measure success by how closely the machine matches humans, or by rationality, an abstract standard of doing the right thing given what it knows? Two lines, two choices each, four definitions: thinking humanly, thinking rationally, acting humanly, acting rationally.

The split is not academic. A human-centred approach is partly an empirical science: you must study people to know whether you have matched them. A rationalist approach is mathematics plus engineering, and borrows from statistics, control theory and economics. Below are eight real definitions from textbooks of the 1970s to 1990s, in my paraphrase. Sort each one into a quadrant, then compare with how Russell and Norvig sorted them.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{human: } s &= 1 - \tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_{i} \mathbb{1}[a_i \neq h_i] \\ \text{rational: } s &= \mathbb{E}[\,U(\text{outcome})\,] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ai is what the system does in situation i and hi is what a person does; the indicator 𝟙[·] is 1 when they differ. The human yardstick is the share of situations in which the system agrees with people, mistakes included. The rational yardstick ignores people altogether: it averages a utility U over the outcomes the system’s actions lead to. A system can score perfectly on one and badly on the other.
Say it“Human score: one minus the share of cases where the machine and the person disagree. Rational score: the expected utility of the outcome.”

Definition 1 · acting humanly

The Turing test

In 1950 Alan Turing replaced the slippery question “can machines think?” with a game. An interrogator types questions to two hidden players, a person and a computer, and must say which is which. If the interrogator can’t tell, the computer passes. To pass, a program would need natural language processing to converse, knowledge representation to store what it knows, automated reasoning to answer questions and draw conclusions, and machine learning to adapt. The total Turing test adds a camera and a hatch for passing objects, so it also needs computer vision and robotics. Those six skills make up most of AI.

Turing made a concrete prediction: by about the year 2000 a machine with 109 bits of storage would play so well that an average interrogator had at most a 70% chance of the right identification after five minutes. Here is a simple model of the game. Each answer from either player gives the interrogator a noisy clue, and a machine’s answers feel, on average, a distance δ more mechanical than a person’s. Make the machine more human-like, or let the interrogator ask more questions, and see when it clears Turing’s bar.

\[ \begin{aligned} P(\text{correct}) &= \Phi\big(\delta\sqrt{n/2}\big) \\ \text{pass} &\iff \delta \le \frac{\sqrt{2}\,\Phi^{-1}(0.7)}{\sqrt{n}} \\ &\iff \delta \le \frac{0.742}{\sqrt{n}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each player gives n answers, and each answer is a clue drawn from a normal distribution with spread 1, centred at 0 for the person and at δ for the machine. The best interrogator averages each player’s clues and names the player with the more mechanical average as the machine. The difference between the two averages is centred at δ with spread √(2/n), and the interrogator is wrong only when noise pushes it below zero. Φ (the standard normal’s cumulative probability) turns the distance δ√(n/2), counted in spreads, into the chance of being right. More questions always help the interrogator, so a machine must be more human-like to survive a longer conversation.
Say it“The chance of a correct call is Phi of delta times root n-over-two; the machine passes when delta is at most nought point seven four two over root-n.”

Researchers have spent little effort on passing the test itself. As Russell and Norvig put it, aircraft engineers did not set out to build machines that fly so exactly like pigeons that they fool other pigeons. Turing’s test is still a good list of skills; it is a poor target.

Definitions 2 and 3

Thinking humanly, thinking rationally

To claim a program thinks like a person we need to know how people think, and there are three ways in: introspection (catching our own thoughts as they go by), psychological experiments (watching people), and brain imaging (watching the brain at work). Once there is a precise theory of the mind, it can be written as a program. If the program’s inputs, outputs and timing match human behaviour, that is evidence its mechanisms may resemble ours. Newell and Simon’s General Problem Solver (1961) was judged this way: they compared the trace of its reasoning steps with people thinking aloud on the same puzzles. That tradition became cognitive science. A cognitive model is graded on matching people, including their mistakes.

Thinking humanly: modelling a famous mistake

Tversky and Kahneman’s taxicab problem: 85% of a city’s cabs are Green and 15% are Blue. A witness says the cab in a hit-and-run was Blue, and tests show the witness names the colour correctly 80% of the time. How likely is it that the cab really was Blue? Most people answer close to 80%; the median answer in the original study was 0.80. Bayes’ rule says 41%. A good model of rational thought gives 41%; a good model of human thought must give something near 80%. One simple cognitive model says people under-weight the base rate by a factor w. Set w, and change the city and the witness.

\[ \underbrace{\operatorname{logit} p}_{\text{belief}} = w\cdot\underbrace{\operatorname{logit} b}_{\text{base rate}} + \underbrace{\operatorname{logit} r}_{\text{witness}} \]
Read it as: b is the share of Blue cabs and r the witness’s accuracy. Beliefs are written as log-odds, logit p = log(p/(1−p)), because then evidence simply adds. With w = 1 this is exactly Bayes’ rule: rational thinking. With w = 0 the base rate is ignored and the answer is just the witness’s accuracy r, which is what most people say. A cognitive scientist fits w to what real people answer; a logician insists on w = 1.
Say it“The log-odds of Blue equal w times the log-odds of the base rate, plus the log-odds of the witness being right.”

Thinking rationally: the laws of thought

Aristotle was among the first to try to pin down “right thinking”: his syllogisms are patterns of argument that always give true conclusions from true premises (Socrates is a man; all men are mortal; so Socrates is mortal). Nineteenth-century logicians built a precise notation for statements about anything, and by 1965 there were programs that could, in principle, solve any solvable problem stated in it. This logicist tradition hopes to build intelligence on such programs. A program checks an argument by brute force: list every possible world (every way of making the statements true or false), keep the worlds where all the premises hold, and see whether the conclusion holds in every one of them. Pick an argument, or build your own, and let the page check it.

\[ \text{KB} \models \alpha \iff M(\text{KB}) \subseteq M(\alpha) \]
Read it as: KB is the knowledge base (the premises) and α the conclusion. M(·) is the set of possible worlds, called models, in which a sentence is true. KB entails α when every world that satisfies the premises also satisfies the conclusion: there is no counter-example.
Say it“KB entails alpha if and only if the models of KB are a subset of the models of alpha.”
\[ P(\alpha \mid \text{KB}) = \frac{|M(\text{KB} \wedge \alpha)|}{|M(\text{KB})|} \]
Read it as: if every world consistent with the premises is equally likely, the probability of the conclusion is the share of those worlds where it holds. Entailment is the special case where the share is 1. Real knowledge is rarely certain, so the logicist programme needs probability to fill the gap.
Say it“The probability of alpha given KB is the number of models of KB and alpha over the number of models of KB.”

Logic has two known snags. Most real knowledge is uncertain (“the witness is right 80% of the time”), which is why probability theory joins logic, as in the taxicab problem above. And thinking correctly is not the same as acting well: something has to turn conclusions into actions. That is the fourth definition.

Definition 4 · acting rationally

Rational agents, and the trouble with objectives

An agent is anything that perceives and acts. A rational agent acts to achieve the best outcome or, when the outcome is uncertain, the best expected outcome. Correct inference is sometimes part of that, but not all of it: snatching your hand off a hot stove is a reflex, and it beats careful deliberation. This definition has won most of the field because it is general (logic, learning, reflexes and search are all ways of acting well) and because rationality, unlike “human-like”, has a precise mathematical meaning. Perfect rationality is usually too expensive to compute, so real agents aim for limited rationality: acting well with the time they have.

The catch, which Russell and Norvig call the value alignment problem, is that the maths assumes someone handed the agent the right objective. A delivery robot below can take three routes. Its designer wrote “minimise minutes”, and maybe a penalty for near-misses with pedestrians on the market square. Leave the penalty at zero, then raise it, and compare what the robot chooses with what the people on the square would choose.

\[ \begin{aligned} a^{*} &= \arg\max_{a} \sum_{s} P(s \mid a)\, U(s) \\ U &= -\,\text{minutes} - \lambda \cdot \text{near-misses} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for each action a, average the utility U over the situations s it might lead to, weighted by their probabilities, and pick the action with the highest average. Here U counts minutes against the robot, and λ says how many minutes one near-miss is worth. The robot uses the λ its designer typed in; the people affected have their own λ. If the two differ, a perfectly rational robot can still do the wrong thing.
Say it“The best action is the arg-max over actions of the sum over outcomes of probability times utility.”

The routes: the ring road takes 20 minutes, or 45 if jammed; the market square takes 12 minutes but carries a 25% chance of a near-miss; the back lanes always take 28 minutes. Russell argues that we should build machines that pursue our objectives while remaining uncertain what they are, so that they defer to us, ask, and allow themselves to be switched off. AIMA calls these beneficial machines (§1.1.5).

Read it as: the St Petersburg game. Flip a fair coin until it lands heads; heads first on flip k pays 2k coins, and a bank that can pay at most 2K stops there. It is an early triumph of the information-processing view: the mind as a channel with a measurable capacity.

Read it as: a thermostat with proportional control. Each step it nudges the temperature x towards the target x* by a fraction K of the error e = x* − x, while a draught d cools the room. History, part 1 · 1943–1969

The birth of AI and the years of great expectations

Inception (1943–1956). The first work now counted as AI is Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts’ 1943 model of artificial neurons. It drew on three sources: what was known of brain physiology, the propositional logic of Russell and Whitehead, and Turing’s theory of computation. Each unit is “on” or “off”, switching on when enough of its neighbours do, and they showed that networks of such units can compute any computable function. In 1949 Donald Hebb proposed a rule for changing the connection strengths, still called Hebbian learning. Marvin Minsky and Dean Edmonds built the first neural-network computer, SNARC, in 1950: 3,000 vacuum tubes simulating 40 neurons. Turing’s 1950 paper set out the imitation game, machine learning, genetic algorithms and reinforcement learning. In the summer of 1956 John McCarthy, Minsky, Claude Shannon and Nathaniel Rochester gathered ten researchers at Dartmouth College for two months; the field took McCarthy’s name for it, artificial intelligence. The star there was Allen Newell and Herbert Simon’s Logic Theorist, which proved most of the theorems in chapter 2 of Russell and Whitehead’s Principia Mathematica, finding a shorter proof for one of them than the book’s own.

Great expectations (1952–1969). Intellectuals of the day insisted that “a machine can never do X”; AI researchers answered by doing X, one X at a time. Newell and Simon’s General Problem Solver imitated human problem-solving and led them to the physical symbol system hypothesis: that manipulating symbols is necessary and sufficient for general intelligence. Arthur Samuel’s checkers program learned to play better than its author and was shown on television in 1956. McCarthy created Lisp (1958). Minsky’s students solved limited “microworlds”: closed-form calculus integration (SAINT), IQ-test analogy puzzles (ANALOGY), algebra story problems (STUDENT), and the blocks world, home of SHRDLU and the robot Shakey. Neural networks flourished too: Widrow and Hoff’s adalines and Rosenblatt’s perceptrons, with his convergence theorem guaranteeing that the learning rule finds weights whenever weights exist. Simon predicted in 1958 that within ten years a computer would be chess champion. It took about forty.

A McCulloch–Pitts unit is simple enough to wire by hand. Set the two weights and the threshold to build each logic gate, then try XOR.

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= \mathbb{1}\big[\, w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 \ge \theta \,\big] \\ x_1, x_2 &\in \{0, 1\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the unit adds up its weighted inputs and fires (y = 1) when the total reaches the threshold θ. The boundary w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ = θ is a straight line, so a single unit can only separate the four input patterns with one cut. AND, OR and NOT-AND are one cut each. XOR needs the two corners (0,1) and (1,0) on one side and (0,0), (1,1) on the other: no straight line does that.
Say it“y is one when w-one x-one plus w-two x-two is at least theta, and zero otherwise.”

History, part 2 · 1966–1993

A dose of reality, expert systems and two winters

A dose of reality (1966–1973). Programs that shone on a few hand-picked examples failed on wider or harder ones, for three reasons. First, many were built from introspection about how people do a task, not from analysis of the task itself. Machine translation is the classic case: translating well needs background knowledge to resolve ambiguity, and after a 1966 report found no prospect of general-purpose translation, US funding for academic translation projects was cancelled. Second, intractability: microworlds had a handful of objects, and programs that worked by trying combinations collapsed when the number of objects grew. Scaling up was assumed to be a matter of faster hardware; the theory of NP-completeness later showed otherwise. In Britain the Lighthill report (1973) criticised AI for failing to cope with this “combinatorial explosion”, and the government cut support for AI research in all but two universities. Third, the basic structures had limits: Minsky and Papert’s book Perceptrons (1969) proved that a single perceptron cannot represent functions such as XOR, and funding for neural-network research all but dried up. Choose a branching factor and a machine and see how deep a full search can go in a chess player’s three minutes.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{nodes}(d) &= \sum_{i=0}^{d} b^{\,i} = \frac{b^{\,d+1}-1}{b-1} \\ \text{pruned leaves} &\approx b^{\,d/2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a search tree with b choices at each step has bi positions at depth i, so looking d steps ahead visits roughly bd positions in total. Chess has b ≈ 35. Every extra step of lookahead multiplies the work by 35, so a computer 35 times faster buys one more step. Alpha–beta pruning (Knuth and Moore, 1975) at best cuts the leaves to about bd/2, which doubles the depth, but the growth is still exponential.

Machine speeds are orders of magnitude, not benchmarks: about 10⁴ positions a second for a 1960s mainframe program, about 2 × 10⁸ for IBM’s Deep Blue (its published figure), and 10¹² as a round number for a modern cluster.

Expert systems (1969–1986). The fix for brittle, general-purpose “weak methods” was knowledge: lots of it, about one narrow domain. DENDRAL (Feigenbaum, Buchanan and Lederberg) inferred molecular structures from mass-spectrometer readings, using chemists’ rules of thumb. MYCIN, with about 450 rules for diagnosing blood infections, did as well as some experts and better than junior doctors. Its rules came from interviewing experts, and because medical rules are uncertain it attached a certainty factor to each. In 1982 R1 (later XCON) began configuring computer orders at Digital Equipment Corporation; by 1986 it was saving the company an estimated $40 million a year. Japan announced its ten-year Fifth Generation project in 1981, and the AI industry grew from a few million dollars in 1980 to billions by 1988.

Then came the AI winter. Companies failed to deliver on extravagant promises; expert systems were hard to build and maintain, could not learn from experience, and their reasoning broke down under uncertainty. The specialised Lisp-machine market collapsed in 1987. (The first winter, after Lighthill, ran through the later 1970s.) Here is a toy rule base in MYCIN’s style, about a car that will not start. Tick the evidence you see and watch the certainty factors combine.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{both} \ge 0{:}\ \ \text{CF} &= c_1 + c_2\,(1 - c_1) \\ \text{both} < 0{:}\ \ \text{CF} &= c_1 + c_2\,(1 + c_1) \\ \text{mixed}{:}\ \ \text{CF} &= \frac{c_1 + c_2}{1 - \min(|c_1|, |c_2|)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each rule that fires contributes a certainty factor between −1 (certainly false) and +1 (certainly true). Two supporting rules close part of the remaining gap to 1: 0.6 then 0.7 gives 0.6 + 0.7 × 0.4 = 0.88. Opposing rules partly cancel. The result doesn’t depend on the order the rules fire. This is the combining function of EMYCIN, MYCIN’s general-purpose shell. Heckerman (1986) later showed that certainty factors behave like probabilities only under strong independence assumptions that real rule bases break.

History, part 3 · 1986–2010

Neural networks return, probability takes over, data piles up

The return of neural networks (1986–). In the mid-1980s at least four groups reinvented the back-propagation learning algorithm, first developed in the early 1960s, and the two-volume Parallel Distributed Processing (Rumelhart and McClelland, 1986) made it famous. These connectionist models were offered as rivals to symbolic systems: they learn from examples by comparing their predictions with the true answers and adjusting their weights, and they handle the fuzzy, hard-to-define concepts of the real world well.

Probabilistic reasoning and machine learning (1987–). The brittleness of expert systems pushed the field towards a more scientific style: probability instead of Boolean logic, learning instead of hand-coding, and experiments on shared benchmarks instead of philosophical claims. Speech recognition went over to hidden Markov models, which have a rigorous mathematical theory and are trained on large corpora of real speech. Judea Pearl’s Probabilistic Reasoning in Intelligent Systems (1988) introduced Bayesian networks for reasoning efficiently under uncertainty, and Rich Sutton tied reinforcement learning to the Markov decision processes of operations research. Shared datasets such as the UCI repository and MNIST let anyone check a claim.

Big data (2001–). Moore’s law and the web produced enormous datasets. Banko and Brill (2001) found that on a word-confusion task (“then” or “than”? “principal” or “principle”?), a mediocre algorithm trained on 100 million words beat the best algorithm trained on 1 million; Hays and Efros (2007) saw photo-hole filling go from poor with 10,000 photos to excellent with 2 million. More data can matter more than a cleverer method. Below, a rigid but well-tuned model (logistic regression, a straight boundary) races a crude but flexible one (k-nearest neighbours with k = √n) on a curvy boundary. Give them more data.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{err}(n) &\approx \text{err}_{\infty} + a\,n^{-\beta} \\ \log(\text{err} - \text{err}_{\infty}) &\approx \log a - \beta \log n \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a learning curve. Test error falls with the number of training examples n roughly as a power law towards a floor err∞. A rigid model has a high floor (its bias); a flexible model has a low floor but needs data to get there. Taking logs turns the power law into a straight line whose slope is −β, so β can be read off by least squares. Here the floor for the flexible model is the label noise: no method can beat it.
Say it“Error at n is about the floor plus a times n to the minus beta.”

History, part 4 · 2011 to now

Deep learning, and the state of the art

Deep learning (2011–). Deep learning means machine learning with many layers of simple, adjustable computing elements. Such networks were tried in the 1970s, and convolutional networks read handwritten digits in the 1990s, but the breakthrough came around 2011, first in speech recognition and then in vision. In the 2012 ImageNet competition, Geoffrey Hinton’s group cut the error of the best system by about ten percentage points; within a few years the winners’ error fell below a human tester’s on that benchmark. Similar jumps followed in machine translation, medical image diagnosis and game playing, with AlphaGo defeating the Go champion Lee Sedol in 2016. It all depends on hardware: graphics processors and purpose-built chips running at 1014 to 1017 operations a second, trained on very large datasets.

The state of the art. Stanford’s AI Index tracks progress across the field, and the list grows each year: driverless cars carrying passengers in several cities; legged robots that walk and recover from shoves; NASA’s Remote Agent (1999) and the Mars rovers planning their own activities; translation between more than a hundred languages; speech recognition approaching human transcribers on conversational benchmarks; recommendation systems; superhuman play at Go, chess, poker and StarCraft; protein-structure prediction by AlphaFold (2020); and since about 2020, large language models that write, summarise, translate and code, and image generators that draw from a sentence. Fit a trend to the ImageNet winners, and ask how long it can go on.

\[ \log_2 \text{err}(t) = \log_2 \text{err}(t_0) - \frac{t - t_0}{h} \]
Read it as: if the error halves every h years, its logarithm falls on a straight line with slope −1/h. A least-squares line through log₂ of the winners’ top-5 error against the year estimates the halving time h. Top-5 error is the share of test images whose correct label is not among the system’s five best guesses.

Project: seventy years of AI in Wikidata

Wikidata is the free, crowd-edited knowledge base behind Wikipedia, released under CC0. I queried its public SPARQL endpoint for three kinds of record. People: everyone with a birth date and at least two Wikimedia sitelinks whose field of work is AI or one of 36 AI subfields, whose occupation is AI researcher, roboticist, cyberneticist or computational linguist, who holds an AI award (AAAI Fellow, IJCAI Computers and Thought, IJCAI Research Excellence, EurAI Fellow), or whom Wikidata names as an author, creator or developer of one of the landmark systems: 1,070 people. Awards: all 79 Turing Award laureates, with the year. Systems: the 48 landmarks from the hero, 25 of which Wikidata dates; I dated the other 23 from the history above (hollow markers).

Each person’s school of thought comes from their Wikidata tags: neural networks and deep learning count as connectionist; logic, knowledge representation, expert systems and planning as symbolic; machine learning, probability and statistics as statistical; robotics, vision, cybernetics and control as embodied; language and cognitive science as mind & language. A person’s school is the one with the most tags (ties go in that order), and a person who built a landmark system also gets one tag for its school. Who was born into which school?

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{logit} p(x) &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 x \\ x &= (\text{born} - 1950)/10 \\ \text{crossover} &= 1950 - 10\,\beta_0/\beta_1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: among people whose school is symbolic, connectionist or statistical, p(x) is the probability of being in one of the two learning schools for someone born in year 1950 + 10x. Its log-odds rise in a straight line with birth decade. eβ₁ is how much the odds multiply per decade; the crossover is the birth year at which the two camps are equally likely. Nothing here is causal: it describes who Wikidata lists, not why.
Say it“The log-odds of learning given birth year are beta-nought plus beta-one times decades since 1950.”

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

D = json.load(open("x001-data.json", encoding="utf8"))   # the records embedded in this page
SCHOOLS = ["connectionist", "symbolic", "mind & language", "embodied", "statistical", "general"]
people = pd.DataFrame(D["people"], columns=["name", "born", "died", "sitelinks", "school", "mask", "qid"])
people["school"] = people["school"].map(dict(enumerate(SCHOOLS)))

for T in (5, 15):
    P = people[people.sitelinks >= T]
    print(f"--- people with at least {T} sitelinks: {len(P)} ---")
    g = P.groupby("school")["born"]
    print(pd.DataFrame({"people": g.size(), "median born": g.median()}).reindex(SCHOOLS).to_string())
    k = P[P.school.isin(["symbolic", "connectionist", "statistical"])]
    x = ((k.born - 1950) / 10).to_numpy()[:, None]
    y = k.school.isin(["connectionist", "statistical"]).to_numpy().astype(int)
    lr = LogisticRegression(C=np.inf, tol=1e-10, max_iter=1000).fit(x, y)   # no penalty
    b0, b1 = lr.intercept_[0], lr.coef_[0, 0]
    print(f"learning vs symbolic, n = {len(k)}: logit p = {b0:.4f} + {b1:.4f} * (born - 1950)/10")
    print(f"  odds x{np.exp(b1):.3f} per decade; p = 0.5 at born = {1950 - 10 * b0 / b1:.1f}")

tu = pd.DataFrame(D["turing"], columns=["name", "year", "qid", "school"])
print(f"Turing Award laureates: {len(tu)}; in the AI cohort: {(tu.school >= 0).sum()}")

Honest caveats about the data: Wikidata is crowd-edited and its tags are patchy. Alan Turing’s only matching tag is “logic”, so he counts as symbolic; some birth dates are placeholders (Wikidata gives Alex Krizhevsky’s as 2000); and many famous names carry only the generic “AI” tag. Sitelinks measure Wikipedia coverage, not importance, and birth year is not when anyone did their work. Treat the picture as a portrait of the record, not of history itself.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • AI has four definitions, from two choices: judge thought or behaviour, and measure against humans or against rationality. Most modern AI builds rational agents: systems that act to get the best expected outcome.
  • The Turing test lists the skills behind acting humanly: language, knowledge, reasoning, learning, and (for the total test) vision and robotics. Cognitive modelling copies human thought, mistakes and all; logic and probability define correct thought.
  • A rational agent is only as good as its objective: if the objective is wrong, a perfect optimiser does the wrong thing perfectly. That is the value alignment problem.
  • AI’s tools came from eight older fields: philosophy, mathematics, economics, neuroscience, psychology, computer engineering, control theory and linguistics.
  • The history ran: birth (1943–56), great expectations, a dose of reality and the combinatorial explosion, expert systems and the winters, then neural networks, probability, big data, and deep learning since 2011.

Check your understanding

A chess program plays nothing like a human grandmaster but wins every game. Which definitions of AI does it satisfy?

Acting rationally (it reliably achieves the goal). It fails acting humanly and thinking humanly, because its play and its search look nothing like a person’s. Whether it “thinks rationally” depends on whether you count exhaustive search as reasoning by rules.

In the taxicab problem a model predicts that people answer 0.80. Is it a good model?

As a model of human thinking, yes: it matches what people actually say. As a model of rational thinking, no: Bayes’ rule gives 0.41. The two definitions grade the same model in opposite ways.

Why did faster computers not rescue the early microworld programs?

Their search was exponential: a tree with branching factor b costs about bd to search to depth d, so a machine b times faster buys only one more step. Scaling needed knowledge or better algorithms, not just speed.

Name two weaknesses that ended the expert-system boom.

Any two of: the rules were expensive to extract from experts and to maintain; the systems could not learn from experience; they were brittle outside their narrow domain; and their ad hoc certainty factors broke down as a way of reasoning under uncertainty.

What did Banko and Brill’s word-confusion experiment show, and why did it matter for AI?

That a mediocre algorithm with 100 times more training data beat the best algorithm with less data. It suggested the knowledge bottleneck could be beaten by learning from large datasets rather than hand-coding, one root of today’s data-hungry deep learning.
