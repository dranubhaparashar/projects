Machine Translation

Encoder-decoder models: read a sentence in one language, write it in another, and learn to line the two up along the way

Translation is the oldest dream of language technology and the task that gave us the encoder-decoder, attention and the transformer. It is hard for reasons that have nothing to do with computers: languages put their words in different orders, pack different information into a word, carve the world into different lexical pieces and leave different things unsaid. The figure links an English sentence to its translation in Velish, a small language invented for this chapter. Pick a sentence and split the Velish words into morphemes: the links cross, one English word becomes two Velish pieces, and some words have no partner at all. An encoder-decoder model has to learn all of that from examples.

Language divergences

Why translation is hard

If every language were English with the words swapped, translation would be a dictionary look-up. It is not, and the ways in which languages differ are what a translation model has to learn. Linguists call the systematic differences translation divergences; some are typological (they recur across many languages and can be catalogued) and some are idiosyncratic (one language just happens to say it that way). Five of them matter most for machine translation.

1. Word order. English is an SVO language: subject, verb, object (the cat ate the apple). Japanese, Hindi, Turkish and most of the world’s languages are SOV (cat apple ate); Irish and Classical Arabic are VSO. Related choices follow: whether adjectives precede or follow the noun, whether the language uses prepositions or postpositions, where the verb’s auxiliaries sit. A translator therefore cannot work left to right in lock-step. The verb that English says second may be the last word of the source sentence, so the model must have read the whole source before it writes. Pick a word order below and watch how many links cross when the same meaning is rearranged.

Basic word order of the target

How common

Of 1,377 languages in the World Atlas of Language Structures (Dryer 2013, feature 81A), 565 are SOV, 488 SVO, 95 VSO, 25 VOS, 11 OVS, 4 OSV and 189 have no single dominant order. Object-before-subject orders are rare; subject-first orders cover three quarters of the sample.

2. Morphology. English packs little into a word: cat, cats, and that is almost the whole paradigm. An agglutinative language such as Turkish or Finnish strings suffixes onto a stem, one meaning each, so a single word can carry number, case, possession and more (ev-ler-im-de, “in my houses”). A fusional language such as Russian or Latin folds several meanings into one ending. Velish, the invented language of this chapter, is agglutinative in a tiny way: mi-rimu-ek-um is my-apple-PLURAL-OBJECT, one word for “my apples” as the object of a verb. The consequence for a learner is sparsity: the more forms a stem has, the rarer each one is. A corpus of a given size simply never shows most of the forms of a rich language. The slider model below makes that concrete: with \(N\) stems and \(k\) suffix slots of \(m\) values each there are \(N\,m^k\) word forms, and a corpus of \(T\) tokens drawn uniformly from them is expected to have seen only a fraction \(1-(1-1/F)^T\) of them. This is why modern systems translate subword units rather than words (section 6).

3. Lexical gaps and lexical divergence. Words do not line up one to one. English know splits into French savoir (a fact) and connaître (a person or place); English wall splits into Spanish pared (inside) and muro (outside); Japanese has separate words for an older and a younger brother and no plain word for “brother”. Going the other way, German Schadenfreude and Danish hygge have no single English word. In Velish there is no article at all: English the maps to nothing, and the model must learn to produce it from nothing. The idiom dagun is one word that means “all day long”, and lun dok is two words for “quietly”. A translation model has to learn these many-to-one and one-to-many mappings, and to pick the right sense of a source word from its context.

4. Referential density. Languages differ in how much they leave out. Pro-drop languages such as Spanish, Italian, Japanese and Chinese routinely omit a subject pronoun that the verb ending or the context makes recoverable; Japanese and Chinese drop objects too. Linguists call languages that spell out their referents hot (the reader is handed everything) and languages that make the reader infer them cold. Translating from a cold language into a hot one is hard because the model must invent the missing pronoun, and choose its gender and number from the context, with all the risks of section 9. Toggle the pronouns in the paragraph below to see how much an English text relies on them.

5. Idioms and everything else. “It’s raining cats and dogs” is not about animals; a French speaker says it is raining ropes. Idioms must be translated as wholes, which word-by-word systems could never do and which modern neural systems do reasonably well only because they have seen the idiom in context thousands of times. Add honorifics (Japanese and Korean change the verb forms depending on who is speaking to whom), evidentiality (Turkish verbs mark whether you witnessed the event), tense and aspect systems that do not match, and cultural references, and you have the real shape of the task. None of these can be handled by rules alone; the lesson of forty years of MT research is that they must be learned from parallel text, and the rest of this chapter is about how.

The architecture

The encoder-decoder: read everything, then write

Machine translation is a sequence-to-sequence problem: a source sentence \(x = x_1 \dots x_S\) in, a target sentence \(y = y_1 \dots y_T\) out, with different lengths and no fixed correspondence between positions. The encoder-decoder architecture (also called seq2seq) handles it with two networks. The encoder reads the entire source and turns it into a set of context vectors \(h_1 \dots h_S\), one per source token. The decoder is a conditional language model: it writes the target one token at a time, each token conditioned on the tokens it has already written and on the encoder’s vectors. Because the whole source is read before the first target word is produced, the verb-last problem of Japanese or Velish is no problem at all.

\[ P(y \mid x) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^{T} P\big(y_t \,\big|\, y_{
Read it as: the probability of a translation is the product, over target positions, of the probability of each target token given everything written so far and the encoded source. The encoder produces one vector per source token; the decoder turns its own state and the encoder’s vectors into a distribution over the target vocabulary.
Say it“P of y given x is the product over t of P of y-t given the earlier y’s and x.”

Three generations of the same idea. Sutskever, Vinyals and Le (2014) built the first large neural translator from two LSTMs: the encoder’s final state, a single vector, was handed to the decoder as its initial state, and that one vector had to hold the whole sentence. It worked, remarkably, but quality fell with sentence length: the vector is a bottleneck. Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio (2015) removed the bottleneck with attention: the decoder keeps all \(S\) encoder vectors and, at every step, computes a weighted average of them with weights that depend on its current state, so that it can look back at the part of the source it is translating now. Vaswani et al. (2017) then replaced the recurrent networks on both sides by stacks of attention layers, the transformer, keeping exactly this encoder-decoder shape. Switch between the three below.

Architecture

Highlight (transformer)

Cross-attention is attention with a twist. In the attention chapter every token of one sequence produced a query, a key and a value, and the sequence attended to itself. Cross-attention uses the same arithmetic with the roles split across the two networks: the queries come from the decoder (its current state, “what am I translating now?”) and the keys and values come from the encoder (the source tokens, “here is what I have to offer”). The result, the context vector \(c_t\), is a weighted mixture of source vectors, and the weights \(\alpha_{tj}\) are, in effect, a soft word alignment that the model learned without ever being shown one. The transformer decoder has three attention blocks per layer: masked self-attention over the target written so far (it may not look at future target tokens, see the causal mask), cross-attention into the encoder, and the feed-forward network. The encoder has ordinary, unmasked self-attention because it may read the whole source.

\[ e_{tj} = \frac{q_t \cdot k_j}{\sqrt{d}}, \qquad \alpha_{tj} = \frac{\exp e_{tj}}{\sum_{i=1}^{S}\exp e_{ti}}, \qquad c_t = \sum_{j=1}^{S} \alpha_{tj}\, v_j, \qquad q_t = W_q s_t,\; k_j = W_k h_j,\; v_j = W_v h_j \]
Read it as: score the decoder’s query against every source key with a dot product (scaled by the square root of the dimension in a transformer); softmax the scores into weights that sum to one; the context is the weighted average of the source values. In the recurrent model of the project the scaling and the three projections collapse to a single matrix: \(e_{tj} = s_t^{\top} W_a h_j\), and the values are the encoder states themselves.
Say it“Alpha-t-j is the softmax over source positions of q-t dot k-j; c-t is the alpha-weighted sum of the values.”

RNN encoder-decoder Sutskever et al. 2014

Two LSTMs; the source is squeezed into the final encoder state. Simple, sequential, and the single vector forgets the beginning of long sentences. Trick that helped: feed the source reversed, so the first source words sit closest to the first target words.

RNN + attention Bahdanau et al. 2015; Luong et al. 2015

All encoder states are kept; each decoder step builds its own context by attention. Length no longer hurts; the weights are a readable alignment. Still sequential in both encoder and decoder, so slow to train on long sentences.

Transformer encoder-decoder Vaswani et al. 2017

Self-attention replaces recurrence on both sides; cross-attention keeps the bridge. Every position of the encoder is computed in parallel, training is fast, and the model scales. The design of choice for dedicated MT systems (Marian, fairseq, NLLB, T5 and mT5).

Decoder-only LLM GPT-style, 2020 onwards

No encoder: the source sentence is simply the beginning of the prompt and the decoder attends to it with the same masked self-attention it uses for everything else. Translation becomes one of many prompted tasks; quality for high-resource pairs now rivals dedicated systems, at a far greater cost per sentence.

Learning from pairs

Training: teacher forcing and the exposure gap

Training data for translation is a parallel corpus: sentence pairs \((x, y)\) where \(y\) is a human translation of \(x\). The objective is the one every language model uses, cross-entropy of the next token, applied to the target side and conditioned on the source. At target position \(t\) the decoder is given the reference prefix \(y_1 \dots y_{t-1}\), predicts a distribution over the vocabulary, and is charged \(-\log P(y_t \mid y_{

\[ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{(x,y)}\;\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log P_\theta\big(y_t \,\big|\, y_{
Read it as: the loss is the average negative log-probability that the model assigns to each reference target token, given the reference prefix and the source. Its gradient with respect to the output logits \(z\) is the familiar “predicted probability minus one-hot truth”: raise the logit of the right word, lower the others in proportion to how much probability they stole.
Say it“Minus the average over pairs and positions of log P of y-t given the earlier y’s and x.”

Teacher forcing. Notice what the decoder was given: the reference prefix, not its own earlier predictions. That is teacher forcing, and it is what makes training fast and stable. Every target position becomes an independent classification problem once the prefix is fixed, so all \(T\) positions of a sentence can be computed in parallel (one masked pass in a transformer, one unrolled pass in an RNN), and an early mistake never contaminates the later positions’ training signal. The price is paid at test time, when there is no teacher. The decoder then feeds its own output back in, and if it writes a wrong word at position 3 it must continue from a prefix it never saw during training. This mismatch between training on gold prefixes and testing on its own prefixes is called exposure bias: the model was never exposed to its own errors, so it never learned to recover from them.

The simulation below makes the gap visible with two numbers. When the prefix so far is correct, the model gets the next token right with probability \(p\) (this is what teacher forcing measures: per-token accuracy on gold prefixes). Once the prefix contains an error, it is in territory it never trained on and its per-token accuracy drops to \(q < p\). Under teacher forcing, every position is right with probability \(p\). In free running, the probability that position \(t\) is still on the gold path is \(p^{t}\), and the sentence-level exact match is \(p^{T}\). Move \(p\), \(q\) and the length: a model that looks 95% accurate per token under teacher forcing gets fewer than half of its 15-word sentences exactly right.

What people do about it. Less than you might think. Scheduled sampling (Bengio et al. 2015) gradually replaces gold prefix tokens by the model’s own samples during training; it helps recurrent models somewhat but breaks the parallel training of transformers and is rarely used there. Sequence-level training (minimum risk training, reinforcement learning with BLEU as the reward) optimises the quality of whole decoded outputs rather than per-token likelihood, at a large cost in compute and stability. In practice the dominant remedies are indirect: more data, bigger models (whose per-token \(p\) is so close to 1 that \(p^T\) stays high), label smoothing (train towards a target distribution that puts 0.9 on the reference token and spreads 0.1 over the rest, which keeps the model from becoming over-confident and improves BLEU by a few tenths of a point), and a decoding procedure that does not commit to the single most probable token at each step. That procedure is the subject of the next section.

Search

Decoding: greedy, beam search and the length problem

Once the model is trained, translating means finding the target sentence it believes in most: \(\hat y = \arg\max_y P(y \mid x)\). That is a search over every possible sentence, \(|V|^T\) of them, and no shortcut computes it exactly, because the probability of a word depends on everything before it. Greedy decoding takes the single most probable token at each step and moves on. It is fast and often fine, but it can be trapped: a token that looks best now may lead only to poor continuations, while a slightly worse token would have opened a much better sentence. The decoding chapter showed the same thing for free-running language models.

Beam search keeps the \(k\) best partial translations (the beam) instead of one. At every step each of the \(k\) hypotheses is extended by every vocabulary word, the \(k \times |V|\) candidates are scored by their total log-probability, and the best \(k\) survive. A hypothesis that produces the end-of-sentence token \(\langle/\!s\rangle\) is complete; it is set aside and the search continues until the beam is empty or a length limit is reached. The answer is the best complete hypothesis. The cost is \(k\) times a greedy decode; typical values are \(k = 4\) to \(10\), and, oddly, larger beams often make translations worse, for a reason the next figure exposes. Widen the beam, soften the model, and toggle length normalisation.

Because every term is negative, longer hypotheses always score lower, and the search is biased towards short outputs. Why bigger beams can hurt. With a wide beam the search finds hypotheses with genuinely higher total probability, and the highest-probability hypothesis under a neural translation model is often too short, or, in the extreme, empty: Stahlberg and Byrne (2019) ran exact search on a trained transformer and found that for over half of the test sentences the single most probable output was the empty string. Beam search with \(k = 4\) works not because it searches well but because it searches badly in a helpful way, and length normalisation and a small beam are the compromise that production systems settled on.

Sampling and minimum Bayes risk. Translation wants the one best output, so the sampling methods of the decoding chapter (temperature, top-k, nucleus) are rarely used directly; they produce varied but less accurate translations. They have found a second life inside minimum Bayes risk decoding: sample a few dozen candidate translations, then pick the candidate that is most similar to all the others under a quality metric such as chrF or COMET (chapter 4.5c). The idea is that the consensus translation, the one that agrees with most of what the model might say, is a safer bet than the single most probable string. MBR with a neural metric has beaten beam search in several recent evaluations, at ten to a hundred times the decoding cost.

Where the pairs come from

Parallel data: alignment and back-translation

Everything so far assumed a pile of sentence pairs. Where do they come from? Humans have been translating documents for millennia, and some institutions do it at scale: the proceedings of the European Parliament (Europarl, 21 languages), United Nations documents (six languages), the Canadian Hansard (English and French), film subtitles, software documentation, religious texts, and increasingly web pages that exist in several languages. OPUS collects hundreds of such sources; ParaCrawl and CCMatrix were mined from web crawls. But a translated document is aligned at the level of the document, not the sentence, and translators do not keep sentence boundaries: they merge two short sentences, split a long one, or leave one out. Turning document pairs into sentence pairs is sentence alignment.

Length-based alignment. Gale and Church (1993) noticed that, across a corpus, the character length of a sentence and of its translation are strongly correlated: long sentences translate into long sentences. That is enough. Treat the two texts as sequences of sentences, allow a small set of beads (1–1, one sentence to one; 2–1 and 1–2, a merge or a split; 1–0 and 0–1, an omission; 2–2), give each bead type a prior probability estimated from hand-aligned data, and score a bead by how surprising the length difference is: \(\delta = (l_2 - c\,l_1)/\sqrt{l_1 s^2}\), where \(c\) is the mean length ratio and \(s^2\) the variance of the ratio per character. The alignment that minimises the total cost is found by dynamic programming over the sentence grid, like edit distance. The demo aligns a short English text with its Velish translation, in which the translator merged two English sentences into one. Change the variance \(s^2\) to see how tolerant the aligner is, and add an untranslated sentence to see its blind spot.

\[ \operatorname{cost}(\text{bead}) = -\log P(\text{type}) \;-\; \log\Big(2\big(1-\Phi(|\delta|)\big)\Big), \qquad \delta = \frac{l_2 - c\,l_1}{\sqrt{l_1\, s^2}}, \qquad D(i,j) = \min_{\text{bead}}\big[D(i-a, j-b) + \operatorname{cost}\big] \]
Read it as: a bead costs the negative log of its prior (1–1 beads are cheap, omissions are dear) plus the negative log of the two-sided tail probability of its standardised length difference under a normal model. \(D(i,j)\) is the cheapest way to align the first \(i\) source and first \(j\) target sentences; it is filled from the bead types that could end there. The priors Gale and Church estimated: 1–1 0.89, 1–0 and 0–1 0.0099 each, 2–1 and 1–2 0.089 each, 2–2 0.011.

Beads on the best path

Length is a weak signal, which is why it works: it needs no dictionary and transfers to any pair of languages. Its weakness shows with omissions. An untranslated sentence is penalised twice, by the rare 1–0 prior and by its full length appearing as a discrepancy, so the aligner would rather merge it into a neighbour as a 2–1 bead; the demo does exactly that, at every setting of the variance, and the merged pair would enter the training data as a wrong translation. Modern aligners (Vecalign, Bleualign, Hunalign) add lexical evidence: a bilingual dictionary, or multilingual sentence embeddings such as LASER, which place a sentence and its translation close together in one vector space, so that a bead is scored by the cosine between the embeddings of its two sides. With embeddings one can go further and mine parallel sentences from texts that were never translations of each other, by searching a huge monolingual pool for the nearest neighbour of each sentence across languages; that is how CCMatrix found 4.5 billion pairs. Mined and crawled pairs are noisy, and filtering them (by length ratio, language identification, embedding similarity, and a translation model’s own score) is a large part of building a system.

Back-translation: monolingual data as parallel data. For most language pairs there is a hundred times more monolingual text than parallel text, and monolingual text in the target language is exactly what a decoder needs to learn fluency. Sennrich, Haddow and Birch (2016) showed how to use it. Train a reverse system, target to source, on the parallel data you have. Translate a large monolingual target-language corpus backwards into synthetic source sentences. Pair each real target sentence with its synthetic source and add the pairs to the training data of the forward system. The target side is human text, so the decoder learns real fluency; the source side is machine output, but the model only has to read it. In their experiments back-translation gained 2 to 3 BLEU, and it has been a standard ingredient of every strong MT system since, including the iterative form where forward and reverse systems take turns improving each other. Tokenisation

One subword vocabulary for two languages

Section 1 showed the problem: a morphologically rich language has far more word forms than any corpus shows, and a word-level translator meets an unknown word in nearly every sentence. The fix, now universal, is to translate subword units learned by byte-pair encoding or a unigram language model (SentencePiece); the tokens chapter built BPE by hand. Two details are specific to translation. First, the vocabulary is usually learned jointly on the concatenation of both sides of the parallel corpus, so that strings shared by the two languages (names, numbers, loanwords, and in related languages whole stems) get the same token id on both sides, which lets the model copy them through and lets related languages share statistics. Second, with a shared vocabulary the source and target embedding tables can be tied into one, saving parameters and helping rare tokens.

Three practical notes. A shared vocabulary between unrelated scripts shares little beyond digits and punctuation, so multilingual systems covering a hundred languages use large vocabularies (250,000 tokens in mT5 and NLLB) and sample the training text for vocabulary learning with a temperature that boosts small languages, or else Swahili ends up spelled out letter by letter while English gets whole words. Subword segmentation also changes what BLEU measures, which is why scores are computed on detokenised text (section 8). And subwords do not remove the need to handle truly new strings: names and numbers are copied through, and a model can still produce a plausible-looking but wrong name, which is one of the characteristic failures of neural MT.

Many languages, one model

Multilingual models, zero-shot translation and the data gap

A translation system for every pair of 100 languages would need 9,900 models. Johnson et al. (2017) at Google showed that one encoder-decoder can learn many pairs at once with a single change to the data: prepend to each source sentence a token naming the target language, such as <2fr>, and train on the union of all the parallel corpora. The model learns to read any source language and to write whichever target the token requests. Two things followed that nobody had engineered. Low-resource pairs improved, because they shared encoder and decoder parameters with high-resource ones (transfer). And the model could translate between pairs it had never seen paired: trained on Portuguese↔English and English↔Spanish, it produced reasonable Portuguese→Spanish, a zero-shot translation.

The explanation that fits the evidence is a partially shared representation space: the encoder maps sentences with the same meaning to nearby vectors regardless of their language, a learned approximation of the interlingua that rule-based MT dreamt of in the 1960s. This is a schematic, not a trained model; real multilingual encoders are only partly language-neutral, and zero-shot output often suffers from off-target translation, where the model writes in the wrong language (typically English, the one it saw most) because the target token is a weak signal compared with the pull of the data. Remedies include adding small amounts of real data for the zero-shot pair, back-translation through a pivot, and explicit training objectives that pull translations together in representation space. Massively multilingual systems also hit the curse of multilinguality: at a fixed model size, adding languages eventually hurts the high-resource ones, because they compete for capacity; the answer has been larger models (NLLB-200 at 54 billion parameters covers 200 languages, Costa-jussà et al. 2022) and language-group-specific experts.

Scoring, briefly

How good is a translation? BLEU in one paragraph

The gold standard is human judgement: bilingual raters score adequacy (does it mean what the source meant?) and fluency (is it good target language?), or rank system outputs against each other; the annual WMT shared tasks still rely on it. It is slow and expensive, so development runs on automatic metrics, and the one that shaped twenty years of MT research is BLEU (Papineni et al. 2002). Its idea: a good translation shares many words and phrases with a human reference translation. For \(n = 1\) to \(4\), count how many of the candidate’s \(n\)-grams appear in the reference (each reference \(n\)-gram may be used only as many times as it occurs: clipped counts, so that “the the the the” does not score), divide by the number of candidate \(n\)-grams, and take the geometric mean of the four precisions. Precision alone would reward a two-word candidate that happens to be right, so multiply by a brevity penalty that falls off exponentially when the candidate is shorter than the reference. BLEU is computed over a whole test set (counts are summed before dividing), scaled to 0–100, and is meaningful only for comparing systems on the same test set with the same tokenisation.

\[ \text{BLEU} = \text{BP}\cdot\exp\Big(\tfrac14\sum_{n=1}^{4}\log p_n\Big), \qquad p_n = \frac{\sum \text{clipped matched } n\text{-grams}}{\sum \text{candidate } n\text{-grams}}, \qquad \text{BP} = \begin{cases}1 & c > r\\ e^{\,1 - r/c} & c \le r\end{cases} \]
Read it as: the geometric mean of the clipped 1- to 4-gram precisions, multiplied by a brevity penalty that is 1 when the candidate corpus (total length \(c\)) is at least as long as the reference corpus (total length \(r\)) and shrinks exponentially when it is shorter.

Sentence-level BLEU is shown here only to make the arithmetic visible; it is noisy and is zero whenever any \(p_n\) is zero. The project reports corpus BLEU over 267 held-out sentences, with the same implementation, in Python and in your browser.

BLEU’s weaknesses are well known: it sees only exact string matches (a correct synonym scores nothing), it is blind to meaning and grammar beyond local word order, it depends on tokenisation (the sacreBLEU tool exists to standardise that), and its correlation with human judgement has weakened as systems improved. chrF scores character \(n\)-grams and is kinder to morphologically rich languages; BERTScore and COMET compare embeddings and learn from human ratings, and correlate with people far better. All of that, including human evaluation protocols and how to read a metric’s confidence interval, is the subject of the next chapter, Scoring Generated Text. For this chapter, remember: BLEU is a precision of shared \(n\)-grams with a length penalty, corpus-level, and comparable only within one test set.

Bias and ethics

Who is “the doctor”? Gender, defaults and harm

Section 1 noted that languages differ in what they leave unsaid. Turkish, Finnish, Hungarian, Persian and many others have a single third-person pronoun with no gender; English forces a choice between he, she and they. Translating o bir doktor from Turkish, a system must decide, and it decides the way it was trained to: by picking the pronoun that most often accompanied doctor in its parallel data. Stanovsky, Smith and Zettlemoyer (2019) built WinoMT, a test set of English sentences in which the gender of a person is fixed by a coreferent pronoun (“The doctor asked the nurse to help her in the operation”), translated them with four commercial and two academic systems into eight languages that mark gender on nouns, and checked whether the doctor came out female. Every system did significantly worse on the sentences that go against occupational stereotypes than on those that follow them, and several were barely above chance overall. The systems were not programmed with stereotypes; they learned the training distribution and, crucially, decoding turned a distribution into a default.

What actually helps. Sampling matches the training frequencies but is wrong for any particular person just as often, and inconsistent across sentences. Real remedies work on the data and the interface: balancing or augmenting training data with counterfactual pairs (swap the gender in sentences about occupations), adding sentence-level gender tags so the user or an upstream coreference step can state the intended gender, producing both translations when the source is ambiguous (as Google Translate began doing for short queries in 2018), and using document context so that a pronoun fixed in sentence 1 stays fixed in sentence 5. None is complete; all are better than a silent default.

Beyond pronouns. The ethical weight of MT comes from where it is used. Machine-translated medical instructions, asylum interviews, police statements and court documents have all caused documented harm, because a fluent output hides a wrong one: neural systems hallucinate, producing confident sentences unrelated to the source, especially for low-resource languages and out-of-domain input, and a monolingual reader cannot tell. The quality gap of the previous section is itself an ethical issue: speakers of a class-0 language get a worse, more biased tool, and the parallel data that exists for their language was often created without their consent (crawled, or translated from religious texts whose register colours everything). Finally, MT trained on human translators’ work now competes with those translators. The practical rules are modest: say when text was machine translated, keep a human in the loop where errors cost something, evaluate on the languages and domains you will actually serve, and report bias measurements alongside BLEU.

Real translation corpora are too large to train on in a browser, and any real language would smuggle in copyrighted text. So this chapter invents one. The language

A Velish sentence is adverbial, subject, object, (negation), verb. A noun phrase is a possessive prefix (mi- my, tu- your, or nothing for the), the noun stem, -ek if plural, -um if it is the object, and then any adjective. The verb is a stem plus -a (present) or -ta (past); nai before the verb negates it. English, by contrast, puts the verb in the middle, marks agreement only in the present singular (sleeps), has irregular pasts (slept, ran, sang, ate, saw, found), irregular plurals (children, fish, wolves), and negates with does not / do not / did not plus the base form. M32 = 0xFFFFFFFF
class Mulberry32:
    def __init__(self, seed): self.a = seed & M32
    def __call__(self):
        self.a = (self.a + 0x6D2B79F5) & M32
        t = self.a
        t = ((t ^ (t >> 15)) * (1 | t)) & M32
        u = ((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & M32
        t = ((t + u) & M32) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M32) / 4294967296
def gauss(r):
    u = 0.0
    while u == 0.0: u = r()
    return math.sqrt(-2 * math.log(u)) * math.cos(2 * math.pi * r())
def pick(r, xs): return xs[int(r() * len(xs))]

def noun_phrase(r, animate_only, obj):

def sentence(r):

    idiom = pick(r, IDIOMS) if r() < 0.5 else None
    verb = pick(r, VERBS)
    past, neg = r() < 0.5, r() < 0.2
    s_eng, s_vel, s_pl = noun_phrase(r, True, False)

# ---------- 3. the model: GRU encoder, GRU decoder, dot-product attention ----------
D, H = 24, 48                     # embedding size, hidden size
def init_params(seed=7):
    rw = Mulberry32(seed)
    def W(n_in, n_out, scale):
        return np.array([[gauss(rw) * scale for _ in range(n_out)] for _ in range(n_in)])
    P = {"Es": W(len(src_vocab), D, 0.1), "Et": W(len(tgt_vocab), D, 0.1)}
    for name in ("e", "d"):       # GRU weights, gates stacked as [z | r | n]
        P["Wx" + name] = W(D, 3 * H, 1 / math.sqrt(D)); P["Wh" + name] = W(H, 3 * H, 1 / math.sqrt(H)); P["b" + name] = np.zeros(3 * H)
    P["Ws"] = W(H, H, 1 / math.sqrt(H)); P["bs"] = np.zeros(H)           # last encoder state -> first decoder state
    P["Wa"] = W(H, H, 1 / math.sqrt(H))                                   # attention score = s . (Wa h)
    P["Wc"] = W(2 * H, H, 1 / math.sqrt(2 * H)); P["bc"] = np.zeros(H)   # [s; context] -> attentional state
    P["Wo"] = W(H, len(tgt_vocab), 1 / math.sqrt(H)); P["bo"] = np.zeros(len(tgt_vocab))
    return P
P = init_params()
print(f"parameters: {sum(v.size for v in P.values())}")

sig = lambda x: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-x))
def gru_step(x, h, Wx, Wh, b):
    g = x @ Wx + b
    zr = sig(g[:, :2 * H] + h @ Wh[:, :2 * H]); z, rg = zr[:, :H], zr[:, H:]
    rh = rg * h
    n = np.tanh(g[:, 2 * H:] + rh @ Wh[:, 2 * H:])
    return (1 - z) * n + z * h, (x, h, z, rg, rh, n)
def gru_back(dh, cache, Wx, Wh, grads, kx, kh, kb):
    x, h, z, rg, rh, n = cache
    dn = dh * (1 - z); dz = dh * (h - n); dh_prev = dh * z
    dpn = dn * (1 - n * n)
    drh = dpn @ Wh[:, 2 * H:].T
    grads[kh][:, 2 * H:] += rh.T @ dpn
    dr = drh * h; dh_prev = dh_prev + drh * rg
    dg = np.concatenate([dz * z * (1 - z), dr * rg * (1 - rg), dpn], axis=1)
    grads[kx] += x.T @ dg; grads[kb] += dg.sum(0)
    grads[kh][:, :2 * H] += h.T @ dg[:, :2 * H]
    dh_prev = dh_prev + dg[:, :2 * H] @ Wh[:, :2 * H].T
    return dg @ Wx.T, dh_prev

def encode(X, mask):
    """X (B,S) source ids, mask (B,S) 1 where real -> states (B,S,H), caches."""
    B, S = X.shape
    h = np.zeros((B, H)); Hs, caches = [], []
    for t in range(S):
        h_new, c = gru_step(P["Es"][X[:, t]], h, P["Wxe"], P["Whe"], P["be"])
        m = mask[:, t:t + 1]
        h = m * h_new + (1 - m) * h           # padded positions keep the previous state
        Hs.append(h); caches.append(c)
    return np.stack(Hs, 1), caches

def attend(s, Hs, Ha, mask):
    """Cross-attention from decoder state s to the encoder states, then the output distribution."""
    sc = np.einsum("bh,bsh->bs", s, Ha) - 1e9 * (1 - mask)
    a = np.exp(sc - sc.max(1, keepdims=True)); a /= a.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    ctx = np.einsum("bs,bsh->bh", a, Hs)
    cat = np.concatenate([s, ctx], 1)
    o = np.tanh(cat @ P["Wc"] + P["bc"])
    logits = o @ P["Wo"] + P["bo"]
    pr = np.exp(logits - logits.max(1, keepdims=True)); pr /= pr.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    return a, ctx, cat, o, pr

def forward_backward(X, mask, Y):
    """Teacher-forced cross-entropy on a batch, averaged over target tokens, and all gradients."""
    B, S = X.shape; T = Y.shape[1] - 1
    Hs, ecaches = encode(X, mask)
    hlast = Hs[:, -1]
    s = np.tanh(hlast @ P["Ws"] + P["bs"]); s0 = s
    Ha = Hs @ P["Wa"]
    grads = {k: np.zeros_like(v) for k, v in P.items()}
    tmask = (Y[:, 1:] != 0).astype(float); ntok = tmask.sum()
    loss = 0.0; steps = []
    for t in range(T):
        s, c = gru_step(P["Et"][Y[:, t]], s, P["Wxd"], P["Whd"], P["bd"])
        a, ctx, cat, o, pr = attend(s, Hs, Ha, mask)
        y = Y[:, t + 1]
        loss -= (np.log(pr[np.arange(B), y] + 1e-12) * tmask[:, t]).sum()
        steps.append((c, s, a, cat, o, pr, y))
    ds = np.zeros((B, H)); dHs = np.zeros_like(Hs); dHa = np.zeros_like(Ha)
    for t in reversed(range(T)):
        c, s, a, cat, o, pr, y = steps[t]
        dlog = pr.copy(); dlog[np.arange(B), y] -= 1; dlog *= tmask[:, t:t + 1] / ntok
        grads["Wo"] += o.T @ dlog; grads["bo"] += dlog.sum(0)
        do = (dlog @ P["Wo"].T) * (1 - o * o)
        grads["Wc"] += cat.T @ do; grads["bc"] += do.sum(0)
        dcat = do @ P["Wc"].T
        ds_t = dcat[:, :H] + ds; dctx = dcat[:, H:]
        da = np.einsum("bh,bsh->bs", dctx, Hs); dHs += a[:, :, None] * dctx[:, None, :]
        dsc = a * (da - (da * a).sum(1, keepdims=True))
        ds_t = ds_t + np.einsum("bs,bsh->bh", dsc, Ha); dHa += dsc[:, :, None] * s[:, None, :]
        dx, ds = gru_back(ds_t, c, P["Wxd"], P["Whd"], grads, "Wxd", "Whd", "bd")
        np.add.at(grads["Et"], Y[:, t], dx)
    dHs += dHa @ P["Wa"].T; grads["Wa"] += np.einsum("bsh,bsk->hk", Hs, dHa)
    dpre = ds * (1 - s0 * s0)
    grads["Ws"] += hlast.T @ dpre; grads["bs"] += dpre.sum(0)
    dHs[:, -1] += dpre @ P["Ws"].T
    dh = np.zeros((B, H))
    for t in reversed(range(S)):
        dh = dh + dHs[:, t]
        m = mask[:, t:t + 1]
        dx, dh_prev = gru_back(dh * m, ecaches[t], P["Wxe"], P["Whe"], grads, "Wxe", "Whe", "be")
        np.add.at(grads["Es"], X[:, t], dx)
        dh = dh_prev + dh * (1 - m)
    return loss / ntok, grads

# ---------- 4. training with Adam, teacher forcing, gradient clipping ----------
def batchify(ps):
    xs = [enc_src(p[0]) for p in ps]; ys = [enc_tgt(p[1]) for p in ps]
    S, T = max(map(len, xs)), max(map(len, ys))
    X = np.zeros((len(ps), S), int); Y = np.zeros((len(ps), T), int); mask = np.zeros((len(ps), S))
    for i, (x, y) in enumerate(zip(xs, ys)):
        X[i, :len(x)] = x; mask[i, :len(x)] = 1; Y[i, :len(y)] = y
    return X, mask, Y

def train_model(data, steps=900, B=32, lr=0.003, verbose=False):
    """Adam on the teacher-forced cross-entropy; gradient norm clipped at 5. Rebuilds the global P."""
    global P
    P = init_params()
    mom = {k: np.zeros_like(v) for k, v in P.items()}; vel = {k: np.zeros_like(v) for k, v in P.items()}
    rt = Mulberry32(11); order = []; t0 = time.time(); losses = []
    for step in range(1, steps + 1):
        if len(order) < B:                           # a fresh shuffled epoch
            idx = list(range(len(data)))
            for i in range(len(idx) - 1, 0, -1):
                j = int(rt() * (i + 1)); idx[i], idx[j] = idx[j], idx[i]
            order += idx
        batch, order = order[:B], order[B:]
        loss, g = forward_backward(*batchify([data[i] for i in batch]))
        gn = math.sqrt(sum((v * v).sum() for v in g.values()))
        if gn > 5: g = {k: v * (5 / gn) for k, v in g.items()}
        for k in P:
            mom[k] = 0.9 * mom[k] + 0.1 * g[k]; vel[k] = 0.999 * vel[k] + 0.001 * g[k] * g[k]
            P[k] -= lr * (mom[k] / (1 - 0.9 ** step)) / (np.sqrt(vel[k] / (1 - 0.999 ** step)) + 1e-8)
        losses.append(loss)
        if verbose and (step % 100 == 0 or step == 1):
            print(f"  step {step:4d}  loss {np.mean(losses[-100:]):.3f}  ({time.time() - t0:.0f} s)")
    return losses

# ---------- 5. decoding: greedy (beam = 1) and beam search ----------
def translate(src, beam=1, max_len=16, length_norm=True):
    X, mask, _ = batchify([(src, "")]); X = X[:1]; mask = mask[:1]
    Hs, _ = encode(X, mask); Ha = Hs @ P["Wa"]
    s0 = np.tanh(Hs[:, -1] @ P["Ws"] + P["bs"])
    hyps = [(0.0, [1], s0, [])]; done = []
    for _ in range(max_len):
        cands = []
        for lp, ys, s, atts in hyps:
            s_new, _ = gru_step(P["Et"][[ys[-1]]], s, P["Wxd"], P["Whd"], P["bd"])
            a, _, _, _, pr = attend(s_new, Hs, Ha, mask)
            for w in np.argsort(-pr[0])[:beam]:
                cands.append((lp + math.log(pr[0, w] + 1e-12), ys + [int(w)], s_new, atts + [a[0]]))
        cands.sort(key=lambda c: -c[0]); hyps = []
        for c in cands[:beam]:
            (done if c[1][-1] == 2 else hyps).append(c)
        if not hyps: break
    done += hyps
    score = (lambda c: c[0] / (len(c[1]) - 1)) if length_norm else (lambda c: c[0])
    best = max(done, key=score)
    words = [tgt_vocab[i] for i in best[1][1:] if i != 2]
    return " ".join(words), np.array(best[3]), best[0]

# ---------- 6. corpus BLEU (Papineni et al. 2002), our own implementation ----------
def ngrams(ws, n): return Counter(tuple(ws[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(ws) - n + 1))
def corpus_bleu(hyps, refs, N=4):
    match = [0] * N; total = [0] * N; hlen = rlen = 0
    for h, r in zip(hyps, refs):
        h, r = h.split(), r.split(); hlen += len(h); rlen += len(r)
        for n in range(1, N + 1):
            hc, rc = ngrams(h, n), ngrams(r, n)
            match[n - 1] += sum(min(c, rc[g]) for g, c in hc.items()); total[n - 1] += max(len(h) - n + 1, 0)
    precs = [m / t if t else 0 for m, t in zip(match, total)]
    if min(precs) == 0: return 0.0, precs, 1.0
    bp = 1.0 if hlen > rlen else math.exp(1 - rlen / hlen)
    return 100 * bp * math.exp(sum(math.log(p) for p in precs) / N), precs, bp

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Translation is hard because languages diverge: in word order (SOV, SVO, VSO and the rest), in how much a word carries (morphology), in how they cut up meaning (lexical gaps, idioms) and in what they leave unsaid (referential density). A translator must read the whole source before it writes.
  • The encoder-decoder does exactly that: an encoder turns the source into one vector per token, and a decoder, a conditional language model, writes the target token by token while cross-attention (queries from the decoder, keys and values from the encoder) picks out the source tokens that matter now. The attention weights are a learned alignment.
  • Training uses parallel sentence pairs, teacher forcing and per-token cross-entropy; the price is exposure bias, since the model never sees its own mistakes until test time. Decoding is a search: greedy commits early, beam search keeps the k best, and length normalisation stops short outputs from winning by default.
  • Parallel data is made, not found: sentence alignment (Gale–Church on lengths, then embeddings) turns translated documents into pairs, back-translation turns monolingual target text into synthetic pairs, and a joint subword vocabulary lets both languages share one token table.
  • One model can serve many languages and even translate pairs it never saw, through a shared representation space, but the data gap between a few rich languages and thousands of poor ones is the central inequity of the field; and defaults learned from skewed data are amplified by arg-max decoding, as pronoun gender shows.

Check your understanding

Why can a left-to-right model that writes the first target word as soon as it reads the first source word never translate well from Velish or Japanese into English?

English puts the verb second, but an SOV language puts it last. The second English word depends on the last source word, so the model must have read the whole source before writing. The encoder-decoder guarantees this by encoding everything first; simultaneous-translation systems that must start early pay for it with explicit waiting policies and lower quality.

In cross-attention, which network supplies the queries and which supplies the keys and values? What would go wrong if the decoder also supplied the keys?

Queries come from the decoder’s current state; keys and values come from the encoder outputs. If the decoder supplied the keys as well, the decoder would be attending to itself (that is its masked self-attention block) and the source would be unreachable: the translation would be a fluent sentence unrelated to the input.

A model scores 96% token accuracy under teacher forcing. Roughly what fraction of 20-token sentences will it decode exactly right, and why is the real figure usually a little better than that estimate?

If each token were independent, 0.9620 ≈ 44%. The real figure is often better because beam search can recover from a single low-probability token, and because errors are not independent: easy sentences are easy throughout. It is also sometimes worse, because after an error the model is off its training distribution and its accuracy drops below 96%.

Beam search with k = 2 produced complete hypotheses with log-probabilities −1.5 (two tokens) and −1.9 (four tokens). Which wins with and without length normalisation (α = 1)?

Without normalisation −1.5 wins: it is the larger number. With normalisation the scores are −1.5/2 = −0.75 and −1.9/4 = −0.475, so the four-token hypothesis wins. Normalisation compares average confidence per token instead of total probability, which systematically favours short outputs.

Why does the Gale–Church aligner prefer to merge an untranslated sentence into a 2–1 bead rather than mark it as a 1–0 deletion?

A 1–0 bead pays the rare prior (0.0099, about 4.6 nats) and also the full length of the sentence as a discrepancy, since it is matched against zero characters. A 2–1 bead pays a milder prior (0.089) and a discrepancy that is only the difference between the combined length and the translation’s length. Unless the variance parameter is small, the merge is the cheaper story, and the aligner has no lexical evidence to tell it otherwise.

In back-translation, why is it the target-side monolingual text that is used, and what is the role of the reverse model’s quality?

The forward model’s decoder must produce fluent target text, and fluency is learned from the target side of the training pairs; synthetic pairs with human target sentences give it exactly that. The source side only needs to be read, so a mediocre reverse model is tolerable (its errors make the synthetic sources noisier, which can even help robustness), though a better reverse model does give better results.

A multilingual model was trained on English↔German and English↔Czech. It is asked to translate German into Czech. Name the phenomenon, say what makes it possible, and name its most common failure.

Zero-shot translation. It is possible when the encoder maps sentences of the same meaning to nearby representations regardless of language and the target-language token steers the decoder. Its common failure is off-target output: the decoder slips into a language it has seen more often as a target, typically English.

The training corpus pairs “doctor” with “he” 60% of the time. What does a well-calibrated model believe, and what does a beam-search decoder output? Why is switching to sampling not a satisfactory fix?

The model believes P(he | doctor) ≈ 0.6. Beam search outputs “he” essentially always, because the arg max of a 60/40 split is 100/0: decoding amplifies the skew. Sampling restores 60/40 in aggregate but still assigns a gender by stereotype for every individual sentence and does so inconsistently across sentences; the real fixes act on the data, add explicit gender information, or offer both translations.
