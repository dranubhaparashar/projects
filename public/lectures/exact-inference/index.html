Exact Inference

The same answer can cost a thousand steps or a billion. The order of the sums decides.

A Bayesian network stores a joint distribution in small pieces. To answer a question you must add up the hidden variables, and the ALARM network in the figure has 37 of them: written out, its joint table would have 1.7 × 1016 rows. Variable elimination never writes that table. It removes one variable at a time, and each removal creates one new table whose size depends on the variable’s neighbours at that moment. Click any node to eliminate it, or play an order, and watch the tables grow or stay small.

The question

Asking a network a question

Chapter 6.1 built Bayesian networks and answered questions by adding up all eight worlds of a three-variable model. This chapter is about doing that addition well. First, some names. Every question splits the variables into three groups. The query variable X is the one we want a distribution for. The evidence variables E have been observed, with values e. Everything else is hidden, Y: unobserved, and not asked about.

The running example is a classic from Russell and Norvig. A house has a burglar alarm. A burglary sets it off, and so, sometimes, does a small earthquake. Two neighbours, John and Mary, have promised to phone when they hear it. John sometimes mistakes the phone ringing for the alarm; Mary plays loud music and sometimes misses it. The five yes/no variables are Burglary (B), Earthquake (E), Alarm (A), JohnCalls (J) and MaryCalls (M). The numbers are the book’s: P(B) = 0.001, P(E) = 0.002, the alarm rings with probability 0.95, 0.94, 0.29 or 0.001 depending on B and E, John calls with probability 0.90 if it rings and 0.05 if not, Mary with 0.70 and 0.01.

\[ P(X \mid \mathbf{e}) \;=\; \alpha\, P(X, \mathbf{e}) \;=\; \alpha \sum_{\mathbf{y}} P(X, \mathbf{e}, \mathbf{y}) \]
Read it as: to get the distribution of X given what we saw, add up the joint probability over every combination y of the hidden variables, once for each value of X. That gives P(X, e), a list of numbers that does not yet sum to 1. The constant α = 1 / P(e) rescales it so that it does. In a Bayesian network each joint probability is a product of CPT entries, so the whole question is “sums of products”.
Say it“P of X given e is alpha times the sum, over all values of the hidden variables, of the joint probability of X, e and y.”

Method 1

Inference by enumeration

Take the most famous query: both neighbours called, so what is the chance of a burglary? The hidden variables are E and A. Substituting the network’s factorisation into the sum gives four terms for each value of B, each a product of five CPT entries:

\[ \begin{aligned} &P(B \mid j, m) \\ &\;= \alpha \sum_{e} \sum_{a} P(B)\, P(e)\, P(a \mid B, e) \\ &\qquad\quad \times P(j \mid a)\, P(m \mid a) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: lower-case j and m mean J = true and M = true. For each value of B, run through the four combinations of e and a, multiply the five numbers that the network supplies for that world, and add. Four terms of four multiplications each: 16 multiplications and 3 additions per value of B.
Say it“Alpha times the sum over e and a of P of B, P of e, P of a given B and e, P of j given a, and P of m given a.”

Most of those factors do not depend on the variable being summed. P(B) does not change with e or a, so it can move outside both sums; P(e) does not change with a, so it can move outside the inner one. This is just the distributive law, x·y + x·z = x·(y + z):

\[ \begin{aligned} &P(B \mid j, m) \\ &\;= \alpha\, P(B) \sum_{e} P(e) \\ &\qquad \times \sum_{a} P(a \mid B, e)\, P(j \mid a)\, P(m \mid a) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: evaluate from the inside out. For each e, the inner sum over a is a small number; multiply it by P(e), add the two e cases, and multiply by P(B). The nested form needs 11 multiplications and 3 additions per value of B instead of 16 and 3.
Say it“Alpha P of B, times the sum over e of P of e, times the sum over a of P of a given B e, P of j given a, P of m given a.”

A computer evaluates the nested sum as a tree, depth first. This is the enumeration algorithm: walk through the variables in a topological order; at a hidden variable, branch on each of its values and add the branches; at an evidence variable, multiply by its CPT entry and carry on. It needs memory only for one path, so its space is linear in the number of variables, but the tree has a leaf for every combination of the hidden variables, so its time is O(2n) for n yes/no variables. Step through the tree, then switch John or Mary to unknown and see the tree double. Purple nodes repeat a calculation already done elsewhere in the tree.

The repeated nodes are the weakness. P(j | a) P(m | a) does not depend on e, yet the tree computes it once under e and again under ¬e. In a larger network the same small product can be recomputed thousands of times. The fix is to compute each one once and store it, which is exactly what the next two sections build.

The data structure

Factors: tables that multiply and sum

A factor is a table with one number for every combination of values of some set of variables, its scope. A CPT is a factor: P(A | B, E) is a table over A, B and E with 8 entries. Evidence shrinks a factor. Once we know J = true, P(j | A) is a table over A alone, with two entries: 0.90 and 0.05. A factor does not have to be a probability distribution; it is just numbers indexed by values.

Variable elimination needs only two operations on factors. The pointwise product of two factors is a factor over the union of their scopes: each entry is the product of the two entries that agree on the shared variables. Summing out a variable adds together the entries that differ only in that variable, and removes it from the scope.

\[ f(X, Y, Z) = f_1(X, Y) \times f_2(Y, Z) \]
Read it as: for every combination x, y, z, look up f1(x, y) and f2(y, z) and multiply them. With yes/no variables, two 4-entry factors make an 8-entry one. The product’s size is exponential in the number of variables in the union, which is the cost that the rest of the chapter is about.
Say it“f of X Y Z is f-one of X Y times f-two of Y Z.”
\[ g(Y, Z) = \sum_{x} f(x, Y, Z) \]
Read it as: for each y and z, add the entries for every value of X. The 8-entry factor becomes a 4-entry one. If a factor does not mention X, it passes through the sum untouched: ∑x f1(Y) f2(x, Y) = f1(Y) ∑x f2(x, Y). That is the distributive law again, and it is why elimination only multiplies the factors that contain the variable being removed.
Say it“g of Y Z is the sum over x of f of x, Y, Z.”

Method 2

Variable elimination, step by step

Variable elimination evaluates the nested sum from the inside out and stores every intermediate result as a factor, so nothing is computed twice. Start with one factor per CPT, with the evidence values plugged in. Then, for each hidden variable in some order: take every factor that mentions it, multiply them together, sum the variable out, and put the new, smaller-scope factor back in the pile. When no hidden variables are left, multiply what remains and normalise.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{for each hidden } Z\text{:} \\ &\quad g = \sum_{z}\; \prod_{f \,\ni\, Z} f \\ &\quad \mathcal{F} \leftarrow \big(\mathcal{F} \setminus \{f \ni Z\}\big) \cup \{g\} \\ &P(X \mid \mathbf{e}) = \alpha \prod_{f \in \mathcal{F}} f(X) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ℱ is the pile of factors, and f ∋ Z means “the factors whose scope contains Z”. Multiply just those, sum Z out to get the new factor g, and swap them for g. Factors that do not mention Z are left alone, which is the distributive law at work. At the end every factor mentions only X, and the product of them is P(X, e).
Say it“For each hidden Z: g is the sum over z of the product of the factors that contain Z; replace them by g. Then P of X given e is alpha times the product of what is left.”

Irrelevant variables

Ask for P(J | B = true) and look at what happens to M. Its only factor is P(m | A). Summing it out gives a factor that is 1 for every value of A, because each row of a CPT sums to 1. M contributes nothing and can be dropped before we start. The general rule: every variable that is not an ancestor of the query or of an evidence variable is irrelevant and can be removed. Repeating the argument from the leaves upwards removes whole branches of a large network. Tick “Drop irrelevant variables first” above to see it.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\sum_{m} P(m \mid A) = 1 \\ &\quad\Longrightarrow\; \sum_{m} f(\ldots)\, P(m \mid A) = f(\ldots) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a leaf that is neither the query nor observed sums to a factor of ones, and multiplying by ones changes nothing. Once that leaf is gone its parents may have become leaves, and the same argument applies to them, unless they are ancestors of the query or the evidence.
Say it“Summing a CPT over its own variable gives one, so an unobserved leaf can be deleted.”
Complexity

Elimination order and the shape of the graph

Every elimination order gives the same answer, because addition can be done in any order. The cost is another matter. When Z is eliminated, the factors that mention it are exactly the ones that touch Z’s neighbours in the network’s moral graph (the network with arrows made undirected and the parents of each node joined). The product therefore has scope Z plus all its current neighbours, and the new factor g links those neighbours to each other. In the graph this adds fill-in edges: eliminating Z turns its neighbourhood into a clique.

The largest scope an order ever creates, minus one, is the induced width of that order. The smallest induced width over all orders is the graph’s treewidth. Variable elimination’s time and space are exponential in it, and only linear in the number of variables.

\[ \text{cost} \;=\; \sum_{\text{steps } t} d^{\,|\mathrm{scope}_t|} \;\le\; n\, d^{\,w+1} \]
Read it as: each step builds one product table, with d|scope| entries when every variable has d values. Over n steps the total is at most n times the largest one, and the largest has w + 1 variables when the order’s induced width is w. A chain or a tree has w = 1; a k × k grid has treewidth k, so its best order still builds tables of 2k+1 entries.
Say it“The cost is the sum of d to the scope size over the steps, at most n times d to the w plus one.”

Polytrees are easy

A network is singly connected, or a polytree, if there is at most one undirected path between any two nodes. The burglary network is one. Its moral graph is a tree of family cliques, so eliminating from the outside in, starting with a node that has only one neighbour in the polytree, never creates fill-in. Every table VE builds is then no bigger than the largest CPT, and the total time is linear in the total size of the CPTs.

Finding the best order is itself hard

Computing the treewidth is NP-hard, so real systems use greedy rules. Min-degree eliminates the node with the fewest current neighbours. Min-fill eliminates the node whose removal adds the fewest fill-in edges. Min-weight eliminates the node whose table would be smallest. They are fast and usually good, but nothing guarantees they find the best order.

Complexity

Why no order can always save you

Good orders help, but some networks have no good order at all, and that is not a gap in our cleverness. Exact inference in general Bayesian networks is NP-hard. The proof turns any logic puzzle into a network. Take a 3-SAT formula: n yes/no variables and m clauses, each clause an OR of three literals such as (x1 ∨ ¬x3 ∨ x4). Build a root node for each variable with probability ½ of being true, a node for each clause that is deterministically the OR of its three literals, and a chain of AND nodes that ends in S, true exactly when every clause is.

\[ P(S = \text{true}) \;=\; \frac{\#\,\text{solutions of } \varphi}{2^{\,n}} \]
Read it as: each of the 2n assignments of the roots has probability 1/2n, and S is true in exactly the satisfying ones. So P(S = true) > 0 if and only if the formula φ can be satisfied. A fast exact inference algorithm would decide 3-SAT, which is NP-complete; and computing the number exactly would count solutions, which is #P-hard, harder still.
Say it“The probability that S is true equals the number of satisfying assignments over two to the n.”

This is why exact inference is used where the structure allows it, as in ALARM and in the chains and trees of hidden Markov models, and why so much of the rest of this part of the book is about approximation. The next chapter shows a second exact method, the sum-product algorithm, which reuses work across many queries at once; on networks with loops, an exact version must first group variables into clusters (the junction tree), and its cost is again exponential in the size of the largest cluster.

The landscape

Inference in deep models: exact, approximate, amortised

Deep generative models have the same problem in a sharper form. A model with visible units v and hidden units h is trained and used through questions like p(h | v), the posterior over the hidden causes, and log p(v), the likelihood. Both need sums over h. With k binary hidden units there are 2k terms, and deep models have hundreds or thousands of hidden units arranged in layers that are densely connected. The treewidth of such a graph is about the width of a layer, so variable elimination is out of the question.

What makes inference hard is not the number of hidden units by itself but their interactions. If the hidden units are independent given v, the posterior factorises and is cheap: the restricted Boltzmann machine is designed so, with no edges between hidden units, so p(h | v) is a product of k separate sigmoids. As soon as hidden units interact, directly or because they compete to explain the same visible unit (explaining away, from chapter 6.1), the posterior couples them and the exact sum is exponential.

The standard way out is to change the question. Pick a simple family of distributions q(h), for example fully factorised ones, and find the member closest to the true posterior. The identity below says how close we got.

\[ \begin{aligned} \log p(v) &= \mathcal{L}(q) + \mathrm{KL}\big(q(h) \,\Vert\, p(h \mid v)\big) \\ \mathcal{L}(q) &= \mathbb{E}_{h \sim q}\big[\log p(v, h)\big] + H(q) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for any q, the log-likelihood splits into the evidence lower bound ℒ(q) and a KL divergence, which is never negative. ℒ(q) needs only expectations under the simple q, never the exact sum. Raising ℒ therefore does two jobs at once: it tightens a lower bound on log p(v) and pulls q towards the true posterior. When q is exactly the posterior the KL term is 0 and the bound is tight.
Say it“Log p of v equals the ELBO plus the KL divergence from q to the true posterior; the ELBO is the expected log joint plus the entropy of q.”

The lab is the smallest case that shows the problem: two binary hidden units after v has been observed, with p(h | v) ∝ exp(b1h1 + b2h2 + w h1h2). The coupling w is the interaction. Up to a constant that we set to zero, log p(v, h) = b1h1 + b2h2 + w h1h2, so log p(v) is the log of the sum of the four exponentials. The page computes the exact posterior by summing the four states, and a factorised mean-field q(h) = q1(h1) q2(h2) by updating each factor in turn: q1 = σ(b1 + w q2), q2 = σ(b2 + w q1), the coordinate ascent rule for ℒ. Set w to 0 and the gap vanishes; make w strongly negative, the explaining-away pattern, and watch the factorised q fail to capture it.

The same computation in Python

The reference script reads the BIF file, runs its own variable elimination with numpy, and checks every posterior against one large numpy.einsum over all 37 CPTs, which chooses its own contraction order. The greedy orders and the seeded random order are implemented the same way as on this page, with ties broken by position in the file.

import re, numpy as np

def read_bif(path):                                   # variables, states, parents, CPT arrays
    txt = open(path).read(); states, order = {}, []
    for m in re.finditer(r"variable\s+(\w+)\s*\{\s*type\s+discrete\s*\[\s*\d+\s*\]\s*\{([^}]*)\}", txt):
        states[m.group(1)] = [s.strip() for s in m.group(2).split(",")]; order.append(m.group(1))
    parents, cpt = {}, {}
    for m in re.finditer(r"probability\s*\(\s*(\w+)\s*(?:\|\s*([^)]*))?\)\s*\{([^}]*)\}", txt):
        v = m.group(1); ps = [p.strip() for p in m.group(2).split(",")] if m.group(2) else []
        T = np.zeros([len(states[p]) for p in ps] + [len(states[v])]); parents[v] = ps
        for line in filter(None, (l.strip() for l in m.group(3).split(";"))):
            if line.startswith("table"): T[...] = np.array(line[5:].split(","), float).reshape(T.shape); continue
            key, vals = re.match(r"\(([^)]*)\)\s*(.*)", line).groups()
            T[tuple(states[p].index(k.strip()) for p, k in zip(ps, key.split(",")))] = np.array(vals.split(","), float)
        cpt[v] = T
    return order, states, parents, cpt

def plan(order, scopes, card):                       # symbolic pass: sizes only
    sc, big, cost = [set(s) for s in scopes], 0, 0
    for x in order:
        u = set().union(*[s for s in sc if x in s]); sc = [s for s in sc if x not in s]
        size = int(np.prod([card[y] for y in u])); big = max(big, size); cost += size
        sc.append(u - {x})
    return big, cost

def eliminate(factors, order):                       # factors: list of (scope list, array)
    for x in order:
        use = [f for f in factors if x in f[0]]; factors = [f for f in factors if x not in f[0]]
        vs = list(dict.fromkeys(v for s, _ in use for v in s)); L = {v: chr(97 + i) for i, v in enumerate(vs)}
        spec = ",".join("".join(L[v] for v in s) for s, _ in use) + "->" + "".join(L[v] for v in vs if v != x)
        factors.append(([v for v in vs if v != x], np.einsum(spec, *[t for _, t in use])))
    return factors

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Every query is P(X | e) = α ∑y P(X, e, y): a sum, over the hidden variables, of products of CPT entries, then normalised.
  • Enumeration evaluates the nested sum as a depth-first tree. Its space is linear, but its time is exponential in the number of variables, and it repeats identical sub-calculations.
  • Variable elimination stores each intermediate result as a factor. Each step multiplies the factors that mention one hidden variable and sums it out. Variables that are not ancestors of the query or the evidence can be dropped first.
  • The cost is set by the largest factor, which is set by the elimination order: exponential in the induced width, linear in the number of variables. Polytrees are linear; min-fill and min-degree are good greedy orders; bad orders can be millions of times worse.
  • Exact inference is NP-hard in general (3-SAT fits in a network) and #P-hard to do exactly. In deep models hidden units interact so densely that approximate inference, variational, sampling or amortised, is the norm.

Check your understanding

Enumeration uses only linear memory. Variable elimination can need far more. Why is VE still preferred?

Because enumeration’s time is exponential in the number of variables, whatever the structure, and it recomputes the same sub-products again and again. VE trades memory for time: it stores each intermediate factor once. On a sparse network such as ALARM its tables stay tiny (the largest has 144 entries with min-fill), so it uses little memory as well as little time. Only when the treewidth is large does VE’s memory become the problem, and then enumeration would be hopeless anyway.

For P(B | j, m), does it matter whether VE eliminates A or E first?

Not for the answer, and hardly for the cost. Eliminating A first multiplies P(A | B, E), P(j | A) and P(m | A) into a table over A, B and E (8 entries) and leaves a factor over B and E. Eliminating E first multiplies P(E) and P(A | B, E) into a table over A, B and E (8 entries) and leaves a factor over A and B. Either way the largest table has 8 entries, because the burglary network is a polytree.

A query asks P(Earthquake | Burglary = true). Which variables are irrelevant?

JohnCalls, MaryCalls and Alarm: none of them is an ancestor of E or of B, so all three are dropped. (Seen step by step, J and M are unobserved leaves, and once they are gone A is one too.) What remains is P(E) and P(B), which do not interact, so the answer is just the prior P(E) = 0.002: knowing about a burglary says nothing about earthquakes unless we also observe the alarm or a call.

What is the induced width of min-fill on a chain of n nodes, and why?

One. Eliminating an end node of a chain creates a table over that node and its single neighbour, and adds no fill-in, so the remaining graph is again a chain. Every table has two variables, so the induced width is 2 − 1 = 1 and the total cost is linear in n. Eliminating a middle node first builds a table over three variables (width 2) and joins its two neighbours with a fill-in edge: the rest is still a chain, but that step cost twice as much. For a chain the end-first order is optimal.

Why does a factorised q(h) = q1(h1) q2(h2) fail on explaining away?

Explaining away makes the posterior favour “one cause or the other, but not both”: mass on (1, 0) and (0, 1), little on (1, 1). A product of two independent marginals cannot put mass on two opposite corners without also putting it on the other two. Mean-field therefore picks one corner, or spreads out, and the KL gap measures what it misses. A richer q, sampling, or exact inference (here only four states) would capture it.
