The Laplace Approximation & Bayesian Logistic Regression

Fit a Gaussian to the peak, then let every probability carry its error bar

Logistic regression from chapter 1.3 hands back one weight vector and one probability. But with a dozen data points, many weight vectors explain the data almost equally well, and a single “73%” hides how unsure the model really is. The Bayesian answer is to keep all of them, weighted by the posterior. The snag: for logistic regression that posterior has no formula. Laplace’s trick is to find its peak, measure how sharply it curves there, and stand a Gaussian in its place. On the right, every thin curve is one weight vector drawn from that Gaussian; the thick curve is their average. Drag any dot along its row: pull the two classes apart and watch the band of plausible curves fan out in the gap.

The idea

A Gaussian at the peak

Many posteriors are easy to write down up to a constant, f(z) = prior × likelihood, yet impossible to normalise, average or sample from in closed form. Near its highest point, though, almost any smooth bump looks like a parabola once you take logs. Pierre-Simon Laplace used this in 1774: expand ln f to second order around the peak z0. The first-order term vanishes because the slope is zero at a peak, and what is left is the log of a Gaussian.

Test it where we know the right answer. A coin with unknown bias θ and a flat prior gives, after h heads and t tails, the posterior f(θ) ∝ θh(1 − θ)t, an exact Beta density. Change the counts, then switch to the log-odds view: the same trick applied to u = ln(θ/(1 − θ)) instead of θ itself.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\ln f(z) \approx \ln f(z_0) - \tfrac12 A\,(z-z_0)^2 \\ &A = -\frac{d^2}{dz^2}\ln f(z)\Big|_{z_0} \\ &q(z) = \sqrt{\tfrac{A}{2\pi}}\;e^{-\frac{A}{2}(z-z_0)^2} \\ &\phantom{q(z)} = \mathcal{N}\big(z \mid z_0,\ A^{-1}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: z0 is the peak, where d ln f / dz = 0. A is how fast the log-density bends downwards there: a sharp peak has a large A. The approximation q is the Gaussian centred on the peak with variance 1/A, so a sharp peak gives a narrow Gaussian. It needs a peak with zero slope inside the range, and it only ever looks at the neighbourhood of that one peak.
Say it“Near the peak, log f is log f at z-nought minus a half A times z minus z-nought squared, where A is minus the second derivative of log f; so q is a normal with mean z-nought and variance one over A.”

The constant

The normaliser for free

Once f is replaced by a Gaussian bump of the same height and width, its area follows at once: height f(z0) times the width factor √(2π/A). That area is the normalising constant Z, and in Bayesian statistics Z is the evidence p(D), the number we need to compare models. In M dimensions the second derivative becomes the Hessian matrix A, and the width factor becomes a determinant.

A famous check: n! = Γ(n + 1) = ∫ zne−z dz. Apply Laplace to the integrand and out comes Stirling’s formula. Slide n and see how close it gets, then try the log scale, where the integrand is far more Gaussian.

\[ Z = \int f(z)\,dz \;\approx\; f(z_0)\,\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{A}} \]
Read it as: the area under the bump is its height at the peak times an effective width. For f(z) = zne−z the peak is at z0 = n with A = 1/n, which gives n! ≈ nne−n√(2πn).
Say it“Z is about f at the peak times the square root of two pi over A.”
\[ \begin{aligned} Z &\approx f(\mathbf{z}_0)\,\frac{(2\pi)^{M/2}}{|\mathbf{A}|^{1/2}} \\ \mathbf{A} &= -\nabla\nabla \ln f(\mathbf{z})\big|_{\mathbf{z}_0} \\ q(\mathbf{z}) &= \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{z}_0,\ \mathbf{A}^{-1}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same recipe in M dimensions. A is the M × M matrix of second derivatives of −ln f at the peak; it must be positive definite, so the peak is a true maximum in every direction. |A| is its determinant, the product of the curvatures along its principal axes; its inverse is the covariance of q.
Say it“Z is about f at the peak times two pi to the M over two, over the square root of the determinant of A.”

Comparing models

Model comparison and the BIC

To compare models by their evidence p(D) = ∫ p(D | θ) p(θ) dθ, apply the normaliser formula with f(θ) = p(D | θ)p(θ). The log evidence splits into how well the best parameters fit, plus an Occam factor: a penalty that grows with how much the data had to squeeze the prior. A model with more parameters fits better but pays more.

Push the approximation one step further and the prior drops out. Suppose the prior is broad and the data points are independent: each one adds its own curvature, so A grows in proportion to N and ln|A| ≈ M ln N plus a constant. Keep only the terms that grow with N and you get Schwarz’s Bayesian information criterion. Below, logistic models with polynomial features of degree 0 to 6 (Legendre polynomials in x/3, which keep every power on a similar scale) are fitted to the same data, each with the prior N(0, α−1) on every weight. Change N and the true shape, and watch which degree each score picks. Then change the prior: the evidence depends on it, BIC does not.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\ln p(\mathcal{D}) \approx \ln p(\mathcal{D}\mid\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{MAP}}) \\ &\qquad + \text{Occam} \\ &\text{Occam} = \ln p(\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{MAP}}) \\ &\qquad + \tfrac{M}{2}\ln 2\pi - \tfrac12\ln|\mathbf{A}| \\[4pt] &\ln p(\mathcal{D}) \approx \text{BIC} \\ &\text{BIC} = \ln p(\mathcal{D}\mid\boldsymbol\theta_{\text{MAP}}) \\ &\qquad - \tfrac12 M\ln N \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first line is the Laplace estimate of the log evidence, with A the Hessian of the negative log posterior at the peak. The Occam factor is negative: it is the log of how much of the prior’s volume survives the data. In the second line, A ≈ N·Ã with Ã fixed, so ln|A| ≈ M ln N + ln|Ã|; drop everything that does not grow with N. BIC needs no prior and no Hessian, but it counts every parameter as fully determined, which fails when parameters are redundant (as in neural networks, where A is far from full rank).
Say it“Log evidence is about the best log-likelihood plus the Occam factor; BIC replaces the Occam factor by minus a half M log N.”

How good is the Laplace evidence? At the starting settings (straight truth, N = 40, α = 0.01) it gives ln p(D) = −22.28 for degree 1; integrating prior × likelihood exactly over both weights (two-dimensional quadrature in SciPy) gives −22.23. Every table entry here also matches a NumPy re-fit of the same points to 10−14.

Bayesian logistic regression

The logistic posterior, and its Gaussian stand-in

Now the real target. Put a Gaussian prior on the weights, p(w) = N(w | m0, S0), and multiply by the logistic likelihood. With a Gaussian likelihood (chapter 1.2d) the posterior would be Gaussian again. With sigmoids it is not: the product of a bell and many S-shaped factors has no name and no normaliser. So find its peak wMAP with Newton’s method (this is the IRLS of chapter 1.3a, with the prior added) and read off the curvature there.

One input x and a bias give two weights, so the whole posterior fits on a plane. Drag the points, or click to add them, and compare the true posterior (shaded, with contours) against the Laplace ellipses. Try the separable preset with a weak prior.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\ln p(\mathbf{w}\mid\mathbf{t}) = \text{const} \\ &\quad -\tfrac12(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{m}_0)^{\!\top}\mathbf{S}_0^{-1}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{m}_0) \\ &\quad + \textstyle\sum_n \big[t_n\ln y_n \\ &\qquad\quad + (1-t_n)\ln(1-y_n)\big] \\[4pt] &\mathbf{S}_N^{-1} = \mathbf{S}_0^{-1} \\ &\qquad + \textstyle\sum_n y_n(1-y_n)\,\boldsymbol\phi_n\boldsymbol\phi_n^{\!\top} \\ &q(\mathbf{w}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\text{MAP}},\,\mathbf{S}_N) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: yn = σ(w⊤φn) is the predicted probability for point n and tn its 0/1 label. The log posterior is the log prior (a bowl) plus the log-likelihood (the negative cross-entropy). SN−1 is minus its second derivative at wMAP: the prior’s precision plus one rank-one piece per point, weighted by y(1 − y). Points the model is sure about (y near 0 or 1) add almost no curvature, so they barely pin the weights down. Here φ = (1, x), m0 = 0 and S0 = α−1I.
Say it“The log posterior is the log prior plus the sum of t log y plus one minus t log one minus y; the posterior precision is the prior precision plus the sum of y times one minus y times phi phi-transpose.”

Predicting

Averaging over the weights

A Bayesian prediction does not pick one w. It asks every plausible w and averages their answers, weighted by the posterior. That looks like an M-dimensional integral, but the sigmoid only sees the weights through one number, the activation a = w⊤φ. Under the Gaussian q(w), a is itself Gaussian, so the integral collapses to one dimension: average the sigmoid over a bell curve on the a-axis.

The data and posterior are the ones you built above. Move the test input x into the data, then far outside it.

\[ \begin{aligned} &p(\mathcal{C}_1\mid\boldsymbol\phi,\mathbf{t}) \\ &\quad \approx \int \sigma(\mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\phi)\,q(\mathbf{w})\,d\mathbf{w} \\ &\quad = \int \sigma(a)\,\mathcal{N}(a\mid\mu_a,\sigma_a^2)\,da \\ &\mu_a = \mathbf{w}_{\text{MAP}}^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\phi \\ &\sigma_a^2 = \boldsymbol\phi^{\!\top}\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol\phi \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the activation at this input has mean μa (what the MAP weights say) and variance σa2 (how much the plausible weights disagree here). The prediction is the average of σ(a) over that bell. Because σ is S-shaped, the average is always pulled towards ½ compared with the plug-in σ(μa), but never across it: the decision boundary μa = 0 stays put.
Say it“The predictive probability is the integral of sigma of a times a normal in a with mean mu-a and variance sigma-a squared; mu-a is w-MAP dot phi and sigma-a squared is phi-transpose S-N phi.”

A closed form

The probit shortcut

The average of a sigmoid over a Gaussian has no closed form, but the average of a probit, the normal CDF Φ from chapter 1.3a, does: blurring a normal CDF with a normal gives another normal CDF, just wider. So swap the sigmoid for the probit that matches it best, average that exactly, and swap back. Matching their slopes at the origin fixes the scale: σ(a) ≈ Φ(λa) with λ2 = π/8. The result is one line: shrink the activation by a factor κ that depends on the uncertainty. Slide μ and σ and read the error of the shortcut.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\sigma(a) \approx \Phi(\lambda a), \qquad \lambda^2 = \pi/8 \\ &\int \Phi(\lambda a)\,\mathcal{N}(a\mid\mu,\sigma^2)\,da \\ &\quad = \Phi\Big(\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{\lambda^{-2}+\sigma^2}}\Big) \\[4pt] &p(\mathcal{C}_1\mid\boldsymbol\phi,\mathbf{t}) \\ &\quad \approx \sigma\big(\kappa(\sigma_a^2)\,\mu_a\big) \\ &\kappa(\sigma^2) = \big(1 + \pi\sigma^2/8\big)^{-1/2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: both σ(a) and Φ(λa) rise from 0 to 1 with slope ¼ at a = 0. Averaging Φ(λa) over the bell just divides μ by a bigger number. Translate back to the sigmoid and the whole Bayesian average becomes σ of a shrunken activation. κ = 1 when σa = 0 (no uncertainty: the plug-in answer) and shrinks towards 0 as σa grows, pulling the probability to ½.
Say it“Sigma of a is about Phi of lambda a with lambda squared pi over eight; the predictive is sigma of kappa times mu-a, where kappa is one over the root of one plus pi sigma squared over eight.”

Playground

Calibrated uncertainty near the boundary

With two inputs the effect becomes a picture. The plug-in model draws parallel contours: 90% is 90% whether you are among the training points or a hundred miles from them. The Bayesian model keeps the same 50% line, but its 10% and 90% contours fan apart wherever the weights are poorly pinned down, so probabilities far from the data and along the boundary relax towards ½. That is what calibrated means here: when the model says 70%, it should be right about 70% of the time, and it should not claim 99% about inputs it has never seen anything like. Drag the points, add new ones, and hover the plot.

\[ \begin{aligned} &p = \sigma\big(\kappa(\sigma_a^2)\,\mu_a\big) \iff \\ &\mathbf{w}_{\text{MAP}}^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\phi = \ln\frac{p}{1-p} \\ &\qquad \times \sqrt{1 + \tfrac{\pi}{8}\,\boldsymbol\phi^{\!\top}\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol\phi} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the contour where the Bayesian model says p sits where the MAP activation equals the plug-in’s logit of p, stretched by the uncertainty factor on the right. At p = ½ the logit is 0 and the stretch has no effect: the boundary is the same line. For p = 0.9 the contour moves further from the boundary wherever φ⊤SNφ is large, which is away from the training data.
Say it“The p contour is where w-MAP dot phi equals the log-odds of p times the root of one plus pi over eight phi-transpose S-N phi.”

Choosing

Laplace among the approximations

Laplace is the quickest way to get a posterior you can use: one optimisation you were going to run anyway, plus one Hessian. Its weaknesses all come from looking at a single point. It cannot see skew, heavy tails or a second peak, and it depends on the parametrisation you choose, as the coin showed. Later chapters develop the alternatives; this table is a map.

Real-world project

Project: credit-default probabilities with error bars

In 2005 a bank in Taiwan recorded 30,000 credit-card clients and whether each failed to pay the following month’s bill; Yeh and Lien (2009) published the data on the UCI repository (CC BY 4.0). A lender wants a probability it can price with, and an honest one: “22% ± 2” for a client like thousands before, and a wider bar for an unusual one. We take a fixed random 9,000 clients: a training pool of 6,000 and a test set of 3,000. Seven features, all standardised with the training pool: the credit limit (log), age, months behind on the last two statements, utilisation (last bill ÷ limit) and the last two payments (log). The model is the one above: prior N(0, α−1) on every weight (the bias gets a prior 100 times wider, because we do not want to shrink the base rate), Newton to the peak, then the Laplace posterior.

Train on fewer clients to see the Bayesian version earn its keep: with lots of data the bars are thin and both models agree; with a few hundred, the plug-in model is over-confident and averaging over the weights repairs part of it.

Every client gets an error bar

The 90% error bar on a client’s probability comes from the bell on the activation: the probabilities σ(μa ± 1.645σa) bracket 90% of the plausible weight vectors’ answers. Below, 150 test clients sorted by their Bayesian probability. Typical clients sit in crowded regions of feature space and get thin bars; clients with unusual combinations, such as a tiny limit with a huge payment, get wide ones.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{from } \sigma(\mu_a - 1.645\,\sigma_a) \\ &\text{to } \ \ \sigma(\mu_a + 1.645\,\sigma_a) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a ~ N(μa, σa2) lies within 1.645 standard deviations of μa 90% of the time, and σ is increasing, so the sigmoid of those two ends brackets 90% of the posterior’s probabilities for this client. The interval is lopsided whenever μa ≠ 0, because the sigmoid bends.
Say it“The ninety-percent bar runs from sigma of mu-a minus 1.645 sigma-a to sigma of mu-a plus 1.645 sigma-a.”

Which features earn their place?

The evidence compares models on the training data alone, with no test set. Three nested feature sets, each fitted to the same N clients: the Laplace log evidence and the BIC both trade the gain in fit against the Occam penalty. Compare their verdicts with the test-set log-loss, which they never saw.

The same project in Python

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from scipy.special import expit, roots_hermitenorm
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

df = pd.read_excel("default of credit card clients.xls", header=1)    # UCI id 350
d = df.iloc[np.random.RandomState(7).permutation(len(df))[:9000]]
F = np.c_[np.log(d.LIMIT_BAL), d.AGE, d.PAY_0.clip(lower=0), d.PAY_2.clip(lower=0),
          (d.BILL_AMT1 / d.LIMIT_BAL).clip(-0.5, 2), np.log1p(d.PAY_AMT1), np.log1p(d.PAY_AMT2)]
t = d["default payment next month"].values
Z = (F - F[:6000].mean(0)) / F[:6000].std(0)          # scale with the 6,000-client training pool
X = np.c_[np.full(len(t), 10.0), Z]                    # a column of 10s: the bias gets a 100x wider prior
Xte, tte = X[6000:], t[6000:]
z, wz = roots_hermitenorm(60); wz = wz / wz.sum()      # Gauss-Hermite nodes for the exact average

def laplace(X, t, alpha=1.0):
    w = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    for _ in range(50):                                # Newton = IRLS on the log posterior
        y = expit(X @ w)
        g = X.T @ (y - t) + alpha * w
        A = X.T @ (X * (y * (1 - y))[:, None]) + alpha * np.eye(len(w))
        w = w - np.linalg.solve(A, g)
    y = expit(X @ w)
    A = X.T @ (X * (y * (1 - y))[:, None]) + alpha * np.eye(len(w))
    loglik = np.sum(t * np.log(y) + (1 - t) * np.log(1 - y))
    M = len(w); _, logdet = np.linalg.slogdet(A)
    evidence = loglik - alpha / 2 * w @ w + M / 2 * np.log(alpha) - logdet / 2
    bic = loglik - M / 2 * np.log(len(t))
    return w, np.linalg.inv(A), loglik, evidence, bic

def scores(p, t):
    ll = -np.mean(t * np.log(p) + (1 - t) * np.log(1 - p))
    bins = np.minimum((p * 10).astype(int), 9)
    ece = sum(abs(p[bins == b].mean() - t[bins == b].mean()) * np.mean(bins == b) for b in range(10) if np.any(bins == b))
    return ll, np.mean((p - t) ** 2), ece

for N in (6000, 200):
    w, SN, ll, ev, bic = laplace(X[:N], t[:N])
    sk = LogisticRegression(C=1.0, fit_intercept=False, tol=1e-12, max_iter=10000).fit(X[:N], t[:N])
    mu, s2 = Xte @ w, np.einsum("ij,jk,ik->i", Xte, SN, Xte)
    p_map, p_probit = expit(mu), expit(mu / np.sqrt(1 + np.pi * s2 / 8))
    p_exact = expit(mu[:, None] + np.sqrt(s2)[:, None] * z) @ wz
    lo, hi = expit(mu - 1.645 * np.sqrt(s2)), expit(mu + 1.645 * np.sqrt(s2))
    print(f"N = {N}: bias = {10 * w[0]:.4f}, weights = {np.round(w[1:], 4)}")
    print(f"  max |w - sklearn| = {np.abs(w - sk.coef_[0]).max():.1e},  log evidence = {ev:.3f},  BIC = {bic:.3f}")
    for name, p in (("plug-in MAP", p_map), ("probit-moderated", p_probit), ("exact average", p_exact)):
        print(f"  {name:17s} log-loss {scores(p, tte)[0]:.4f}  Brier {scores(p, tte)[1]:.4f}  ECE {scores(p, tte)[2]:.4f}")
    print(f"  max |probit - exact| = {np.abs(p_probit - p_exact).max():.4f},  median 90% bar width = {np.median(hi - lo):.4f}")

for name, cols in (("limit + age", [0, 1, 2]), ("+ months late", [0, 1, 2, 3, 4]), ("all seven", list(range(8)))):
    w, SN, ll, ev, bic = laplace(X[:6000][:, cols], t[:6000])
    print(f"{name:14s} M = {len(cols)}  log-lik = {ll:9.2f}  log evidence = {ev:9.2f}  BIC = {bic:9.2f}")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • The Laplace approximation replaces a posterior by the Gaussian centred on its peak with covariance A−1, where A is minus the Hessian of the log posterior there.
  • The same Gaussian gives the evidence: Z ≈ f(z0)(2π)M/2/|A|1/2, the best fit times an Occam factor. Keep only what grows with N and you get the BIC, ln p(D | θ) − ½M ln N.
  • Bayesian logistic regression: find wMAP by Newton (IRLS plus the prior), then SN−1 = S0−1 + Σ y(1 − y)φφ⊤.
  • Predictions average the sigmoid over a ~ N(μa, σa2); the probit trick turns that into σ(κμa) with κ = (1 + πσa2/8)−1/2.
  • Moderation never moves the 50% boundary, but it softens over-confident probabilities where the data is thin, and it gives every prediction an error bar: on credit default, it matters most when training data is scarce.

Check your understanding

Why does the first-order term vanish in the Laplace expansion, and what goes wrong if the peak is on the edge of the range?

At an interior maximum the gradient of ln f is zero, so only the constant and quadratic terms remain. If the maximum sits on a boundary (a coin with no tails, in θ), the slope there is not zero and the quadratic picture is simply wrong; changing variable (to the log-odds) moves the peak inside.

A logistic model is fitted to perfectly separable data with a very weak prior. What does the posterior look like, and how does Laplace do?

The likelihood keeps rising as the weights grow along the separating direction, so the posterior is a long, lopsided ridge held back only by the prior. Its peak sits far out and the curvature there is small and misleading: the Laplace ellipse misses most of the mass.

Why does the BIC penalty grow like ln N per parameter?

Each data point adds curvature to the log posterior, so the Hessian grows in proportion to N and its determinant like NM. The Occam factor contains −½ ln|A|, which is −½M ln N plus terms that do not grow with N.

Can averaging over the posterior change which class a point is assigned to, with a 0.5 threshold?

No. σ(κμa) is above ½ exactly when μa is positive, because κ > 0. Moderation changes how sure the model is, not which side it picks; it does change decisions with other thresholds or costs.

Why is λ2 = π/8 the right scale for the probit stand-in?

It makes the two curves have the same slope at the origin: σ′(0) = ¼ and the slope of Φ(λa) at 0 is λ/√(2π). Setting them equal gives λ = √(2π)/4, so λ2 = π/8.
