The Sorting Machine

Teaching a computer to tell salmon from sea bass

A fish factory gets thousands of fish a day on one conveyor belt. A camera measures each one, and a machine has to push it into the right bin. Building that machine is the whole of discriminant analysis: this page builds it one click at a time, from a single cut to a runnable Python project.

Step 01 · you are the machine

Where would you cut?

Start with one measurement: length. Each dot below is a fish from this morning's catch of 40, placed by its length. Salmon tend to be shorter, sea bass longer, but the two groups overlap. The simplest machine is a single cut: everything left of it goes in the salmon bin, everything right goes in the sea bass bin.

Click on the plot to place your cut. Fish with a red ring are the ones your rule gets wrong.

Say thisCall one student up. Ask them to click where they'd separate the two colours, and read out the mistakes counter. Then press "Show the best cut". Point out that even the best cut makes mistakes, because some salmon are long and some sea bass are short. The groups overlap, and a machine has to live with that.
0mistakes out of 40

No cut gets zero mistakes on an overlapping catch. The error you cannot remove by moving the cut is called the Bayes error; every method on this page is about getting as close to it as possible.

Step 02 · Bayes' rule

Bet on the likelier fish

A cut is a rule, but it is not a reason. Here is the reason. Smooth each group's dots into a bell-shaped hill: the normal distribution of lengths for that fish, with a mean \(\mu\) (where the hill sits) and a standard deviation \(\sigma\) (how wide it spreads). Then scale each hill by how common that fish is, its prior \(\pi\). For a new fish of length \(x\), compare the two scaled hills at \(x\) and pick the taller one.

That is Bayes' rule, and it is all LDA and QDA ever do. The border between the bins is simply where the two scaled hills cross.

Bayes' rule for classification
\[ P(\text{salmon}\mid x) \;=\; \frac{\pi_{\text{s}}\, f_{\text{s}}(x)}{\pi_{\text{s}}\, f_{\text{s}}(x) + \pi_{\text{b}}\, f_{\text{b}}(x)} \]
Read it as: the chance it is salmon = (how common salmon are) × (how typical this length is for a salmon), divided by the same thing summed over both fish so the two chances add to one. \(f_{\text{s}}\) is the salmon hill, a normal density with its own \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\).
Say thisDrag the slider slowly from left to right and watch the chance bars flip from salmon to sea bass. Where they are 50-50 is the border. Now press "80% salmon". The salmon hill gets taller, so the border moves right: when most fish are salmon anyway, a fish needs to be longer before we call it a sea bass. Finally press "Sea bass vary more". Now there are two borders. Remember this one, because it is the reason QDA exists.

Step 03 · two measurements

Clouds and their shapes

Now the camera also measures brightness. Each fish becomes a point on a plane, and each group becomes a cloud. A normal cloud in two dimensions is described by two things: its centre \(\mu\), a pair of means, and its shape \(\Sigma\), the covariance matrix. The matrix holds three numbers: how much length varies, how much brightness varies, and whether they move together (the tilt).

Play with the sliders. Watch the off-diagonal number: it is zero when the cloud is upright and grows as you tilt it, because long fish then tend to be bright fish too.

Say thisMake the cloud wide, then tall, then tilt it. Point at the matrix each time: the top-left number is the spread in length, the bottom-right is the spread in brightness, and the two matching off-diagonal numbers say whether the two go together. Everything LDA and QDA argue about is whether two clouds share this matrix.

The normal cloud in two dimensions

Read it as: height falls off with the Mahalanobis distance from the centre, a distance that counts steps in units of the cloud's own spread in that direction. \(|\Sigma|\) is the determinant: a measure of the cloud's area.
Step 04 · Linear Discriminant Analysis

Same shape, straight border

Suppose both clouds have the same shape \(\Sigma\) and differ only in where they sit. Apply Bayes' rule to the two clouds and something pleasant happens: the border between the bins is always a straight line. This is Linear Discriminant Analysis.

Drag the two big dots to move the clouds. The border moves and turns but never bends. Then change the tilt: both clouds tilt together, and the line leans to follow them. It is not the perpendicular bisector of the two centres; it respects the shape.

Say thisDrag the orange dot around. The border always stays a straight line; it just moves and turns. Now turn the tilt dial: both clouds tilt together and the border leans to match them. The ringed fish are the mistakes. That is LDA: when both groups have the same shape, the best border is a straight line.

35°
LDA score for class k
\[ \delta_k(x) = x^\top \Sigma^{-1}\mu_k \;-\; \tfrac12\,\mu_k^\top \Sigma^{-1}\mu_k \;+\; \log \pi_k \]
Read it as: a score that is linear in \(x\). Compute it for salmon and for sea bass and pick the bigger. The term \(x^\top\Sigma^{-1}x\) appears in both scores, because both share \(\Sigma\), so it cancels when you compare them. That cancellation is why the border is straight.
Step 05 · Quadratic Discriminant Analysis

Own shapes, curved border

Real groups rarely share a shape. Salmon might be a tight cloud and sea bass a wide one. Give each cloud its own \(\Sigma_k\), apply Bayes' rule again, and the \(x^\top\Sigma_k^{-1}x\) terms no longer cancel. The border becomes a curve: an ellipse, a parabola or a hyperbola. This is Quadratic Discriminant Analysis.

Make the sea bass cloud wide and the salmon cloud tight. The border bends around the tight group. Press "Make an island" and the salmon end up inside a closed loop, which no straight line could ever draw. The faint dotted line is what LDA would still insist on.

Say thisMake the blue cloud wide and the orange one small. The border bends around the orange group. Now press "Make an island": the salmon are inside a closed loop, and a straight line can never do that. Compare the two scores; QDA wins here. Press "Same shapes again" and the curve flattens back into LDA's straight line.

QDA score for class k
\[ \delta_k(x) = -\tfrac12 \log|\Sigma_k| \;-\; \tfrac12\,(x-\mu_k)^\top \Sigma_k^{-1}(x-\mu_k) \;+\; \log \pi_k \]
Read it as: a penalty for a big cloud (the log-determinant), minus half the squared Mahalanobis distance to that cloud's centre, plus the prior. Nothing cancels between classes, so the border is quadratic in \(x\).
Step 06 · worked calculation

One fish, two opinions, by hand

Back to one measurement so the arithmetic fits on a napkin. We have measured four salmon and four sea bass. A new fish arrives at 65 cm. LDA and QDA will disagree about it, and the numbers show exactly why. (The parent chapter does the same calculation with two features and full matrices.)

Say thisWalk the class through the steps in order, opening "how exactly?" wherever someone looks lost. The punchline: LDA uses one averaged spread for both fish and just asks which centre is nearer. QDA notices that 65 cm is nearly two of the salmon's own standard deviations away but under one of the sea bass's. Same fish, two reasonable answers.

    \( \pi_s=\pi_b=0.5,\qquad \hat\mu_s = 56,\qquad \hat\mu_b = 75 \)
    how exactly?
    priors: 4 salmon out of 8 fish → 4/8 = 0.5, same for sea bass
    μ̂_s = (50+54+58+62)/4 = 224/4 = 56
    μ̂_b = (60+70+80+90)/4 = 300/4 = 75
  2. 2 · Each fish's own variance
    \( \hat\sigma_s^2 = 26.67 \;(\sigma_s = 5.2),\qquad \hat\sigma_b^2 = 166.67 \;(\sigma_b = 12.9) \)

    Sea bass vary more than six times as much as salmon.

    how exactly?
    variance = Σ(value − mean)² / (n − 1)
    salmon: deviations −6, −2, +2, +6 → (36+4+4+36)/3 = 80/3 = 26.67
    sea bass: deviations −15, −5, +5, +15 → (225+25+25+225)/3 = 500/3 = 166.67
  3. 3 · LDA pools them into one spread
    \( \hat\sigma^2 = \dfrac{3(26.67)+3(166.67)}{8-2} = 96.67 \)
    how exactly?
    pooled = (sum of both squared-deviation totals) / (n − K) = (80 + 500) / (8 − 2) = 580/6 = 96.67
  4. 4 · LDA scores at x* = 65
    \( \delta_s = \dfrac{65\cdot 56}{96.67} - \dfrac{56^2}{2\cdot 96.67} = 21.43,\qquad \delta_b = \dfrac{65\cdot 75}{96.67} - \dfrac{75^2}{2\cdot 96.67} = 21.34 \)

    Equal priors, so the \(\log 0.5\) terms match and drop out. With one shared \(\sigma\) and equal priors, LDA's border is just the midpoint of the means: 65.5 cm.

    how exactly?
    δ_s = 3640/96.67 − 3136/193.33 = 37.66 − 16.22 = 21.43
    δ_b = 4875/96.67 − 5625/193.33 = 50.43 − 29.10 = 21.34
    gap = 0.10 in favour of salmon → P(salmon) = 1/(1+e−0.10) = 0.525
  5. 5 · QDA scores at x* = 65
    \( \delta_s = -\tfrac12\ln 26.67 - \dfrac{(65-56)^2}{2\cdot 26.67} = -1.64 - 1.52 = -3.16 \)
    \( \delta_b = -\tfrac12\ln 166.67 - \dfrac{(65-75)^2}{2\cdot 166.67} = -2.56 - 0.30 = -2.86 \)
    how exactly?
    spread penalties: −½ ln 26.67 = −1.64 · −½ ln 166.67 = −2.56 (the wide fish pays more)
    distances: salmon (65−56)² = 81 → 81/53.33 = 1.52 · sea bass (65−75)² = 100 → 100/333.33 = 0.30
    gap = −2.86 − (−3.16) = 0.30 in favour of sea bass → P(sea bass) = 1/(1+e−0.30) = 0.575
Step 07 · the bias–variance trade-off

Small catch, big surprise

If QDA can draw every border LDA can and more, why not always use it? Because flexibility has a price. With two measurements, LDA learns 7 numbers (two centres and one shared shape) and QDA learns 10 (two centres and two shapes). Those extra numbers have to be estimated from the catch, and a small catch estimates them badly.

The experiment: catch a few fish, fit both methods, then test each one on 1,600 new fish from the same population. Repeat. Each run draws a different catch, so you see how much each method's border jumps around.

Say thisStart with 10 fish and same shapes. Press "Catch 30 times". Watch QDA's border jump around wildly: it is guessing shapes from tiny data. LDA stays calm and wins more often. Now switch to 200 fish and different shapes and run it again. Now QDA wins. The lesson: with little data, the simple method wins. With lots of data and genuinely different shapes, the flexible one wins. That is the bias-variance trade-off.

In general. With \(p\) measurements and \(K\) classes, LDA estimates one shared shape of \(p(p+1)/2\) numbers; QDA estimates \(K\) of them. For 30 tumour measurements and two classes, as in the parent chapter's project, that is 465 shape numbers for LDA and 930 for QDA. The rule of thumb: LDA when the catch is small or the shapes look alike; QDA when the catch is large and the shapes clearly differ.

Step 08 · Fisher's trick, 1936

Fisher's angle

Here is a different way to think about LDA, and the way Ronald Fisher first found it. Squash the whole picture onto one line, like casting a shadow. From most angles the two groups' shadows pile on top of each other. From the right angle they split cleanly apart. Fisher asked for the angle that makes the shadows' centres far apart relative to how spread out each shadow is, and found it has a formula.

The obvious guess, the line joining the two centres, is usually wrong when the clouds are stretched. Fisher's direction leans away from the long axis of the clouds. It is exactly the direction perpendicular to LDA's border, and it is also a way to compress many measurements down to the few that matter for telling classes apart.

Say thisTurn the dial and watch the shadows in the strip under the picture. At most angles orange and blue pile up together. Press "Connect the centres", the obvious guess. There is still overlap, because the clouds are stretched. Now press "Ask Fisher". His formula finds the angle where the groups separate best. That one direction is what LDA uses.

J = (distance between shadow centres)² ÷ (sum of the shadows' variances). Higher is better.

Fisher's criterion and its maximiser
\[ J(w) = \frac{\big(w^\top(\mu_s - \mu_b)\big)^2}{w^\top S_W\, w}, \qquad w^\star \propto S_W^{-1}(\mu_s - \mu_b) \]
Read it as: \(S_W\) is the within-group spread (the pooled covariance). Dividing by it steers the direction away from the stretched side of the clouds. The same \(\Sigma^{-1}(\mu_s-\mu_b)\) appears in the LDA score of step 04: Fisher's angle and LDA's border are one idea seen from two sides.

Build it yourself in Python

This script is self-contained: it makes its own fish, so there is nothing to download. It needs numpy and scikit-learn (pip install numpy scikit-learn). The last loop runs the small-catch race of step 07 for both kinds of population. Change the seed, the cloud shapes or the catch sizes and run it again.

sorting_machine.py
import numpy as np
from sklearn.discriminant_analysis import (LinearDiscriminantAnalysis,
                                           QuadraticDiscriminantAnalysis)
from sklearn.metrics import confusion_matrix

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)

# The "true" fish. Measurements: length (cm), brightness (0-100).
MU = {"salmon": [56, 62], "sea bass": [75, 45]}
COV = {"salmon":   [[30,   8], [  8,  40]],      # tight, slightly tilted
       "sea bass": [[170, -40], [-40, 120]]}     # wide, tilted the other way

def catch(n_each, same_shapes=False):
    """n_each fish of each kind. Label 0 = salmon, 1 = sea bass."""
    cov = dict(COV)
    if same_shapes:
        cov["sea bass"] = cov["salmon"]
    X = np.vstack([rng.multivariate_normal(MU[k], cov[k], n_each) for k in MU])
    y = np.repeat([0, 1], n_each)
    return X, y

# 1. Train on 200 fish, test on 4,000 new ones
X_train, y_train = catch(100)
X_test, y_test = catch(2000)

lda = LinearDiscriminantAnalysis().fit(X_train, y_train)
qda = QuadraticDiscriminantAnalysis().fit(X_train, y_train)
for name, model in [("LDA", lda), ("QDA", qda)]:
    pred = model.predict(X_test)
    print(name, "accuracy:", round((pred == y_test).mean(), 3))
    print(confusion_matrix(y_test, pred))        # rows: salmon, sea bass

# 2. One fish, two opinions
fish = [[65, 55]]                                # 65 cm, brightness 55
print("P(salmon) for a 65 cm fish  LDA:", lda.predict_proba(fish)[0, 0].round(3),
      " QDA:", qda.predict_proba(fish)[0, 0].round(3))

# 3. Small catch, big surprise: train on 5 fish of each kind, 200 times
for same in (False, True):
    X_big, y_big = catch(2000, same)
    wins = {"LDA": 0, "QDA": 0}
    for _ in range(200):
        X_small, y_small = catch(5, same)
        a = LinearDiscriminantAnalysis().fit(X_small, y_small).score(X_big, y_big)
        b = QuadraticDiscriminantAnalysis(reg_param=1e-3).fit(X_small, y_small).score(X_big, y_big)
        wins["LDA" if a > b else "QDA"] += 1
    print("same shapes:" if same else "different shapes:", wins)

Wrap-up

What to carry home

  • Bet on the likelier fish. Multiply how common a class is by how typical the measurement is for it, and pick the bigger product. That is Bayes' rule, and it is the whole engine.
  • Same shape → straight border (LDA). Sharing one covariance makes the squared terms cancel.
  • Own shapes → curved border (QDA). Nothing cancels; the border can even close into an island.
  • Little data → prefer the simple one. QDA's extra shape numbers are estimated badly from a small catch and its border swings. This is the bias–variance trade-off.
  • Fisher's angle is LDA seen sideways. The direction that best separates the shadows is \(\Sigma^{-1}(\mu_s-\mu_b)\), the same vector that sets LDA's border.

Check your understanding

In step 02, why did the border move right when you said 80% of the catch is salmon?

Because the salmon hill is scaled by its prior. A taller salmon hill crosses the sea bass hill further to the right, so a fish needs to be longer before sea bass becomes the better bet. In the LDA score this is the \(\log\pi_k\) term.

Two kinds of fish have the same average length and brightness, but one varies far more. Which method can separate them?

QDA. LDA's border depends only on the difference between centres, so with equal centres it has nothing to work with. QDA uses the different shapes and draws a closed curve around the tight group, like the island in step 05.

In the worked calculation, why did QDA call a 38 cm fish a sea bass even though it is nearer the salmon centre?

Because 38 cm is 3.5 of the salmon's own standard deviations away, an almost impossible salmon, but only 2.9 of the sea bass's much larger standard deviations, merely an unusual sea bass. QDA measures distance in each class's own units.

You have 20 measurements and 15 fish of each kind. Which method is safer?

LDA. QDA would need to estimate a 20 × 20 shape (210 numbers) for each class from 15 points; with \(n_k \le p\) the matrix cannot even be inverted. LDA pools all 30 fish into one estimate.
