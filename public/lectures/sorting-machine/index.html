The Sorting Machine

A fish factory gets thousands of fish a day: salmon and sea bass. A camera measures each one. Today we teach a computer to sort them, and on the way you'll meet LDA and QDA.

The math, if a student asks

P(salmon | length) = P(length | salmon) × P(salmon) / P(length)

“How typical is this length for salmon” × “how common are salmon”. Do the same for sea bass and pick the bigger one. The bottom part, P(length), is the same for both, so you can ignore it.

Step 3 · Two measurements → LDA

Same shapes make a straight border

Now the camera also measures brightness, so each group becomes a cloud. When both clouds have the same shape, the best border is always a straight line. That's Linear Discriminant Analysis.

0of 120 fish sorted right

Look closely: the border doesn't just cut halfway between the two big dots at a right angle. It leans with the shape of the clouds.

The math, if a student asks

δₖ(x) = xᵀΣ⁻¹μₖ − ½ μₖᵀΣ⁻¹μₖ + log πₖ

μ is a cloud's centre, Σ its shape (shared by both), π how common it is. Because both groups share Σ, the x-squared parts cancel and what's left is a straight line.

Drag the two big dots · x = length, y = brightness
Step 4 · Different shapes → QDA

Different shapes bend the border

Real groups rarely share a shape. Give each cloud its own spread and the best border curves. That's Quadratic Discriminant Analysis. The faint dotted line is what LDA would still draw.

The math, if a student asks

δₖ(x) = −½ log|Σₖ| − ½ (x − μₖ)ᵀ Σₖ⁻¹ (x − μₖ) + log πₖ

Now every group k has its own shape Σₖ. The x-squared part no longer cancels, so the border can be a curve: an ellipse, a parabola or a hyperbola.

Why does QDA need more data?

With p measurements and K groups, LDA learns K·p centre numbers plus one shared shape of p(p+1)/2 numbers. QDA learns a shape for every group: K·p(p+1)/2. With 100 measurements and 2 groups that's 5,050 shape numbers for LDA and 10,100 for QDA.

Step 6 · Fisher's trick, 1936

Find the best angle to look from

Squash the picture onto one line, like a shadow. From most angles the two groups' shadows pile on top of each other. From the right angle they split apart. Ronald Fisher found a formula for that angle.

Separation scoreBest cut on this line 00 mistakes of 140

Separation score = how far apart the two shadow centres are, compared with how spread out each shadow is. Higher is better.

The math, if a student asks

J(w) = (wᵀ(μ₁ − μ₂))² / (wᵀ S_W w)  is largest at  w ∝ S_W⁻¹ (μ₁ − μ₂)

S_W is the within-group spread. Dividing by it steers the direction away from the long, stretched side of the clouds.
