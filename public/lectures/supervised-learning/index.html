Supervised Learning

Show the machine labelled examples; it learns the rule

Spam or not. Tumour or not. House price from size. Every one of these is the same game: you hand over examples where the right answer is attached, and the algorithm builds a rule that works on examples it has never seen. This lecture is the map of that game and its players — try being the teacher yourself, on the right.

Input
Pairs (x, y): features with the correct label attached
Output
A rule ŷ(x) that generalises to new data

Foundations

What "supervised" means

Of the three ways machines learn, supervised learning is the one with a teacher: for every training input, someone supplies the correct answer. (Unsupervised learning gets no answers and looks for structure; reinforcement learning gets only a delayed score. Both have their own lectures here.)

\[ \mathcal{D} = \{(x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \dots, (x_n, y_n)\} \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{learning}\;}\; \hat{f}: x \mapsto \hat{y} \]
Read it as: the data is a list of solved exercises; learning turns them into a general rule. The rule's quality is judged on new exercises, never on the solved ones — that single sentence is the hardest idea in this lecture, and the overfitting lab below makes it visible.
Say it"From a dataset of question–answer pairs, find a function f-hat that maps a new question to a good answer."

Two flavours of the answer

Classification

y is a category

Spam / not spam · benign / malignant · which of 3 grape cultivars. The rule draws decision boundaries in feature space — the playground below is all about their shapes. Judged by accuracy, sensitivity, specificity (defined with visuals in the LDA & QDA project).

Regression

y is a number

House price from size and location · tomorrow's demand · a patient's length of stay. The rule draws a fitted curve through the points. Judged by how far predictions miss, e.g. mean squared error.

The vocabulary, in one table

  • Features xThe measurements you feed in: 13 wine chemicals, 30 cell-nucleus numbers, the pixels of a photo. Also called inputs, predictors, covariates.
  • Label yThe right answer attached to each training example — the "supervision". Expensive: usually a human produced it.
  • Training setThe solved exercises the algorithm learns from.
  • Test setSolved exercises hidden during learning, used once at the end to estimate performance on the real world. Never let the model peek.
  • GeneralisationDoing well on new data. The whole point. Memorising the training set is trivial and worthless — a lookup table scores 100% on training and knows nothing.
  • Loss functionThe number being minimised: how wrong is the rule, averaged over examples.
\[ \hat{f} = \arg\min_{f} \; \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} L\big(y_i, f(x_i)\big), \qquad L_{\text{MSE}} = (y - \hat{y})^2, \quad L_{\text{0-1}} = \mathbb{1}[y \neq \hat{y}] \]
Read it as: pick the rule with the smallest average loss on the training set — squared error for regression, "was it wrong?" for classification (in practice its smooth stand-in, cross-entropy). The catch: minimising training loss too eagerly destroys test performance. That tension has a name — the bias–variance trade-off, drawn live in the LDA & QDA lecture.
Say it"f-hat is the function that minimises the average loss over the training examples — subject to not merely memorising them."
The cast

The map of algorithms

Every supervised algorithm answers the same question — what rule connects x to y? — with a different kind of rule. Group them by the shape of the rule and the zoo becomes four families:

Linear models

Linear regression

Fit a straight line (plane) through the points: ŷ = w·x + b. The starting point of all regression.

regression · interpretable · closed form
Linear models

Logistic regression

A linear score squashed to a probability. The default first classifier in industry.

classification · linear boundary
Generative models

LDA & QDA

Model each class as a Gaussian cloud and apply Bayes. Linear or curved boundaries, superb on small data. Full lecture →

classification · closed form · small n
Generative models

Naive Bayes

Bayes with a bold shortcut: pretend features are independent. Crude, fast, shockingly useful for text (spam filters).

classification · very fast
Instance-based

k-nearest neighbours

No formula at all: a new point is classified by a vote of its k closest training points. The data is the model.

both · no training · k = flexibility dial
Trees & ensembles

Decision tree

A flowchart of yes/no questions ("is radius > 15?"). Boundaries are axis-aligned rectangles. Humans can read it.

both · interpretable · depth = flexibility
Trees & ensembles

Random forest

Hundreds of decorrelated trees, each trained on a reshuffled sample, voting together. Averaging slashes variance.

both · strong tabular default
Trees & ensembles

Gradient boosting

Trees built one after another, each correcting the last one's mistakes. XGBoost/LightGBM win most tabular-data competitions.

both · state of the art on tables
Margin & kernels

Support vector machine

Place the boundary to maximise the safety gap to the nearest points; kernels let the "line" live in a bent space.

classification · medium n
Neural networks

Neural nets / deep learning

Stacks of linear models with bends between them — a universal shape-fitter. Unbeatable on images, audio, text; data-hungry on small tables.

both · perception tasks · big data

The central danger

Overfitting: when memorising beats learning

Give any flexible algorithm enough freedom and it will fit the training set perfectly — noise, mistakes and all. The train score climbs to 100% while the test score turns around and falls. Watch it happen: a decision tree on the two-moons data, with depth as the freedom dial.

3

Teal line: train accuracy — always rises with freedom.
Crimson line: test accuracy — rises, peaks, then falls as the tree starts carving rectangles around individual noisy points.

The gap between the lines is memorisation. Model selection = choosing the depth where the crimson line peaks — in practice via a held-out validation set or cross-validation.

Method

The universal recipe

Whatever the algorithm, a supervised project follows the same five moves — both lectures on this site follow them to the letter:

  1. Split before anything else. Lock a test set away (say 30%). It is touched exactly once, at the very end. Peeking = fooling yourself.
  2. Look at the data. Class balance, feature ranges, obvious separations, outliers. (The tumour project starts with exactly this table.)
  3. Fit a simple baseline first. Logistic regression or LDA. Any fancier model must beat it to earn its complexity.
  4. Tune flexibility on validation data, not on the test set: cross-validation chooses k, depth, regularisation — the peak of the crimson curve above.
  5. Report on the untouched test set, with the metric that matches the real cost — in screening, a missed cancer is not worth four false alarms. (The threshold explorer makes this choice physical.)
Judgement

Choosing an algorithm

Reach for simple + generative (logistic, LDA/QDA, naive Bayes) when

  • data is small or expensive — hundreds of rows, not millions
  • you must explain the rule to a regulator, doctor or examiner
  • a stable baseline is needed in a day, not a month

Reach for flexible (forests, boosting, nets) when

  • data is plentiful and the truth is clearly non-linear
  • tabular business data → gradient boosting first
  • images, audio, text → neural networks, no contest

The honest summary: flexibility is bought with data. Simple models have bias but survive small samples; flexible models need volume to tame their variance — the bias–variance lab shows the purchase price, and the tumour project shows a 1936 linear model beating a curved one on 569 real patients.

Wrap-up

The lecture in five lines

  • Supervised learning turns solved examples (x, y) into a rule ŷ(x) — judged only on unseen data.
  • Two flavours: classification draws boundaries, regression fits curves.
  • Every algorithm is a different shape of rule: lines, Gaussian clouds, neighbour votes, rectangle flowcharts, committees of trees.
  • Flexibility is a dial, and overfitting is what happens at the far end: train score up, test score down.
  • The method is fixed: split → explore → baseline → tune on validation → report once on test, with the metric that matches the real cost.

Check your understanding

A model scores 100% on training data and 71% on test data. What happened, and what would you turn down?

Overfitting: it memorised the training set, noise included. Turn down flexibility — smaller depth, bigger k, more regularisation — or get more data. The overfitting lab shows exactly this signature.

Why must the test set stay untouched until the very end?

Every decision you make after looking at test results leaks information into the model. The test set then measures your tuning, not the real world. Tune on validation folds; spend the test set once.

k-NN with k = 1 vs k = 51 — which overfits, which underfits?

k = 1 overfits: every training point rules its own tiny island, noise included. k = 51 underfits: the vote averages over half the dataset and smears real structure. Watch both in the playground.

Where do LDA, QDA and reinforcement learning sit relative to this lecture?

LDA & QDA are supervised classifiers of the generative family — their lecture is the deep dive into one cell of this map. Reinforcement learning is a different paradigm entirely: no labels, only delayed rewards from interaction.
