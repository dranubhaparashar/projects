The idea

Averaging models: Bayes or committee?

There are two quite different reasons to combine several models, and they lead to different mathematics. In Bayesian model averaging (BMA) we believe that one of the candidate models produced the whole data set; we just don’t know which. So we weight each model’s prediction by how probable that model is after seeing the data. In a model combination, such as a mixture or a committee, each data point may come from a different component, and the mix itself is part of the model.

The difference shows up as data accumulate. Below, the data come from a 60/40 mixture of two bumps, N(−1.5, 1) and N(+1.5, 1). Model h₁ says “everything comes from the left bump”, h₂ says “everything comes from the right bump”. Slide the number of points N up and watch BMA put almost all of its belief on one bump, while the mixture keeps both.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(x \mid X) &= \sum_{h} p(x \mid h)\, p(h \mid X) \\ p(h \mid X) &\propto p(h) \prod_{n=1}^{N} p(x_n \mid h) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Bayesian model averaging. X is the data set and h runs over the candidate models. The forecast for a new x is each model’s forecast, weighted by that model’s posterior probability. The posterior multiplies the likelihood of every point under one model, so a small advantage per point turns into an overwhelming one: as N grows, p(h | X) heads to 1 for a single model.
Say it“p of x given X is the sum over models of p of x given h, times the posterior of h; the posterior is the prior times the product of the likelihoods.”
\[ p(X) = \prod_{n=1}^{N} \sum_{k} \pi_k\, p(x_n \mid k) \]
Read it as: model combination, here a two-component mixture. The sum sits inside the product: every point gets its own blend of components. The mixing weights πk are parameters, fitted to the data (here by EM), and they settle on the true mix rather than on 0 or 1.
Say it“p of X is the product over points of the sum over components of pi-k times p of x-n given k.”
BMA: p(h₂ | X)
mixture: π̂₂

Neither is wrong; they answer different questions. BMA is the right tool when you really are unsure which single model is true (which polynomial degree, which set of predictors); its weights are a statement of uncertainty that shrinks with data. A committee or mixture is right when the truth itself is a blend. Random forests and boosting are committees: nobody believes one tree generated the data. The rest of this chapter is about committees, and how to choose their weights well.

Choosing the weights

Model averaging and stacking

Suppose you have fitted several models f̂1, …, f̂M and want one prediction Σ wmf̂m(x). Three recipes. Pick the best by cross-validation: all weight on one model. BIC weights: a cheap approximation to the BMA posterior, wm ∝ e−BICm/2. And stacking (Wolpert 1992, Breiman 1996): choose the weights that predict best out of sample. The catch is that the weights must not be fitted to the models’ training-set predictions, or the most flexible model would win every time. So stacking uses each model’s prediction for point i made without point i.

Below, four polynomial fits (degrees 1, 3, 5 and 9) compete on a wiggly curve. Drag the points, change N and the noise, and compare how the three recipes weight the four models.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf w}^{\,\text{st}} &= \arg\min_{\mathbf w \ge 0}\ \sum_{i=1}^{N} r_i^2 \\ r_i &= y_i - \sum_{m=1}^{M} w_m\, \hat f_m^{-i}(x_i) \\ \hat f_{\text{st}}(x) &= \sum_{m} \hat w^{\,\text{st}}_m\, \hat f_m(x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: f̂m−i(xi) is model m’s prediction at xi when it was trained without point i (leave-one-out; for least-squares fits it has the closed form yi − ei/(1 − hii)). Regress y on these honest predictions, keeping the weights non-negative. Then apply the weights to the models fitted on all the data. In general (AIMA’s version) the “regression” can be any learner, a meta-model, trained on held-out predictions of the base models.
Say it“The stacking weights minimise the squared error of y-i against the weighted sum of the leave-one-out predictions, with every weight at least zero.”

Searching, not averaging

Bumping: let the bootstrap shake you out of a bad split

The bootstrap can also be used to choose a model. Greedy tree growing can get stuck: on the XOR pattern below, no single first split helps, so the greedy algorithm makes an almost arbitrary first cut and the tree never recovers. Bumping (Tibshirani & Knight 1999) fits the same small model to many bootstrap samples, then keeps whichever one fits the original training data best. A resampled data set nudges the greedy search into a different, sometimes much better, region. All candidates have the same size (here four leaves), so comparing training errors is fair. Raise the number of bootstrap draws B.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat b &= \arg\min_{b \,\in\, \{0,\dots,B\}} R_b \\ R_b &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} L\big(y_i,\ \hat f^{*b}(x_i)\big) \\ \hat f_{\text{bump}}(x) &= \hat f^{*\hat b}(x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: f̂*b is the model fitted to bootstrap sample b (b = 0 is the original data set itself, so bumping can never do worse on the training data). Score every candidate on the original N points and keep the winner. Nothing is averaged; the bootstrap is used purely as a way to perturb the search.
Say it“b-hat is the draw whose model has the smallest loss on the original data; the bumped model is that one.”

Random forests, analysed

Variance, bias and the m dial

A random forest grows B trees, each on a bootstrap sample, and at every split lets the tree look at only m randomly chosen features out of p. The defaults are m = ⌊√p⌋ with leaves down to one point for classification, and m = ⌊p/3⌋ with at least five points per leaf for regression, though m is worth tuning. Why does restricting the features help? Because the average of B trees, each with variance σ² and pairwise correlation ρ, has variance

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}\hat f_{\text{rf}}(x) &= \rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2 \\ &\to \rho\,\sigma^2 \ \text{ as } B\to\infty \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: at a test point x, σ² = σ²(x) is how much a single random tree’s prediction at x varies from one training set to another; ρ(x) is the correlation between two trees of the same forest, again across training sets. More trees kill the second term only. The first term, ρσ², is a floor that no number of trees removes; the only way down is to make the trees less alike, and a small m does exactly that. The price is bias: the forest’s expected prediction equals one random tree’s, so the forest is exactly as biased as its trees, and trees forced to use weak features are more biased.
Say it“The forest’s variance is rho sigma squared, plus one minus rho over B times sigma squared.”

Reading a forest, part 1

Out-of-bag error and variable importance

Each bootstrap sample leaves out about 37% of the rows (the chance a row is never drawn in N draws is (1 − 1/N)N ≈ e−1). Predict every row using only the trees that did not see it and you get the out-of-bag (OOB) error: a test error almost for free, close to what N-fold cross-validation would give. The same idea gives the best-known measure of which features matter.

There are two importance measures, and they can disagree. Impurity importance (mean decrease in impurity, MDI) adds up how much each split on a feature reduced the Gini index. Permutation importance shuffles one feature among a tree’s OOB rows, breaking its link to the class, and records how much that tree’s OOB accuracy drops. The data below have three real signals (x₁ strong, x₂ medium, x₃ weak) and three pure-noise features. Add a near-copy of x₁ and change m to see where the two measures part ways.

\[ \mathcal I^{\text{MDI}}_j = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} \sum_{t \in T_b:\ v(t)=j} \frac{n_t}{N}\,\Delta i(t) \]
Read it as: for every split t in every tree that uses feature j, take the drop in Gini impurity Δi(t), weighted by the share of rows reaching that node; sum and average over trees (then normalise to add up to 1). It is computed on the in-bag rows, so it rewards any feature that offers many places to split, signal or not.
Say it“Impurity importance of j is the average over trees of the weighted impurity decreases at the splits on j.”
\[ \mathcal I^{\text{perm}}_j = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} \big( a_b - a_b^{(j)} \big) \]
Read it as: ab is tree b’s accuracy on its out-of-bag rows, the rows it never saw; ab(j) is that accuracy after column j is shuffled among those rows. So: measure, shuffle column j among those rows and measure again. The average drop is how much the forest relies on xj to predict new data. A pure-noise feature scores about zero, however often it was split on.
Say it“Permutation importance of j is the average over trees of the out-of-bag accuracy lost when j is shuffled.”

Reading a forest, part 2

Proximity plots: the forest’s own map of the data

Two rows that keep landing in the same leaf are, in the forest’s eyes, similar. Count how often that happens and you get an N × N proximity matrix. Turn proximities into distances, 1 − prox, and multidimensional scaling draws the rows in two dimensions so that rows the forest treats alike sit close together. The plot is a picture of what the forest has learned, whatever the number of input features. It typically looks like a star: one arm per class, with the pure, easy rows at the tips and the confusable ones near the centre. Click a point in either panel to see its proximities; change the leaf size.

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{prox}(i,j) &= S_{ij} \,/\, O_{ij} \\ O_{ij} &= \#\{b:\ i, j \text{ both OOB}\} \\ S_{ij} &= \#\{b:\ \ldots \text{ same leaf}\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Oij counts the trees b that saw neither row during training; Sij counts those of them that also send both rows to the same leaf. The proximity is the share. Using out-of-bag pairs keeps a tree from calling two rows similar just because it memorised them. MDS then places the points so that ordinary distances in the picture approximate 1 − prox(i, j).
Say it“The proximity of i and j is the fraction of trees, among those where both are out of bag, that put them in the same leaf.”

Analysis

Do forests overfit? And what they really are

A popular claim is that random forests “cannot overfit”. Half of it is true: adding trees never hurts. The forest converges to the average over all possible random trees, and the test error settles down rather than turning up as it would for boosting. But that limit can itself overfit, because each tree can be grown until every leaf holds one noisy point. Leaf size is a real tuning parameter, even if forests are forgiving about it. Change the leaf size and the number of trees; click anywhere on the plot to choose a point x₀.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat f_{\text{rf}}(x_0) &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i(x_0)\, y_i \\ w_i(x_0) &= \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{c_{bi}\ \mathbf 1\{x_i \in \ell_b(x_0)\}}{n_b(x_0)} \\ n_b(x_0) &= \textstyle\sum_k c_{bk}\ \mathbf 1\{x_k \in \ell_b(x_0)\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ℓb(x₀) is the leaf of tree b that x₀ falls into and cbi counts how many times row i was drawn into tree b’s bootstrap sample, so nb(x₀) is the number of bootstrap rows in that leaf. Each tree predicts the average y of its leaf; averaging over trees gives a weighted average of the training y’s. The weights are non-negative and add to 1. So a forest is a nearest-neighbour smoother (Lin & Jeon 2006), and the leaf size plays the role of k.
Say it“The forest’s prediction at x-zero is a weighted average of the training y’s; row i’s weight is its average share of x-zero’s leaf across the trees.”

Learning ensembles

Learning a good ensemble: grow a dictionary, then prune it

Every ensemble so far is a sum of basis functions, f(x) = α0 + Σ αmTm(x), where each Tm is a tree. Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman split the job in two. Step 1: grow a large, varied dictionary of trees. Step 2: forget the weights the growing procedure implied (1/B for a forest, ν for boosting) and learn new ones with the lasso, which sets most of them to exactly zero.

For step 1, Friedman & Popescu’s ISLE (importance-sampled learning ensembles) has two dials. η is the fraction of the rows each tree sees: a smaller η makes trees more varied and cheaper. ν is the memory: ν = 0 fits every tree to the same target (forest-like), ν > 0 fits each tree to what the previous ones left over (boosting-like). Pick a dictionary, then slide λ and watch a handful of re-weighted trees match or beat the whole ensemble.

\[ \begin{aligned} \gamma_m &= \arg\min_{\gamma} \sum_{i \in S_m(\eta)} L_i(\gamma) \\ L_i(\gamma) &= L\big(y_i,\ f_{m-1}(x_i) + b(x_i;\gamma)\big) \\ f_m(x) &= f_{m-1}(x) + \nu\, b(x;\gamma_m) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ISLE’s generator. Sm(η) is a random subsample holding a fraction η of the rows; b(x; γ) is a small tree with split points and leaf values γ. Fit each new tree to the current residuals on its subsample, then add a fraction ν of it. The trees Tm(x) = b(x; γm) are the dictionary.
Say it“Tree m minimises the loss on a random fraction eta of the rows, starting from f m-minus-one; then f-m adds nu times that tree.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\boldsymbol\alpha}(\lambda) &= \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\alpha} \sum_{i=1}^{N} r_i^2 + \lambda\sum_{m=1}^{M} |\alpha_m| \\ r_i &= y_i - \alpha_0 - \sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_m T_m(x_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: post-processing by the lasso. Treat each tree’s predictions as one column of a design matrix and fit a lasso regression of y on the M columns. The L1 penalty zeroes the weights of redundant or useless trees, so a large λ keeps only a few. Tune λ on held-out data, or by cross-validation.
Say it“Alpha-hat minimises the squared error of the weighted trees plus lambda times the sum of the absolute weights.”

Boosting and regularisation paths, revisited

Why does post-processing with the lasso sit so naturally beside boosting? Run gradient boosting with squared error, tiny steps and a fixed dictionary of trees, and it becomes incremental forward stagewise regression: find the tree most correlated with the residual and nudge its coefficient by ε. As ε → 0, the coefficient paths it traces are very close to the lasso’s (identical when every path is monotone). Shrinkage in boosting is, approximately, an L1 penalty. The plot shows both paths over the trees of the dictionary above.

\[ \begin{aligned} j^* &= \arg\max_j\ \big|\langle \mathbf z_j,\ \mathbf r\rangle\big| \\ s &= \operatorname{sign}\langle \mathbf z_{j^*}, \mathbf r\rangle \\ \beta_{j^*} &\leftarrow \beta_{j^*} + \varepsilon s \\ \mathbf r &\leftarrow \mathbf r - \varepsilon s\, \mathbf z_{j^*} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: zj is tree j’s standardised column of predictions, r the current residual, s the direction that helps. Each step moves the single best tree’s coefficient by a tiny ε towards fitting r. After t steps, the total size Σ|βj| is at most tε: like the lasso’s budget, it grows slowly, and most trees never get picked at all.
Say it“Pick the tree most correlated with the residual, move its coefficient a tiny epsilon in that direction, and update the residual.”

Learning ensembles, readable

Rule ensembles: a forest you can read

A tree is a bundle of rules. Every node, not just every leaf, is reached by a conjunction of conditions such as “x₁ > 0.61 and x₂ ≤ 0.39”, and that conjunction is a 0/1 feature. Friedman & Popescu’s RuleFit grows a varied set of small trees, turns every node into a rule, adds the raw features as linear terms, and fits a lasso over the lot. The survivors are short rules with coefficients, a model a person can read like a scorecard. The data here have a hidden box, a hidden band and a gentle slope. Slide λ and see whether the lasso finds them.

\[ \begin{aligned} r_k(x) &= \prod_{j \in P_k} \mathbf 1\{x_j \in s_{jk}\} \\ f(x) &= a_0 + \sum_{k} a_k\, r_k(x) + \sum_{j} b_j\, x_j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: rule k is the product of indicator functions along the path Pk to one node; sjk is the allowed range of feature j. The model adds weighted rules and linear terms; the a’s and b’s come from a lasso, exactly as in the post-processing above. A rule’s importance is |ak|√(sk(1 − sk)), where sk is its support, the share of rows it covers: a rule that fires for almost everyone, or almost no one, explains little.
Say it“Each rule is one when every condition on its path holds; f is a constant plus weighted rules plus weighted raw features.”

Real-world project

Project: stacking models to map Colorado’s forest cover

Blackard & Dean (1999) asked whether the dominant tree species on a 30 × 30 m patch of the Roosevelt National Forest could be predicted from cartographic data alone: elevation, slope, aspect, distances to water, roads and past fire points, hill-shade at three times of day, wilderness area and soil type. The Covertype data set (UCI, 581,012 patches, CC BY 4.0) has seven cover types, from spruce/fir and lodgepole pine (85% between them) to rare cottonwood/willow.

We take 8,000 random patches: 4,000 to learn from, 4,000 to test on. The 12 features are stored as codes: each of the ten numeric features is cut at up to 63 quantiles of the training data (so “elevation” becomes one of 64 bands, typically 10–20 m wide and wider at the extremes), wilderness area is 1–4 and soil type is 1–40. The soil codes follow the US Forest Service’s land-type codes, whose first digit is the climatic zone, so their order means something. Three different learners are trained in your browser: a multinomial logistic regression (on standardised numbers plus one-hot wilderness and soil), a random forest (100 trees, m = 3) and gradient boosting (100 rounds of depth-3 trees, learning rate 0.1). Then a stacker, a second logistic regression, learns how to combine their 5-fold out-of-fold probabilities. Press Train (it takes 10–30 seconds; the three single models report first).

The same project in scikit-learn

import json, numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier, GradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, log_loss

J = json.load(open("x026-data.json", encoding="utf8"))   # 8,000 random rows of UCI Covertype
B64 = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZabcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz0123456789+/"
C = np.array([[B64.index(ch) for ch in col] for col in J["cols"]]).T   # 12 features as bin codes
y = np.array([int(ch) for ch in J["y"]]); N = J["ntr"]                # 4,000 train / 4,000 test
num = np.column_stack([np.array(J["rep"][j])[C[:, j]] for j in range(10)])
X = np.hstack([(num - num[:N].mean(0)) / num[:N].std(0),             # linear model: standardised numbers
               np.eye(4)[C[:, 10]], np.eye(40)[C[:, 11]]])            # + one-hot wilderness and soil
ytr, yte, fold = y[:N], y[N:], np.arange(N) % 5

def make(name, seed):
    if name == "lr": return LogisticRegression(C=1.0, tol=1e-8, max_iter=5000), X
    if name == "rf": return RandomForestClassifier(100, max_features="sqrt", random_state=seed), C
    return GradientBoostingClassifier(n_estimators=100, learning_rate=0.1, max_depth=3, random_state=0), C

def level(name, seed):           # test probabilities, plus 5-fold out-of-fold probabilities for stacking
    m, A = make(name, seed); P = m.fit(A[:N], ytr).predict_proba(A[N:]); Z = np.zeros((N, 7))
    for f in range(5):
        m, A = make(name, seed + 100 + f)
        Z[fold == f] = m.fit(A[:N][fold != f], ytr[fold != f]).predict_proba(A[:N][fold == f])
    return P, Z

fixed = {k: level(k, 0) for k in ["lr", "gb"]}          # deterministic: fit once
runs = []
for seed in range(3):                                     # the forest is random: try 3 seeds
    L = dict(fixed, rf=level("rf", seed)); P = {k: L[k][0] for k in L}
    Z = np.hstack([L[k][1] for k in ["lr", "rf", "gb"]])
    P["avg"] = (P["lr"] + P["rf"] + P["gb"]) / 3
    P["stack"] = LogisticRegression(C=1.0, tol=1e-8, max_iter=5000).fit(Z, ytr).predict_proba(np.hstack([P["lr"], P["rf"], P["gb"]]))
    runs.append(P)
print("model   test accuracy (3 forest seeds)     test log-loss")
for k in ["lr", "rf", "gb", "avg", "stack"]:
    a = [accuracy_score(yte, P[k].argmax(1)) for P in runs]; l = [log_loss(yte, P[k], labels=range(7)) for P in runs]
    print(f"{k:6s}  {100*np.mean(a):6.3f}%  (range {100*min(a):.3f} to {100*max(a):.3f})   {np.mean(l):.4f}")

print()
for seed in [2, 1, 0]:                                    # the last one (seed 0) is kept below
    rf = RandomForestClassifier(100, max_features="sqrt", random_state=seed, oob_score=True).fit(C[:N], ytr)
    print(f"forest seed {seed}: OOB accuracy {rf.oob_score_:.4f}, test accuracy {rf.score(C[N:], yte):.4f}")
rng, perm = np.random.RandomState(0), np.zeros(12)     # ESL's OOB permutation importance, tree by tree
for t, inbag in zip(rf.estimators_, rf.estimators_samples_):
    oob = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(N), inbag); Xo, yo = C[oob].astype(float), ytr[oob]
    base = (t.predict(Xo) == yo).mean()
    for j in range(12):
        Xp = Xo.copy(); Xp[:, j] = rng.permutation(Xp[:, j]); perm[j] += (base - (t.predict(Xp) == yo).mean()) / 100
print(f"{'feature':28s} impurity (MDI)   OOB permutation")
for j in np.argsort(-rf.feature_importances_):
    print(f"{J['names'][j]:28s} {rf.feature_importances_[j]:.4f}           {perm[j]:.4f}")

The page grows its own trees: the same CART splitting rules as scikit-learn (Gini for the forest, squared error on the gradients for boosting, scikit-learn’s Newton leaf values and its prior-based starting point), and an L-BFGS solver for the same penalised logistic objective. The logistic regression therefore matches scikit-learn exactly, and the boosted model’s probabilities agree to 15 decimal places for the first seven rounds. After that, two candidate splits occasionally tie exactly (they separate the same handful of rare cottonwood patches), scikit-learn breaks ties by a random feature order, the page by its own, and the two models drift apart by a few predictions. A forest’s accuracy depends on its random seed, so compare the page’s forest and stack with scikit-learn’s range over seeds rather than a single number.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Bayesian model averaging weights models by their posterior and, as data grow, bets on one; committees, mixtures and forests combine models that are all partly right.
  • Stacking learns the combination weights from out-of-fold predictions, never from training-set fits; bumping uses the bootstrap to search, keeping the one model that fits the original data best.
  • A forest’s variance falls to ρσ² as B grows; a small m lowers the correlation ρ but raises the bias, which the forest inherits from its trees. More trees never overfit; deep trees can.
  • Read a forest with OOB error, permutation importance (trust it over impurity importance) and proximity plots; its prediction is a weighted nearest-neighbour average with adaptive neighbourhoods.
  • Learning an ensemble = grow a varied dictionary (ISLE: η, ν), then let the lasso re-weight it; with rules instead of trees (RuleFit), the result is short enough to read.

Check your understanding

With 10,000 data points, BMA puts weight 0.999 on one of two models even though the data clearly come from both. Is BMA broken?

No. BMA assumes exactly one of the models generated all the data, and its weights measure uncertainty about which one. If you believe the truth is a blend, you want a mixture (model combination), whose weights are parameters that settle on the blend.

Why must stacking use leave-one-out or out-of-fold predictions?

On its own training data the most flexible model always fits best, so weights fitted to in-sample predictions would pile onto the most overfitted model. Out-of-fold predictions show how each model does on data it hasn’t seen, which is what the combination will face.

Trees in a forest have variance 4 and pairwise correlation 0.3. What is the variance of a forest of 100 trees, and of an infinite one?

0.3 × 4 + 0.7 × 4 / 100 = 1.2 + 0.028 = 1.228. With infinitely many trees, 1.2: the floor ρσ².

A noise feature with thousands of distinct values ranks third by impurity importance but near zero by permutation importance. Which do you believe?

Permutation importance. Impurity importance is measured on the training (in-bag) rows and rewards any feature that offers many split points, even random ones. Shuffling the feature on out-of-bag rows shows the forest doesn’t need it to predict new data.

What does lasso post-processing buy you over the ensemble as grown?

It re-weights the trees by how much they help together, zeroing redundant ones. You usually get similar or lower test error with a small fraction of the trees: a faster, smaller model, and with rules instead of trees, a readable one.
