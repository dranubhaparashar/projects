Support Vector Machines

The widest street between two classes

When two classes can be split by a line, there are infinitely many lines that do it. Which one should you trust with tomorrow's data? Vladimir Vapnik's answer: the one that leaves the widest street between the classes, so new points have the most room before they cross over. On the right, drag any dot. Only the ringed ones, the support vectors, hold the street in place; move any other dot and nothing happens.

The idea

Which line would you trust?

A perceptron or a logistic regression stops as soon as the training points are on the right sides. But a line that scrapes past a point is fragile: a new example a hair away lands on the wrong side. Measure how much room a line leaves with its margin, the distance from the line to the nearest training point. Steer the line yourself and try to make the street as wide as you can, then compare with the best one.

\[ d_i = \frac{y_i\,(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i + b)}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert}, \qquad \text{margin} = \min_i \, d_i \]
Read it as: labels are y = +1 or −1. w·x + b is positive on one side of the line and negative on the other; dividing by ‖w‖ turns it into a true distance. Multiplying by yi makes it positive when point i is on its correct side. The margin is the worst case over all points; a negative margin means some point is misclassified.
Say it“The signed distance of point i is y-i times w-dot-x-i plus b, over the length of w. The margin is the smallest of these.”

The optimisation

Support vectors: the few that matter

Scale w and b so that the nearest points satisfy y(w·x + b) = 1 exactly. The street's edges are then the lines w·x + b = ±1, its width is 2/‖w‖, and making the street wide means making w short. That gives a clean problem with one best answer (it is convex, so there are no local traps). Its solution has a striking property: it depends only on the points on the street's edges. Click a dot to remove it; click again to bring it back.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{\mathbf{w},\,b}\ \tfrac12 \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 \\ &\text{s.t.}\ \ y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1 \ \text{ for all } i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: find the shortest w (the widest street, 2/‖w‖) such that every point sits on its own side, at or beyond the street's edge.
Say it“Minimise half the squared length of w, subject to y-i times w-dot-x-i plus b being at least one for every point.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{w} = \sum_i \alpha_i\, y_i\, \mathbf{x}_i \\ &\alpha_i \ge 0, \qquad \sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the solution is a weighted sum of training points. Each αi is how hard point i pushes on the street. Points away from the street have αi = 0: they could vanish and nothing would change. The ones with αi > 0 are the support vectors.
Say it“w is the sum of alpha-i, y-i, x-i; the alphas are non-negative and balance between the classes.”

Real data

Soft margins and the C dial

Real classes overlap, and then no street is empty: the hard-margin problem has no solution at all. Cortes & Vapnik (1995) let points trespass, at a price. Each point gets a slack ξi: how far it is inside the street (or beyond it, on the wrong side). The dial C sets the price of slack. Turn C: small C buys a wide, calm street that tolerates mistakes; large C fights for every training point and narrows the street. You can drag the dots too.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{\mathbf{w},\,b,\,\xi}\ \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + C\sum_i \xi_i \\ &\text{s.t.}\ \ y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i+b) \ge 1-\xi_i,\ \ \xi_i \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: two goals pulling against each other: a wide street (small ‖w‖) and little trespassing (small total slack). ξi = 0 for points outside the street, between 0 and 1 for points inside it but on the right side, and more than 1 for misclassified points. In the dual, this simply caps every αi at C.
Say it“Minimise half w squared plus C times the total slack, where each point may fall short of the margin by its slack xi-i.”

Another view

Hinge loss: the SVM as a loss function

At the optimum each slack is as small as allowed: ξi = max(0, 1 − yif(xi)). Substitute that in and the constraints disappear. The SVM becomes the same recipe as every model so far: a loss on the training points plus a penalty on the weights. Its loss is the hinge: zero once a point is safely beyond the street, then rising in a straight line. Compare it with logistic regression's loss from chapter 1.3. Slide the point's score.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{\mathbf{w},\,b}\ \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2C}\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2}_{\text{wide street}} + \sum_i \underbrace{\max\!\big(0,\ 1 - y_i f(\mathbf{x}_i)\big)}_{\text{hinge loss}} \\ &\text{where } f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: z = y·f(x) is the point's score: positive when it's on the correct side, at least 1 when it's beyond the street. The hinge charges nothing for z ≥ 1, so points far from the boundary have no say at all. That is exactly why only support vectors matter. Logistic loss never quite reaches zero, so every point keeps a little influence.
Say it“Minimise one over two C times w squared, plus the sum of the max of zero and one minus y f of x.”

Bending the boundary

The kernel trick: lift, then cut

Below, orange points sit in the middle and blue points on both ends. No single cut on the line separates them. But give every point a second coordinate, x², and the middle points stay low while the ends rise. In the lifted space a straight street separates them, and back on the line that street becomes two cuts. Drag the lift slider and watch the street appear.

Here's the trick. Look back at the dual: the solution w = Σ αiyixi means every prediction only needs dot products between points. So we never have to build the lifted features; we only need a function K that returns their dot product. That function is a kernel, and it can stand for a lift into hundreds, or infinitely many, dimensions at the cost of one small formula.

\[ \begin{aligned} &K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}) = \varphi(\mathbf{x})\cdot\varphi(\mathbf{z}) \\ &\varphi(x) = (x,\ x^2) \ \Rightarrow\ K(x,z) = xz + x^2z^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a kernel is a dot product in some lifted space φ, computed without ever building φ. For the parabola lift above, K is two multiplications.
Say it“K of x and z is phi of x dot phi of z.”
\[ f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i \in \text{SV}} \alpha_i\, y_i\, K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}) + b \]
Read it as: to classify a new point, compare it with each support vector through K, weight by αiyi, add up, and read the sign. Training and prediction both see the data only through K. Swap the kernel and you swap the shape of the boundary.
Say it“f of x is the sum over support vectors of alpha-i y-i K of x-i and x, plus b.”
Playground

RBF kernels: similarity bumps

The most popular kernel, the radial basis function (RBF, or Gaussian), measures how close two points are. Each support vector becomes a bump of influence, and f(x) adds up the bumps, plus for one class and minus for the other. γ sets the bumps' reach: small γ gives broad bumps and smooth boundaries; large γ gives narrow bumps that wrap around single points, which is overfitting. Click to add points, try the presets, and move the mouse over the plot to see f(x) built from its bumps.

\[ K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}) = \exp\!\big(-\gamma\,\lVert \mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\rVert^2\big) \]
Read it as: 1 when the points coincide, falling towards 0 as they move apart. 1/√γ is roughly the distance at which a support vector's influence fades. This kernel is a dot product in an infinite-dimensional space, yet it costs one exponential.
Say it“K is e to the minus gamma times the squared distance between x and z.”

Choosing

When to reach for an SVM

SVMs ruled machine learning from the late 1990s until deep learning arrived: they were the best tools for handwriting, text categorisation and gene-expression data. They are still excellent when you have hundreds to tens of thousands of examples, many features, and want a strong, principled model. Their weak spots are scale (training cost grows faster than the number of examples), the need to scale features, and the lack of built-in probabilities.

Real-world project

Project: catching forged banknotes

Counterfeit detection is a classic machine-vision task. Lohweg and colleagues (2013) photographed 1,372 banknote-like specimens, 762 genuine and 610 forged, with an industrial camera. A wavelet transform turned each image into four numbers that describe its fine print texture: the variance, skewness and kurtosis of the wavelet coefficients, and the image's entropy. The data is on the UCI repository. We shuffle it once, learn from 1,000 notes and test on the other 372. Features are standardised (SVMs measure distances, so scale matters). Press Train.

The same project in scikit-learn

import numpy as np
from sklearn.svm import SVC
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

D = np.loadtxt("data_banknote_authentication.txt", delimiter=",")   # UCI repository
X, y = D[:, :4], D[:, 4].astype(int)                                  # 0 = genuine, 1 = forged
p = np.random.RandomState(42).permutation(len(y))
X_tr, y_tr, X_te, y_te = X[p][:1000], y[p][:1000], X[p][1000:], y[p][1000:]

models = {"logistic regression": LogisticRegression(),
          "linear SVM": SVC(kernel="linear", C=1),
          "polynomial SVM (d=3)": SVC(kernel="poly", degree=3, gamma=0.25, coef0=1, C=1),
          "RBF SVM": SVC(kernel="rbf", gamma=0.25, C=1)}
for name, m in models.items():
    pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), m).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
    n_sv = m.n_support_.sum() if hasattr(m, "n_support_") else "-"
    print(f"{name:21s} accuracy = {pipe.score(X_te, y_te):.4f}   support vectors = {n_sv}")

linear SVM            accuracy = 0.9866   support vectors = 60
polynomial SVM (d=3)  accuracy = 1.0000   support vectors = 43
RBF SVM               accuracy = 1.0000   support vectors = 92

The page runs its own port of libsvm's SMO solver (the algorithm inside scikit-learn's SVC), with the same stopping tolerance, so every accuracy above matches scikit-learn's. Support-vector counts can differ by one or two: a few points sit with α within the solver's tolerance of zero, and tiny rounding differences between machines decide which side of zero they land on. Solve to a tolerance of 10−8 and scikit-learn itself reports 93 support vectors for the RBF model, not 92; one of them has α ≈ 0.00005.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Of all the boundaries that separate the classes, the SVM picks the one with the widest margin: minimise ½‖w‖² while keeping every point beyond y·f(x) = 1.
  • The solution depends only on the support vectors, the points on or inside the street; every other point could be deleted.
  • Soft margins let points trespass at price C: small C gives a wide, tolerant street; large C a narrow, strict one. Equivalently, the SVM minimises the hinge loss plus a weight penalty.
  • The kernel trick swaps every dot product for K(x, z), giving curved boundaries from a lift we never compute. RBF's γ sets how local each support vector's bump is.
  • Scale your features, tune C and γ together, and an SVM is a strong all-rounder: on banknotes, 372 of 372 test notes right.

Check your understanding

Why is the street's width 2/‖w‖?

The edges are the lines w·x + b = +1 and w·x + b = −1. Moving from one to the other changes w·x by 2, and moving a distance d along the direction of w changes it by d‖w‖. So d = 2/‖w‖.

You delete a training point that is not a support vector and retrain. What changes?

Nothing: its α was 0, so it contributed nothing to w or b, and the old solution still satisfies every remaining constraint.

Your RBF SVM gets 100% training accuracy and almost every point is a support vector. What happened, and what do you turn?

Overfitting: γ is so large that each point gets its own small bump. Lower γ (broader bumps) and/or lower C (more tolerance), and choose both by cross-validation.

Why must features be standardised before an RBF SVM?

The kernel uses squared distances. A feature measured in large units would dominate ‖x − z‖² and the others would be ignored.

The hinge loss is zero for a point with y·f(x) = 2. What does that say about the point's influence on the model?

None: it's beyond the street, contributes no loss and no gradient, and has α = 0. Logistic loss would still give it a small pull.
