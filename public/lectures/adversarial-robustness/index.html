Adversarial Examples, Tangent Prop & Robustness

Why a push you can barely see fools a network, and how to teach it not to care

A network that reads clothes correctly four times out of five can be made to misread almost all of them by moving every pixel a tiny, carefully chosen amount. The push is not random: it points exactly where the network is most sensitive. This chapter builds the attacks, explains why they work, and then makes networks care less, first by training on the attacks, then by building in the invariances we already know about. Touch the figure: tap the pictures for another garment, slide ε, the most any pixel may move, and switch to the network that was trained against the attack.

The puzzle

A small push, a wrong answer

Szegedy and colleagues (2014) found something unsettling: for almost any correctly classified image, a change too small for a person to notice can make a trained network give a confident, wrong answer. Such an input is an adversarial example. To talk about it precisely we fix a threat model: the attacker may add a perturbation η to the image as long as no pixel moves by more than ε (the L∞ norm of η is at most ε), and the image stays a valid image, with every pixel in [0, 1]. Here ε = 0.1 is one and a half of the 15 grey-level steps in our pictures.

The key point is that size is not what matters; direction is. A random ±ε pattern of the same size barely changes the network’s answers, because its effects on the output mostly cancel. A pattern aligned with the gradient of the loss does not cancel. Slide ε and compare the two curves: same budget, same pixels, very different damage.

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{\boldsymbol x} &= \boldsymbol x + \boldsymbol\eta, \qquad \|\boldsymbol\eta\|_\infty \le \epsilon, \quad \tilde{\boldsymbol x} \in [0,1]^d \\ \boldsymbol\eta^\star &= \arg\max_{\|\boldsymbol\eta\|_\infty \le \epsilon} J(\boldsymbol\theta,\, \boldsymbol x + \boldsymbol\eta,\, y) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: x is a clean image with d pixels and true label y; the attacker adds η, no entry bigger than ε in size. J is the network’s training loss (cross-entropy) with weights θ held fixed. The attacker looks for the η that makes the loss as large as possible; if the resulting x̃ is classified as anything but y, it is an adversarial example.
Say it“Eta star is the perturbation, no bigger than epsilon in any pixel, that maximises the loss at x plus eta.”

Why it happens

The linearity explanation

The first guess was that adversarial examples come from networks being wildly non-linear. Goodfellow, Shlens and Szegedy (2015) argued the opposite: they come from networks being too linear. Take a single linear unit wTx. Pushing every input by ε in the direction of the sign of its weight changes the output by ε times the sum of the absolute weights. No single input moves much, but the effects all point the same way and add up over all d inputs. If the weights have average size m, the change is about εmd, growing in proportion to the dimension, while a random-sign push grows only like the square root of d because its terms cancel.

ReLU networks are piecewise linear, and networks are deliberately trained to stay in their easy-to-optimise, nearly linear regime, so the same arithmetic applies to them. Raise the dimension d in the toy, then switch to our real network and walk along the attack direction: the true change in its decision margin hugs the straight line predicted by its gradient.

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol w^\top(\boldsymbol x + \boldsymbol\eta) &= \boldsymbol w^\top \boldsymbol x + \boldsymbol w^\top \boldsymbol\eta \\ \max_{\|\boldsymbol\eta\|_\infty \le \epsilon} \boldsymbol w^\top \boldsymbol\eta &= \epsilon\,\|\boldsymbol w\|_1 \approx \epsilon\, m\, d \\ &\text{reached at } \boldsymbol\eta = \epsilon\,\mathrm{sign}(\boldsymbol w) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the change in a linear unit’s output is just wTη. Within the ε budget it is largest when every ηi is ±ε with the sign of wi, giving ε times the L1 norm of w, which is about εmd when the d weights have average size m. With random signs instead, the terms cancel and the typical change is only ε‖w‖2, about εm√d.
Say it“The most a linear unit can move is epsilon times the L-one norm of w: epsilon, m, d.”

Attack 1

FGSM: one step along the sign of the gradient

If the loss is nearly linear in the input, the best push within the ε-box is easy: take the gradient of the loss with respect to the input (backpropagation gives it for the price of one backward pass) and move every pixel by ε in the direction of its sign. That is the fast gradient sign method. Its first-order gain is ε times the L1 norm of the gradient, which beats a step of the same L2 length by up to a factor of √d, because the box’s corners stick out further than the ball inside it.

The plane below is a two-input logistic classifier; the point x belongs to the teal class. Drag the point and the tip of the weight arrow, then grow ε. FGSM always lands on a corner of the box; the L2 step lands on the circle, in the gradient’s own direction.

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol\eta_{\text{FGSM}} &= \epsilon\, \mathrm{sign}\big(\nabla_{\boldsymbol x} J(\boldsymbol\theta, \boldsymbol x, y)\big) \\ J(\boldsymbol x + \boldsymbol\eta) &\approx J(\boldsymbol x) + \boldsymbol\eta^\top \nabla_{\boldsymbol x} J \\ &= J(\boldsymbol x) + \epsilon\, \|\nabla_{\boldsymbol x} J\|_1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ∇xJ is the gradient of the loss with respect to the input pixels, with the weights fixed. FGSM keeps only its signs. A first-order Taylor expansion says the loss then rises by ε times the sum of the gradient’s absolute values. The L2 step ε∇J/‖∇J‖2 would gain only ε‖∇J‖2.
Say it“Eta is epsilon times the sign of the gradient of J with respect to x; the loss goes up by about epsilon times the gradient’s L-one norm.”

Attack 2

PGD: many small steps, projected back into the box

FGSM bets everything on one linear guess. When the loss curves inside the box, that guess can overshoot or point the wrong way. The fix is to take several smaller signed steps of size α, re-computing the gradient each time, and after every step to project back into the allowed set: clip each pixel to within ε of the original (and to the valid range). This is projected gradient descent on the negative loss (Kurakin et al., 2017; Madry et al., 2018). Starting from a random point in the box and trying several restarts makes it harder for a lucky flat spot to hide a weakness.

Here a small network has learnt the two-moons problem and its boundary bends. Drag the point near the boundary, then press Step and watch PGD feel its way along the curve. The attacker’s label is the class the network gives the starting point.

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol x^{(0)} &= \boldsymbol x + \boldsymbol u, \quad \boldsymbol u \sim \mathcal U[-\epsilon, \epsilon]^d \ \text{(or } \boldsymbol u = \boldsymbol 0) \\ \boldsymbol g^{(t)} &= \nabla_{\boldsymbol x} J(\boldsymbol\theta, \boldsymbol x^{(t)}, y) \\ \boldsymbol x^{(t+1)} &= \Pi_{\mathcal B_\epsilon(\boldsymbol x)}\big(\boldsymbol x^{(t)} + \alpha\, \mathrm{sign}\, \boldsymbol g^{(t)}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: start at x, or at a random point of the box around it. Each step moves every coordinate by α in the sign of the current input gradient g(t), then Π projects back onto the box Bε(x): any coordinate that strayed more than ε from the original is clipped back (for images, also into [0, 1]). With one step, α = ε and no random start, this is exactly FGSM.
Say it“The next iterate is the projection onto the epsilon-box of the current one plus alpha times the sign of the gradient.”

The defence

Adversarial training: learn from the worst case

If attacks find the inputs a network handles worst, train on exactly those inputs. Goodfellow et al. (2015) mixed the ordinary loss with the loss at the FGSM point; Madry et al. (2018) wrote the goal as a game, an outer minimisation over weights of an inner maximisation over perturbations, and solved the inner problem with PGD. In the language of regularisation, adversarial training imposes a local constancy prior: the network’s output should not change within ε of any training point. A purely linear model cannot satisfy that and fit the data well; a network can, which is why it gets robustness and keeps most of its accuracy.

The same trick works without labels. For an unlabelled point, use the network’s own current answer ŷ as the label, find the push that most changes that answer, and train the network to keep its answer there too. These are virtual adversarial examples (Miyato et al., 2016), and they let the many unlabelled points shape the boundary. Pick a training recipe and an ε, then press Train; the robust accuracy is measured with a fresh 10-step PGD attack on 400 test points.

\[ \min_{\boldsymbol\theta}\; \mathbb E_{(\boldsymbol x, y)} \Big[ \max_{\|\boldsymbol\eta\|_\infty \le \epsilon} J(\boldsymbol\theta, \boldsymbol x + \boldsymbol\eta, y) \Big] \]
Read it as: for every training pair, an inner player finds the worst perturbation in the box (approximately, with PGD); the outer player, the optimiser, then lowers the loss at those worst points. Plain training is the special case ε = 0.
Say it“Minimise over theta the expected worst-case loss inside the epsilon-box.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde J(\boldsymbol\theta) &= \tfrac12 J(\boldsymbol x, y) + \tfrac12 J\big(\boldsymbol x + \epsilon\,\mathrm{sign}\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol x} J,\; y\big) \\ J_{\text{virtual}} &= J\big(\boldsymbol x + \epsilon\,\mathrm{sign}\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol x} J(\boldsymbol x, \hat y),\; \hat y\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first line is Goodfellow’s FGSM training, half clean loss and half loss at the FGSM point. The second replaces the unknown label by the network’s own prediction ŷ, so it can be added for unlabelled points: it penalises any change of mind within the box.
Say it“Half the clean loss plus half the loss at the FGSM point; for unlabelled points, use y-hat as the label.”

Known invariances

Tangent vectors and tangent distance

Adversarial training asks for invariance in every direction at once. Often we know more: a coat shifted one pixel, turned a few degrees or made a little bigger is still a coat. Apply such a transformation s(x, ξ) with a continuous amount ξ and the image traces out a curve, a one-dimensional manifold, through image space. Its direction at ξ = 0 is the tangent vector τ, and for small ξ the curve is close to the straight line x + ξτ. In practice τ is computed from a slightly blurred copy of the image, so that it is smooth.

Simard, LeCun and Denker (1993) used tangents to fix nearest-neighbour classification. Plain Euclidean distance says a garment and the same garment shifted by one pixel are far apart. Tangent distance lets each image slide along its own tangent plane first and measures the gap that remains. Pick a transformation and move ξ: the Euclidean distance between the image and its transformed copy shoots up, the tangent distance stays small until the straight-line approximation breaks down.

\[ \boldsymbol\tau = \frac{\partial\, s(\boldsymbol x, \xi)}{\partial \xi}\Big|_{\xi = 0}, \qquad s(\boldsymbol x, \xi) \approx \boldsymbol x + \xi\, \boldsymbol\tau \]
Read it as: s(x, ξ) is the image x transformed by an amount ξ (pixels of shift, radians of rotation, or a fractional change of scale), with s(x, 0) = x. Its derivative at zero is the tangent vector, an image-sized pattern; to first order, transforming x is the same as adding ξ copies of that pattern.
Say it“Tau is the derivative of the transformed image with respect to xi, at xi equals zero.”
\[ \begin{aligned} d_T(\boldsymbol x_1, \boldsymbol x_2) &= \min_{\boldsymbol a, \boldsymbol b} \big\| \boldsymbol r(\boldsymbol a, \boldsymbol b) \big\|_2 \\ \boldsymbol r &= (\boldsymbol x_1 + T_1 \boldsymbol a) - (\boldsymbol x_2 + T_2 \boldsymbol b) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the columns of T1 are x1’s tangent vectors (here four: shift right, shift down, rotate, scale) and a says how far to move along each; likewise T2, b for x2. The minimum over a and b is a small least-squares problem. We add a tiny ridge, 0.01(‖a‖2 + ‖b‖2), so the solver never slides absurdly far.
Say it“Tangent distance is the smallest gap between the two tangent planes.”

Invariance, learned

Tangent propagation and the manifold tangent classifier

Tangent distance builds invariance into the comparison; tangent propagation (Simard et al., 1992) builds it into the network. If the output should not change when the input moves along τ, then the directional derivative of the output along τ, the Jacobian times the tangent, should be zero. So add its square to the loss. The penalty needs only the inputs and their tangents, not labels, so it can be evaluated on unlabelled points too. Training on transformed copies of the data asks for the same invariance by example and for finite transformations; tangent prop asks for it exactly but only infinitesimally.

Two concentric rings: the inner ring is one class, the outer the other, and only six points are labelled, all in a narrow wedge. The label depends on the radius alone, so the right invariance is rotation about the centre, τ(x) = (−x2, x1). Train with λ = 0, then with tangent prop (tangents scaled to unit length), and watch the boundary wrap around the circle.

What if we don’t know the transformation? The manifold tangent classifier (Rifai et al., 2011) learns the tangents from unlabelled data: it trains a contractive autoencoder and takes, at each point, the directions its code is most sensitive to as the local tangent plane of the data manifold; then it runs tangent prop with those. Our stand-in for the autoencoder is simpler: local PCA, the main direction of each point’s ten nearest unlabelled neighbours. Choose Learned from the data to try it.

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial y_k}{\partial \xi}\Big|_{\xi=0} &= \sum_{i=1}^{d} J_{ki}\, \tau_i, \qquad J_{ki} = \frac{\partial y_k}{\partial x_i} \\ \Omega &= \frac12 \sum_n \sum_k \Big( \sum_i J_{nki}\, \tau_{ni} \Big)^2 \\ \tilde E &= E + \lambda\, \Omega \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: J is the network’s Jacobian at input xn, and Jτ is how fast output k changes as the input slides along its tangent. Ω sums the squares of those rates over all outputs and points; λ trades it against the usual error E. Here the outputs yk are the network’s two logits, and the gradient of Ω is computed by hand for its one hidden tanh layer.
Say it“Omega is half the sum of squared directional derivatives of the outputs along the tangents; minimise E plus lambda omega.”
\[ \begin{aligned} C(\boldsymbol x) &= \frac1k \sum_{\boldsymbol x' \in \mathcal N_k(\boldsymbol x)} (\boldsymbol x' - \bar{\boldsymbol x})(\boldsymbol x' - \bar{\boldsymbol x})^\top \\ \boldsymbol\tau(\boldsymbol x) &= \text{top eigenvector of } C(\boldsymbol x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: take the k = 10 unlabelled points nearest to x and their mean x̄. Their covariance C is stretched along the direction in which the data spread locally, the manifold; its leading eigenvector is the estimated tangent. The manifold tangent classifier gets the same thing from the leading singular vectors of a contractive autoencoder’s Jacobian, which also works in high dimensions.
Say it“The learned tangent is the top eigenvector of the local covariance of x’s nearest neighbours.”

Evaluating and certifying robustness

Robust accuracy measured with an attack is an upper bound: it counts the images the attack failed to break, and a stronger attack might break more. History is full of defences that looked robust because they broke the attacker’s gradients rather than the attack itself (Athalye, Carlini & Wagner, 2018). Good practice: use many PGD steps and random restarts, check that accuracy falls to zero as ε grows, try gradient-free and transfer attacks, and design attacks that target the defence (Carlini et al., 2019).

A certificate goes the other way: a proof that no perturbation in the box changes the answer, so certified accuracy is a lower bound. The simplest is interval bound propagation (Gowal et al., 2018): push the whole box through the network as intervals, one layer at a time, and check that the true class’s logit still beats every other at its worst. The truth lies between the two curves. Move ε, then strengthen the attack and see how little the upper bound moves, a sign that PGD is not being fooled here.

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol z &= W\boldsymbol h + \boldsymbol b, \quad \boldsymbol h \in [\boldsymbol l, \boldsymbol u] \\ \boldsymbol\mu &= \tfrac12(\boldsymbol u + \boldsymbol l), \quad \boldsymbol r = \tfrac12(\boldsymbol u - \boldsymbol l) \\ \boldsymbol z &\in \big[\, W\boldsymbol\mu + \boldsymbol b - |W|\boldsymbol r,\;\; W\boldsymbol\mu + \boldsymbol b + |W|\boldsymbol r \,\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: if each input to a linear layer lies in an interval with centre μ and radius r, every output lies in an interval with centre Wμ + b and radius |W|r (|W| takes absolute values entry by entry). ReLU is monotone, so it maps an interval [l, u] to [max(l, 0), max(u, 0)]. Start from the box [x − ε, x + ε] clipped to [0, 1] and repeat.
Say it“The centre goes through W, the radius goes through the absolute value of W.”
\[ \underline{z_y - z_k} > 0 \;\; \text{for all } k \ne y \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{certified at } \epsilon \]
Read it as: the underline is a guaranteed lower bound over the whole box. We bound the differences zy − zk directly (one interval pass with the rows wy − wk), which is tighter than subtracting two separate intervals. For a linear model the bound is exact: the worst case is the margin minus ε‖wy − wk‖1, the linearity argument again. Other certificates use linear relaxations or randomised smoothing.
Say it“If the lower bound of z-y minus z-k is positive for every other class k, the image is certified.”

Real-world project

Project: attack and harden a Fashion-MNIST classifier

Fashion-MNIST (Xiao, Rasul & Vollgraf, 2017; MIT licence) holds 70,000 greyscale photos of clothing from Zalando’s catalogue in ten classes, whose names come from the dataset’s own label table. To train live in your browser, this page carries the first 3,000 training images and the first 1,000 test images of the official files, shrunk to 14×14 pixels with 16 grey levels and scaled to [0, 1]. The networks have one hidden layer of 64 ReLU units. All three start from the same weights and see the same mini-batches (50 images, SGD with momentum 0.9, learning rate 0.05, 15 epochs); the only difference is what they train on: clean images, an FGSM point with a random start (Wong, Rice & Kolter, 2020), or a 3-step PGD point, all with ε = 0.1.

import gzip, json, math, time, numpy as np
D = "x054_dl/"
def idx(f, off, shape):
    with gzip.open(D + f) as fh: return np.frombuffer(fh.read(), np.uint8, offset=off).reshape(shape)
def small(X):                                   # 28x28 -> 2x2 average pool -> 14x14, 16 grey levels, scaled to [0, 1]
    p = X.astype(float).reshape(-1, 14, 2, 14, 2).mean(axis=(2, 4))
    return np.rint(p / 255 * 15).reshape(len(X), 196) / 15
Xtr = small(idx("train-images-idx3-ubyte.gz", 16, (-1, 28, 28))[:3000]); ytr = idx("train-labels-idx1-ubyte.gz", 8, -1)[:3000].astype(int)
Xte = small(idx("t10k-images-idx3-ubyte.gz", 16, (-1, 28, 28))[:1000]); yte = idx("t10k-labels-idx1-ubyte.gz", 8, -1)[:1000].astype(int)

class Mulberry:                                 # the page's seeded generator, bit for bit
    def __init__(s, a): s.a = a & 0xFFFFFFFF
    def __call__(s):
        M = 0xFFFFFFFF; s.a = (s.a + 0x6D2B79F5) & M; a = s.a
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & M
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & M)) & M) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M) / 4294967296
def gauss(r):
    u = 0.0
    while u == 0: u = r()
    return math.sqrt(-2 * math.log(u)) * math.cos(2 * math.pi * r())

def init(seed=7, H=64):                         # He-normal first layer, 1/sqrt(H) second
    r = Mulberry(seed)
    W1 = np.array([[gauss(r) * math.sqrt(2 / 196) for i in range(196)] for j in range(H)])
    W2 = np.array([[gauss(r) * math.sqrt(1 / H) for j in range(H)] for k in range(10)])
    return dict(W1=W1, b1=np.zeros(H), W2=W2, b2=np.zeros(10))
def forward(P, X):
    A = X @ P["W1"].T + P["b1"]; Hh = np.maximum(A, 0); return A, Hh, Hh @ P["W2"].T + P["b2"]
def backward(P, X, y):                          # cross-entropy gradients w.r.t. logits and pre-activations
    A, Hh, Z = forward(P, X); E = np.exp(Z - Z.max(1, keepdims=True)); G = E / E.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    G[np.arange(len(X)), y] -= 1; return Hh, G, (G @ P["W2"]) * (A > 0)
def pgd(P, X, y, eps, alpha, steps, r=None):    # L-inf PGD; r = random start; FGSM is steps=1, alpha=eps
    Xa = X.copy() if r is None else np.clip(X + eps * (2 * np.array([[r() for _ in range(196)] for _ in X]) - 1), 0, 1)
    for _ in range(steps):
        Xa = np.clip(np.clip(Xa + alpha * np.sign(backward(P, Xa, y)[2] @ P["W1"]), X - eps, X + eps), 0, 1)
    return Xa
def train(mode, eps=0.1, K=3, epochs=15, lr=0.05, mom=0.9, bs=50):
    P = init(); V = {k: 0 * v for k, v in P.items()}; r = Mulberry(1007); n = len(Xtr)
    for ep in range(epochs):
        idx_ = list(range(n))
        for i in range(n - 1, 0, -1): j = int(r() * (i + 1)); idx_[i], idx_[j] = idx_[j], idx_[i]
        for s in range(0, n, bs):
            b = idx_[s:s + bs]; X, y = Xtr[b], ytr[b]
            if mode == "fgsm": X = pgd(P, X, y, eps, 1.25 * eps, 1, r)        # random start + one step
            if mode == "pgd":  X = pgd(P, X, y, eps, 2.5 * eps / K, K, r)    # random start + K steps
            Hh, G, dA = backward(P, X, y)
            g = dict(W1=dA.T @ X / len(b), b1=dA.mean(0), W2=G.T @ Hh / len(b), b2=G.mean(0))
            for k in P: V[k] = mom * V[k] - lr * g[k]; P[k] = P[k] + V[k]
    return P
acc = lambda P, X: (forward(P, X)[2].argmax(1) == yte).mean()
def certified(P, eps):                          # interval bound propagation: lower bound of z_y - z_k > 0 for all k
    l, u = np.clip(Xte - eps, 0, 1), np.clip(Xte + eps, 0, 1)
    mu, rad = forward(P, (l + u) / 2)[0], (np.abs(P["W1"]) @ ((u - l) / 2).T).T
    hl, hu = np.maximum(mu - rad, 0), np.maximum(mu + rad, 0); ok = np.ones(len(Xte), bool)
    for k in range(10):
        Wd = P["W2"][yte] - P["W2"][k]; bd = P["b2"][yte] - P["b2"][k]
        low = bd + (Wd * (hl + hu) / 2).sum(1) - (np.abs(Wd) * (hu - hl) / 2).sum(1)
        ok &= (yte == k) | (low > 0)
    return ok.mean()

M, out = {}, {}
for mode in ("std", "fgsm", "pgd"):
    t = time.time(); M[mode] = P = train(mode)
    strong = np.ones(1000, bool); r = Mulberry(99)

    print(f"{mode:5s} clean {row[0]:.3f}  FGSM {row[1]:.3f}  PGD-10 {row[2]:.3f}  PGD-50x4 {row[3]:.3f}  IBP@0.01 {row[4]:.3f}   ({time.time() - t:.0f} s)")

# ---- tangent distance: 1-NN over the 3,000 training images, re-ranking each query's 20 Euclidean neighbours
U, V = np.meshgrid(np.arange(14.0), np.arange(14.0)); c = 6.5
def sample(img, u, v):                          # bilinear interpolation, zero outside the frame
    u0, v0 = np.floor(u).astype(int), np.floor(v).astype(int); fu, fv = u - u0, v - v0
    px = lambda a, b: np.where((a >= 0) & (a < 14) & (b >= 0) & (b < 14), img[np.clip(b, 0, 13), np.clip(a, 0, 13)], 0)
    return (1-fu)*(1-fv)*px(u0, v0) + fu*(1-fv)*px(u0+1, v0) + (1-fu)*fv*px(u0, v0+1) + fu*fv*px(u0+1, v0+1)
def transform(x, kind, t):                      # 0 shift right, 1 shift down (pixels), 2 rotate (radians), 3 scale (1 + t)
    img, du, dv = x.reshape(14, 14), U - c, V - c
    if kind == 0: return sample(img, U - t, V).ravel()
    if kind == 1: return sample(img, U, V - t).ravel()
    if kind == 2: return sample(img, c + math.cos(t)*du + math.sin(t)*dv, c - math.sin(t)*du + math.cos(t)*dv).ravel()
    return sample(img, c + du / (1 + t), c + dv / (1 + t)).ravel()
g = np.exp(-0.5 * (np.arange(-2, 3) / 0.8) ** 2); g /= g.sum()
def tangents(x):                                # central differences of the transformed, Gaussian-smoothed image
    p = np.pad(x.reshape(14, 14), 2); s = sum(g[k] * p[:, k:k+14] for k in range(5)); s = sum(g[k] * s[k:k+14] for k in range(5)).ravel()
    return np.stack([(transform(s, k, 0.01) - transform(s, k, -0.01)) / 0.02 for k in range(4)], 1)
def tdist(x1, T1, x2, T2, lam=0.01):            # two-sided tangent distance with a small ridge on (a, b)
    Mx, r = np.hstack([T1, -T2]), x2 - x1; a = np.linalg.solve(Mx.T @ Mx + lam * np.eye(8), Mx.T @ r)
    return np.linalg.norm(r - Mx @ a)
Ttr = [tangents(x) for x in Xtr]; ref["td"] = {}
for name, Xq in (("as given", Xte), ("shifted 1 px", [transform(x, 0, 1) for x in Xte]),
                 ("rotated 10 deg", [transform(x, 2, math.radians(10)) for x in Xte]), ("scaled +10%", [transform(x, 3, 0.1) for x in Xte])):
    Xq = np.array(Xq); d2 = (Xq ** 2).sum(1)[:, None] - 2 * Xq @ Xtr.T + (Xtr ** 2).sum(1)
    top = np.argsort(d2, 1, kind="stable")[:, :20]
    Tq = [tangents(x) for x in Xq]
    td = [top[i][np.argmin([tdist(x, Tq[i], Xtr[j], Ttr[j]) for j in top[i]])] for i, x in enumerate(Xq)]
    ref["td"][name] = [(ytr[top[:, 0]] == yte).mean(), (ytr[td] == yte).mean()]
    print(f"1-NN, test images {name:15s} Euclidean {ref['td'][name][0]:.3f}   tangent distance {ref['td'][name][1]:.3f}")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • An adversarial example is a correctly classified input moved by at most ε per pixel (or in some other norm) so that the network gets it wrong; direction, not size, does the damage.
  • The linearity explanation: a push of ε aligned with the weights changes a linear unit by ε‖w‖1, which grows with the input dimension; nearly linear networks inherit the problem.
  • FGSM takes one step of ε·sign(∇xJ); PGD takes many smaller ones, projecting back into the box, and is the standard attack for evaluation.
  • Adversarial training minimises the worst-case loss in the box and buys robustness with some clean accuracy; virtual adversarial training does the same with the model’s own labels on unlabelled data.
  • Known invariances can be built in directly: tangent distance compares tangent planes, tangent prop penalises Jτ, the manifold tangent classifier learns τ from data; and robustness claims need strong attacks (upper bounds) and, ideally, certificates (lower bounds).

Check your understanding

Random ±0.1 noise barely hurts the standard network, but FGSM noise of exactly the same size ruins it. Why?

Each output is roughly a weighted sum of the pixels. Random signs make the contributions cancel, so the change grows only like √d; the FGSM pattern lines every contribution up with the gradient, so the change grows like d. Same size per pixel, very different total effect.

Why is FGSM’s step ε·sign(∇J) rather than ε·∇J/‖∇J‖2?

Because the budget is an L∞ box. To first order, the best point of the box is the corner matching the gradient’s signs, gaining ε‖∇J‖1; the normalised gradient step is optimal for an L2 ball and gains only ε‖∇J‖2.

A new defence reports 90% accuracy under FGSM at ε = 0.1. What would you check before believing it?

Run PGD with many steps and random restarts, check that accuracy falls towards zero as ε grows, try a gradient-free or transfer attack and an attack adapted to the defence. If FGSM is much weaker than PGD on it, or larger ε does not help the attacker, the gradients are probably masked.

How does tangent propagation differ from training on shifted copies of the images?

Both ask for invariance to shifts. Augmentation shows the network finite shifts and asks for the right label; tangent prop asks that the derivative of the output along the shift direction be zero at each point, exactly but only for infinitesimal shifts, and it needs no labels for the penalty.

Interval bounds certify 4% of images at ε = 0.1 while PGD leaves 48% correct. Which number is the true robust accuracy?

Neither, necessarily: the true value lies between them. 4% is proven (no perturbation can break those images); 48% is only what this attack failed to break. The gap is due both to loose interval bounds and, possibly, to attacks that miss something.
