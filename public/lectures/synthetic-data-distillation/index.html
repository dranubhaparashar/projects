Synthetic Data & Distillation

When the training set is written by a model: soft targets, self-made instructions, verifiers, and the collapse that waits at the end of the loop

Most of the text a language model learns from was written by people. More and more of what it is fine-tuned on was written by another model: instructions a model invented, answers a stronger model gave, solutions a checker approved, rationales that happened to reach the right answer. Done well, this is distillation: a small model inherits what a large one knows. Done carelessly, it is a photocopier pointed at its own output. The motive

Why synthesise data at all?

Chapter 4.4a treated training data as something you find: crawl it, filter it, deduplicate it. This chapter treats it as something you can make. The pressure to do so comes from four directions. The web is finite, and the high-quality part of it is small: estimates such as Villalobos et al. (2024) put the stock of public human text in the range of tens of trillions of tokens, which frontier training runs are already approaching. Human annotation is slow and expensive: a careful preference label or a worked solution costs minutes of expert time, and a fine-tuning set wants tens of thousands of them. Some data cannot be shared at all (medical notes, customer messages), so a generated stand-in with the same statistics is the only thing that leaves the building. And real data is distributed the way the world is: a Zipf curve with a fat head and a long tail of rare situations that matter (unusual languages, edge cases of a tool call, the question nobody asked yet), so even a huge corpus leaves the tail nearly empty. A generator can be aimed at the tail.

Every one of those gains has a shadow. A generator can only hand on what it knows, so the quality ceiling is the generator’s own, and its mistakes become labelled truth. Generated text is less diverse than people’s: the common phrasing becomes more common, the rare one disappears. Trained on its own output, a model drifts toward a narrower and narrower distribution, the model collapse of section 08. And data made by someone else’s model carries that model’s licence terms and its memorised benchmarks. The chapter is organised around that tension: how to get the gains (sections 02–07) and how to keep the shadow in check (sections 08–09).

Gain 1

Scarcity

Specialised domains, low-resource languages and new tasks have little text to learn from. A model that already speaks the language can write more of it.

Gain 2

Annotation cost

Labels, rationales, preference judgements and test cases cost expert minutes each. A model drafts them in milliseconds; people check a sample.

Gain 3

Privacy

When the real records cannot leave a hospital or a bank, a generator trained inside can release look-alike data that keeps the statistics and drops the people.

Gain 4

Targeted coverage

Real data follows a Zipf curve. Synthesis can be pointed at the thin tail: rare intents, hard arithmetic, long contexts, tool calls that fail.

Risk 1

The generator’s ceiling

A student learns the teacher’s errors as truth. Synthetic data cannot contain knowledge that no model and no checker had.

Risk 2

Homogenisation

Sampled text is smoother and more repetitive than human text; frequent phrasings get more frequent, rare ones vanish.

Risk 3

Collapse in the loop

Re-training on your own outputs, generation after generation, shrinks the variance and deletes the tails (section 08).

Risk 4

Provenance

Outputs of another provider’s model come with terms of use; the generator may also have memorised the very benchmarks you test on (section 09).

The tail is where synthesis earns its keep

Here is the coverage argument in numbers. Imagine K = 120 kinds of situation your model must handle, ranked by how often they occur in the wild. Natural data falls on them as a Zipf law, \(p_k \propto k^{-s}\): with s = 1 the hundredth kind is a hundred times rarer than the first. Synthetic data, generated from a list of the 120 kinds, lands on them uniformly. Say a kind is covered once it has at least m examples. Move the synthetic share and watch how many of the 120 kinds cross the line, and how many examples you would need to cover all of them with natural data alone.

Of N examples, a share 1 − f is natural and lands in proportion to pk; the share f is synthetic and is spread evenly, 1/K per kind. nk is the expected count for kind k and Cm counts the kinds with at least m examples. Traditional synthesis

Rules, templates and perturbation

Long before language models could write their own training sets, people built data by hand-written rule. The oldest tool is the template: a sentence with slots and a list of fillers for each slot. Three templates with five slots of six fillers each give thousands of distinct strings for the price of an afternoon, which is how early dialogue systems got their intent-classification data, how grade-school arithmetic benchmarks are stamped out, and how unit tests for a parser are generated. The catch is that combinatorial variety is not semantic variety: every output has the same skeleton, and a model trained on it learns the skeleton.

The second family perturbs real examples rather than inventing new ones. In vision this is cropping and flipping; for text, Wei and Zou’s EDA (2019) swaps, deletes, inserts and replaces words with synonyms, and back-translation (Sennrich, Haddow & Birch, 2016) runs a sentence through a translation model and back to get a paraphrase, or translates monolingual target-side text to manufacture parallel data (chapter 4.5b). Noise injection builds the noisy channel of chapter 4.0b in reverse: start from correct text, add typos by rule, and you have a spelling-correction corpus of any size. For numeric features, SMOTE (Chawla et al., 2002) interpolates between a minority-class point and one of its nearest neighbours to rebalance a classifier’s training set. And in robotics and vision, simulation renders scenes a camera never saw. The danger shared by all perturbations is the label flip: a random swap that moves a “not” changes the meaning but not the label on the file.

    The core idea

    Knowledge distillation: learn the teacher’s probabilities

    Buciluă, Caruana and Niculescu-Mizil (2006) wanted the accuracy of an ensemble of classifiers at the cost of one small network. Their trick was to let the ensemble label a very large set of unlabelled inputs (they had to synthesise the inputs, because they had no spare data) and train the small network to reproduce those labels. They called it model compression, and it worked because the labels were not the original noisy human labels but the ensemble’s smoothed judgement. Hinton, Vinyals and Dean (2015) sharpened the idea and gave it its name: distillation. The point is not only the teacher’s answer but its whole probability vector. When a digit classifier says an image is a 2 with probability 0.9, a 3 with 0.08 and a 7 with 0.01, the 0.08 and the 0.01 are information: this particular 2 looks a bit like a 3 and nothing like a 7. A hard label “2” throws that away. Hinton called the information in the small probabilities dark knowledge.

    Two details make it work. A well-trained teacher is confident, so at the usual softmax its wrong-class probabilities are tiny and barely influence the loss; dividing the logits by a temperature T > 1 before the softmax flattens the distribution and lets the small probabilities speak. Both teacher and student use the same T during training; the student is used at T = 1 afterwards. And because flattening shrinks the gradients by a factor of about 1/T², the distillation term is multiplied by T² so that its weight relative to the ordinary hard-label term does not depend on T. Their MNIST demonstration is still the cleanest: a student that had never seen a single 3 in its transfer set recognised 98.6% of the test threes once its bias for that class was corrected, because every other digit’s soft label had quietly told it what a 3 looks like.

    \[ p_i^{(T)} = \frac{\exp(v_i / T)}{\sum_j \exp(v_j / T)}, \qquad q_i^{(T)} = \frac{\exp(z_i / T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j / T)} \]
    Read it as: v are the teacher’s logits and z the student’s. Dividing by T before the softmax keeps the ranking of the classes but shrinks the gaps between probabilities: at T = 1 you get the ordinary softmax, as T grows every class drifts toward 1/K. p(T) is the soft target.
    Say it“Soft target i is e to the v-i over T, normalised over the classes.”
    \[ \begin{aligned} L &= \alpha\, T^2\, \mathrm{KL}\!\left(p^{(T)} \,\|\, q^{(T)}\right) + (1-\alpha)\, \mathrm{CE}\!\left(y, q^{(1)}\right) \\ \frac{\partial\, T^2\,\mathrm{KL}}{\partial z_i} &= T\left(q_i^{(T)} - p_i^{(T)}\right) \approx \frac{z_i - v_i}{K} \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: the first term pulls the student’s softened distribution toward the teacher’s (KL(p‖q) = Σ pi log(pi/qi); minimising it in q is the same as minimising the cross-entropy −Σ pi log qi). The second is the usual cross-entropy with the true label y when one is available; α weights the two. The gradient line is why T² is there: the raw gradient of KL is (qi − pi)/T, and for large T, with zero-mean logits, qi − pi ≈ (zi − vi)/(KT). Multiplying by T² cancels the 1/T² and turns distillation, in the high-temperature limit, into plain logit matching.
    Say it“Loss is alpha T-squared KL of teacher-soft from student-soft, plus one minus alpha times the hard-label cross-entropy.”

    Distilling a generator

    Sequence-level or token-level: what does the student copy?

    A classifier’s knowledge is one probability vector per input. A language model’s knowledge is a distribution over sequences, and there are two very different places to attach the distillation loss. Token-level (Kim and Rush’s “word-level”) distillation takes a training sequence, and at every position matches the student’s next-token distribution to the teacher’s with a KL term over the whole vocabulary: the classifier recipe applied once per token. It needs the teacher’s logits and a shared tokeniser. Sequence-level distillation (Kim & Rush, 2016) runs the teacher on each input, usually with beam search, and trains the student with plain cross-entropy on the teacher’s output as if it were the reference. The student then learns the teacher’s mode rather than its full distribution. For translation this worked remarkably well: their student ran ten times faster than the teacher with little loss, and beat the same architecture trained on the human references by 4.2 BLEU with greedy decoding and 1.7 with beam search. The teacher’s outputs are simpler and more consistent than human translations, so they are easier to learn.

    Nearly everything called “distillation” in the LLM era is the sequence-level kind: Alpaca learned from text-davinci-003’s answers, Vicuna from shared ChatGPT conversations, Orca from GPT-4’s explanations, and the small “reasoning” models of 2025 from a large model’s solutions. No logits change hands; the student imitates text. Two refinements matter. The forward KL(pteacher ‖ q) that token-level distillation minimises is mode-covering: the student is punished for assigning low probability anywhere the teacher puts mass, so a small student smears itself over outputs it cannot all produce well. Reverse KL(q ‖ pteacher) is mode-seeking and lets a small student commit to the part of the teacher it can imitate (MiniLLM, Gu et al., 2024). And distillation on the teacher’s or the dataset’s sequences trains the student on contexts it will never see at inference, where it conditions on its own earlier tokens; on-policy distillation (GKD, Agarwal et al., 2024) samples from the student and asks the teacher to score each step, which closes that gap.

      Reasoning traces: distilling a chain of thought

      The newest and most consequential form of sequence-level distillation copies not answers but reasoning. A large model is asked to think step by step; its traces, kept only when the final answer checks out, become the fine-tuning set of a small model. Hsieh et al. (2023) showed that training a small model to produce the teacher’s rationale as well as its label (“distilling step-by-step”) beats both standard fine-tuning and standard distillation with far less data. Ho et al. (2023) and Magister et al. (2023) did the same for arithmetic and commonsense reasoning. Orca (Mukherjee et al., 2023) fine-tuned a 13B model on millions of GPT-4 explanations. And DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025) released a family of 1.5B–70B models fine-tuned on about 800,000 samples written by the big reasoning model, reporting that this plain distillation gave the small models better reasoning scores than running the reinforcement-learning recipe on them directly. The lesson is consistent with Kim and Rush: a teacher’s traces are cleaner and more learnable than anything a small model can discover for itself.

      Two cautions. Gudibande et al. (2023) fine-tuned open models on large amounts of ChatGPT output and found that imitation closed the gap in style, enough to fool human raters on casual prompts, while the gap in factual knowledge and hard reasoning stayed where it was; what transfers cheaply is the format of a good answer, not the knowledge behind it. And a trace that reaches the right answer is not necessarily a correct trace; filtering by final answer admits lucky nonsense, which is where section 06’s verifiers come in.

      AI-powered synthesis

      Self-Instruct and evolution: a model writes its own curriculum

      Instruction tuning (chapter 4.6) needs thousands of (instruction, response) pairs, and until 2022 someone had to write them. Self-Instruct (Wang et al., 2023) showed that a pretrained model could write most of them itself. The loop starts from 175 seed tasks, each a hand-written instruction with one example. In every round, eight tasks are sampled from the pool (six human-written, two already generated) and placed in a prompt that asks for more; the model continues the list. For each new instruction it is then asked whether the task is a classification task, and to produce an instance: input then output for most tasks, output first for classification tasks so that the labels come out balanced instead of all being the first class. The result is filtered before it rejoins the pool: an instruction is dropped if its ROUGE-L similarity to any existing instruction is 0.7 or more, if it mentions things a text model cannot do (images, pictures, graphs), if it is very short or very long, and if its instance duplicates one already present. Run from GPT-3 for a few thousand dollars of API calls, this produced 52,000 instructions and 82,000 instances; GPT-3 fine-tuned on them gained 33 points on SuperNaturalInstructions and landed close to the human-supervised InstructGPT-001.

      Alpaca (Taori et al., 2023) ran a simplified version against text-davinci-003 (twenty instructions per request, instance and instruction in one go, no classification split) for under $500, and fine-tuned LLaMA-7B on the 52,000 pairs in three hours on eight A100s; in a blind pairwise comparison the small model won about as often as it lost against the much larger generator. Evol-Instruct (Xu et al., 2024) replaced “write more like these” with “make this one harder”: each instruction is evolved by a prompt that adds a constraint, deepens the question, replaces a general idea with a specific one, asks for more reasoning steps, or complicates the input (in-depth), or that mutates it into a rarer topic of the same difficulty (in-breadth); evolutions that fail (no new information, a copy of the input, an apology) are deleted, and four rounds from Alpaca’s set gave the 250,000 instructions behind WizardLM. The same machinery produces AI feedback as well as data: Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) has a model critique and revise its own answers against written principles, then label preference pairs in place of human raters (RLAIF; see chapter 4.7).

Self-Instruct used τ = 0.7. The score is 1 for identical word sequences and 0 when no word is shared.

        Verifying and filtering

        Generate many, keep what passes: rejection sampling, verifiers and bootstrapping

        A generator that is right 30% of the time is a poor oracle but a fine proposer, provided something can tell the 30% from the 70%. That something is a verifier, and synthetic-data pipelines are organised around how good a verifier is available. The best are exact: a compiler, a unit test, a symbolic algebra check, an answer key, a schema validator, an interpreter that runs the generated program against the generated test. One step down are learned verifiers: a reward model (chapter 4.7) or a language model acting as judge (chapter 4.10), which are cheap, broad and wrong in correlated ways. Weaker still is consistency: sample many answers and keep the majority (chapter 4.8), which works when errors are scattered and fails when they are systematic. The procedure itself is called rejection sampling: draw k candidates per prompt, keep the ones that pass. Yuan et al. (2023) fine-tuned on verified solutions this way (rejection sampling fine-tuning) and the Llama 2 and Llama 3 post-training recipes both include a rejection-sampling stage in which the model’s own best-of-k outputs, chosen by a reward model or by execution, become its next training set.

        The arithmetic is simple and worth knowing. If a problem is solved with probability p per attempt, k independent attempts give at least one pass with probability 1 − (1 − p)k, and k p accepted samples on average, so the cost per accepted sample is 1/p generator calls regardless of k. Three things spoil it. Easy problems pass more often, so a filtered set is skewed toward the easy unless you cap the accepted samples per problem or weight the hard ones. A verifier that accepts wrong answers with probability ε contaminates the kept set with a wrong share of (1 − p)ε / (p + (1 − p)ε), which for hard problems (small p) can be most of it. And whatever the verifier rewards is what the kept data over-represents: a judge that likes long answers yields a training set of long answers (Goodhart’s law, again).

        \[ \Pr(\text{at least one pass}) = 1 - (1-p)^k, \qquad \mathbb E[\text{accepted}] = kp, \qquad \text{wrong share of accepted} = \frac{(1-p)\,\varepsilon}{p + (1-p)\,\varepsilon} \]
        Read it as: p is the generator’s pass rate on this problem, k the number of attempts, ε the verifier’s false-accept rate (how often a wrong answer is waved through). The first formula is the yield per problem; the second the yield per generator call times k; the third the fraction of the kept set that is wrong once the verifier is imperfect.
        Say it“One minus one-minus-p to the k; k times p accepted; wrong share is false accepts over true plus false accepts.”

        B · Bootstrapping: STaR and the self-improvement loop

        Rejection sampling becomes a loop when the kept samples are used to retrain the generator and the generator then proposes again. STaR, the Self-Taught Reasoner (Zelikman, Wu, Mu & Goodman, 2022), is the clean version for reasoning. Prompt the model with a few worked rationales; let it write a rationale and answer for every training question; keep the rationales whose answers are right; for the questions it got wrong, show it the correct answer and ask it to write a rationale that leads there (rationalisation), and keep those too; fine-tune the original model on everything kept; repeat. Each iteration starts from the base model to avoid piling up the previous round’s quirks. On arithmetic and CommonsenseQA the loop lifted a 6B model to the neighbourhood of a model thirty times larger. The same shape appears as expert iteration in game playing (Anthony, Tian & Barber, 2017; AlphaZero’s self-play is the extreme case, with a perfect verifier: the rules of the game), as ReST and ReSTEM for language models (Gulcehre et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2024), and as self-rewarding models that judge their own samples (Yuan et al., 2024).

        The loop has a natural ceiling: it can only learn from problems it already solves sometimes. Rationalisation moves the ceiling, because a hint turns an unsolvable problem into a solvable one, but it also admits more post-hoc rationales that reach the answer for the wrong reason.         Synthetic pretraining data

        Textbooks and code: synthetic data before fine-tuning

        Fine-tuning sets are small enough that a model can write them. Could a model write a pretraining corpus? Two 2023 papers from Microsoft Research made the case. TinyStories (Eldan & Li, 2023) had GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 write millions of short stories using only the vocabulary a four-year-old knows, each prompt seeded with three random words and a required feature (a dialogue, a twist, a moral) to keep the stories varied. Models with under ten million parameters trained on it wrote fluent, grammatical, mostly consistent English, which models of that size trained on the web never do: the lesson was that simpler data lets a small model learn the structure of language that noisy web text buries. phi-1 (Gunasekar et al., 2023, “Textbooks Are All You Need”) applied the idea to code. Its 1.3-billion-parameter model was pretrained on about seven billion tokens: six billion of web code filtered by a GPT-4-labelled quality classifier for “educational value”, and under one billion of synthetic Python textbooks written by GPT-3.5 with random topic words injected for diversity. It was then fine-tuned on 180 million tokens of synthetic exercises with solutions. The result, 50.6% pass@1 on HumanEval, beat models ten to a hundred times larger trained on a hundred times more code; without the synthetic exercises the same pretrained model scored 29%, and a 350M version scored 45%.

        The evidence is real and so are the caveats. First, the benchmark is narrow: HumanEval is 164 short Python functions, and the exercise set was built to resemble exactly that kind of problem. The authors checked for leakage with n-gram overlap and an embedding-and-syntax similarity search, pruned the exercises that resembled test items most closely, retrained, and found the gains largely intact; critics have still argued that the phi family is tuned toward its benchmarks in ways general web-trained models are not. Second, the generator sets the ceiling: the textbooks know what GPT-3.5 knew in 2023, phrased the way it phrases things. Third, the diversity had to be engineered; left alone, a model asked for “a textbook chapter on lists” a thousand times writes a thousand near-copies, so the random-word injection of TinyStories and phi-1, and the topic × audience × style seeding of Hugging Face’s open replication Cosmopedia (25 billion tokens, 2024), are doing load-bearing work. Fourth, cost: a billion tokens through a commercial API is not cheap, and the pipeline is only as open as the generator’s terms of use allow (section 09). Since then synthetic pretraining and mid-training data has become routine in a supporting role: Llama 3’s post-training used generated code checked by execution, translated between languages and paired with generated tests; NVIDIA’s Nemotron-4 340B shipped with an openly licensed pipeline in which over 98% of the alignment data was synthetic; Magicoder’s OSS-Instruct (Wei et al., 2024) seeded code instructions from random open-source snippets to inherit the diversity of real code. Whether a frontier model can be pretrained mostly on model-written text without the degradation of section 08 is, at the time of writing, an open question that each laboratory answers behind closed doors.

        The loop closes

        Model collapse: what recursive training does to a distribution

        Suppose generation after generation of a model is trained on text written by the generation before. Shumailov et al. (2024) named what happens model collapse and showed it in Gaussian mixtures, variational autoencoders and a language model (OPT-125M fine-tuned on WikiText-2, each generation trained on the previous one’s samples): the tails of the distribution go first, as rare events that a finite sample happened to miss are forgotten forever (early collapse); then the distribution contracts toward something narrow and only loosely related to the original, and perplexity on real text climbs generation by generation (late collapse). They trace it to three sources of error that compound: statistical approximation error, because a finite sample under-represents the tails; functional expressivity error, because the model cannot represent the true distribution; and functional approximation error, because learning itself is imperfect. The first is enough on its own, and it is the one you can watch below. Alemohammad et al. (2024) found the same “MAD” behaviour in image generators, and Dohmatob et al. (2024) worked out how collapse enters the scaling laws.

        Is it inevitable? Not if real data stays in the loop. Gerstgrasser et al. (2024) showed, in theory and in experiments across language models, diffusion models and VAEs, that the collapse comes from replacing the data each generation; if generated data accumulates alongside the original, the test error stays bounded. Bertrand et al. (2024) proved stability of the iterative-retraining fixed point when the share of real data is large enough and the model fits well. Section 06’s verifiers are a mitigation too, but a double-edged one: keeping only the samples that pass a check removes garbage and also trims the tails, so a verified synthetic corpus is narrower than its generator. For the field as a whole the question is what happens to the web itself, now that a growing share of it is model-written; archived pre-2022 crawls have become a reserve of guaranteed-human text.

        \[ \begin{aligned} x_1, \dots, x_n &\sim \mathcal N(\mu_g, \sigma_g^2) \\ \mu_{g+1} = \bar x, \quad \sigma_{g+1}^2 &= \tfrac1n \textstyle\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 \\ \mathbb E\!\left[\sigma_{g+1}^2 \mid \sigma_g\right] &= \left(1 - \tfrac1n\right)\sigma_g^2 \end{aligned} \]
        Read it as: generation g is a Gaussian with parameters (μg, σg); generation g + 1 fits the maximum-likelihood mean and variance to n of its samples. The fitted variance is biased low by the factor 1 − 1/n, so the variance shrinks by about that much per generation in expectation, and it is also random: the sample variance of n Gaussians has a relative standard deviation of about √(2/n). Even with the unbiased estimator the variance performs a random walk in log scale whose only absorbing state is zero. The mean drifts as a random walk with step σg/√n.
        Say it“Each generation re-fits a Gaussian to n samples of the last; the expected variance shrinks by one minus one over n.”
        \[ \begin{aligned} \hat p_{g+1}(k) &= \frac{c_k}{n}, \qquad (c_1, \dots, c_K) \sim \mathrm{Multinomial}\big(n, \hat p_g\big) \\ \Pr\big(\hat p_{g+1}(k) = 0\big) &= \big(1 - \hat p_g(k)\big)^n \end{aligned} \]
        Read it as: for a categorical distribution over K kinds (word types, topics, answer styles), each generation is the empirical frequency of n samples from the last. The estimate is unbiased, but a kind that draws zero samples has probability zero in the next generation and, with pure replacement, can never come back. The second line is the chance of that happening in one step; for a kind with 5% mass and n = 10 it is 0.9510 = 0.60. Mixing in a share f of real samples each generation makes every kind re-appear with its real frequency.

        Contamination, licences and detecting synthetic text

        Three practical questions follow every synthetic dataset. Did the generator leak the test? A large model has read the public benchmarks, so when it writes “new” exercises it can reproduce test items from memory, and a student trained on them scores well for the wrong reason. The defences are the ones from chapter 4.4a applied to the synthetic set: n-gram overlap against every benchmark you will report, embedding similarity for paraphrased leaks, and, best of all, evaluation sets built after the data was made or kept private. Whose data is it? The terms of use of the main commercial APIs forbid using outputs to build models that compete with the provider; that is why Alpaca was released for research only, and why a model trained on another model’s text inherits two sets of conditions, its own base model’s licence and the generator’s terms. Open-weight licences often attach attribution or naming requirements to derivatives, and NVIDIA’s Nemotron-4 340B release was notable precisely because its licence allowed synthetic data generation for any purpose. The practical rule is to record, for every synthetic set, the generator, its version, the prompts, the filters and the applicable terms, which is the datasheet discipline of chapter 4.4a with two extra rows.

        Can model-written text be recognised? Three families of detector exist, none perfect. Zero-shot detectors use the generator’s own statistics: model text sits at a local maximum of log-probability under the model (DetectGPT, Mitchell et al., 2023, perturbs the text and checks whether the probability drops), or has a low ratio of perplexity to the perplexity another model expected (Binoculars, Hans et al., 2024). Trained classifiers are brittle across generators and domains and have been shown to flag non-native English writers at alarming rates (Liang et al., 2023). Watermarks are the only method that works by design: Kirchenbauer et al. (2023) partition the vocabulary at every step into a “green” list chosen by hashing the previous token and add a small bonus δ to the green logits; a text with many more green tokens than chance is flagged by a one-sided z-test that needs no access to the model. Google’s SynthID-Text (Dathathri et al., 2024, deployed in Gemini) is a production variant. Watermarks survive light editing, degrade under paraphrase, and cannot be imposed on open-weight models that anyone can run unwatermarked. Try the toy watermark below: the generator is a small random bigram language model, and the detection statistic is the real one.

        \[ G_t = \{\, v : h(x_{t-1}, v) < \gamma \,\}, \qquad \tilde\ell_t(v) = \ell_t(v) + \delta\,\mathbb 1[v \in G_t], \qquad z = \frac{|s|_G - \gamma T}{\sqrt{T\,\gamma\,(1-\gamma)}} \]
        Read it as: at step t, a hash of the previous token xt−1 selects a share γ of the vocabulary as the green list Gt; the generator adds δ to those tokens’ logits before sampling. A detector that knows the hash counts green tokens |s|G in a text of T tokens. Unwatermarked text is green about γT times, so z is approximately standard normal under “no watermark”; z = 4 corresponds to a false-positive rate of about 3 in 100,000. The watermark is only detectable where the model had entropy to spare: at steps where one token has nearly all the probability, δ changes nothing. One practical detail, used below: a repeated (previous token, token) pair is always green or always red, so the counts are not independent; the paper’s remedy is to score each distinct pair once, with T the number of distinct pairs.

        Everything in this project runs in the browser from a seeded generator, so it opens the same way every time and nothing is downloaded.         The distillation experiment in Python

        The listing below is complete and needs only numpy. It re-implements the page’s random-number generator bit for bit, so its data, initialisations and teacher are the ones in your tab at the default settings (floating-point summation order differs, so late-training numbers can drift in the last digits). It then repeats the whole experiment over twenty seeds, which the page does not, and sweeps the temperature.

        import numpy as np

# ---------- the page's random-number generator, bit for bit ----------
def mulberry32(a):
    def nxt():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return nxt

def gauss(r):                                   # Box-Muller, same call order as the page
    u = 0.0
    while u == 0.0: u = r()
    return np.sqrt(-2 * np.log(u)) * np.cos(2 * np.pi * r())

# ---------- the task: three classes, each a pair of Gaussian blobs ----------
CENTRES = [[(-1.6, 1.0), (1.6, -1.0)],        # class 0
           [(-1.6, -1.0), (1.6, 1.0)],        # class 1
           [(0.0, 1.7), (0.0, -1.7)]]         # class 2
SD = 0.55

def sample(n, seed):
    r = mulberry32(seed); X = np.zeros((n, 2)); y = np.zeros(n, dtype=int)
    for i in range(n):
        c = int(r() * 3); b = int(r() * 2); cx, cy = CENTRES[c][b]
        X[i] = (cx + SD * gauss(r), cy + SD * gauss(r)); y[i] = c
    return X, y

def bayes(X):                                   # the best possible classifier: the true posterior's argmax
    d = np.zeros((len(X), 3))
    for c in range(3):
        for cx, cy in CENTRES[c]:
            d[:, c] += np.exp(-((X[:, 0] - cx) ** 2 + (X[:, 1] - cy) ** 2) / (2 * SD * SD))
    return d.argmax(1)

# ---------- a tiny MLP with tanh units, trained by full-batch Adam ----------
def init(sizes, seed):                          # Glorot-uniform weights, zero biases
    r = mulberry32(seed); W = []
    for a, b in zip(sizes[:-1], sizes[1:]):
        lim = np.sqrt(6 / (a + b))
        W.append([np.array([[(2 * r() - 1) * lim for _ in range(b)] for _ in range(a)]), np.zeros(b)])
    return W

def forward(W, X):
    hs = [X]
    for i, (w, b) in enumerate(W):
        z = hs[-1] @ w + b
        hs.append(np.tanh(z) if i < len(W) - 1 else z)
    return hs                                   # hs[-1] are the logits

def softmax(z, T=1.0):
    z = z / T; z = z - z.max(1, keepdims=True); e = np.exp(z); return e / e.sum(1, keepdims=True)

def train(W, X, target, kind, T=1.0, epochs=400, lr=0.02):
    """kind 'hard': target = integer labels, cross-entropy.
       kind 'soft': target = teacher logits, loss = T^2 * KL(softmax(t/T) || softmax(s/T)).
       Returns the loss per epoch."""
    m = [[np.zeros_like(w), np.zeros_like(b)] for w, b in W]
    v = [[np.zeros_like(w), np.zeros_like(b)] for w, b in W]
    b1, b2, eps = 0.9, 0.999, 1e-8; n = len(X); hist = []
    for ep in range(1, epochs + 1):
        hs = forward(W, X); s = hs[-1]
        if kind == 'hard':
            q = softmax(s); loss = -np.log(q[np.arange(n), target] + 1e-12).mean()
            g = q.copy(); g[np.arange(n), target] -= 1; g /= n                    # dL/dlogits
        else:
            p = softmax(target, T); q = softmax(s, T)
            loss = T * T * (p * (np.log(p + 1e-12) - np.log(q + 1e-12))).sum(1).mean()
            g = T * (q - p) / n                                                   # T^2 * (q-p)/T, per example
        hist.append(loss)
        grads = []
        for i in range(len(W) - 1, -1, -1):                                       # backpropagation
            grads.append([hs[i].T @ g, g.sum(0)])
            if i > 0: g = (g @ W[i][0].T) * (1 - hs[i] ** 2)
        grads.reverse()
        for i in range(len(W)):                                                   # Adam
            for j in range(2):
                m[i][j] = b1 * m[i][j] + (1 - b1) * grads[i][j]
                v[i][j] = b2 * v[i][j] + (1 - b2) * grads[i][j] ** 2
                W[i][j] -= lr * (m[i][j] / (1 - b1 ** ep)) / (np.sqrt(v[i][j] / (1 - b2 ** ep)) + eps)
    return hist

acc = lambda W, X, y: (forward(W, X)[-1].argmax(1) == y).mean()
def kl_to_teacher(teacher, student, X):
    p = softmax(forward(teacher, X)[-1]); q = softmax(forward(student, X)[-1])
    return (p * (np.log(p + 1e-12) - np.log(q + 1e-12))).sum(1).mean()

        Wrap-up

        Five lines to remember

        • Synthetic data buys coverage, scale, privacy and cheap labels, and pays for them with the generator’s ceiling, less diversity, inherited terms of use and, in a closed loop, collapse. Rules and perturbations are the oldest generators and still the most controllable; their weakness is semantic sameness and the label flip.
        • Distillation trains a student on a teacher’s probabilities, not its labels. Divide the logits by a temperature T to expose the dark knowledge in the small probabilities, multiply the KL term by T² so its gradient keeps its scale, and mix in the hard-label cross-entropy when true labels exist. In the high-T limit it is logit matching.
        • For generators, token-level distillation matches next-token distributions along given sequences and is mode-covering; sequence-level distillation trains on the teacher’s decoded outputs and is mode-seeking, simpler, and what nearly all LLM “distillation” (including reasoning traces) actually is. Imitation copies style more readily than knowledge.
        • AI-powered synthesis is a pipeline, not a prompt: seeds, generation, instance creation, and filters (ROUGE-L near-duplicates, rules, verifiers). Rejection sampling yields 1 − (1 − p)k per problem at a cost of 1/p calls per keeper, skews toward easy problems, and inherits every false accept of its verifier; STaR-style loops can only climb as far as their hints reach.
        • A distribution re-estimated from its own samples loses its tails first and its variance eventually: expected variance shrinks by (1 − 1/n) per generation and a kind with mass p dies in one step with probability (1 − p)n. Keep real data in every generation, accumulate rather than replace, record provenance, and treat detection and watermarking as aids, not guarantees.

        Check your understanding

        Why multiply the distillation term by T², and what would go wrong without it?

        The gradient of the soft-target KL with respect to a student logit is (qi − pi)/T, and for large T the difference qi − pi itself shrinks like 1/T, so the whole gradient scales as 1/T². Without the factor, raising T would silently turn the soft term off relative to the hard-label cross-entropy, and the best α would change every time you changed T. With it, the soft term’s gradient tends to (zi − vi)/K: direct logit matching.

        In the project, the student taught at T = 1 did as well as or better than the one at T = 4. Does that contradict Hinton, Vinyals and Dean?

        No. Their teacher was trained to near-zero loss on MNIST, so its T = 1 outputs were almost one-hot and a higher temperature was needed to reveal the inter-class similarities. The teacher here faces genuinely overlapping classes and never becomes that confident; its T = 1 probabilities already approximate the posterior, which is the information a hard label lacks. Raising T then flattens targets that were informative to begin with. Temperature is a knob for over-confident teachers, not a universal improvement.

        Sequence-level distillation trains the student on the teacher’s beam-search output. In what sense is that a KL, and between which distributions?

        Cross-entropy on the teacher’s mode is the KL from a point mass at ŷ = argmax pT(y|x) to the student. It approximates matching the teacher’s sequence distribution by collapsing it onto its most likely member, which is mode-seeking: the student learns a simplified, lower-entropy version of the teacher, which is exactly what makes a small student able to learn it.

        A pipeline keeps every candidate whose ROUGE-L against the pool is below 0.7. What does lowering the threshold to 0.4 trade for what?

        Fewer paraphrases and more variety in the pool, at the price of rejecting many genuinely different instructions that happen to share a frame (“Write a … about …”), so the pool grows slowly and drifts toward unusual phrasings. The toy pipeline shows the acceptance rate collapsing as τ falls while the pool stays small.

        Why does rejection sampling tend to produce a training set of easy problems, and what are two fixes?

        With k attempts a problem passes at least once with probability 1 − (1 − p)k and contributes kp accepted samples on average; both grow with p, so easy problems contribute more samples and are more likely to appear at all. Fixes: cap the accepted samples per problem (one or a few), or weight problems by difficulty or by 1/p so that the hard ones count as much as the easy ones.

        In the categorical collapse simulation, why can a kind never return once it has probability zero, and what breaks that?

        Under pure replacement each generation samples from the previous estimate only; a kind with estimated probability zero is never drawn, so its next estimate is zero again. Mixing a share f of real samples into every generation restores a positive probability of drawing it, and accumulating data instead of replacing it keeps the original samples in the fit forever.

        A watermark detector sees T = 100 tokens, γ = 0.5, and 68 green tokens. Is the text flagged at z > 4?

        z = (68 − 50)/√(100 · 0.5 · 0.5) = 18/5 = 3.6, so not at the z = 4 threshold, though it would be at z = 3. Longer texts help: at the same green rate, 200 tokens give z = 36/√50 = 5.1. Low-entropy passages (code boilerplate, quotations) carry almost no watermark because δ cannot change a near-certain token.
