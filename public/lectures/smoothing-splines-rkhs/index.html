Smoothing Splines & Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces

Put a price on wiggles, and the best curve picks itself

The last chapter made you choose knots: how many, and where. Here is a cleaner deal. Put a knot at every data point, so nothing is decided in advance, and then charge the curve for how much it bends. One dial, λ, sets the price. At zero the curve threads every point; at infinity it is a straight line; in between it finds the shape the data really has. The same idea, written in the language of kernels, gives kernel ridge regression, kernel logistic regression and the support vector machine. On the right, drag any dot, and turn the dial.

The idea

A price on wiggles

Fit a curve by least squares alone and you get nonsense: infinitely many curves pass through every point, so the residual sum of squares can be made zero in countless silly ways. We need to say which curves we prefer. A roughness penalty does that. Measure how much a curve bends by the area under its squared second derivative, and add that, times a price λ, to the misfit. A straight line has f″ = 0 everywhere, so it is free; a curve that whips up and down between points pays heavily. Slide λ and watch the two costs trade against each other.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{PRSS}(f,\lambda) &= \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N} \big(y_i - f(x_i)\big)^2 \\ &\quad + \lambda \int \big(f''(t)\big)^2\,dt \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the penalised residual sum of squares. The first term rewards closeness to the data; the second charges for curvature. λ = 0 lets any interpolating curve win; as λ → ∞, only curves with f″ = 0, straight lines, can afford to compete, and the winner is the least-squares line. We minimise over every function with two derivatives, not over a fixed family.
Say it“P-R-S-S of f and lambda is the sum of squared residuals plus lambda times the integral of f-double-prime squared.”

The Bayesian reading. Penalties are prior beliefs in disguise. Suppose the noise is Gaussian with variance σ², and before seeing data we believe smooth curves are more likely than rough ones, with prior density proportional to exp(−λJ(f)/2σ²). Then the log-posterior is, up to a constant, minus PRSS over 2σ², and minimising PRSS finds the most probable curve after seeing the data. λ says how firmly you believed in smoothness. The prior is Gaussian: a wiggly direction with roughness d gets variance σ²/(λd), so the wilder the wiggle, the less the prior allows it, while straight lines cost nothing and are left completely free. The posterior is Gaussian too, centred on the penalised fit. Press the button to draw curves from it: with a small λ they are as jumpy as the data, with a large one they are nearly straight.

\[ \begin{aligned} -\log p(f \mid \mathbf{y}) &= \tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\mathrm{RSS}(f) \\ &\quad + \tfrac{\lambda}{2\sigma^2}J(f) + c \\ &= \tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\,\mathrm{PRSS}(f,\lambda) + c \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: RSS(f) is the misfit Σ(yi − f(xi))² and c a constant. Gaussian likelihood times a prior p(f) ∝ exp(−λJ(f)/2σ²). Taking minus the log turns the product into a sum: the likelihood gives the RSS term, the prior gives the penalty. So the penalised fit is the posterior mode. λ/σ² plays the role of the prior's precision on roughness.
Say it“Minus log posterior equals RSS plus lambda J, over two sigma squared, plus a constant.”
The solution

The answer is a natural cubic spline

The search runs over an infinite-dimensional space of curves, yet the winner has a finite, explicit form: a natural cubic spline with a knot at every distinct xi. Here is why. Take any smooth curve and note its values at the data points. The natural cubic spline through those same values has exactly the same RSS, and it never bends more: among all curves through given points, it has the smallest ∫f″². So whatever the winner is, swapping it for that spline cannot hurt, and the winner must be the spline. Because it is natural, f″ falls to zero at the outermost knots and the curve carries on as a straight line beyond them. Hover over the plot to see the cubic piece under the pointer, and watch the curvature strip below it.

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x) &= \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{N} N_j(x)\,\theta_j \\ \mathrm{PRSS}(\theta,\lambda) &= (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{N}\theta)^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{N}\theta) \\ &\quad + \lambda\,\theta^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_N\theta \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: write the spline in a basis of N natural-spline functions Nj. The matrix N holds Nj(xi), and ΩN holds ∫Nj″Nk″, so the roughness of the curve is the quadratic form θTΩNθ. The infinite problem has become a ridge regression with N coefficients.
Say it“f is the sum of N-j of x times theta-j; the criterion is y minus N theta, squared, plus lambda theta-transpose Omega theta.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\theta &= \big(\mathbf{N}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{N} + \lambda\boldsymbol{\Omega}_N\big)^{-1}\mathbf{N}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{y} \\ \hat f(x) &= \textstyle\sum_j N_j(x)\,\hat\theta_j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: set the gradient to zero, exactly as for ridge regression, but the penalty matrix is ΩN instead of the identity. It does not shrink coefficients towards zero; it shrinks the curve towards a straight line, because lines have zero roughness.
Say it“Theta-hat is N-transpose N plus lambda Omega, inverted, times N-transpose y.”

Linear smoother

Smoother matrices and degrees of freedom

The fitted values are a fixed matrix times y: ŷ = Sλy. For a fixed λ, the smoothing spline is a linear smoother, just as a regression spline with fixed knots is a projection ŷ = Hξy. But the two matrices behave differently. A projection is idempotent (HH = H): it keeps some directions entirely and kills the rest. Sλ instead shrinks: applying it twice smooths more. Counting parameters no longer works, since there are N coefficients but heavy shrinkage. The natural replacement is the trace. With the ESL test bed below (N = 100 points from a wiggly curve with noise of standard deviation 1), slide the degrees of freedom and click a bar to see the direction it belongs to.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{f}} &= \mathbf{S}_\lambda\mathbf{y} \\ \mathbf{S}_\lambda &= \mathbf{N}\big(\mathbf{N}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{N}+\lambda\boldsymbol{\Omega}_N\big)^{-1}\mathbf{N}^{\mathsf T} \\ \mathrm{df}_\lambda &= \operatorname{trace}(\mathbf{S}_\lambda) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Sλ is symmetric and positive semi-definite, and its eigenvalues lie between 0 and 1. Its trace, the sum of those eigenvalues, is the effective degrees of freedom. For a projection onto M basis functions the same trace gives exactly M, so the definition agrees with ordinary counting where counting makes sense.
Say it“f-hat is S-lambda times y, and the degrees of freedom are the trace of S-lambda.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{S}_\lambda &= (\mathbf{I}+\lambda\mathbf{K})^{-1} \\ &= \textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\rho_k(\lambda)\,\mathbf{u}_k\mathbf{u}_k^{\mathsf T} \\ \rho_k(\lambda) &= \dfrac{1}{1+\lambda d_k} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the Reinsch form. K, the penalty matrix, does not depend on λ, so neither do the eigenvectors uk. With the dk in increasing order, uk wiggles more as k grows. The smoother keeps a fraction ρk of the data's component along each one. d1 = d2 = 0 (constants and straight lines are free), so ρ1 = ρ2 = 1 always.
Say it“S-lambda is the sum over k of rho-k times u-k u-k-transpose, where rho-k is one over one plus lambda d-k.”

Degrees of freedom for any fitting method

The trace is a special case of a general definition (ESL §7.6). The more a fitted value follows its own observation, the more freedom the method has. Measure that as a covariance: nudge yi by noise and see how much ŷi moves with it. For any linear smoother the sum of those covariances, divided by σ², is exactly trace(S); the definition also works for adaptive methods where no matrix exists. Run the simulation: it redraws the noise again and again, refits, and estimates the covariances from scratch.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{df}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}) &= \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{Cov}(\hat y_i,\,y_i) \\ &= \operatorname{trace}(\mathbf{S}) \ \text{ if } \hat{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{y} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: if ŷ = Sy and the noise has covariance σ²I, then Cov(ŷ, y) = Sσ², whose diagonal sums to σ²trace(S). A method that ignores the data (ŷ constant) has 0 df; one that copies it (ŷ = y) has N.
Say it“The degrees of freedom of y-hat are the sum of the covariances between y-hat-i and y-i, over sigma squared.”

Choosing λ

Choosing λ automatically

λ is hard to think about: its scale depends on the units of x and y. Degrees of freedom are not. So the first trick is to fix the df you want and solve trace(Sλ) = df for λ; the trace falls steadily as λ grows, so a few bisection steps find it. The second is to let the data choose. Too few df and the fit is biased, too stiff to follow the curve; too many and it has high variance, chasing the noise. In a simulation we know the truth and can compute the expected prediction error exactly; on real data, cross-validation estimates it, and for a linear smoother leave-one-out costs no more than a single fit. Drag the df, or click the error curves, and press New sample to see how much CV's choice moves around.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{EPE}(\hat f_\lambda) &= \sigma^2 + \overline{\mathrm{Bias}^2} + \overline{\mathrm{Var}} \\ \text{bias} &= \mathbf{f} - \mathbf{S}_\lambda\mathbf{f} \\ \operatorname{Cov}(\hat{\mathbf{f}}) &= \sigma^2\,\mathbf{S}_\lambda\mathbf{S}_\lambda^{\mathsf T} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: bars mean averages over the N data points. The error on a fresh y at the data points splits three ways: noise we can never remove, squared bias (how far the average fit is from the truth) and variance (how much the fit jumps from sample to sample). Because the fit is Sλy, all three are exact formulas when f and σ are known.
Say it“Expected prediction error is sigma squared plus average bias squared plus average variance.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{CV} &= \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(y_i - \hat f^{(-i)}(x_i)\big)^2 \\ &= \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(\frac{y_i-\hat f(x_i)}{1-S_{ii}}\Big)^2 \\ \mathrm{GCV} &= \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(\frac{y_i-\hat f(x_i)}{1-\mathrm{df}/N}\Big)^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: leave point i out, refit, predict it, and average the squared misses. The miracle: the left-out residual is the ordinary residual inflated by 1/(1 − Sii), so all N refits come from one fit. Generalised CV swaps each Sii for their average, df/N.
Say it“CV is the average over i of the residual divided by one minus S-i-i, squared.”

Under the hood

A smoothing spline in O(N) time

N knots means an N×N system, and solving a general one costs about N³/3 operations. Yet smoothing splines are fitted to millions of points. The trick is a good basis (ESL appendix 5.A). In the B-spline basis each function lives on just four neighbouring intervals, so BTB and ΩB are zero except near the diagonal, and a banded Cholesky factorisation, which creates no new non-zeros, solves the system in time proportional to N. This page uses an equivalent banded form due to Reinsch, written in terms of the curve's values and its curvatures γi = f″(xi) at the knots: a system with only five non-zero diagonals. Pick a view of the matrix, then race the two solvers.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{B-splines: } &\big(\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{B}+\lambda\boldsymbol{\Omega}_B\big)\hat\theta = \mathbf{B}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{y} \\ \text{Reinsch: } &\big(\mathbf{R}+\lambda\,\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{Q}\big)\gamma = \mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{y} \\ &\hat{\mathbf{f}} = \mathbf{y}-\lambda\,\mathbf{Q}\gamma \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the B-spline matrix has 7 non-zero diagonals; Reinsch’s has 5. Q (N×(N−2)) takes second differences of the values, R ((N−2)×(N−2)) links neighbouring curvatures; both are tridiagonal, built from the knot gaps hi = xi+1 − xi. The left-hand matrix is pentadiagonal, its Cholesky factor has just two sub-diagonals, and the fit follows in a handful of operations per knot. The same banded factor also gives every Sii in O(N) operations, so CV and GCV stay cheap (this page takes the simpler O(N²) route for them). The roughness of the fit is γTRγ.
Say it“R plus lambda Q-transpose Q, times gamma, equals Q-transpose y; the fit is y minus lambda Q gamma.”

Classification

Nonparametric logistic regression

The roughness penalty works with any likelihood. For a yes/no outcome, model the log-odds as a smooth function, log[p(x)/(1 − p(x))] = f(x), and maximise the log-likelihood minus the penalty. The argument from before still holds: the best f is a natural cubic spline with knots at the xi. There is no closed form now, but Newton’s method turns each step into a weighted smoothing spline of a “working response” z: the current log-odds plus each point’s surprise, scaled up where p(1 − p) is small. Below, 150 simulated people with one risk score each; dots at the top had the event. Press “One Newton step” a few times, then change λ.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell(f;\lambda) &= \textstyle\sum_{i}\big[y_i f_i - \log(1+e^{f_i})\big] \\ &\quad - \tfrac12\lambda\textstyle\int f''(t)^2\,dt \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: yi is 0 or 1 and fi = f(xi). The sum is the Bernoulli log-likelihood written in log-odds; the penalty pulls f towards a straight line, which would be ordinary linear logistic regression. We maximise ℓ.
Say it“ell is the sum of y-i f of x-i minus log of one plus e to the f, minus a half lambda times the roughness.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{f}^{\text{new}} &= \mathbf{S}_{\lambda,\mathbf{w}}\,\mathbf{z} \\ \mathbf{z} &= \mathbf{f}^{\text{old}} + \mathbf{W}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{p}) \\ \mathbf{S}_{\lambda,\mathbf{w}} &= \mathbf{N}\big(\mathbf{N}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{N}+\lambda\boldsymbol{\Omega}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{N}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{W} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: W is diagonal with wi = pi(1 − pi). One Newton step is one weighted smoothing spline: fit z, with points weighted by how informative they are. A point with p near 0.5 has the largest weight; one the model is already sure about has little. Repeat until f stops changing (iteratively reweighted least squares).
Say it“The new f is the weighted smoother applied to z, where z is the old f plus W-inverse times y minus p.”

More inputs

Splines in two or more dimensions

With two inputs there are two classic routes. Tensor products multiply one-dimensional bases: M1 functions of x1 times M2 of x2 give M1M2 functions of both, and the count explodes as dimensions are added. Thin-plate splines generalise the roughness penalty instead: charge every second derivative, including the twist ∂²f/∂x1∂x2. The penalty measures the bending energy of a thin metal sheet forced through the data, hence the name. The solution again has one basis function per data point, a radial bump η(‖x − xj‖), plus a plane. A cheaper middle road is the additive model f = α + f1(x1) + f2(x2), a smoothing spline in each input (chapter 9 of ESL). Click on the map to add a measurement, pick a surface and slide λ.

\[ \begin{aligned} g_{jk}(\mathbf{x}) &= h_{1j}(x_1)\,h_{2k}(x_2) \\ J[f] &= \textstyle\iint \big(f_{11}^2 + 2f_{12}^2 + f_{22}^2\big)\,d\mathbf{x} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: top, the tensor-product basis: every pairing of a function of x1 with a function of x2. Bottom, the thin-plate roughness, with fjk = ∂²f/∂xj∂xk: curvature along each axis plus twice the twist. A plane has J = 0, so a huge λ gives the least-squares plane. The penalty does not change if you rotate the axes.
Say it“J of f is the double integral of f-one-one squared, plus two f-one-two squared, plus f-two-two squared.”
\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathbf{x}) &= \beta_0 + \beta^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x} \\ &\quad + \textstyle\sum_{j}\alpha_j\,\eta\big(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_j\rVert\big) \\ \eta(r) &= r^2\log r \\ &\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{E}+\lambda'\mathbf{I} & \mathbf{T}\\ \mathbf{T}^{\mathsf T} & \mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha\\ \beta\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}\\ \mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the minimiser of RSS + λJ is a plane plus one radial bump per data point. E holds η(‖xi − xj‖); T has rows (1, xi1, xi2); TTα = 0 keeps the bumps from smuggling in a plane. With this η the roughness is J = 8παTEα, so λ′ = 8πλ.
Say it“f is a plane plus the sum of alpha-j times eta of the distance to x-j, with eta of r equal to r squared log r.”

The general theory

Regularisation in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space

Every method in this chapter has the shape min over f of Σ L(yi, f(xi)) + λJ(f). Reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS) give one framework for all of them. Start with a kernel K(x, x′), a similarity that is positive definite: every matrix of values K(xi, xj) has non-negative eigenvalues. Its eigen-expansion supplies a basis of functions φk with weights γk. The space HK holds the functions built from them, and its norm charges each coefficient by 1/γk. Directions the kernel rates highly are cheap; the wiggly ones it barely contains are expensive. That norm is the penalty.

\[ \begin{aligned} K(x,x') &= \textstyle\sum_{k}\gamma_k\,\phi_k(x)\,\phi_k(x') \\ f(x) &= \textstyle\sum_k c_k\,\phi_k(x) \\ \lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal H_K}^2 &= \textstyle\sum_k c_k^2/\gamma_k \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the kernel's eigenfunctions φk are a basis; γk says how much of each the kernel “contains”. The squared norm adds up the coefficients, each divided by its γk: a function made of directions with tiny γk has a huge norm. Functions with finite norm form HK.
Say it“K is the sum of gamma-k phi-k of x phi-k of x-prime; the squared norm of f is the sum of c-k squared over gamma-k.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\langle K(\cdot,x_i),\, f\rangle_{\mathcal H_K} = f(x_i) \\ &\min_{f\in\mathcal H_K} \textstyle\sum_{i} L\big(y_i,f(x_i)\big) + \lambda\lVert f\rVert^2_{\mathcal H_K} \\ &\Rightarrow\ \hat f(x) = \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_i\,K(x,x_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the reproducing property: taking the inner product with the kernel bump at xi evaluates f there. It follows that any part of f orthogonal to the N bumps changes no f(xi), so it adds norm without helping the fit and the optimum drops it. That is the representer theorem: whatever the loss, the solution is a combination of N kernel bumps centred on the data.
Say it“The inner product of the kernel at x-i with f is f of x-i; so f-hat is the sum of alpha-i K of x and x-i.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{\alpha}\ L(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{K}\alpha) + \lambda\,\alpha^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{K}\alpha \\ &\text{squared error: } \hat\alpha = (\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y} \\ &\hat{\mathbf{f}} = \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: plug the representer form in. f at the data is Kα, and the norm is ‖f‖² = ΣΣαiαjK(xi, xj) = αTKα. An infinite-dimensional search has become N numbers. With squared error this is kernel ridge regression, and its smoother K(K + λI)−1 has df = Σ dk/(dk + λ) over K’s eigenvalues dk.
Say it“Alpha-hat is K plus lambda I, inverted, times y.”

Kernels as function spaces

Which functions does a kernel make cheap? Look at its eigen-expansion. Below, the kernel is evaluated on 60 evenly spaced points in [0, 1] and the resulting matrix is decomposed: its eigenvectors trace out the eigenfunctions φk, and its eigenvalues dk show how fast the γk fall. Kernel ridge on data at those points keeps a share dk/(dk + λ) of each component, the same shrinkage pattern as the smoothing spline’s ρk. A polynomial kernel (1 + xx′)d spans only polynomials of degree d: d + 1 eigenvalues, then zeros. This is penalised polynomial regression, and with p inputs its space has (p+d choose d) dimensions. The Gaussian kernel has infinitely many eigenfunctions, each wigglier than the last, with eigenvalues that fall off extremely fast. Switch kernels and move ν or d.

Losses and kernels

One function space, many loss functions

The representer theorem never asked what the loss was. Keep the RKHS penalty and change only L, and you get a family of famous methods (ESL §12.3.3). The support vector machine is exactly this recipe with the hinge loss; the kernel trick of chapter 1.9 is the representer theorem at work. Below, 30 points carry labels y = ±1 along a line, and three classifiers live in the same Gaussian RKHS with the same λ. They draw much the same boundary, but only the hinge loss is exactly zero for points that are safely classified, so only the SVM ends up with many αi exactly zero. Regression and kernels: basis or kernel, your choice

Kernels and basis expansions are two descriptions of one model (ESL §12.3.7). Take M basis functions hm(x) and fit ridge regression: you solve an M×M system. Or define K(x, x′) = Σm hm(x)hm(x′) and fit kernel ridge: you solve an N×N system. The fitted curves are identical. Pick the smaller system; and when M is infinite, as for the Gaussian kernel, only the kernel route exists. The polynomial kernel (1 + xx′)d is the basis hm(x) = √C(d, m)·xm, m = 0…d. Raise the degree and compare the two routes.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{f}} &= \mathbf{H}\big(\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{H}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_M\big)^{-1}\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{y} \\ &= \mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf T}\big(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{\mathsf T}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_N\big)^{-1}\mathbf{y} \\ &= \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_N)^{-1}\mathbf{y} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first line solves an M×M system, the last an N×N one. H is the N×M matrix of basis values. The identity H(HTH + λI)−1 = (HHT + λI)−1H moves the inverse from the M side to the N side, and HHT is the kernel matrix. Cost: about M³ + NM² one way, N³ the other.
Say it“H times H-transpose H plus lambda, inverted, H-transpose y, equals K times K plus lambda, inverted, y.”

Part 2: an RKHS classifier for red wine

Cortez and colleagues (2009) collected 1,599 Portuguese vinho verde red wines, each with 11 lab measurements (acidity, sugar, sulphur dioxide, sulphates, alcohol and so on) and a median taste score from at least three assessors. Call a wine good if it scored 6 or more: 855 of 1,599 do. The page shuffles once, learns from 800 wines and tests on the other 799, with features standardised. Three models compete: linear logistic regression, kernel ridge regression on labels ±1, and kernel logistic regression, both kernel models in the Gaussian RKHS with K(x, x′) = exp(−ν‖x − x′‖²), ν = 0.1. ν and λ were chosen by 5-fold cross-validation on the 800 training wines. Each kernel model solves 800×800 systems in your browser. Press Train.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{ridge: } &\min_\alpha \lVert\mathbf{y}_{\pm1}-\mathbf{K}\alpha\rVert^2 \\ &\qquad + \lambda\,\alpha^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{K}\alpha \\ \text{logistic: } &\min_\alpha \textstyle\sum_i \log\big(1+e^{-y_i f_i}\big) \\ &\qquad + \lambda\,\alpha^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{K}\alpha \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: kernel ridge uses λ = 1, kernel logistic regression λ = 0.15. Two losses, one RKHS penalty, both solved with the representer form. Kernel ridge is a single linear solve; kernel logistic regression needs Newton steps, each a weighted kernel ridge solve, like the spline version of logistic regression earlier. Neither model has a separate intercept; with almost balanced classes, adding one (a constant added to the kernel) moves kernel logistic regression’s test accuracy by less than half a percentage point.
Say it“Kernel ridge minimises squared error plus lambda alpha-K-alpha; kernel logistic minimises the logistic loss plus the same penalty.”

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A smoothing spline minimises RSS + λ∫f″² over all smooth curves; the answer is a natural cubic spline with a knot at every xi, and the penalty is a Gaussian prior on roughness.
  • For fixed λ it is a linear smoother ŷ = Sλy. Sλ shrinks the eigen-directions by 1/(1 + λdk) instead of keeping or killing them, and df = trace(Sλ) = ΣCov(ŷi, yi)/σ².
  • Choose λ through df, or let leave-one-out CV choose it; for a linear smoother CV needs only one fit and the diagonal Sii. Banded matrices make all of this O(N).
  • The same penalty extends to logistic regression (each Newton step is a weighted smoothing spline) and to several inputs (thin-plate splines, tensor products, additive models).
  • In an RKHS the penalty is ‖f‖²HK, and the representer theorem reduces any loss to N coefficients: squared error gives kernel ridge, deviance gives kernel logistic regression, hinge gives the SVM.

Check your understanding

What does the smoothing spline become as λ → 0, and as λ → ∞?

As λ → 0 it tends to the natural cubic spline that interpolates the data (N df). As λ → ∞ only zero-roughness curves are affordable, so it becomes the least-squares straight line (2 df).

A smoothing spline has N knots. Why doesn’t it overfit like a regression spline with N knots would?

Because the penalty shrinks the coefficients. In the eigenbasis of Sλ each wiggly direction keeps only a fraction 1/(1 + λdk), which is tiny for large dk. The effective number of parameters, trace(Sλ), can be 5 even with 100 knots.

Why can leave-one-out CV for a smoothing spline be computed from a single fit?

For a linear smoother the left-out prediction satisfies yi − f̂(−i)(xi) = (yi − f̂(xi))/(1 − Sii). So the full-data residuals and the diagonal of Sλ give all N left-out errors.

State the representer theorem in one sentence. Why does it matter?

For any loss on the data plus λ‖f‖² in an RKHS, the minimiser is a combination ΣαiK(x, xi) of kernel functions centred on the data. It turns a search over an infinite-dimensional space into one over N numbers.

You have N = 50,000 points and a polynomial kernel of degree 2 in 5 inputs. Kernel route or basis route?

Basis route. The space has M = (5+2 choose 2) = 21 dimensions, so ridge regression on 21 features costs almost nothing, whereas kernel ridge would need a 50,000×50,000 system. The two give the same fit.
