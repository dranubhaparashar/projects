Hidden Markov Models: Filtering, Smoothing & Viterbi

What was happening, given everything the sensors said?

A phone on someone’s waist cannot see what its owner is doing. It feels a few seconds of shaking at a time, and each few seconds on their own are ambiguous: sitting and standing feel much the same. But activities last. A hidden Markov model puts that persistence into the arithmetic, so each moment’s evidence is weighed together with its neighbours’. The figure is a real recording of one volunteer from the UCI smartphone data. The top ribbon is what the volunteer was actually doing; below it are four guesses at the same thing. Touch or drag along the ribbons to see what the model believes at that moment, from the past alone and from the whole recording.

Russell & Norvig 14, 14.1, 14.1.1, 14.1.2 · time and uncertainty

States, evidence, and two small tables

Most of the probabilistic models so far describe a world that holds still while you reason about it. Many problems are not like that. A patient’s condition changes through the night; a robot moves while it senses; a person stands up, walks, sits down. To reason about a world that changes, cut time into slices t = 0, 1, 2, … a fixed interval apart, and give every slice the same two kinds of variable: the state Xt, which you cannot see, and the evidence Et, which you can. Here the state is what the phone’s owner is doing, Still, Walking or Climbing stairs, and the evidence is how much the phone shakes in that slice, low, mid or high. Slice 0 carries only a prior belief about the state; evidence starts arriving at t = 1.

Left alone, the model would need the distribution of each state given every earlier state, a table that grows without end. Two assumptions cut it down to two small tables. The Markov assumption says the present state depends on the past only through the previous state; the sensor Markov assumption says each reading depends only on the current state. If the same two tables serve at every t, the process is stationary. The assumptions are choices, not facts: if yesterday’s state also matters, either let Xt depend on Xt−2 as well (a second-order process) or put more into the state, say how long the owner has been walking. Move the two sliders and simulate a day: stickier states give longer runs, a sharper sensor makes the readings track them.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{P}(X_t \mid X_{0:t-1}) \\ &\quad= \mathbf{P}(X_t \mid X_{t-1}) \\ &\mathbf{P}(E_t \mid X_{0:t}, E_{1:t-1}) \\ &\quad= \mathbf{P}(E_t \mid X_t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: once you know the previous state, older states tell you nothing more about the next one (the transition model); once you know the current state, nothing else tells you more about the current reading (the sensor model). In the lab the first is a 3 × 3 table T and the second a 3 × 3 table O.
Say it“X-t given all the past states is X-t given the last one; E-t given everything so far is E-t given X-t.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{P}(X_{0:t}, E_{1:t}) = \mathbf{P}(X_0) \\ &\quad\times \prod_{i=1}^{t} \Big[ \mathbf{P}(X_i \mid X_{i-1}) \\ &\qquad\quad\times \mathbf{P}(E_i \mid X_i) \Big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the two tables and a prior are the whole model. Any joint configuration of states and readings has probability: the prior of the first state, then for every slice one transition factor and one sensor factor. It is a Bayesian network unrolled in time, with arrows Xt−1 → Xt and Xt → Et.
Say it“The joint is the prior on X-zero times, for each i, the transition from X-i-minus-one to X-i times the sensor reading at i.”

Bishop 13.2 · Russell & Norvig 14.3 · hidden Markov models

The hidden Markov model, drawn as a lattice

When the state is a single discrete variable with K values, the model is a hidden Markov model (HMM). Russell and Norvig reach it from the temporal side: any model whose state is several discrete variables can be turned into one by merging them into a single “megavariable” whose values are all their combinations. Bishop reaches it from mixture models: an HMM is a mixture in which the choice of component at step n depends on the choice at step n−1. He writes the state as a 1-of-K vector zn (one entry 1, the rest 0), so that the model has three parts: the initial probabilities πk = p(z1k = 1), the transition matrix Ajk = p(znk = 1 | zn−1,j = 1), each row summing to 1, and an emission distribution p(xn | zn, φ) for each state, which may be a table (as here) or a density over continuous readings (as in the project, where each state emits a Gaussian).

Unfold the K states over N steps and you get the lattice (or trellis): one column per step, one node per state, and an edge for every transition with Ajk > 0. Every hidden sequence is one path through it from left to right, and there are KN of them. Generating data from the model is ancestral sampling: pick z1 from π, emit x1, pick z2 from the row of A for z1, and so on. Setting Ajk = 0 for k < j gives a left-to-right HMM, whose state can only stay or move forward. Such models suit signals with a fixed order of parts, like the phonemes of a spoken word or the strokes of a handwritten digit; because a state may repeat any number of times, they cope with the same parts being drawn out faster or slower. Sample paths, then switch to left-to-right and watch the lattice lose its backward edges.

\[ \begin{aligned} &p(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{Z} \mid \theta) = p(\mathbf{z}_1 \mid \boldsymbol{\pi}) \\ &\quad\times \prod_{n=2}^{N} p(\mathbf{z}_n \mid \mathbf{z}_{n-1}, \mathbf{A}) \\ &\quad\times \prod_{m=1}^{N} p(\mathbf{x}_m \mid \mathbf{z}_m, \boldsymbol{\phi}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the joint of all the readings X and all the hidden states Z, given the parameters θ = {π, A, φ}: one initial factor, N−1 transition factors along the path, and N emission factors. It is the same sentence as the unrolled network above, in Bishop’s notation (his first state is z1, with π playing the part of P(X1)).
Say it“p of X and Z is p of z-one given pi, times the transitions along the path, times every emission.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &p(\mathbf{z}_n \mid \mathbf{z}_{n-1}, \mathbf{A}) \\ &\quad= \prod_{j=1}^{K}\prod_{k=1}^{K} A_{jk}^{\,z_{n-1,j}\,z_{nk}} \\ &\text{left-to-right: } A_{jk} = 0 \text{ for } k < j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with 1-of-K vectors, exactly one exponent is 1 (the entry for the pair of states actually visited) and all the others are 0, so the double product just picks out Ajk for that pair. A left-to-right model zeroes the entries below the diagonal. The model has K−1 free numbers in π and K(K−1) in A, plus the emission parameters.
Say it“The product picks out A-j-k for the two states actually visited; left-to-right means no step goes backwards.”

Russell & Norvig 14.2, 14.2.1 · Bishop 13.2.2 · filtering and prediction

Filtering: keep a belief, update it every step

Given the model and the readings so far, four questions come up again and again. Filtering: what is the state now, given everything up to now, P(Xt | e1:t)? Prediction: what will it be k steps from now? Smoothing: what was it at some earlier step, given everything up to now? Most likely explanation: which whole sequence of states best accounts for the readings? (A fifth, learning the tables from data, is the next chapter.) Filtering comes first because the others are built from it.

The trick is to never look back. Keep the current belief, a list of K numbers called the forward message f1:t, and when a new reading arrives do two things. Predict: push the belief through the transition table, which spreads it out. Update: multiply each state’s share by how well it explains the new reading, then rescale so the shares add to 1. The cost per step is K², however long the history. Predicting further ahead is the first half alone, repeated; with no new evidence the belief drifts towards the chain’s long-run distribution, and the number of steps that takes is the mixing time. The normalising totals are worth keeping too: their product is the likelihood of all the readings, which is how you compare two models of the same data. Build a tape of readings with the three buttons, or pick a preset, and move the focus along it.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{P}(X_{t+1} \mid e_{1:t+1}) \\ &\;= \alpha\, \mathbf{P}(e_{t+1} \mid X_{t+1}) \\ &\quad\times \sum_{x_t} \mathbf{P}(X_{t+1} \mid x_t)\, P(x_t \mid e_{1:t}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the sum is the prediction, the one-step-ahead belief; the factor in front is the update by the new reading; α rescales the result to sum to 1. In short, f1:t+1 = FORWARD(f1:t, et+1), starting from f1:0 = P(X0).
Say it“The new belief is alpha times the sensor model at the new reading, times the old belief pushed through the transitions.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{P}(X_{t+k+1} \mid e_{1:t}) \\ &\;= \sum_{x_{t+k}} \mathbf{P}(X_{t+k+1} \mid x_{t+k}) \\ &\qquad\times P(x_{t+k} \mid e_{1:t}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: prediction is filtering without the update step. Each extra step multiplies by the transition table once more, and the belief forgets the evidence until it reaches the long-run (stationary) distribution of the chain.
Say it“The belief k-plus-one steps ahead is the belief k steps ahead, pushed through the transitions once more.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha(\mathbf{z}_n) &= p(\mathbf{x}_n \mid \mathbf{z}_n) \\ &\quad\times \sum_{\mathbf{z}_{n-1}} \alpha(\mathbf{z}_{n-1})\, p(\mathbf{z}_n \mid \mathbf{z}_{n-1}) \\ p(\mathbf{X}) &= \sum_{\mathbf{z}_N} \alpha(\mathbf{z}_N) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Bishop’s version of the same pass, without the rescaling. His α(zn) is the joint p(x1, …, xn, zn), so adding it up over the last state gives the likelihood of the whole sequence. (Russell and Norvig call the unnormalised message ℓ1:t.)
Say it“Alpha of z-n is the emission times the sum over the previous state of alpha times the transition; p of X is the sum of the last alpha.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &p(\mathbf{x}_{N+1} \mid \mathbf{X}) \\ &\;= \sum_{\mathbf{z}_{N+1}} p(\mathbf{x}_{N+1} \mid \mathbf{z}_{N+1}) \\ &\quad\times \sum_{\mathbf{z}_N} p(\mathbf{z}_{N+1} \mid \mathbf{z}_N)\, p(\mathbf{z}_N \mid \mathbf{X}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: to forecast the next reading rather than the next state, predict the state one step and then average the emission over it. It needs only the current filtered belief, so it can run alongside the forward pass for ever.
Say it“The next reading’s forecast is the emission averaged over the predicted next state.”

Russell & Norvig 14.2.2 · Bishop 13.2.2 · smoothing

Smoothing: let the future vote on the past

Filtering at step k uses the readings up to k. Once the tape is finished you can do better: the readings after k also carry information about Xk. If the phone reads high once in the middle of a long quiet stretch, the filter has to take the blip seriously at the moment it happens; looking back, with quiet readings on both sides, it was probably a bump while sitting still. That hindsight estimate, P(Xk | e1:t) for k < t, is smoothing.

Split the evidence at k. The readings up to k give the forward message f1:k, already computed. The readings after k give a backward message bk+1:t, the likelihood of the rest of the tape for each possible state at k. It is built by the mirror image of the forward pass, from the end of the tape back to k, starting from a vector of ones. Multiply the two and rescale. Doing this for every k is the forward–backward algorithm: one forward pass storing every f, one backward pass, O(K²t) time in all. Bishop’s names are α, β and γ for the result; he also needs ξ, the joint of two neighbouring states, which costs nothing extra and is exactly what learning the transition table will need. Pick “A lone blip” and compare the two rows.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{P}(X_k \mid e_{1:t}) \\ &\quad= \alpha\, \mathbf{f}_{1:k} \times \mathbf{b}_{k+1:t} \\ &\mathbf{b}_{k+1:t} = \sum_{x_{k+1}} P(e_{k+1} \mid x_{k+1}) \\ &\qquad\times b_{k+2:t}(x_{k+1})\, \mathbf{P}(x_{k+1} \mid X_k) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the smoothed belief is the forward message times the backward message, entry by entry (that is what × means here), rescaled. The backward message at k is the next one pushed back through the sensor and transition tables. It starts at bt+1:t = 1 for every state: an empty tape is equally likely whatever the state.
Say it“Smoothed is alpha times forward times backward; backward at k is the sum over the next state of sensor, times the next backward, times the transition.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\gamma(\mathbf{z}_n) = \frac{\alpha(\mathbf{z}_n)\,\beta(\mathbf{z}_n)}{p(\mathbf{X})} \\ &\xi(\mathbf{z}_{n-1}, \mathbf{z}_n) = \frac{1}{p(\mathbf{X})}\, \alpha(\mathbf{z}_{n-1}) \\ &\quad\times p(\mathbf{x}_n \mid \mathbf{z}_n)\, p(\mathbf{z}_n \mid \mathbf{z}_{n-1})\, \beta(\mathbf{z}_n) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Bishop’s β(zn) = p(xn+1, …, xN | zn) is the same backward message. γ is the smoothed marginal of one state; ξ is the smoothed joint of two neighbours, a K × K table that uses the link between them. Summing ξ over either state gives back γ.
Say it“Gamma is alpha times beta over p of X; xi is alpha, emission, transition and beta, over p of X.”

Russell & Norvig 14.2.3 · Bishop 13.2.5 · the most likely sequence

Viterbi: the single best story

Smoothing gives the best guess for each slice on its own. Strung together, those guesses are not always the most likely sequence: each one is the winner of a vote over all paths, and the winners of separate votes need not lie on a good path together. If some transitions are impossible, the string of individual winners can even be a sequence that could never happen. When you want one coherent story, a transcription or a route, you want the path that maximises the joint probability with the evidence.

There are Kt paths, but the best path to state j at step t+1 must pass through some state i at step t, and its first part must be the best path to that i (otherwise swapping in a better one would improve the whole). So keep, for every state, the probability m1:t of the best path that ends there, and a back-pointer to its predecessor. The recursion is the forward pass with the sum replaced by a max, which is the max-sum algorithm of the previous chapters on a chain. At the end, take the best final state and follow the pointers back. In practice it runs in logs, as Bishop writes it, so that products become sums and nothing underflows. Pick the “Viterbi trap” tape and compare the best path with the row of individual winners.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{m}_{1:t+1} &= \mathbf{P}(e_{t+1} \mid X_{t+1}) \\ &\quad\times \max_{x_t} \big[ \mathbf{P}(X_{t+1} \mid x_t) \\ &\qquad\qquad \times m_{1:t}(x_t) \big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: m1:t(j) is the probability of the best path of states that ends in j at step t, jointly with the readings so far. One step on: pick the best predecessor for each state, then multiply in the new reading. It starts from m1:1 = P(X1, e1).
Say it“m at t-plus-one is the sensor model times the max over x-t of the transition times m at t.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \omega(\mathbf{z}_{n+1}) &= \ln p(\mathbf{x}_{n+1} \mid \mathbf{z}_{n+1}) \\ &\quad+ \max_{\mathbf{z}_n} \big\{ \ln p(\mathbf{z}_{n+1} \mid \mathbf{z}_n) \\ &\qquad\quad + \omega(\mathbf{z}_n) \big\} \\ k_n^{\max} &= \psi(k_{n+1}^{\max}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Bishop’s log form: ω is ln m, the maximisation is over the previous state, and ψ stores which previous state won. After the last step, start from the best final state and apply ψ backwards to read off the path.
Say it“Omega of the next state is the log emission plus the best of log transition plus omega; then follow psi back.”

Russell & Norvig 14.3, 14.3.1 · simplified matrix algorithms

Matrix form: every pass is a matrix–vector product

With one discrete state variable, the tables become matrices and the algorithms become a few lines of linear algebra. Put the transition model in a K × K matrix T with Tij = P(Xt = j | Xt−1 = i). For each reading, build a diagonal matrix Ot whose diagonal holds P(et | Xt = j) for each j: the column of the sensor table for the reading actually seen. Then the forward step is “multiply by T transposed, then by O, then rescale”, and the backward step is “multiply by O, then by T”.

The matrix view pays for itself twice. First, if T and every O are invertible, the forward step can be run backwards, recovering f1:t from f1:t+1, so smoothing needs only constant memory: run forward to the end, then walk back recomputing f as you go, next to b. Second, it gives fixed-lag smoothing, an online estimate of the state d steps ago, P(Xt−d | e1:t), updated in constant time per step: keep the product B of the last d “T O” matrices, and when a new reading arrives, peel the oldest factor off the left and add the new one on the right. Both need inverses, so both fail if a reading has zero probability in some state or T is singular. Step through the tape and set a lag d; the stats check both tricks against the plain forward–backward answer.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{f}_{1:t+1} &= \alpha\, \mathbf{O}_{t+1} \mathbf{T}^{\top} \mathbf{f}_{1:t} \\ \mathbf{b}_{k+1:t} &= \mathbf{T}\, \mathbf{O}_{k+1}\, \mathbf{b}_{k+2:t} \\ \mathbf{f}_{1:t} &= \alpha' (\mathbf{T}^{\top})^{-1} \mathbf{O}_{t+1}^{-1}\, \mathbf{f}_{1:t+1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the forward and backward messages are column vectors; each step is one matrix product with T (or its transpose) and one with a diagonal O. The third line inverts the first, which lets smoothing walk back through the tape without storing every f.
Say it“f next is alpha O T-transpose f; b is T O b-next; and f can be recovered from f-next with the two inverses.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{B}_{t-d+1:t} = \textstyle\prod_{i=t-d+1}^{t} \mathbf{T}\mathbf{O}_i \\ &\mathbf{P}(X_{t-d} \mid e_{1:t}) \\ &\quad= \alpha\, \mathbf{f}_{1:t-d} \times \mathbf{B}_{t-d+1:t}\mathbf{1} \\ &\mathbf{B}_{t-d+2:t+1} \\ &\quad= \mathbf{O}_{t-d+1}^{-1}\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{t-d+1:t}\,\mathbf{T}\mathbf{O}_{t+1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the backward message over the last d readings is the product B of d matrices applied to a vector of ones. When reading t+1 arrives, strip the oldest factor T Ot−d+1 off the left (by its inverse) and multiply the new one on the right: a fixed amount of work per step, however long the tape.
Say it“B is the product of d T-O matrices; the lagged estimate is forward times B times ones; to move on, peel one T-O off the left and add one on the right.”

Bishop 13.2.4 · scaling factors

Scaling: why the raw numbers vanish, and the fix

Bishop’s α(zn) is a joint probability of n readings, and every reading multiplies it by a number below 1. After a few hundred steps it is smaller than the smallest number a computer can store, and it becomes exactly 0: every state, every path, and the likelihood itself all read 0, and the posterior becomes 0/0. Logs rescue Viterbi, whose recursion only multiplies and maximises. Forward–backward also adds, and adding logs is awkward, so Bishop rescales instead.

Replace α by α̂, the conditional p(zn | x1, …, xn), which always sums to 1. The rescaling factor at each step, cn, is the probability of reading n given the earlier ones: of order 1, never tiny. The likelihood is the product of the cn, so its log is a sum, which never underflows. The backward messages are rescaled by the same cn, and then the smoothed marginal is simply α̂ times β̂. These are exactly the rescaled f and the normalisers of the filtering lab; Russell and Norvig’s α does the same job. Lengthen the sampled sequence past a few hundred steps and watch the raw computation die while the scaled one carries on.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\widehat{\alpha}(\mathbf{z}_n) = \frac{\alpha(\mathbf{z}_n)}{p(\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n)} \\ &c_n = p(\mathbf{x}_n \mid \mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_{n-1}) \\ &c_n\, \widehat{\alpha}(\mathbf{z}_n) = p(\mathbf{x}_n \mid \mathbf{z}_n) \\ &\quad\times \sum_{\mathbf{z}_{n-1}} \widehat{\alpha}(\mathbf{z}_{n-1})\, p(\mathbf{z}_n \mid \mathbf{z}_{n-1}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: run the usual forward step on the rescaled message, and cn is whatever total comes out; dividing by it keeps α̂ summing to 1. The raw α is α̂ times the product of all the c’s so far.
Say it“Alpha-hat is alpha divided by the probability of the readings so far; c-n alpha-hat is the usual forward step.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\ln p(\mathbf{X}) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \ln c_n \\ &c_{n+1}\, \widehat{\beta}(\mathbf{z}_n) = \sum_{\mathbf{z}_{n+1}} \widehat{\beta}(\mathbf{z}_{n+1}) \\ &\quad\times p(\mathbf{x}_{n+1} \mid \mathbf{z}_{n+1})\, p(\mathbf{z}_{n+1} \mid \mathbf{z}_n) \\ &\gamma(\mathbf{z}_n) = \widehat{\alpha}(\mathbf{z}_n)\, \widehat{\beta}(\mathbf{z}_n) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the log-likelihood is a sum of N moderate numbers. The backward pass is rescaled by the same factors, which makes the smoothed marginal a plain product with no division by p(X). (The pair marginal becomes ξ = cn−1 α̂(zn−1) p(xn | zn) p(zn | zn−1) β̂(zn).)
Say it“Log p of X is the sum of log c-n; beta-hat is rescaled by the same c; gamma is alpha-hat times beta-hat.”

Russell & Norvig 14.3.2 · HMM example: localisation

Where is the robot? An HMM with one state per square

A small robot wanders a maze it has a map of, but it does not know where it is. Each step it moves to a neighbouring free square, chosen at random, and then senses whether there is a wall to its north, east, south and west. Each of the four bits is wrong, independently, with probability ε. The state is the square, so K is the number of free squares, and the whole problem is filtering with the two matrices: T says “move to one of my neighbours, equally likely”, and Ot scores every square by how many of its walls disagree with the four bits seen.

At the start the robot could be anywhere, so the belief is spread over the whole maze. One reading rules out the squares whose walls look very different; the next, after a move, has to be consistent with having come from a likely square, and so on. A few steps usually pin the robot down, unless the maze has places that look alike and the walk keeps it among them. Viterbi answers a different question: not “where is it now” but “which route did it take”. Step the robot and watch the belief collapse; then raise ε.

\[ \begin{aligned} &P(X_{t+1} = j \mid X_t = i) = \mathbf{T}_{ij} \\ &\quad= \begin{cases} 1/N(i) & \text{if } j \text{ is next to } i \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: N(i) is the number of free squares next to square i; the robot moves to each of them with equal probability and never stays put. T is a sparse K × K matrix.
Say it“From square i the robot moves to each neighbour with probability one over the number of neighbours.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &P(E_t = e_t \mid X_t = i) = \mathbf{O}_{t,ii} \\ &\quad= (1-\epsilon)^{4-d_{it}}\, \epsilon^{\,d_{it}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: dit is the number of the four wall bits that disagree between what square i really has around it and what the robot sensed at step t. Each disagreement costs a factor ε, each agreement a factor 1 − ε.
Say it“The chance of the reading in square i is one-minus-epsilon to the number of agreements, times epsilon to the number of disagreements.”

Real-world project

Project: decoding what people did from a phone on their waist

The data is the Human Activity Recognition Using Smartphones dataset from the UCI Machine Learning Repository (Reyes-Ortiz, Anguita, Ghio, Oneto and Parra). Thirty volunteers aged 19 to 48 wore a Samsung Galaxy S II on the waist while they stood, sat, lay down, walked, and walked down and up stairs, following the same script twice; the sessions were video-recorded so that every moment could be labelled by hand. The phone’s accelerometer and gyroscope were sampled at 50 Hz and cut into windows of 2.56 s with 50% overlap, and each window was summarised by 561 features. The authors split the volunteers 21 for training and 9 for testing. The UCI repository distributes the dataset under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 licence; its own README asks that the ESANN 2013 paper (reference 6 below) be cited and says the data may not be used commercially. This page embeds, rounded to three decimals, six of the 561 features for all 10,299 windows, with their labels and volunteer runs.

Within each volunteer the windows are stored in time order, so each volunteer’s data is a sequence, and the activities come in long runs. That is the HMM’s chance. The hidden state is the activity (K = 6). The emission model is Gaussian naive Bayes: for each activity, an independent Gaussian for each feature, with mean and variance from the training volunteers. The transition matrix is counted from the training volunteers’ label sequences, with one added to every count so that no transition is impossible, and π is the share of each activity in training. Everything is fitted in your browser from the embedded training windows when the project scrolls into view.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\ln p(\mathbf{x}_n \mid z_n = j) \\ &\quad= \tau \sum_{f=1}^{F} \ln \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_{nf} \mid \mu_{jf}, \sigma^2_{jf}\right) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the evidence a window gives for activity j is the sum, over the F features in use, of how well each feature fits that activity’s Gaussian. Naive Bayes treats the features as independent given the activity, which they are not, so it is overconfident; the weight τ ≤ 1 tempers it. τ = 1 is the plain model.
Say it“The log emission is tau times the sum over features of the log Gaussian density.”
\[ A_{jk} = \frac{n_{jk} + 1}{\sum_{k'} n_{jk'} + K} \]
Read it as: njk counts how often activity j was followed by activity k in the next window, over the 21 training volunteers. Adding one to every count keeps every transition possible. Because activities last for dozens of windows, the diagonal is close to 1.
Say it“A-j-k is the count of j followed by k, plus one, over the row total plus K.”

The same experiment in Python

The script reads the same embedded data file and fits the same model with numpy: scaled forward–backward as in Bishop 13.2.4, checked against an independent log-space forward pass with scipy’s logsumexp, and Viterbi in logs. It also repeats the toy labs’ default numbers and the robot experiment, using the page’s random-number generator, so the two can be compared line by line.

import json, sys, math, itertools
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import logsumexp
S = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "./"
M32 = 0xFFFFFFFF
def rng(a):                                   # mulberry32, the generator the page uses
    st = [a & M32]
    def r():
        st[0] = (st[0] + 0x6D2B79F5) & M32; a = st[0]
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & M32
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & M32)) & M32) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M32) / 4294967296
    return r
def pick(p, u):                               # first index whose cumulative probability exceeds u
    c = 0.0
    for i, v in enumerate(p):
        c += v
        if u < c: return i
    return len(p) - 1
out = {}

# ---------- 1. the toy model of the labs: Still / Walking / Climbing, readings low / mid / high ----------
def TT(s): return np.array([[s, .75 * (1 - s), .25 * (1 - s)], [.5 * (1 - s), s, .5 * (1 - s)], [.25 * (1 - s), .75 * (1 - s), s]])
def OO(r): return np.array([[r, 2 / 3 * (1 - r), 1 / 3 * (1 - r)], [(1 - r) / 2, r, (1 - r) / 2], [1 / 3 * (1 - r), 2 / 3 * (1 - r), r]])
T, O, p0 = TT(0.8), OO(0.7), np.ones(3) / 3
def fb(ev):                                   # Russell & Norvig: f, b, smoothed, P(e_1:t)
    f = [p0]; c = []
    for e in ev: u = O[:, e] * (T.T @ f[-1]); c.append(u.sum()); f.append(u / u.sum())
    b = [np.ones(3)]
    for e in reversed(ev): b.insert(0, T @ (O[:, e] * b[0]))
    return f, b, [fk * bk / (fk * bk).sum() for fk, bk in zip(f, b)], float(np.prod(c))
def viterbi(ev):
    lm = np.log(O[:, ev[0]] * (T.T @ p0)); bp = []
    for e in ev[1:]: sc = lm[:, None] + np.log(T); bp.append(sc.argmax(0)); lm = sc.max(0) + np.log(O[:, e])
    path = [int(lm.argmax())]
    for b in reversed(bp): path.append(int(b[path[-1]]))
    return path[::-1], float(lm.max())
def brute(ev):                                # every one of the 3^n paths
    first = T.T @ p0; best, bx, tot = -1, None, 0.0
    for x in itertools.product(range(3), repeat=len(ev)):
        p = first[x[0]] * O[x[0], ev[0]]
        for t in range(1, len(ev)): p *= T[x[t - 1], x[t]] * O[x[t], ev[t]]
        tot += p
        if p > best: best, bx = p, list(x)
    return bx, best, tot
N3 = "SWC"
DEF, VIT = [0, 0, 1, 2, 2, 1, 0, 0, 2, 0], [0, 0, 0, 1, 2]
f, b, sm, L = fb(DEF)
print("toy model, s = 0.8, r = 0.7, default tape low low mid high high mid low low high low")
print("  filtered f_1:10 =", np.round(f[-1], 6).tolist(), "  P(e_1:10) = %.6e" % L)
print("  slice 4: filtered", np.round(f[4], 6).tolist(), " smoothed", np.round(sm[4], 6).tolist())
path, lp = viterbi(VIT); bx, best, tot = brute(VIT); _, _, smv, Lv = fb(VIT)
print("Viterbi trap tape low low low mid high: best path", "".join(N3[i] for i in path),
      " smoothed winners", "".join(N3[int(v.argmax())] for v in smv[1:]))
print("  P(best path, e) = %.6e (brute force %.6e over %d paths), P(best path | e) = %.6f" % (math.exp(lp), best, 3 ** len(VIT), best / tot))
out["toy"] = {"f10": f[-1].tolist(), "L10": L, "sm4": sm[4].tolist(), "vit": path, "pvit": best / tot}

for n in range(999): x = pick(T[x], r()); ev.append(pick(O[x], r()))

for n, e in enumerate(ev[1:], start=2):

    u = O[:, e] * (T.T @ ah); lnc.append(math.log(u.sum())); ah = u / u.sum()
la = np.log(O[:, ev[0]] * (T.T @ p0))

# ---------- 3. robot localisation: 40 walks of 40 steps in the page's maze ----------
MAZE = ["....#.....#.....", ".##.#.##.#..##..", ".#......#...#...", "...##.#...#...#.",
        "#.#...#.#.#.#...", "..#.#.....#...#.", "....#..#.....#.."]
FREE = [(i, j) for i in range(7) for j in range(16) if MAZE[i][j] == "."]; IDX = {p: k for k, p in enumerate(FREE)}; K = len(FREE)
DIRS = ((-1, 0), (0, 1), (1, 0), (0, -1))                         # N, E, S, W
NB = [[IDX[(i + di, j + dj)] for di, dj in DIRS if (i + di, j + dj) in IDX] for i, j in FREE]
WALL = [sum(8 >> d for d, (di, dj) in enumerate(DIRS) if (i + di, j + dj) not in IDX) for i, j in FREE]
def walk(seed, eps, steps=40):
    r = rng(seed); pos = math.floor(r() * K); xs, es = [], []
    for t in range(steps):
        nb = NB[pos]; pos = nb[math.floor(r() * len(nb))]; e = 0
        for d in range(4): e |= (((WALL[pos] >> (3 - d)) & 1) ^ (1 if r() < eps else 0)) << (3 - d)
        xs.append(pos); es.append(e)
    return xs, es
def sens(e, eps):                             # (1-eps)^(4-d) eps^d by repeated multiplication, as on the page
    out = []
    for w in WALL:
        d = bin(e ^ w).count("1"); p = 1.0
        for k in range(4): p *= eps if k < d else 1 - eps
        out.append(p)
    return out
def localise(xs, es, eps):
    f = [1 / K] * K; m = None; bp = []; err, acc = [], []
    for t, e in enumerate(es):
        o = sens(e, eps); pr = [0.0] * K
        for i in range(K):
            for j in NB[i]: pr[j] += f[i] / len(NB[i])
        u = [pr[j] * o[j] for j in range(K)]; z = sum(u); f = [v / z for v in u]
        err.append(sum(f[k] * (abs(FREE[k][0] - FREE[xs[t]][0]) + abs(FREE[k][1] - FREE[xs[t]][1])) for k in range(K)))
        if m is None: m = [pr[j] * o[j] for j in range(K)]; ptr = None
        else:
            nm, ptr = [], []
            for j in range(K):
                best, bi = -1.0, -1
                for i in NB[j]:                                    # predecessors of j are its neighbours
                    v = m[i] / len(NB[i])
                    if v > best: best, bi = v, i
                nm.append(best * o[j]); ptr.append(bi)
            m = nm
        mx = max(m); m = [v / mx for v in m]; bp.append(ptr)
        s = max(range(K), key=lambda k: (m[k], -k)); hit = 0
        for u in range(t, -1, -1):
            hit += s == xs[u]
            if u: s = bp[u][s]
        acc.append(hit / (t + 1))
    return err, acc
ts = [1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 40]; out["loc"] = {}
for eps in (0.1, 0.2):
    E = np.zeros(40); A = np.zeros(40)
    for run in range(40):
        xs, es = walk(1 + run, eps); e, a = localise(xs, es, eps); E += e; A += a
    E /= 40; A /= 40; out["loc"][str(eps)] = {"err": E.tolist(), "acc": A.tolist()}
    print(f"\nrobot, {K} free squares, eps = {eps}, 40 walks of 40 steps, at t = {ts}:")

D = json.load(open(S + "x117-har.json")); K, NF = 6, len(D["feat"])
def unpack(p):
    X = np.array(p["X"], float).reshape(-1, NF) / 1000; y = np.array([int(c) for c in p["y"]])
    cut = np.cumsum([0] + [n for _, n in p["subj"]]); return X, y, [slice(cut[i], cut[i + 1]) for i in range(len(p["subj"]))]
Xtr, ytr, Str = unpack(D["tr"]); Xte, yte, Ste = unpack(D["te"])
def fit(subs, k):
    idx = np.concatenate([np.arange(s.start, s.stop) for s in subs]); X, y = Xtr[idx, :k], ytr[idx]
    pi = np.bincount(y, minlength=K) / len(y); N = np.ones((K, K))
    for s in subs:
        for a, b in zip(ytr[s][:-1], ytr[s][1:]): N[a, b] += 1
    mu = np.array([X[y == j].mean(0) for j in range(K)]); var = np.array([X[y == j].var(0) for j in range(K)]) + 1e-4
    return pi, N / N.sum(1, keepdims=True), mu, var
def loglik(X, mu, var, tau): return tau * (-0.5 * (((X[:, None, :] - mu) ** 2) / var + np.log(2 * np.pi * var)).sum(2))
def fb_scaled(logB, A, pi):                   # Bishop 13.2.4: alpha-hat, beta-hat and c_n
    n = len(logB); mx = logB.max(1, keepdims=True); B = np.exp(logB - mx); ah = np.zeros((n, K)); c = np.zeros(n)
    for t in range(n):
        a = pi * B[0] if t == 0 else (ah[t - 1] @ A) * B[t]; c[t] = a.sum(); ah[t] = a / c[t]
    bh = np.ones((n, K))
    for t in range(n - 2, -1, -1): bh[t] = A @ (B[t + 1] * bh[t + 1]) / c[t + 1]
    return ah, ah * bh, float(np.log(c).sum() + mx.sum())
def fwd_log(logB, A, pi):                     # independent check: the forward pass in log space
    la = np.log(pi) + logB[0]
    for t in range(1, len(logB)): la = logsumexp(la[:, None] + np.log(A), 0) + logB[t]
    return float(logsumexp(la))
def vit_log(logB, A, pi):
    lA = np.log(A); w = np.log(pi) + logB[0]; bp = []
    for t in range(1, len(logB)): s = w[:, None] + lA; bp.append(s.argmax(0)); w = s.max(0) + logB[t]
    path = [int(w.argmax())]
    for b in reversed(bp): path.append(int(b[path[-1]]))
    return np.array(path[::-1])
def score(model, X, y, subs, tau):
    pi, A, mu, var = model; hit = np.zeros(4); worst = 0.0
    for s in subs:
        lb = loglik(X[s][:, :mu.shape[1]], mu, var, tau); ah, g, lp = fb_scaled(lb, A, pi); worst = max(worst, abs(lp - fwd_log(lb, A, pi)))
        hit += [((np.log(pi) + lb).argmax(1) == y[s]).sum(), (ah.argmax(1) == y[s]).sum(), (g.argmax(1) == y[s]).sum(), (vit_log(lb, A, pi) == y[s]).sum()]
    return hit / sum(s.stop - s.start for s in subs), worst

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A temporal model is a prior, a transition model P(Xt | Xt−1) and a sensor model P(Et | Xt). The Markov assumptions make those small tables the whole story; an HMM is the case of one discrete state variable.
  • Filtering keeps one belief and updates it each step: predict through T, multiply by the reading’s likelihood, rescale. Prediction is the same without the update, and drifts to the stationary distribution.
  • Smoothing multiplies the forward message by a backward message built from the end of the tape. Forward–backward gives every smoothed marginal, and every neighbouring pair’s joint, in O(K²t).
  • Viterbi replaces the sum by a max and keeps back-pointers. It finds the single most likely sequence, which need not be the string of individually most likely states.
  • Raw forward probabilities underflow after a few hundred steps. Rescale each step and add the logs of the scaling factors, or work in logs throughout.

Check your understanding

The robot’s battery level affects how it moves. What goes wrong with the Markov assumption, and what are the two fixes?

With the state as position alone, the next position depends on the battery, which depends on the whole history of moves, so the previous position no longer screens off the past. Either add the battery level to the state (a bigger state, still first-order), or make the transition depend on more past states (a higher-order process). The first is usually the better fix: enlarge the state until the Markov assumption is a good approximation.

You have the filtered belief at step t. What do you need to compute the filtered belief at step t + 1, and what do you not need?

You need the transition table, the sensor table and the new reading et+1. You do not need any earlier reading or earlier belief: the forward message summarises them all. That is why filtering runs in constant time and memory per step.

Why can smoothing change the best guess for a slice that filtering got wrong, but never change the belief at the last slice of the tape?

At slice k < t, the backward message carries the readings after k, which filtering had not seen. At the last slice there are no later readings, the backward message is all ones, and the smoothed belief equals the filtered one.

Give a reason the string of smoothed winners can be a poor answer when you want a whole sequence.

Each winner is chosen separately, by summing over all paths through that slice. Two neighbouring winners may each be likely on their own but rarely occur together; if their transition has probability 0, the string is impossible. Viterbi maximises over whole paths, so its answer is always a sequence that can happen, and the most probable one.

In the project, the counted transitions help far more than the “sticky” ones with the same stay probability. Why, and is it fair?

Every volunteer followed the same script, so the counted matrix knows which activity follows which (standing is followed by sitting, sitting by laying, and so on) and almost rules out other changes. That is fair for this protocol but would not carry over to people living normally, where any change can follow any other. The sticky matrix only knows that activities last, which is the part that generalises.
