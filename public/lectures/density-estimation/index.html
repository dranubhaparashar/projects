Density Estimation

Histograms, kernels and radial basis function networks

A density p(x) says where data are likely to fall. Most models in this book assume a shape for it, such as a Gaussian, and fit a few numbers. This chapter assumes no shape at all and lets the data speak: count how many points fall near x, divide by how many points there are and by the size of the neighbourhood, and you have an estimate of p(x). Every method here is a variation on that one sentence. In the figure are 20,000 real yellow-taxi pickups from one day in New York; the dots alone draw the streets. Move your pointer or finger over the map: the ring grows until it holds 40 pickups, and its area turns that count into a density.

The idea

Where do the eruptions pile up?

Old Faithful, in Yellowstone, erupts every hour or two. Below are 272 eruptions and how long each one lasted, in minutes. Some are short, about two minutes; most are long, about four and a half. The oldest way to see that shape is a histogram: chop the line into bins of width Δ, count the eruptions in each, and scale the bars so that their total area is 1. Then the bar height is an estimate of the density. It has two dials, and both change the picture. Slide the bin width, then slide the origin (where the first bin starts) and watch the humps appear and vanish.

\[ \hat p(x) = \frac{n_i}{N\,\Delta} \quad \text{for } x \text{ in bin } i \]
Read it as: ni of the N points fell into the bin that contains x, and the bin is Δ wide. ni/N is the fraction of the data in that bin; dividing by the width turns a fraction into a height, so the bars' areas add up to exactly 1. The estimate is flat inside each bin and jumps at the edges.
Say it“p-hat of x is n-i over N times delta, for x in bin i.”

Histograms are quick, and once the counts are made the data can be thrown away. But the estimate has jumps that the true density does not, it depends on an arbitrary origin, and, as the next section shows, it falls apart when x has more than two or three dimensions. Every method in this chapter fixes one of those faults.

The master formula

Counting in a small region: p ≈ K / NV

Take a small region R around x, of size V (a length in one dimension, an area in two). Let P be the chance that one data point lands in R. Of N independent points, the number K that land in R is binomial, so K/N is close to P when N is large. If R is small, p hardly changes inside it and P is about p(x)·V. Put the two together and you get the rule behind this whole chapter. It needs V small, so that p is constant inside R, and K large, so that K/N is close to P: two wishes that pull against each other. Here the true density is known (a mixture of two Gaussians, dashed). Shrink and grow the window V, then add more data with N.

\[ \begin{aligned} P &= \int_R p(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}, \quad K \sim \text{Bin}(N, P) \\ \mathbb{E}\Big[\tfrac{K}{N}\Big] &= P \\ \operatorname{var}\Big[\tfrac{K}{N}\Big] &= \frac{P(1-P)}{N} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: P is the probability mass inside R. Each point lands in R with chance P, independently, so the count K is binomial. Its fraction K/N is right on average, and its spread shrinks like 1/N.
Say it“P is the integral of p over R; K is binomial with N and P; K over N has mean P and variance P times one minus P over N.”
\[ P \approx p(\mathbf{x})\,V \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p(\mathbf{x}) \approx \frac{K}{N\,V} \]
Read it as: for a small region, the mass is the height times the size. Swap the unknown P for its estimate K/N and divide by V. Fix V and count K: that gives histograms and kernel estimators. Fix K and measure V: that gives k-nearest-neighbour estimators.
Say it“P is about p of x times V, so p of x is about K over N V.”

Why histograms die in high dimensions

A histogram with M bins along each of D axes has MD cells. With 10 bins per axis that is 100 cells in two dimensions and ten billion in ten. Any realistic N leaves almost every cell empty, so most cells say “density zero” and the occupied ones hold one point each. This is the curse of dimensionality again, and it is why the regions in K/NV must be centred on the query point, not laid out in a fixed grid. Raise D: below, 1,000 points from a standard Gaussian in D dimensions are dropped into a grid over [−3, 3]D (the few beyond ±3 go into the edge cells).

\[ \begin{aligned} &\#\text{cells} = M^D \\ &\text{points per occupied cell} \to 1 \text{ as } D \text{ grows} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the number of cells multiplies by M with every extra dimension, while the number of points stays N. Once MD is far larger than N, each point sits alone in its cell, and the histogram is just a list of the data.
Say it“The number of cells is M to the power D.”

Fix V, count K

Kernel density estimators: a bump on every point

Centre the window on x itself and count the points inside: that is K/NV with a window that moves with the query, so there is no origin to choose. Written with a kernel k(u), a function that is 1 inside the window and 0 outside, the count is a sum over the data. Read the same sum the other way round and every data point puts down a little block of area 1/N, and the estimate is the pile of blocks. Swap the blocks for smooth bumps and the jumps disappear. This is the Parzen window or kernel density estimator (KDE). Pick a kernel, slide h, and click the plot to add or remove eruptions.

\[ \begin{aligned} &k(\mathbf{u}) = \begin{cases} 1 & |u_i| \le \tfrac12 \text{ for all } i \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \\ &\hat p(\mathbf{x}) = \frac1N \sum_{n=1}^{N} \frac{1}{h^D}\, k\!\Big(\frac{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_n}{h}\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: k is a unit cube centred on the origin. k((x − xn)/h) is 1 when xn lies in the cube of side h around x, so the sum is K, and hD is V: this is K/NV exactly.
Say it“p-hat of x is one over N times the sum over n of one over h to the D, times k of x minus x-n over h.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat p(\mathbf{x}) &= \frac1N \sum_{n=1}^{N} \frac{1}{(2\pi h^2)^{D/2}}\, e^{-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_n\rVert^2 / 2h^2} \\ k(\mathbf{u}) &\ge 0, \qquad \int k(\mathbf{u})\,d\mathbf{u} = 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the Gaussian kernel: a bell of width h on every point. Any kernel that is never negative and has area 1 works, and then p̂ is never negative and has area 1 too. ESL writes the width as λ; it is the same dial.
Say it“p-hat is the average of Gaussian bumps of width h, one centred on each data point.”

In the lab the box and Epanechnikov kernels reach h on either side of each point (so the box here has side 2h, not Bishop's h), and the Gaussian has standard deviation h. The price of a kernel estimator is memory: there is no summary to keep, every evaluation touches all N training points. That is why it is called nonparametric: the number of things you store grows with the data.

Bias against variance

Choosing the bandwidth

The kernel's shape barely matters; its width matters enormously. A narrow h makes a spike on every point: little bias, a lot of variance. A wide h smooths away real features: low variance, a lot of bias. The trade-off has a neat form. Squared bias grows like h4, variance shrinks like 1/(Nh), and the best h shrinks only like N−1/5. In practice you either use a rule of thumb that pretends the data are roughly Gaussian, or let the data choose h by cross-validation: score each h by how well the other points predict each left-out point. Slide h and watch the score below the plot, then try the three buttons.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{bias} &\approx \tfrac12 h^2 \mu_2(k)\, p''(x) \\ \text{var} &\approx \frac{p(x)\,R(k)}{N h} \\ h^{\ast} &= \Big[\frac{R(k)}{\mu_2(k)^2\, R(p'')\, N}\Big]^{1/5} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: μ2(k) is the kernel's variance and R(g) = ∫ g² measures how peaked a function is. The bias follows the curvature p″: averaging flattens peaks and fills valleys. The variance is large where few points share a window. Adding squared bias and variance over all x and minimising gives h* ∝ N−1/5.
Say it“Bias is about half h squared times mu-two times p double-prime; variance is about p times R of k over N h.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &h_{\text{Silverman}} = 0.9\,\min\!\big(\hat\sigma,\ \tfrac{\text{IQR}}{1.34}\big)\, N^{-1/5} \\ &\text{CV}(h) = \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \hat p_{-i}(x_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Silverman's rule plugs a Gaussian into R(p″), with a safety margin for skewed data. Leave-one-out likelihood cross-validation builds p̂−i from every point except xi and asks how much density it gives to xi; the best h makes left-out points look likely.
Say it“CV of h is the average log density that the other points give each left-out point.”

Fix K, measure V

k-nearest-neighbour density: let the window grow

A kernel uses the same width everywhere, too wide where the data are dense and too narrow in the sparse tails. Turn K/NV round: fix K and grow a ball around x until it holds K points. Its radius rK(x) is small where data are crowded and large where they are scarce, so the window adapts by itself. This is how the hero map works. It has a catch: the result is not a proper density. Far from the data, rK grows only like the distance, so p̂ falls like 1/|x|, too slowly for its area to stay finite. Slide K and watch the area under the curve; hover to see the window.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat p(\mathbf{x}) &= \frac{K}{N\, c_D\, r_K(\mathbf{x})^D}, \qquad c_1 = 2,\ c_2 = \pi \\ \hat p(x) &\approx \frac{K}{2N\,|x|} \ \text{ far away} \ \Rightarrow\ \int \hat p = \infty \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: rK(x) is the distance from x to its K-th nearest data point, and cDrD is the volume of that ball (a length 2r on a line, an area πr² on a plane). K is the dial: small K is spiky, large K is smooth. AIMA chooses K, like a kernel's width, by cross-validation.
Say it“p-hat is K over N times the volume of the smallest ball around x that holds K points.”

From densities to decisions

The k-nearest-neighbour classifier is a Bayes classifier

A density for each class turns into a classifier through Bayes' theorem: P(class | x) ∝ P(class) · p(x | class). Estimate every piece with K/NV, using one ball around x that holds K points of all classes together, and something lovely happens: the volumes cancel, the class sizes cancel, and the posterior is simply the share of the K neighbours that belong to each class. The familiar k-NN vote from chapter 1.6 was a density-based Bayes classifier all along. Hover to see the ball and its vote, slide K, and click to add points.

\[ \begin{aligned} &p(\mathbf{x}\mid \mathcal{C}_k) = \frac{K_k}{N_k V}, \quad p(\mathcal{C}_k) = \frac{N_k}{N} \\ &p(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{K}{NV} \\ &\Rightarrow\ p(\mathcal{C}_k \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathcal{C}_k)\,p(\mathcal{C}_k)}{p(\mathbf{x})} = \frac{K_k}{K} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the ball around x of volume V holds K points, Kk of them from class k, which has Nk points in all. Each density is K/NV for its own points. Multiply and divide, and V, N and Nk all cancel. To minimise mistakes, pick the class with the most neighbours.
Say it“The posterior of class k given x is K-k over K: the fraction of the K nearest neighbours in class k.”

Real data

Kernel density classification

The same recipe works with kernels: estimate a density for each class separately, weight it by the class's share of the data, and divide by the total. Here are 462 men from a heart-disease survey in the Western Cape, South Africa (the data behind ESL's example in §6.6.2); 160 had coronary heart disease (CHD). Each class's density is a Gaussian KDE with its own Silverman width, times the dial below. Notice something ESL points out: the densities can be rough or wrong in places that do not matter, because only the ratio of the two curves sets the decision, and only near where they cross. Pick a risk factor and slide the width; hover to read the posterior.

\[ \hat P(G = j \mid X = x) = \frac{\hat\pi_j\, \hat f_j(x)}{\sum_{l} \hat\pi_l\, \hat f_l(x)} \]
Read it as: f̂j is the kernel density of class j, built only from that class's points, and π̂j is the share of the data in class j. The top curve plots π̂jf̂j(x) for both classes; the bottom plots their ratio to the total, the posterior. Classify as CHD where it passes 0.5.
Say it“The probability of class j given x is pi-j times f-j of x, over the sum of pi-l f-l of x over all classes.”

With many inputs, one density per class runs straight into the curse of dimensionality. The usual escape is the naive Bayes classifier: pretend the inputs are independent within each class, so each class density is a product of one-dimensional KDEs like these. The independence is rarely true, but as the picture above shows, a classifier can survive rough densities.

Bumps for regression

Radial basis function networks

Kernel bumps are good for more than densities. Use them as features: put a bump D(‖x − ξj‖/λ) at each of M centres ξj, and fit a weighted sum of bumps to a target by least squares. That is a radial basis function (RBF) network: a one-hidden-layer network whose hidden units respond to distance, not to a weighted sum. Put one bump on every training point and the network can pass through every target exactly (that was the original use, exact interpolation), which also means it fits the noise. Use fewer centres, placed by k-means, and it smooths. Watch the gap in the data between 0.55 and 0.78: with narrow bumps the plain network falls back towards its constant term there, leaving a hole. Dividing each bump by the sum of all of them, a normalised RBF, fills the holes. Change the centres, the width and the normalisation.

\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathbf{x}) &= \beta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{M} \beta_j\, D\!\Big(\frac{\lVert \mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_j\rVert}{\lambda}\Big) \\ \text{exact: } M &= N,\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_n = \mathbf{x}_n,\ \ \boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{t} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: D is a bump, here the Gaussian D(u) = e−u²/2; ξj are the centres and λ their width. With the centres fixed, f is linear in the βs, so least squares fits them in one solve (with a whisker of ridge, 10−8, for stability). With a centre on every point, Φnm = D(‖xn − xm‖/λ) is square and, in exact arithmetic, the fit can hit every target; in practice Φ is nearly singular, and the ridge keeps the weights finite.
Say it“f of x is beta-nought plus the sum over j of beta-j times a bump of the distance from x to centre xi-j, over lambda.”
\[ \begin{aligned} h_j(\mathbf{x}) &= \frac{D(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_j\rVert/\lambda)}{\sum_{k=1}^{M} D(\lVert\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_k\rVert/\lambda)} \\ y(x) &= \sum_{n=1}^{N} t_n\, \frac{k(x-x_n)}{\sum_{m} k(x-x_m)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: normalised bumps always add up to 1, so somewhere in every gap one bump is in charge and there are no holes. The second line is Nadaraya–Watson (chapter 1.6a), which Bishop derives from a kernel density of (x, t): a normalised RBF with a centre on every point and the targets themselves as weights, so nothing is fitted at all.
Say it“h-j is bump j divided by the sum of all the bumps; Nadaraya-Watson weights each target by its normalised kernel.”

How else can the centres be chosen? A random subset of the data works, and so does a greedy search that adds, one at a time, the data point whose bump most reduces the error (orthogonal least squares). Centres and widths can also be learned by gradient descent along with the weights; then the fit is no longer a single linear solve, it can stick in local optima, and the RBF network has become an ordinary neural network with unusual hidden units. Bishop gives one more reason to normalise: if the inputs are noisy, the best prediction averages over where each training input might really have been, and that average comes out as a sum of normalised bumps.

Choosing

Which density model when?

A parametric model, such as one Gaussian, compresses the data into a few numbers and is wrong whenever the real shape is not in its family (two humps, a coastline of taxi pickups). A nonparametric model keeps the data themselves, so its size grows with N and it can follow any shape, given enough points. Between the two sit mixture models, a handful of Gaussians fitted by EM, which you meet with clustering. All the nonparametric estimators here share the same weakness: in more than a few dimensions there are never enough points near x.

Real-world project

Project: a geyser and a city

Two real densities, one small and one large. First the geyser: R's faithful data records, for 272 eruptions of Old Faithful, how long the eruption lasted and how long it was until the next one. In two dimensions the two humps become two clouds. A product kernel gives each axis its own width, h1 for the duration and h2 for the wait, and leave-one-out cross-validation picks the pair. Slide the two widths, then press the button to search a 21 × 21 grid of pairs.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat p(x_1, x_2) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} &\frac{1}{h_1}\varphi\Big(\frac{x_1 - x_{n1}}{h_1}\Big) \\ \times\ &\frac{1}{h_2}\varphi\Big(\frac{x_2 - x_{n2}}{h_2}\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: φ is the standard normal bump. Each eruption puts down an elliptical bump, h1 wide along the duration axis and h2 along the waiting axis, because the durations spread over about 3.5 minutes and the waits over more than 50, so one width cannot suit both.
Say it“p-hat of x-one, x-two is the average over eruptions of a product of two normal bumps, one per axis.”

The same project in Python

import json
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar
from scipy.special import logsumexp
from scipy.stats import multivariate_normal
from sklearn.neighbors import KernelDensity

# 1. Old Faithful eruption durations: two rules of thumb and leave-one-out likelihood CV
x = np.loadtxt("faithful.csv", delimiter=",", skiprows=1, usecols=1)          # R's datasets::faithful
N, sd = len(x), x.std(ddof=1)
iqr = np.subtract(*np.percentile(x, [75, 25]))
silverman, scott = 0.9 * min(sd, iqr / 1.34) * N ** -0.2, 1.06 * sd * N ** -0.2

def loo(h, X=x):                       # mean log density each eruption gets from the other 271
    K = np.exp(-0.5 * ((X[:, None] - X[None, :]) / h) ** 2)
    np.fill_diagonal(K, 0)
    return np.mean(np.log(K.sum(1) / ((len(X) - 1) * h * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))))

grid = np.geomspace(0.02, 1.5, 121)
i = int(np.argmax([loo(h) for h in grid]))
r = minimize_scalar(lambda t: -loo(np.exp(t)), method="bounded",
                    bounds=(np.log(grid[i - 1]), np.log(grid[i + 1])), options={"xatol": 1e-10})
print(f"eruptions: N = {N}, sd = {sd:.4f}, IQR = {iqr:.4f}")
print(f"  Silverman h = {silverman:.4f}   Scott h = {scott:.4f}   CV at Silverman = {loo(silverman):.4f}")
print(f"  best h by LOO-CV = {np.exp(r.x):.4f}   CV = {-r.fun:.4f}")

# 2. Old Faithful in 2-D: product Gaussian kernel, LOO-CV over a 21 x 21 grid of widths
D = np.loadtxt("faithful.csv", delimiter=",", skiprows=1, usecols=(1, 2))
de, dw = D[:, :1] - D[:, 0], D[:, 1:] - D[:, 1]
def loo2(he, hw):
    K = np.exp(-0.5 * (de / he) ** 2 - 0.5 * (dw / hw) ** 2)
    np.fill_diagonal(K, 0)
    return np.mean(np.log(K.sum(1) / ((N - 1) * 2 * np.pi * he * hw)))
cv, h1, h2 = max((loo2(a, b), a, b) for a in np.geomspace(0.05, 1, 21) for b in np.geomspace(0.5, 15, 21))
print(f"2-D: best h1 = {h1:.4f} min, h2 = {h2:.4f} min, CV = {cv:.4f}")

# 3. kernel density classification of CHD, one risk factor at a time (leave-one-out)
S = np.genfromtxt("SAheart.data", delimiter=",", names=True, dtype=None, encoding=None)
y = S["chd"]
def silv(v): return 0.9 * min(v.std(ddof=1), np.subtract(*np.percentile(v, [75, 25])) / 1.34) * len(v) ** -0.2
print(f"heart: {len(y)} men, {y.sum()} with CHD; always 'no CHD' = {1 - y.mean():.4f}")
for f in ["sbp", "age", "tobacco", "ldl"]:
    v = S[f].astype(float)
    score = []
    for c in (0, 1):
        h, me = silv(v[y == c]), (y == c)
        s = np.exp(-0.5 * ((v[:, None] - v[me][None, :]) / h) ** 2).sum(1) - me   # drop yourself
        m = me.sum() - me                                                        # class size without you
        score.append(m / (len(v) - 1) * s / (m * h))
    print(f"  {f:8s} LOO accuracy = {np.mean((score[1] > score[0]) == (y == 1)):.4f}")

# 4. NYC yellow-cab pickups, 10 June 2015 (km), 12,000 train / 4,000 validation / 4,000 test
ny = json.load(open("x021-data.json"))["ny"]
P = {k: np.c_[ny[k]["x"], ny[k]["y"]] / 100 for k in ("tr", "va", "te")}
tr, va, te = P["tr"], P["va"], P["te"]
g = multivariate_normal(tr.mean(0), np.cov(tr.T, bias=True))
print(f"taxi: {ny['total']} pickups in the box")
print(f"  one Gaussian       val {g.logpdf(va).mean():8.4f}   test {g.logpdf(te).mean():8.4f}")

def hist_score(c, Q):
    keys, n = np.unique(np.floor(tr / c), axis=0, return_counts=True)
    look = {tuple(k): m for k, m in zip(keys, n)}
    m = np.array([look.get(tuple(k), 0) for k in np.floor(Q / c)])
    return (np.log(m / (len(tr) * c * c)).mean() if m.all() else -np.inf), int((m == 0).sum())
res = []
for c in [0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1.6]:
    s, z = hist_score(c, va)
    print(f"  histogram {c:4} km  val {s:8.4f}  ({z} validation pickups in empty cells)")
    res.append((s, -z, c))                   # best score; if all are -inf, fewest empty-cell hits
c = max(res)[2]
s, z = hist_score(c, te)
print(f"  -> {c} km cells: test {s:8.4f}  ({z} test pickups in empty cells)")

def kde_logpdf(T, Q, h):               # exact Gaussian KDE, summed in log space over every training point
    out = [logsumexp(-((Q[i:i + 500, None] - T[None]) ** 2).sum(-1) / (2 * h * h), axis=1) for i in range(0, len(Q), 500)]
    return np.concatenate(out) - np.log(len(T) * 2 * np.pi * h * h)

best = None
for h in [0.02, 0.03, 0.05, 0.07, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0]:
    s = kde_logpdf(tr, va, h).mean()
    print(f"  kernel h = {h:4} km  val {s:8.4f}")
    if best is None or s > best[0]: best = (s, h)
print(f"  -> h = {best[1]} km: test {kde_logpdf(tr, te, best[1]).mean():8.4f}   "

Two differences in method. To stay fast, the page's kernel sums skip training pickups more than 7h away, and fall back to the full sum for isolated pickups, which moves no log density by more than about 10−4; the Python reference sums over every pickup in log space. And scikit-learn's KernelDensity is not a safe reference at small bandwidths: for isolated pickups its tree-based sum loses precision (at h = 0.02 km its mean validation score is −23.94, against the exact −23.68), though it agrees at the chosen 0.3 km, as printed. The colour maps use a binned kernel estimate on a 100 m grid, for display only. One caveat belongs to the method: a single fixed width must serve both crowded Midtown and sparse Queens, and the score, which punishes any pickup given almost no density, picks a wide 0.3 km that blurs Midtown. A width that adapts, like the k-NN radius, is the usual cure.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Every nonparametric density estimate is p(x) ≈ K/(NV): K of the N points fall in a region of size V around x. It needs V small enough for p to be flat and K large enough for K/N to be reliable.
  • Fix V and count K: histograms (fixed bins, an origin and jumps) and kernel estimators (a bump of area 1/N on every point, smooth with a smooth kernel).
  • The bandwidth matters far more than the kernel. Squared bias grows like h4, variance like 1/(Nh); Silverman's rule assumes a bell, leave-one-out likelihood lets the data choose (0.10 min for the two-humped eruptions, against Silverman's 0.33).
  • Fix K and measure V: the k-NN density adapts its window but is not a true density. Used per class it gives the k-NN classifier, P(Ck | x) = Kk/K; kernel densities per class give the kernel classifier.
  • Bumps also make features: an RBF network fits weights to M Gaussian bumps by least squares; normalising the bumps removes holes, and Nadaraya–Watson is the normalised RBF that needs no fitting at all.

Check your understanding

A histogram of 1,000 points with bin width 0.2 has 50 points in one bin. What is its bar height, and what are its units?

50 / (1,000 × 0.2) = 0.25 per unit of x. A density is a probability per unit length, so if x is in minutes the height is 0.25 per minute; only areas under the curve are probabilities.

Why does a kernel density estimate with a Gaussian kernel always have area exactly 1?

It is an average of N functions, each of which is a Gaussian density with area 1. The average of functions of area 1 has area 1.

You double the number of data points. By roughly how much should the bandwidth shrink?

By a factor of 2−1/5 ≈ 0.87, about 13%. The optimal h falls like N−1/5, which is why kernel estimates improve slowly with more data.

Why is the k-NN density estimate not a proper density, and does that hurt the k-NN classifier?

Far from the data its radius grows only linearly with distance, so p̂ decays like 1/|x| and its integral diverges. The classifier is unharmed: in Kk/K the volumes cancel, and only the vote of the neighbours is used.

A plain Gaussian RBF network with narrow bumps predicts nonsense in a gap in the data. What happened, and what are two fixes?

No bump reaches into the gap, so every basis function is nearly zero there and the fit drops to its constant term: a hole. Widen the bumps (larger λ), or normalise them so they always sum to 1 and the nearest one takes over.
