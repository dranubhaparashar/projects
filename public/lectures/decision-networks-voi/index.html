Decision Networks & the Value of Information

Put beliefs, choices and goals in one picture, then ask what a test is worth before you pay for it

A doctor rarely knows enough to act at once. Each test costs money and time, and a test is worth ordering only if its result could change what she does next. A decision network holds the whole problem in one diagram: what is uncertain, what can be chosen, and what is wanted. The value of information then puts a price on every question you could still ask. On the right is a small network fitted to 297 real patients from the Cleveland Clinic. Their interview answers are already known, and each test carries the Ontario fee-schedule price that comes with the data. Click a test to order it: its real result comes back, the belief about heart disease moves, and the remaining tests are re-priced.

The picture

Three kinds of node

A Bayesian network says what you believe. A decision network (also called an influence diagram; Howard and Matheson, 1984) adds what you can do and what you want. It has three kinds of node. Chance nodes (ovals) are random variables, each with a conditional probability table, just as before. Decision nodes (rectangles) are choices the agent makes. Utility nodes (diamonds) score the outcome as a function of their parents.

The arcs mean different things depending on where they end. An arc into a chance node is probabilistic dependence, and it may come from a decision, because acting can change the world. An arc into a utility node says what the score depends on. An arc into a decision node is an information arc: it says what will be known at the moment of choosing. Our running example is the smallest useful network. A patient may have a disease D (prior p). A test T comes back positive with probability 0.85 if she has it and 0.10 if she doesn’t. The doctor chooses whether to treat, and the outcome is scored on a 0–100 utility scale: 100 for a healthy patient left alone, 90 for a healthy patient treated needlessly, 75 for a sick patient treated, and 20 for a sick patient sent home.

Sometimes the chance nodes between a decision and its utility are folded away. What remains is an action–utility table: for each thing the doctor might know, the expected utility of each action. It is smaller and faster to use, but the probabilities are baked into it. If the disease becomes twice as common, the table must be rebuilt, whereas the full network only needs its prior changed. Reinforcement learning’s Q-function, in the next chapter, is exactly such a table. Switch the information arc on and off, then collapse the network to its table.

\[ \begin{aligned} EU(a \mid \mathbf e) &= \sum_{d} P(d \mid \mathbf e)\, U(d, a) \\ \#\text{policies} &= |A|^{\,m} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: e is everything known when the choice is made, which is exactly what the information arcs bring in. The expected utility of action a weighs each possible state d by how likely it is now, and scores it with the utility table. A policy picks one action for each of the m things the decision could see, so with |A| actions there are |A|m policies. Blind, m = 1 and there are 2; with the test result visible, m = 2 and there are 4.
Say it“The expected utility of a given the evidence is the sum over states of the probability of the state times the utility of the state and the action.”

The algorithm

Evaluating a decision network

Solving a decision network takes three steps. First, set the evidence variables to what has been observed. Second, for each possible value of the decision node, fix the decision at that value, compute the posterior probabilities of the utility node’s parents with any Bayesian-network inference method, and from them the expected utility. Third, return the action with the highest expected utility. No new machinery is needed: evaluating a decision network is ordinary inference, run once per action.

With one uncertain state and two actions the result can be drawn. Each action’s expected utility is a straight line in p = P(disease | e): it runs from the action’s utility for a healthy patient at p = 0 to its utility for a sick one at p = 1. The lines cross at a threshold p*. Treat above it, wait below it. The test’s only job is to move p, and the decision changes only when p crosses p*. This threshold view of clinical decisions goes back to Pauker and Kassirer (1980). Pick the evidence, then step through the evaluation.

\[ P(d \mid t) = \frac{P(t \mid d)\,P(d)}{\sum_{d'} P(t \mid d')\,P(d')} \]
Read it as: step two’s inference, here a single use of Bayes’ rule. For a positive test, P(t | d) is the sensitivity and P(t | ¬d) is one minus the specificity.
Say it“The probability of d given t is the probability of t given d times the prior, normalised.”
\[ \begin{aligned} a^{*} &= \arg\max_a \textstyle\sum_d P(d \mid \mathbf e)\,U(d,a) \\ p^{*} &= \frac{U_{\bar d\bar a} - U_{\bar d a}}{(U_{\bar d\bar a} - U_{\bar d a}) + (U_{da} - U_{d\bar a})} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: step three picks the best action. With two actions (a = treat, ā = don’t) the lines cross where the harm of needless treatment, weighted by 1 − p, equals the benefit of treating the sick, weighted by p.
Say it“Treat when p exceeds the harm of needless treatment divided by harm plus benefit.”

The value of information

What is a survey worth?

So far the evidence simply arrived. Usually you choose what to find out, and finding out costs something. Information value theory (Howard, 1966) prices a question by the decisions it improves. Here is a clean case. Six plots of land are for sale. Exactly one of them sits on a mineral seam worth C = £1.2m, and each plot costs C/n = £200k. Buying blind, you break even on average: a 1-in-6 chance of £1.2m for £200k. A surveyor offers to inspect plot 1 and tell you whether the seam is there. What is her report worth to you?

If she says yes (probability 1/n), you buy plot 1 and make C − C/n. If she says no, the seam is in one of the other n − 1 plots, so any of them now has a 1/(n − 1) chance. Buying one of them makes, on average, C/(n − 1) − C/n. Average the two branches and the survey is worth exactly C/n, the price of one plot. Real surveyors make mistakes, so the lab lets her be right only with probability q. At q = ½ her report is a coin toss and worth nothing. Vary the number of plots and the surveyor’s accuracy, and watch which plot you would buy after each report.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{VOI} &= \tfrac{1}{n}\Big(C - \tfrac{C}{n}\Big) \\ &\quad + \tfrac{n-1}{n}\Big(\tfrac{C}{n-1} - \tfrac{C}{n}\Big) \\ &= \tfrac{C}{n} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first term is the “yes” branch: chance 1/n, then a sure profit of C minus the price. The second is the “no” branch: chance (n − 1)/n, then a better-than-blind bet on another plot. Without the survey the best you can expect is 0, so the whole average is the value of the information. It equals the plot price: a perfect survey is worth paying up to one plot’s cost for.
Say it“The value is one over n times C minus C over n, plus n minus one over n times C over n minus one minus C over n, which is C over n.”

The general formula

The value of perfect information

The survey generalises. With current evidence e, the agent’s best action α has expected utility EU(α | e). Suppose it could learn the exact value of some variable Ej. After seeing Ej = ejk it would choose a new best action αejk, perhaps the same one. It doesn’t know which value will turn up, so it averages over them using its current beliefs. The value of perfect information (VPI) is that average minus what it can get now.

When is information worth a lot? Picture two plans whose utilities you are unsure of, each belief a bell curve. If one plan is clearly better, no plausible discovery would change your mind, and the information is worth almost nothing. If the two are close and your uncertainty is wide, learning the truth will often switch your choice, and the switch can be worth a lot. If they are close but you are almost sure of both, you may well switch, but it hardly matters which you pick. Information pays when it is likely to change the plan and when the new plan is much better. For two independent bell-curve beliefs the answer even has a closed form (Clark, 1961). Try the three presets, then move the gap and the spreads.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{VPI}_{\mathbf e}(E_j) = {} & \sum_k P(E_j{=}e_{jk} \mid \mathbf e) \\ & \times EU(\alpha_{e_{jk}} \mid \mathbf e, E_j{=}e_{jk}) \\ & - EU(\alpha \mid \mathbf e) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for every value Ej could take, weight it by how likely it is now, and take the expected utility of the best action once that value is known. Subtract the expected utility of the best action today. The result is how much better off, on average, knowing Ej would leave you, measured in utility.
Say it“The value of perfect information about E-j is the expected utility of acting after you see E-j, minus the expected utility of acting now.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{VPI} &= \mathbb E[\max(U_A, U_B)] \\ &\quad - \max(\mu_A, \mu_B) \\ &= \theta\big(\varphi(z) - z\,\Phi(-z)\big) \\ z &= |\mu_A-\mu_B| \,/\, \theta \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the two plans’ utilities are independent normals UA ~ N(μA, σA2) and UB ~ N(μB, σB2), and the information reveals both exactly. Here θ = √(σA2 + σB2) is the spread of their difference, z is the gap measured in units of θ, and φ and Φ are the standard normal density and CDF. VPI grows in proportion to θ and dies away quickly once z exceeds about 2.
Say it“VPI is theta times phi of z minus z times capital Phi of minus z, where z is the gap over theta.”

Three properties

Never negative, not additive, order-free

VPI is never negative. A single result can be bad news: a positive test lowers the patient’s expected utility. But the expected value of looking is never below zero. The picture shows why. The best you can do at belief p is the upper envelope of the actions’ straight lines, and a maximum of straight lines is convex. Your current belief is exactly the probability-weighted average of the posteriors you might end up with. By Jensen’s inequality, the average height of a convex curve at those posteriors is at least its height at their average. The gap is the VPI. It is zero exactly when every possible posterior leads to the same action, so that they all sit on one straight segment.

VPI is not additive. Two tests together can be worth less than the sum of their values, when they tell you the same thing, or more, when neither alone can push p across the threshold but both together can. VPI is order-independent. Learning Ej and then Ek leaves you knowing the same as learning them the other way round, so the total value is the same either way. Load the two presets and compare the bars.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{VPI}_{\mathbf e}(E_j) &= \textstyle\sum_k P_k\, f(p_k) \\ &\quad - f\big(\textstyle\sum_k P_k\, p_k\big) \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: f(p) = maxa EU(a | p) is the convex envelope, pk the posterior after result k, and Pk that result’s probability. Since Σ Pk pk = p, the prior, Jensen’s inequality makes the difference non-negative.
Say it“The average of f at the posteriors, minus f at their average, is never negative.”
\[ \begin{aligned} & \text{VPI}_{\mathbf e}(E_j, E_k) \\ &\ = \text{VPI}_{\mathbf e}(E_j) \\ &\qquad + \textstyle\sum_v P(v \mid \mathbf e)\,\text{VPI}_{\mathbf e, v}(E_k) \\ &\ = \text{VPI}_{\mathbf e}(E_k) \\ &\qquad + \textstyle\sum_w P(w \mid \mathbf e)\,\text{VPI}_{\mathbf e, w}(E_j) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: v ranges over the values of Ej and w over those of Ek. The pair is worth what the first test is worth, plus the expected value of the second once the first is known, in either order. The pair need not equal VPI(Ej) + VPI(Ek).
Say it“The value of both is the value of one plus the expected value of the other given the first, in either order.”

Putting it to work

An information-gathering agent

An agent that knows the VPI of every observation it could make can decide for itself what to ask. The simplest such agent is myopic, or greedy. At each step it prices every observation it hasn’t made yet as if that were the last one it would ever make. It requests the most worthwhile one if the value beats the cost, updates its beliefs with the answer, and repeats. When no observation is worth its price, it acts. Two scoring rules are common: value minus cost (the net gain), or value per unit cost (the best value for money, still requesting only if value beats cost).

Here the agent works on the real heart-disease network from the hero, fitted to all 297 patients. In this network utility is counted in dollars: a missed disease costs $5,000 and a needless treatment $1,000. Those two numbers are illustrative choices, not clinical figures, and the project lets you change them. VPI is then the expected drop in the decision’s loss. Pick a patient and step the agent.

function INFORMATION-GATHERING-AGENT(percept) returns an action
  update the belief state with percept
  for each observation E_j not yet made:  value_j ← VPI(E_j)          # what seeing it would be worth now
  j ← the observation with the best score   (VPI_j − Cost_j,  or  VPI_j / Cost_j)
  if VPI_j > Cost_j then return REQUEST(E_j)
  else return the action with the highest expected utility
\[ \begin{aligned} j^{*} &= \arg\max_{j}\, \Delta_j \\ \Delta_j &= \text{VPI}_{\mathbf e}(E_j) - C(E_j) \\ \text{ask only if } & \Delta_{j^*} > 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: among the tests not yet done, pick the one with the largest net value Δj, its VPI minus its cost C(Ej). With losses instead of utilities, VPI is today’s smallest expected loss minus the expected smallest loss after the result. If even the best test isn’t worth its price, stop and act.
Say it“Pick the test with the largest value minus cost; order it only if its value beats its cost.”

Beyond greed

Looking further ahead

The myopic agent asks “is this one test worth it, if I stop straight afterwards?” That question can mislead it. Take the two weak tests from the previous section. Neither alone can move p across the threshold, so each has VPI 0 and the greedy agent orders nothing. Yet a plan that orders A, and then orders B only if A was positive, can be worth its cost. A nonmyopic agent plans over sequences of observations, where each later choice may depend on earlier results. Exactly, that is a dynamic program over everything that might have been observed. Its size grows exponentially with the number of tests, and in general it is a partially observable planning problem, the subject of later chapters. Greedy selection is often close to optimal in practice, and for some well-behaved objectives it has guarantees (Krause and Guestrin, 2005).

Some sequential problems do have a clean answer. You have lost your keys. They are in exactly one of five places, with probabilities pi, and looking in place i takes ci minutes. Search in which order? Looking where they are most likely, or where looking is quickest, are both myopic and both can be wrong. Compare two neighbouring places in any order. Swapping them changes only the term where one is searched first, and doing i before j is better exactly when pi/ci > pj/cj. So the best order sorts the places by chance per minute. Drag the bars to change the chances, and compare the four orders.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[\text{time}] &= \sum_{k=1}^{n} c_{\sigma_k}\Big(1 - \sum_{l \lt k} p_{\sigma_l}\Big) \\ i \text{ before } j &\iff p_i / c_i > p_j / c_j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: σ is the search order. You pay for the k-th place only if the keys were not in any of the earlier ones, which happens with probability 1 minus their total chance. Swapping neighbours i and j changes the expected time by cjpi − cipj, which gives the ratio rule.
Say it“Expected time is the sum of each place’s cost times the chance you still haven’t found them; search in order of chance per minute.”

How sure are the numbers?

Sensitivity analysis and robust decisions

Every number in a decision network is an estimate: the prior from a small study, the utilities from a questionnaire. Sensitivity analysis asks how much the recommendation depends on them. Vary each number over a plausible range and see whether the best action changes. If no plausible change flips it, the decision is robust and you can stop worrying. If a small change in one number flips it, that number is worth measuring better. That is the value of information again, this time about the model itself.

When you can’t pin the numbers down, there are two classical responses. A robust (maximin) decision takes the action whose worst expected utility, over every model you consider plausible, is best. It is safe but can be very conservative. The Bayesian response puts a probability distribution over the uncertain parameters and averages. Here expected utility is linear in each parameter, so averaging just means plugging in the mean values. Widen the uncertainty and watch which bars cross zero.

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta(\theta) &= EU(\text{treat}) - EU(\text{don't}) \\ &= p\,(U_{da} - U_{d\bar a}) \\ &\quad - (1-p)(U_{\bar d\bar a} - U_{\bar d a}) \\ a^{*}_{\text{robust}} &= \arg\max_a \min_{\theta \in \Theta} EU(a;\theta) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: θ collects the five numbers (the prior and four utilities). Δ is positive when treating is better. Sensitivity analysis moves one number at a time and records the range of Δ; a range that straddles 0 means that number alone can flip the choice. Θ is the whole box of plausible values, and the robust action maximises its worst case over the box.
Say it“Delta is p times the benefit of treating the sick minus one minus p times the harm of treating the healthy; the robust action maximises the minimum expected utility.”

Whose utility?

When you don’t know what you want

Every network so far had a known utility node. Sometimes the uncertainty is in the preferences themselves. Would you enjoy a dish you have never tasted, or a job in a city you have never visited? Uncertainty about your own utility can be handled with the tools you already have. Make the unknown taste θ a hidden chance node, with Uθ(a) as the utility under each value. Expected utility then averages over θ exactly as it averages over the state of the world. If nothing you can do will reveal θ, you act on the average. If something will (a small taste first), that observation has a value of information like any other.

The same mathematics gives a striking result about machines that act for people. A robot is about to take an action whose value U to its human it doesn’t know; its belief about U is a bell curve. It can act now (expected value μ), switch itself off (value 0), or defer: tell the human and let her switch it off if she wants. If she is rational she lets it go ahead exactly when U > 0, so deferring is worth E[max(U, 0)], which is never less than max(μ, 0). A robot that is uncertain about what its human wants therefore has a positive reason to leave its off-switch alone. In this off-switch game (Hadfield-Menell, Dragan, Abbeel and Russell, 2017) that reason shrinks to nothing as the robot becomes certain, and it can turn negative if the human is unreliable. Make the robot surer, then make the human sloppier.

\[ EU(a) = \sum_{\theta} P(\theta)\,U_{\theta}(a) \]
Read it as: uncertain preferences are just one more hidden variable. The utility under each possible taste θ is weighted by how likely that taste is. Learning θ before choosing is worth Eθ[maxa Uθ(a)] − maxa EU(a), which is the VPI formula again.
Say it“The expected utility of a is the sum over tastes of the probability of the taste times the utility of a under it.”
\[ \begin{aligned} EU(\text{defer}) &= \mathbb E\big[\,U\,\pi_H(U)\big] \\ \pi_H(U) &= 1 / \big(1+e^{-\beta U}\big) \\ \beta\to\infty: \ \ EU &= \mathbb E[\max(U,0)] \\ &= \mu\,\Phi(\tfrac{\mu}{\sigma}) + \sigma\,\varphi(\tfrac{\mu}{\sigma}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: πH(U) is the chance the human lets the action go ahead. A noisily rational human says yes more often the better the action is, with reliability β. A perfectly reliable human (β → ∞) says yes exactly when U > 0, which gives the closed form for U ~ N(μ, σ2).
Say it“Deferring is worth the expected value of U times the chance the human says yes; with a perfect human, the expected value of max of U and zero.”

Real-world project

Project: which test should the clinic order first?

The data are the Cleveland Clinic Foundation records from the UCI Heart Disease collection (Janosi, Steinbrunn, Pfisterer and Detrano, 1988; Detrano et al., 1989), licensed CC BY 4.0. Each of the 303 patients had coronary angiography. The label is whether any major vessel was more than 50% narrowed. Six patients with missing values are dropped, leaving 297, of whom 137 have heart disease. The test prices come with the dataset: Peter Turney (1995) attached costs in Canadian dollars from the Ontario Health Insurance Program’s fee schedule, with discounts when several measurements come from one procedure. Grouping the measurements as his files do gives five procedures.

The network is naive Bayes: heart disease is the parent of every other node, and each node is conditionally independent of the others given the disease. The four interview items (age band, sex, chest-pain type, resting blood pressure) are known at the start. Each procedure is one chance node whose value is the joint result of everything it measures. Its table is estimated by counting with Laplace smoothing. Every patient is evaluated by leave-one-out: the tables used for patient i are counted from the other 296. The utility is minus the dollars spent: tests, plus $LFN for a missed disease or $LFP for a needless treatment. Those two losses are a value judgement, so they are sliders.

The same evaluation in Python

import json, sys, numpy as np
from sklearn.naive_bayes import CategoricalNB
D = json.load(open("x066-data.json")); A, y = np.array(D["X"]), np.array(D["y"]); N = len(y)
KA = [4, 2, 4, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 2, 3, 3, 4, 3]          # categories per attribute: age band, sex, chest pain, BP band, chol band,
                                                       #   fbs, restecg, max-HR band, exang, ST-depression band, slope, ca, thal
NODES = [[0], [1], [2], [3], [4, 5], [6], [8, 9, 10], [7, 12], [11]]   # one network node per interview item or procedure
def joint(a, at):                                      # a procedure's result = the joint value of everything it measures
    o = 0
    for j in at: o = o * KA[j] + a[j]
    return o
X = np.array([[joint(a, g) for g in NODES] for a in A]); K = [int(np.prod([KA[j] for j in g])) for g in NODES]; nF = len(K)
INTERVIEW = [0, 1, 2, 3]                               # known before any test is ordered
PROC = [("Blood tests", 4, 10.37), ("Resting ECG", 5, 15.50), ("Exercise ECG", 6, 89.30),
        ("Thallium scan", 7, 103.90), ("Fluoroscopy", 8, 100.90)]    # Turney (1995) costs in CAD, group discounts applied
LFN, LFP = (float(sys.argv[1]), float(sys.argv[2])) if len(sys.argv) > 2 else (5000.0, 1000.0)

cnt = np.zeros((nF, 2, max(K))); cls = np.bincount(y, minlength=2).astype(float)
for x, d in zip(X, y): cnt[np.arange(nF), d, x] += 1
def model(i=None):                                     # naive Bayes with Laplace smoothing, leaving patient i out
    c, n = cnt.copy(), cls.copy()
    if i is not None: c[np.arange(nF), y[i], X[i]] -= 1; n[y[i]] -= 1
    return np.log(n / n.sum()), [np.log((c[j, :, :K[j]] + 1) / (n[:, None] + K[j])) for j in range(nF)]
sig = lambda L: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-L))
stop = lambda p: np.minimum(p * LFN, (1 - p) * LFP)    # expected loss of the better action at P(disease) = p
treat = lambda p: p * LFN > (1 - p) * LFP

nb = CategoricalNB(alpha=1.0, min_categories=K).fit(X, y); lpri, lp = model()
L = lpri[1] - lpri[0] + sum(lp[j][1, X[:, j]] - lp[j][0, X[:, j]] for j in range(nF))
print(f"patients {N}, with disease {y.sum()}; max |P(disease | all nodes) - sklearn CategoricalNB| = {np.abs(sig(L) - nb.predict_proba(X)[:, 1]).max():.1e}")
def mi(j):                                              # I(node; disease) in bits, from the raw counts
    c = cnt[j, :, :K[j]] / N; px = c.sum(0); pd = cls / N
    return sum(c[d, t] * np.log2(c[d, t] / (px[t] * pd[d])) for d in range(2) for t in range(K[j]) if c[d, t] > 0)
print("mutual information with disease (bits):", ", ".join(f"{mi(j):.3f}" for j in range(nF)))

nP, nO = len(PROC), [K[f] for _, f, _ in PROC]
def plan(L0, T):                                        # exact optimal (nonmyopic) test plan: dynamic programming over subsets
    llr = [t[1] - t[0] for t in T]; P1 = [np.exp(t[1]) for t in T]; P0 = [np.exp(t[0]) for t in T]; V, Act = {}, {}
    for m in sorted(range(1 << nP), key=lambda m: -bin(m).count("1")):
        S = [j for j in range(nP) if m >> j & 1]; Lm = np.full([nO[j] for j in S], L0)
        for a, j in enumerate(S): Lm = Lm + llr[j].reshape([-1 if b == a else 1 for b in range(len(S))])
        p = sig(Lm); v = stop(p); act = np.full(v.shape, -1)
        for j in range(nP):
            if m >> j & 1: continue
            Vc = np.moveaxis(V[m | 1 << j], sum(1 for s in S if s < j), -1)
            cont = PROC[j][2] + ((p[..., None] * P1[j] + (1 - p[..., None]) * P0[j]) * Vc).sum(-1)
            better = cont < v - 1e-9; v = np.where(better, cont, v); act = np.where(better, j, act)
        V[m], Act[m] = v, act
    return V, Act
def vpi(L, T, j):                                       # expected drop in decision loss from seeing procedure j
    p = sig(L); Po = p * np.exp(T[j][1]) + (1 - p) * np.exp(T[j][0])
    return stop(p) - (Po * stop(sig(L + T[j][1] - T[j][0]))).sum()
def run(x, L0, T, rule, Act=None):
    done, L, m = [], L0, 0
    while True:
        left = [j for j in range(nP) if j not in done]; j = -1
        if rule == "dp":
            j = int(Act[m][tuple(x[PROC[s][1]] for s in range(nP) if m >> s & 1)])
        elif rule in ("net", "ratio") and left:
            g = {k: vpi(L, T, k) for k in left}
            score = {k: g[k] - PROC[k][2] if rule == "net" else g[k] / PROC[k][2] for k in left}

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A decision network is a Bayesian network plus decision nodes (rectangles) and utility nodes (diamonds). Arcs into a decision say what is known when it is made.
  • Evaluate it by inference: set the evidence, try each action, compute the expected utility from the posterior over the utility node’s parents, and keep the best.
  • The value of perfect information is the expected utility of acting after you see a variable, minus that of acting now. It is never negative, and it is zero unless some result would change your choice.
  • VPI is not additive but it is order-independent. A myopic agent orders the test whose value most exceeds its cost, and can miss pairs of tests that are only worth ordering together.
  • Check how sensitive the decision is to its numbers, and remember that the utilities may be uncertain too. A machine unsure of what you want has a reason to let you switch it off.

Check your understanding

A test is cheap and very accurate, yet its VPI is zero. How can that be?

Because no result could change the decision. If the patient’s probability is far above the treatment threshold, even a negative result from an excellent test leaves it above, so you would treat either way. Information is valuable only through the decisions it changes.

After a positive result the patient’s expected utility went down. Doesn’t that contradict “VPI is never negative”?

No. VPI is an average over the results you might get, computed before you look. A particular result can be bad news, but the good-news branch and the better decisions it enables more than make up for it on average. The convex envelope guarantees the average is at least today’s value.

Two tests each have VPI 0, yet ordering both is worth it. What property is this, and which agent misses it?

Non-additivity, here superadditivity: neither result alone can move the belief across the threshold, but two agreeing results can. The myopic agent prices each test as if it were the last, sees zero for both, and orders neither; a nonmyopic planner sees the value of the pair.

What does an action–utility table give up compared with the full network?

The probabilities are baked into it. If the disease prevalence or a test’s accuracy changes, the table has to be rebuilt from scratch, whereas the network only needs one table updated. The table is smaller and faster to use, which is why reinforcement learning’s Q-functions take this form.

Why does a robot that is certain of its human’s preferences gain nothing from deferring?

If it is certain that U > 0, the human would say yes anyway, so asking only costs time. If it is certain that U < 0 it would switch itself off. The value of deferring is the VPI of the human’s answer, E[max(U, 0)] − max(E[U], 0), which is zero when there is no uncertainty about the sign of U.
