Linear Dynamical Systems & the Kalman Filter

A hidden state that moves smoothly, a sensor that lies a little, and a belief that never stops being a bell curve.

An HMM tracks a hidden state that takes one of a few values. Many real states are continuous: where a car is, how fast it is going. If the state moves linearly and every noise is Gaussian, the belief about it stays exactly Gaussian for ever, and keeping it up to date costs a few small matrix products per step. That is the Kalman filter. In the figure, the faint dots are noisy position fixes and the purple ellipse is the filter’s belief. Move your pointer (or finger) over the figure to drive the car, and watch the ellipse stay close to it while the dots scatter.

The key idea

Beliefs that stay Gaussian

Chapter 6.14 filtered an HMM: keep a belief over the hidden state, push it through the transition model, then reweight it by how well each state explains the new observation. The same two steps work for a continuous state, but now the belief is a density, and the prediction step becomes an integral over every value the previous state could have had:

\[ \begin{aligned} &p(z_{t+1} \mid x_{1:t}) \\ &\quad = \int p(z_{t+1} \mid z_t)\; p(z_t \mid x_{1:t})\; dz_t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: z is the hidden state and x the observations, as in Bishop. To predict where the state will be next, take every possible current value zt, weight it by how much we believe in it, move it forward with the transition model, and add up the results. For a general density this integral has no closed form, and a filter that has to approximate it every step slowly drifts.
Say it“p of z at t plus one given x one to t is the integral of p of z t-plus-one given z t, times p of z t given x one to t, d z t.”

Gaussians escape the problem. If the belief is Gaussian and the motion is linear plus Gaussian noise, zt+1 = a zt + u + noise, the integral is again a Gaussian: the mean moves to aμ + u and the variances add. Then the update multiplies the predicted density by the likelihood of the new reading and renormalises:

\[ \begin{aligned} &p(z_{t+1} \mid x_{1:t+1}) \\ &\quad = \alpha\; p(x_{t+1} \mid z_{t+1})\; p(z_{t+1} \mid x_{1:t}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the new belief is the prediction times the sensor model, scaled by α so that it integrates to one. If the sensor reads the state plus Gaussian noise, both factors are exponentials of quadratics in zt+1. Their product is the exponential of a sum of quadratics, which is again a quadratic: the result is Gaussian. The family is closed under both steps, so two numbers (or a mean vector and a covariance matrix) describe the belief exactly, at every step.
Say it“The filtered belief is alpha times the sensor likelihood times the predicted belief.”

Completing the square in that product gives the rule for multiplying two bells. It is the whole Kalman filter in one dimension:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathcal{N}(z;\, m, s^2)\, \mathcal{N}(x;\, z, r^2) \\ &\qquad \propto \mathcal{N}(z;\, \mu_{\text{new}}, \sigma^2_{\text{new}}) \\[4pt] &\mu_{\text{new}} = \frac{r^2 m + s^2 x}{s^2 + r^2}, \quad \frac{1}{\sigma^2_{\text{new}}} = \frac{1}{s^2} + \frac{1}{r^2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the prediction says m with variance s²; the sensor says x with variance r². The new mean is a weighted average of the two, each weighted by the other’s variance, so the more precise source pulls harder. Precisions (one over variance) add, so the new belief is always narrower than either source alone.
Say it“A Gaussian at m times a Gaussian at x is a Gaussian at r-squared m plus s-squared x over s-squared plus r-squared, with precision one over s-squared plus one over r-squared.”

A first filter

A one-dimensional example

Here is the smallest useful model, the one Russell and Norvig use. A quantity wanders as a random walk: each step it moves by Gaussian noise of variance σx², with no drift. Each step we read it through a sensor with Gaussian noise of variance σz². The belief starts as a Gaussian with mean μ0 and variance σ0². One prediction (variance grows by σx²) followed by one update (multiply by the reading’s bell) gives the new mean and variance:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_{t+1} &= \frac{(\sigma_t^2 + \sigma_x^2)\, x_{t+1} + \sigma_z^2\, \mu_t}{\sigma_t^2 + \sigma_x^2 + \sigma_z^2} \\ &= \mu_t + k_{t+1}\,(x_{t+1} - \mu_t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the new estimate is the old one, moved towards the reading xt+1 by a fraction kt+1 = (σt² + σx²) / (σt² + σx² + σz²) of the surprise. That fraction is the Kalman gain. A noisy sensor (large σz) gives a small gain; a fast-moving state or a vague belief gives a gain near 1.
Say it“Mu at t plus one is mu t plus k times the reading minus mu t.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma^2_{t+1} &= \frac{(\sigma_t^2 + \sigma_x^2)\, \sigma_z^2}{\sigma_t^2 + \sigma_x^2 + \sigma_z^2} \\ &= (1 - k_{t+1})(\sigma_t^2 + \sigma_x^2) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the predicted variance σt² + σx² is cut by the factor 1 − k. Notice what is missing: the reading x. The variance sequence depends only on the noise levels, so it can be computed before any data arrive, and it settles quickly to a fixed point.
Say it“Sigma squared at t plus one is one minus k, times sigma t squared plus sigma x squared.”
\[ \sigma_\infty^2 = \frac{-\sigma_x^2 + \sqrt{\sigma_x^4 + 4\sigma_x^2\sigma_z^2}}{2} \]
Read it as: set σt+1 = σt in the variance update and solve the quadratic σ4 + σx²σ2 − σx²σz² = 0. Its positive root is the steady-state variance, the best the filter can ever do with these two noise levels. It is below σz²: averaging over time beats any single reading.
Say it“Sigma infinity squared is minus sigma x squared plus the root of sigma x to the fourth plus four sigma x squared sigma z squared, all over two.”

The model

Linear dynamical systems

A linear dynamical system (LDS), also called a linear-Gaussian state-space model, is the vector version. The hidden state zt is a vector, it moves by a matrix A plus Gaussian noise, and each observation xt is a matrix C times the state plus Gaussian noise. The graph is exactly the HMM chain of chapter 6.14; only the kind of variable has changed, from a few discrete values to a point in a continuous space.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(z_t \mid z_{t-1}) &= \mathcal{N}(z_t \mid A z_{t-1},\, \Gamma) \\ p(x_t \mid z_t) &= \mathcal{N}(x_t \mid C z_t,\, \Sigma) \\ p(z_1) &= \mathcal{N}(z_1 \mid \mu_0,\, V_0) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the next state is A times the current one, blurred by noise with covariance Γ. What we see is C times the state, blurred by noise with covariance Σ. The first state has its own Gaussian. Equivalently, zt = A zt−1 + wt and xt = C zt + vt, with w and v independent zero-mean Gaussian noise. Because every piece is linear-Gaussian, all the z’s and x’s together form one big joint Gaussian.
Say it“z t given z t-minus-one is normal with mean A z t-minus-one and covariance Gamma; x t given z t is normal with mean C z t and covariance Sigma.”

The two textbooks name the same parts differently. This chapter uses Bishop’s letters, but writes time as t rather than n:

Choosing A is choosing the physics. In the lab, A rotates the two-dimensional state by an angle θ and shrinks it by a factor ρ each step, so the state spirals. Its eigenvalues are ρe±iθ: if ρ < 1 the system is stable and settles into a cloud whose size balances the noise, and the stationary covariance P solves P = APAT + Γ:

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= \rho \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}, \quad \Gamma = \gamma^2 I \\ &\Rightarrow\; P_\infty = \frac{\gamma^2}{1 - \rho^2}\, I \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a rotation does not change the size of the cloud, so only the shrink ρ fights the noise. Each step the cloud’s variance is multiplied by ρ² and then grows by γ², which balances at γ² / (1 − ρ²). As ρ approaches 1 that blows up: an undamped system wanders without limit.
Say it“A is rho times a rotation by theta; with Gamma equal to gamma squared times the identity, the stationary covariance is gamma squared over one minus rho squared, times the identity.”

Change the angle, the damping and the two noise levels, and choose whether the sensor sees one coordinate or both. With C = (1 0) the second coordinate is never observed at all: it can only be inferred through the dynamics, which is exactly how a Kalman filter recovers a car’s speed from position fixes.

Inference, forward

The general Kalman filter

Filtering asks for p(zt | x1:t), the belief about the current state given every observation so far. Since the joint is Gaussian, this is a Gaussian with some mean μt and covariance Vt, and the job is to update those two from one step to the next. It is the HMM’s forward recursion with sums turned into integrals, and every integral done in closed form. First the prediction, which pushes the belief through the dynamics:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{mean: }& A\mu_{t-1} \\ \text{covariance: }& P_{t-1} = A V_{t-1} A^{\mathsf T} + \Gamma \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: move the mean with the dynamics. The covariance is transformed by A on both sides (a linear map of a Gaussian), then the fresh process noise Γ is added. Uncertainty always grows during a prediction.
Say it“Predicted mean A mu; predicted covariance P equals A V A-transpose plus Gamma.”

Then the correction, which compares the new observation with the one the prediction expected. The difference, xt − CAμt−1, is the innovation: the part of the reading the model did not see coming.

\[ K_t = P_{t-1} C^{\mathsf T} \big( C P_{t-1} C^{\mathsf T} + \Sigma \big)^{-1} \]
Read it as: the gain compares how uncertain the prediction is, as seen through the sensor (CPCT), with how noisy the sensor is (Σ). It is the matrix version of k = s² / (s² + r²). Its rows also say how much each unobserved part of the state should move: through the correlations in P, a position surprise changes the velocity estimate too.
Say it“K t equals P C-transpose times the inverse of C P C-transpose plus Sigma.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_t &= A\mu_{t-1} + K_t\,\big(x_t - C A \mu_{t-1}\big) \\ V_t &= (I - K_t C)\, P_{t-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: new mean = prediction + gain × innovation. New covariance = predicted covariance with the part the sensor explained removed. At the first step, use μ0 and V0 in place of Aμt−1 and Pt−1. If an observation is missing, skip the correction and keep the prediction.
Say it“Mu t is A mu plus K times the innovation; V t is I minus K C, times P.”
\[ \begin{aligned} c_t &= p(x_t \mid x_{1:t-1}) \\ &= \mathcal{N}\big(x_t \mid C A\mu_{t-1},\; C P_{t-1} C^{\mathsf T} + \Sigma\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: before the reading arrives, the filter predicts it as a Gaussian. Evaluating that Gaussian at the actual reading gives the factor ct, and the product of all of them is the likelihood of the whole sequence, p(x1:T) = ∏t ct. EM and model comparison both need it, and it comes free with the filter.
Say it“c t, the probability of x t given the past, is normal at C A mu with covariance C P C-transpose plus Sigma.”

Inference, backward

Smoothing: using the future

The filter is causal: its estimate at time t ignores everything after t. Once the whole sequence is in hand we can do better and ask for p(zt | x1:T). In the HMM this needed a backward message β. For the LDS the standard form, the Rauch–Tung–Striebel (RTS) smoother, runs backwards from the last filtered belief and corrects each earlier one directly:

\[ \begin{aligned} J_t &= V_t A^{\mathsf T} P_t^{-1} \\ \hat\mu_t &= \mu_t + J_t\big(\hat\mu_{t+1} - A\mu_t\big) \\ \hat V_t &= V_t + J_t\big(\hat V_{t+1} - P_t\big) J_t^{\mathsf T} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Pt = AVtAT + Γ is the filter’s prediction covariance for step t + 1. Jt says how much a change in our belief about zt+1 should move our belief about zt. The smoothed mean is the filtered mean plus J times the amount by which hindsight moved the next state away from where the filter predicted it. The smoothed covariance can only shrink, because V̂t+1 ≤ Pt. The recursion starts from μ̂T = μT, V̂T = VT.
Say it“J is V A-transpose P-inverse; mu-hat t is mu t plus J times mu-hat t-plus-one minus A mu t; V-hat t is V t plus J times V-hat t-plus-one minus P, times J-transpose.”
\[ \operatorname{cov}\big(z_{t}, z_{t-1} \mid x_{1:T}\big) = \hat V_{t} J_{t-1}^{\mathsf T} \]
Read it as: the smoother also gives the covariance between neighbouring states for free. Learning needs these pairwise terms, just as Baum–Welch needed the pairwise ξ in chapter 6.15.
Say it“The covariance of z t and z t-minus-one, given all the data, is V-hat t times J t-minus-one transpose.”

Two more facts come with the Gaussian. A missing reading is no problem: the filter skips the update and the smoother bridges the gap. And because the joint posterior over the whole path is Gaussian, its single most probable path (the LDS version of Viterbi) is simply the sequence of smoothed means; no separate max-product pass is needed. Prediction of the future is the filter with every later reading missing.

Learning

Learning an LDS with EM

So far A, Γ, C and Σ were given. Usually they are not: we know the readings and must learn the model. If the states were visible, fitting would be linear regression of zt on zt−1 and of xt on zt. They are hidden, so we use EM, exactly as Baum–Welch does for the HMM. The E-step runs the filter and the smoother with the current parameters and collects three expectations under the posterior:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[z_t] &= \hat\mu_t \\ \mathbb{E}[z_t z_t^{\mathsf T}] &= \hat V_t + \hat\mu_t \hat\mu_t^{\mathsf T} \\ \mathbb{E}[z_t z_{t-1}^{\mathsf T}] &= \hat V_t J_{t-1}^{\mathsf T} + \hat\mu_t \hat\mu_{t-1}^{\mathsf T} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the mean, the second moment and the lag-one cross moment of the hidden path, each “covariance plus mean times mean”. They are the sufficient statistics: the expected complete-data log-likelihood depends on the data only through these sums.
Say it“The expected z is mu-hat; the expected z z-transpose is V-hat plus mu-hat mu-hat-transpose; the expected z t z t-minus-one transpose is V-hat J-transpose plus mu-hat t mu-hat t-minus-one transpose.”

The M-step maximises the expected complete-data log-likelihood. Each update is a regression with the unknown states replaced by their expected moments:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_0 &= \mathbb{E}[z_1] \\ V_0 &= \mathbb{E}[z_1 z_1^{\mathsf T}] - \mathbb{E}[z_1]\mathbb{E}[z_1^{\mathsf T}] \\ A &= \Big(\textstyle\sum_{t=2}^{T} \mathbb{E}[z_t z_{t-1}^{\mathsf T}]\Big) \\ &\qquad \times \Big(\textstyle\sum_{t=2}^{T} \mathbb{E}[z_{t-1} z_{t-1}^{\mathsf T}]\Big)^{-1} \\ C &= \Big(\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T} x_t\, \mathbb{E}[z_t^{\mathsf T}]\Big) \\ &\qquad \times \Big(\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}[z_t z_t^{\mathsf T}]\Big)^{-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first state’s prior becomes the posterior over z1. A is “cross moment over second moment”, the least-squares slope of zt on zt−1; C is the slope of xt on zt. In one dimension A = ∑ E[ztzt−1] / ∑ E[zt−1²].
Say it“A is the sum of the cross moments times the inverse of the sum of the previous second moments; C is the sum of x times expected z-transpose, times the inverse of the sum of second moments.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \Gamma &= \tfrac{1}{T-1} \textstyle\sum_{t=2}^{T} \Big( \mathbb{E}[z_t z_t^{\mathsf T}] - A\,\mathbb{E}[z_{t-1} z_t^{\mathsf T}] \\ &\qquad - \mathbb{E}[z_t z_{t-1}^{\mathsf T}] A^{\mathsf T} + A\,\mathbb{E}[z_{t-1} z_{t-1}^{\mathsf T}] A^{\mathsf T} \Big) \\ \Sigma &= \tfrac{1}{T} \textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T} \Big( x_t x_t^{\mathsf T} - C\,\mathbb{E}[z_t]\, x_t^{\mathsf T} \\ &\qquad - x_t\, \mathbb{E}[z_t^{\mathsf T}] C^{\mathsf T} + C\,\mathbb{E}[z_t z_t^{\mathsf T}] C^{\mathsf T} \Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each bracket is the expected value of (zt − Azt−1)(zt − Azt−1)T or (xt − Czt)(xt − Czt)T expanded out: the average squared residual of each regression, where the average also counts the posterior uncertainty about the states. EM never lowers the likelihood, so the log-likelihood from the filter must rise every iteration.
Say it“Gamma is the average expected squared residual of the state regression; Sigma is the average expected squared residual of the observation regression.”

One caution. The model can be rescaled without changing what it predicts: replace z by 2z, C by C/2, Γ by 4Γ and V0 by 4V0, and every distribution over x is the same. So C and the scale of z cannot both be identified from data. The lab therefore fixes C = 1 in a one-dimensional model and learns a, γ², σ², μ0 and V0 with exactly the updates above.

Limits and extensions

When the Kalman filter applies, and when it breaks

The Kalman filter is exact only when the model is linear and Gaussian. Within that world it is hard to beat, and it has been used almost everywhere something must be tracked from noisy readings: aircraft and missiles by radar, ships and submarines by sonar, spacecraft (it flew on the Apollo missions), robots and phones by GPS and inertial sensors, and quantities in economics, weather and process control. Outside that world, three things go wrong.

Nonlinear motion or sensors. If zt = f(zt−1) + noise or xt = h(zt) + noise, a Gaussian pushed through f is no longer Gaussian. The extended Kalman filter (EKF) linearises f and h around the current mean, replacing A and C by Jacobians, and runs the ordinary filter:

\[ A_t = \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\Big|_{\mu_{t-1}}, \qquad C_t = \frac{\partial h}{\partial z}\Big|_{f(\mu_{t-1})} \]
Read it as: near the mean, approximate each curve by its tangent. The predicted mean is f(μt−1) and the predicted reading is h of that; the covariances use the slopes. This works when the function is close to straight over the width of the belief, and fails when it is not.
Say it“A t is the derivative of f at the last mean; C t is the derivative of h at the predicted mean.”

Beliefs with more than one hump. Russell and Norvig’s picture is a bird flying straight at a tree. A sensible model says it will swerve left or right; the true belief has two humps, one each side of the trunk. A single Gaussian cannot have two humps, so the filter puts its mean in the middle, inside the tree. A switching Kalman filter runs several filters in parallel, one per mode of behaviour (“swerve left”, “swerve right”), and weights them by how well each predicts the readings: a mixture of Gaussians. Bishop’s switching state-space model is the same idea with a hidden discrete Markov chain choosing which of several LDS is active.

Non-Gaussian noise. If the emission density is a mixture of K Gaussians (say, mostly good readings plus occasional wild ones), the exact posterior after t steps is a mixture of Kt Gaussians, one for every way of labelling the readings, which explodes. The practical answer is assumed density filtering: after each step, replace the mixture by the single Gaussian with the same mean and covariance (moment matching), and carry on. The general answer, for any model at all, is to represent the belief by samples: the particle filter of the next chapter.

The lab makes these failures visible. It runs an exact filter on a fine grid of 301 state values (the shaded density, which can take any shape) next to the EKF (the amber band). Outliers: the reading is z plus noise of sd 1 nine times in ten, and of sd 10 otherwise.

Real-world project

Project: cleaning up a real phone’s GPS track

The data are real trips recorded by Android phones with the Go!Track app in Aracaju, Brazil: the GPS Trajectories dataset of Cruz, Macedo, Barreto and Guimarães, from the UCI Machine Learning Repository, licensed CC BY 4.0. Each track is a list of latitude, longitude and time, a fix every five or six seconds. The page embeds six car trips, identified by the track id in the file, converted to metres east and north of each track’s centre. (Google’s Smartphone Decimeter Challenge data, which come with survey-grade ground truth, can only be downloaded through a Kaggle account, so this openly downloadable phone dataset is used instead.)

There is no ground truth, so we cannot measure the error of a fix directly. Instead we hide some fixes, run everything on the rest, and see how well each method predicts the hidden ones. The model is the constant-velocity car in two dimensions: state (east, north, veast, vnorth), A = ((I, ΔtI), (0, I)) with the real time gap Δt between fixes, C = (I 0), and the white-noise-acceleration process noise

\[ \begin{aligned} \Gamma_t &= q \begin{pmatrix} \Delta_t^3/3 & \Delta_t^2/2 \\ \Delta_t^2/2 & \Delta_t \end{pmatrix} \text{ per axis} \\ \Sigma &= r\, I \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: between fixes the car’s acceleration is random, with intensity q (in m²/s³). Integrating it over a gap Δt gives this covariance for position and velocity: longer gaps mean much more uncertain positions. Each fix is the true position plus independent noise of variance r on each axis. Only two numbers are unknown, q and r, and EM learns both: the M-step is the general one above with Γ and Σ restricted to these shapes, so q = (1/4(T−1)) ∑ tr(Γt(1)−1 E[…]) and r = (1/2T) ∑ E[|xt − Czt|²].
Say it“Gamma t is q times the matrix delta-cubed over three, delta-squared over two, delta-squared over two, delta, on each axis; Sigma is r times the identity.”

Four ways to guess a hidden fix are compared, two causal and two not. Last fix repeats the most recent visible fix. Kalman is the filter’s prediction from the visible fixes before it. Linear interpolates in time between the visible fixes on either side. RTS is the smoother’s estimate from all visible fixes. Even a perfect method would not score zero, because the hidden fix has its own noise.

The same computation in Python

The reference script reads the two CSV files, converts each track to local metres, rounds to the centimetre exactly as the page does, and runs the same filter, smoother and EM with numpy. As an independent check it also builds the whole posterior as one large Gaussian with scipy for the first 150 fixes of track 41: the filter’s log-likelihood and the smoother’s means must match exact Gaussian conditioning.

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
H = np.zeros((2, 4)); H[0, 0] = H[1, 1] = 1           # we observe position only
def model(dt, q):                                     # constant velocity, white-noise acceleration
    F = np.eye(4); F[0, 2] = F[1, 3] = dt
    B = np.array([[dt**3 / 3, dt**2 / 2], [dt**2 / 2, dt]]); Q = np.zeros((4, 4))
    Q[np.ix_([0, 2], [0, 2])] = B; Q[np.ix_([1, 3], [1, 3])] = B
    return F, q * Q

def kalman(X, dt, seen, q, r, m0, V0):                # forward pass; unseen fixes are predicted only
    N = len(X); mu, V, mp, Pp = np.zeros((N, 4)), np.zeros((N, 4, 4)), np.zeros((N, 4)), np.zeros((N, 4, 4)); ll = 0.0
    for n in range(N):
        if n == 0: m, P = m0.copy(), V0.copy()
        else: F, Q = model(dt[n], q); m, P = F @ mu[n - 1], F @ V[n - 1] @ F.T + Q
        mp[n], Pp[n] = m, P
        if seen[n]:
            S = H @ P @ H.T + r * np.eye(2); K = P @ H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S); e = X[n] - H @ m
            ll += -0.5 * (e @ np.linalg.solve(S, e) + np.log(np.linalg.det(S)) + 2 * np.log(2 * np.pi))
            m, P = m + K @ e, (np.eye(4) - K @ H) @ P
        mu[n], V[n] = m, P
    return mu, V, mp, Pp, ll

def rts(mu, V, mp, Pp, dt, q):                        # Rauch-Tung-Striebel backward pass
    ms, Vs, J = mu.copy(), V.copy(), np.zeros((len(mu), 4, 4))
    for n in range(len(mu) - 2, -1, -1):
        F, _ = model(dt[n + 1], q); J[n] = V[n] @ F.T @ np.linalg.inv(Pp[n + 1])
        ms[n] = mu[n] + J[n] @ (ms[n + 1] - mp[n + 1]); Vs[n] = V[n] + J[n] @ (Vs[n + 1] - Pp[n + 1]) @ J[n].T
    return ms, Vs, J

def em(X, dt, seen, q, r, m0, V0, iters):             # EM for the two noise scales q and r
    hist = []
    for it in range(iters + 1):
        mu, V, mp, Pp, ll = kalman(X, dt, seen, q, r, m0, V0); hist.append((q, r, ll))
        if it == iters: break
        ms, Vs, J = rts(mu, V, mp, Pp, dt, q)
        e = X[seen] - ms[seen, :2]
        r = (np.sum(e * e) + sum(np.trace(H @ Vs[n] @ H.T) for n in np.where(seen)[0])) / (2 * seen.sum())
        s = 0.0
        for n in range(1, len(X)):
            F, Q1 = model(dt[n], 1.0)
            Ezz = Vs[n] + np.outer(ms[n], ms[n]); Ez1 = Vs[n] @ J[n - 1].T + np.outer(ms[n], ms[n - 1])
            Eyy = Vs[n - 1] + np.outer(ms[n - 1], ms[n - 1])
            s += np.trace(np.linalg.solve(Q1, Ezz - F @ Ez1.T - Ez1 @ F.T + F @ Eyy @ F.T))
        q = s / (4 * (len(X) - 1))
    return q, r, hist

The browser runs the same algorithm in double precision, so it reproduces the Python numbers to the digits shown; the only differences are rounding in the last places, because the page inverts its small matrices with its own Gauss–Jordan routine where numpy calls LAPACK. What the numbers say is honest about the data too. EM’s r is only about 2 m of noise per axis, far less than the error a phone’s fix really has. That is because most GPS error drifts slowly (reflections, the satellites in view), and a model with independent noise at every fix cannot separate a slow drift from the car’s own motion: it puts the drift into the state. The smoother is excellent at removing jitter and bridging gaps, but it cannot see a bias that every fix shares.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A linear dynamical system is an HMM with a continuous state: zt = Azt−1 + noise, xt = Czt + noise, all Gaussian. Gaussians are closed under linear prediction and under multiplication by a Gaussian likelihood, so the belief stays exactly Gaussian.
  • The Kalman filter alternates predict (μ → Aμ, V → AVAT + Γ) and update (add K times the innovation, shrink the covariance). The gain K = PCT(CPCT + Σ)−1 weighs prediction against sensor; in one dimension it is s² / (s² + r²).
  • The covariances and gains do not depend on the readings, and they converge to a steady state. The filter’s predictive densities multiply to the exact likelihood of the data.
  • The RTS smoother runs backwards with Jt = VtATPt−1 to give p(zt | x1:T) and the pairwise covariances. EM uses those moments as its E-step and regression-like updates for A, Γ, C, Σ as its M-step; the likelihood never falls.
  • The filter is exact only for linear-Gaussian models. The EKF linearises; switching filters and assumed density filtering handle several modes or mixture noise; arbitrary beliefs need particle filters.

Check your understanding

The prediction has sd 3 and the sensor sd 4. What is the gain, and what sd does the updated belief have?

The gain is s² / (s² + r²) = 9 / 25 = 0.36: the belief moves 36% of the way to the reading. The precisions add, 1/9 + 1/16 = 25/144, so the new variance is 144/25 = 5.76 and the sd is 2.4, smaller than both 3 and 4.

You could compute the whole sequence of Kalman gains before switching the sensor on. Why?

The covariance recursion V → AVAT + Γ → (I − KC)P uses only A, Γ, C, Σ and V0, never the readings. So the gains and covariances are fixed in advance, which is why embedded systems often store the steady-state gain and skip the covariance update entirely. (This holds only for a known linear model; the EKF’s Jacobians depend on the estimate, so its gains do not.)

A sensor reports only position. How does the filter ever learn the velocity?

Through the dynamics. The prediction P = AVAT + Γ with A = ((1, Δ), (0, 1)) correlates position with velocity: a faster car would be further ahead. The gain’s velocity entry is that covariance divided by the innovation variance, so every position surprise nudges the velocity. Over a few steps the velocity sd falls well below its starting value, as the filter lab shows.

Why is the smoothed variance never larger than the filtered one?

V̂t = Vt + J(V̂t+1 − Pt)JT, and V̂t+1 ≤ Pt because the smoothed belief about the next step uses more data than the one-step prediction. So the correction term is negative semi-definite. Intuitively, conditioning on more observations can never make a Gaussian belief less certain.

In the project, why does the RTS smoother beat linear interpolation, and why does EM’s r look too small?

Linear interpolation trusts the two neighbouring fixes completely, noise and all, and assumes straight-line motion between them. The smoother averages many fixes, weighting each by the learned noise, and follows curved motion through the velocity. EM’s r is small because it measures only the part of the GPS error that is independent from fix to fix; error that drifts slowly looks to the model like real motion and is absorbed into the state.
