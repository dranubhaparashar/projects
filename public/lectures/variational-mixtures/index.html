Variational Mixture of Gaussians

Put a prior on everything, and let the data switch components off

Maximum likelihood cannot tell you how many clusters there are: more components always fit better, and a component can even shrink onto a single point and send the likelihood to infinity. Give the weights, means and precisions priors, approximate the posterior with one factorisation, and three things change at once. Nothing collapses, the fit comes with error bars, and components the data do not need are emptied out. The figure starts with eight components on the points you see; variational Bayes runs live, and the bars underneath show each component’s expected weight. Click anywhere to drop a new clump of points, then watch how many components it keeps.

PRML 10.2 · the Bayesian mixture

A mixture with a prior on everything

The mixture of Gaussians from the EM chapter has three kinds of unknown. Each point xn has a hidden label zn, a 1-of-K vector saying which component produced it. The mixing weights π say how common each component is. Each component k has a mean μk and a precision matrix Λk, the inverse of its covariance. EM finds one best value for the parameters. Here every one of them gets a distribution instead.

The priors are chosen to be conjugate, so that each posterior has the same form as its prior and the updates stay in closed form. The weights get a Dirichlet prior with one concentration α0 shared by all K components, so no component is favoured. α0 acts like a number of imaginary points given to each component before any data arrive. Each mean and precision get a Gaussian–Wishart prior: the precision Λk is drawn from a Wishart with scale W0 and ν0 degrees of freedom, then the mean is drawn around m0 with precision β0Λk, so a tight component also has a well-placed mean. The sampler below runs the model forwards and makes whole worlds from the prior. Slide α0 down and draw again: most of the weight piles onto one or two components. Then shrink β0 to scatter the means, or ν0 to make the shapes wilder.

\[ \begin{aligned} &p(\mathbf Z \mid \boldsymbol\pi) = \prod_{n=1}^{N} \prod_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k^{\,z_{nk}} \\ &p(\mathbf X \mid \mathbf Z, \boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda) \\ &\quad = \prod_{n=1}^{N} \prod_{k=1}^{K} \mathcal N\big(\mathbf x_n \mid \boldsymbol\mu_k, \boldsymbol\Lambda_k^{-1}\big)^{z_{nk}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: znk is 1 for the component that made point n and 0 for the others, so each product picks out one factor per point: the chance of that label, and the Gaussian density of the point under that component. This is the same likelihood EM uses.
Say it“Each point picks component k with probability pi-k, then is drawn from a Gaussian with mean mu-k and precision Lambda-k.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(\boldsymbol\pi) &= \operatorname{Dir}(\boldsymbol\pi \mid \boldsymbol\alpha_0) \\ &= C(\boldsymbol\alpha_0) \prod_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k^{\alpha_0 - 1} \\ C(\boldsymbol\alpha) &= \frac{\Gamma(\hat\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha_1)\cdots\Gamma(\alpha_K)} \\ \hat\alpha &= \textstyle\sum_k \alpha_k \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a distribution over weight vectors that add up to 1. With α0 < 1 the density is highest in the corners, where a few weights take nearly everything; with α0 large it piles up at equal weights. C is the constant that makes it integrate to 1.
Say it“pi is Dirichlet with every concentration equal to alpha-nought.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda) &= \prod_{k=1}^{K} \mathcal N\big(\boldsymbol\mu_k \mid \mathbf m_0, (\beta_0 \boldsymbol\Lambda_k)^{-1}\big) \\ &\qquad\times \mathcal W\big(\boldsymbol\Lambda_k \mid \mathbf W_0, \nu_0\big) \\ \mathbb E[\boldsymbol\Lambda_k] &= \nu_0 \mathbf W_0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: first draw a precision matrix from a Wishart; its average is ν0W0, and larger ν0 means less variety around that average. Then draw the mean around m0 with covariance (β0Λk)−1: a small β0 lets the means wander far. In the sampler W0 is set to (ν0σ02)−1I, so the average precision stays at 1/σ02 whatever ν0 is.
Say it“Lambda-k is Wishart, W-nought and nu-nought; given it, mu-k is Gaussian around m-nought with precision beta-nought Lambda-k.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &p(\mathbf X, \mathbf Z, \boldsymbol\pi, \boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda) \\ &\quad = p(\mathbf X \mid \mathbf Z, \boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda)\, p(\mathbf Z \mid \boldsymbol\pi) \\ &\qquad \times p(\boldsymbol\pi)\, p(\boldsymbol\mu \mid \boldsymbol\Lambda)\, p(\boldsymbol\Lambda) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the joint distribution of everything is the product of the pieces, following the arrows of the graphical model: π points to each zn; zn, μ and Λ point to xn; Λ points to μ. Only X is observed. Every other symbol is a latent variable we must infer, and the parameters are now latent variables too.
Say it“The joint is likelihood times label prior times the priors on pi, mu given Lambda, and Lambda.”

PRML 10.2.1 · part 1: the factor for the labels

The variational distribution, and responsibilities with doubt

The exact posterior p(Z, π, μ, Λ | X) is hopeless: summing over every labelling of N points has KN terms. Variational inference picks a simpler family and finds its best member. The one assumption made here is that the labels and the parameters are independent under q. Everything else, including every functional form below, comes out of the mean-field recipe from the previous chapter: the best factor for a group of variables is the exponential of the log joint, averaged over all the other factors.

Applied to Z, the recipe gives a product of independent categorical distributions, one per point, with probabilities rnk: the responsibilities, exactly as in EM. What differs is what goes into them. EM plugs in point estimates of πk, μk and Λk. Variational Bayes averages over its current uncertainty about them, and three averages appear: of ln πk, of ln |Λk|, and of the squared distance. The first two come out below their plug-in values and the distance gains a penalty, all by more for a component that rests on few points. The lab sets up two one-dimensional components as if each had been fitted to Nk points with the mean and spread shown. Drag the test point, then shrink component 2’s count N2 and watch the VB responsibility pull away from the EM one.

\[ \begin{aligned} &q(\mathbf Z, \boldsymbol\pi, \boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda) = q(\mathbf Z)\, q(\boldsymbol\pi, \boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda) \\ &\ln q^\star(\mathbf Z) = \text{const} \\ &\quad + \mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\pi, \boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda}\big[\ln p(\mathbf X, \mathbf Z, \boldsymbol\pi, \boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda)\big] \\ &q^\star(\mathbf Z) = \prod_{n=1}^{N} \prod_{k=1}^{K} r_{nk}^{\,z_{nk}} \\ &r_{nk} = \rho_{nk} \Big/ \textstyle\sum_{j} \rho_{nj} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the only assumption is the split between labels and parameters. Averaging the log joint over the parameter factor leaves a sum over points and components of znk times a number ln ρnk, so the best q(Z) is a product of one categorical distribution per point. Normalising ρ over k gives the responsibilities.
Say it“q-star of Z is the exponential of the expected log joint; it factorises over points, with probabilities r-n-k.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\ln \rho_{nk} = \mathbb E[\ln \pi_k] + \tfrac12 \mathbb E\big[\ln |\boldsymbol\Lambda_k|\big] \\ &\qquad - \tfrac{D}{2} \ln 2\pi - \tfrac12\, \mathbb E\big[\Delta_{nk}^2\big] \\ &\Delta_{nk}^2 = (\mathbf x_n - \boldsymbol\mu_k)^{\mathsf T} \boldsymbol\Lambda_k (\mathbf x_n - \boldsymbol\mu_k) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: EM’s log responsibility, before normalising, is ln πk + ½ ln |Λk| − (D/2) ln 2π − ½Δnk2, where Δnk2 is the squared (Mahalanobis) distance of xn from component k. Variational Bayes has the same four terms, each replaced by its average under q. D is the dimension of x.
Say it“Log rho is the expected log weight, plus half the expected log-determinant, minus half the expected squared distance.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb E\big[\Delta_{nk}^2\big] = D \beta_k^{-1} + \nu_k\, d_{nk}^2 \\ &d_{nk}^2 = (\mathbf x_n - \mathbf m_k)^{\mathsf T} \mathbf W_k (\mathbf x_n - \mathbf m_k) \\ &\ln \tilde\Lambda_k \equiv \mathbb E\big[\ln |\boldsymbol\Lambda_k|\big] \\ &\quad = \sum_{i=1}^{D} \psi\Big(\frac{\nu_k + 1 - i}{2}\Big) \\ &\qquad + D \ln 2 + \ln |\mathbf W_k| \\ &\ln \tilde\pi_k \equiv \mathbb E[\ln \pi_k] = \psi(\alpha_k) - \psi(\hat\alpha) \\ &r_{nk} \propto \tilde\pi_k\, \tilde\Lambda_k^{1/2} \exp\Big\{ -\frac{D}{2\beta_k} - \frac{\nu_k}{2} d_{nk}^2 \Big\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the three averages under the posterior factors of the next section (a Dirichlet with counts αk, a Gaussian–Wishart with mk, βk, Wk, νk). ψ is the digamma function, the derivative of ln Γ. The expected squared distance is νk times dnk2, the distance from mk measured with Wk, plus an extra D/βk for not knowing the mean exactly. Since ψ(a) < ln a, the averaged log weight sits below ln E[πk], and the averaged log-determinant below ln |E[Λk]|, by more when the counts are small. (The effective spread E[Λk]−1 also differs from the plug-in one, because the prior adds width; that can move the responsibilities either way far from the means.)
Say it“r-n-k is proportional to pi-tilde times Lambda-tilde to the half, times e to minus D over two beta, minus nu over two times the W-distance.”

PRML 10.2.1 · part 2: the factor for the parameters

A Bayesian M-step: posteriors, not point estimates

Now hold q(Z) fixed and apply the recipe to the parameters. The responsibilities enter only through three weighted statistics per component: the effective count Nk, the weighted mean x̄k and the weighted covariance Sk, the same numbers EM’s M-step computes. With conjugate priors, each posterior factor is the prior with its counts topped up. The Dirichlet’s αk becomes α0 + Nk. The mean’s precision weight becomes β0 + Nk, and its centre becomes a weighted average of the prior mean m0 and the data mean. The Wishart’s degrees of freedom become ν0 + Nk, and its inverse scale adds the data’s scatter to the prior’s.

Alternating the two factors is variational EM: a VB-E step computes responsibilities from the current posteriors, and a VB-M step recomputes the posteriors from the responsibilities. Each step can only raise the lower bound of the next section. Notice what never had to be assumed: the parameter factor splits by itself into q(π) times one Gaussian–Wishart per component (we come back to why in the section on induced factorisations). Step E and M by hand, then click an ellipse to read that component’s posterior. With α0 = 0.001, unneeded components lose their points and fall back to the prior.

\[ \begin{aligned} N_k &= \sum_{n=1}^{N} r_{nk} \\ \bar{\mathbf x}_k &= \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{n=1}^{N} r_{nk}\, \mathbf x_n \\ \mathbf S_k &= \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{n=1}^{N} r_{nk} (\mathbf x_n - \bar{\mathbf x}_k)(\mathbf x_n - \bar{\mathbf x}_k)^{\mathsf T} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: how many points component k effectively owns, their weighted centre, and their weighted scatter. Every point contributes to every component in proportion to its responsibility.
Say it“N-k is the sum of the responsibilities; x-bar-k and S-k are the responsibility-weighted mean and covariance.”
\[ \begin{aligned} q^\star(\boldsymbol\pi) &= \operatorname{Dir}(\boldsymbol\pi \mid \boldsymbol\alpha), \quad \alpha_k = \alpha_0 + N_k \\ \mathbb E[\pi_k] &= \frac{\alpha_0 + N_k}{K\alpha_0 + N} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the posterior over the weights is again Dirichlet, with each component’s imaginary count α0 plus the real count it has collected. Its expected weight is the share of all counts, real and imaginary. A component that owns no points keeps αk = α0, and with a tiny α0 its expected weight is close to zero.
Say it“Alpha-k is alpha-nought plus N-k; the expected weight is alpha-k over the total.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &q^\star(\boldsymbol\mu_k, \boldsymbol\Lambda_k) = \mathcal N\big(\boldsymbol\mu_k \mid \mathbf m_k, (\beta_k \boldsymbol\Lambda_k)^{-1}\big) \\ &\qquad\qquad \times \mathcal W(\boldsymbol\Lambda_k \mid \mathbf W_k, \nu_k) \\ &\beta_k = \beta_0 + N_k, \qquad \nu_k = \nu_0 + N_k \\ &\mathbf m_k = \frac{1}{\beta_k}\big(\beta_0 \mathbf m_0 + N_k \bar{\mathbf x}_k\big) \\ &\mathbf W_k^{-1} = \mathbf W_0^{-1} + N_k \mathbf S_k \\ &\qquad + \frac{\beta_0 N_k}{\beta_0 + N_k} (\bar{\mathbf x}_k - \mathbf m_0)(\bar{\mathbf x}_k - \mathbf m_0)^{\mathsf T} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same Gaussian–Wishart form as the prior. The mean’s centre mk is a tug of war between m0 (weight β0) and the data mean (weight Nk). Wk−1 collects three kinds of spread: the prior’s, the data’s own scatter, and the distance between the data mean and the prior mean. The expected covariance of the component is E[Λk]−1 = Wk−1/νk, drawn as the solid ellipse.
Say it“Beta-k and nu-k add N-k to their priors; m-k averages m-nought and x-bar-k; W-k inverse adds the scatter and the shift to W-nought inverse.”

PRML 10.2.1 · what the priors buy

No collapse, and the large-data limit

Maximum likelihood for a mixture has a known disease. Put one component exactly on a single data point and let its variance shrink: that point’s density grows without limit, so the likelihood goes to infinity, and nothing in EM stops it. In practice people add a small constant to the covariances, or restart when it happens. The variational mixture needs no such patch. Its expected covariance is Wk−1/νk, and Wk−1 always contains the prior’s W0−1. A component that owns one point has νk ≈ ν0 + 1, so its width cannot fall below a floor set by the prior.

The other side of the coin: with plenty of data the priors stop mattering. Nk swamps α0, β0 and ν0, the posteriors narrow like 1/√Nk, the digamma terms approach logarithms, and the VB updates become EM’s. Both runs below start from the same responsibilities, with component 3 placed on the right-most point. Turn on the lone point and watch EM collapse; then raise N and see the two fits merge everywhere else.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal N(x_n \mid \mu_k = x_n, \sigma_k^2) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_k} \\ &\to \infty \quad \text{as } \sigma_k \to 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a component centred exactly on one point gives that point an unbounded density as its width shrinks. Every other point is still covered by the other components, so the total likelihood is unbounded too: the maximum-likelihood problem has no well-behaved maximum.
Say it“A Gaussian sitting on a point has height one over root two pi sigma there, which blows up as sigma goes to zero.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[\boldsymbol\Lambda_k]^{-1} &= \frac{\mathbf W_k^{-1}}{\nu_k} \\ &= \frac{\mathbf W_0^{-1} + N_k \mathbf S_k + \cdots}{\nu_0 + N_k} \\ &\succeq \frac{\mathbf W_0^{-1}}{\nu_0 + N_k} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the expected covariance of a variational component is never smaller (in the matrix sense, ⪰) than the prior’s inverse scale divided by its count. For a one-point component in one dimension, its standard deviation is at least √(W0−1/(ν0 + 1)). As Nk grows, the prior’s share fades and the expression tends to Sk, EM’s answer.
Say it“The expected covariance is W-k inverse over nu-k, which can never drop below W-nought inverse over nu-nought plus N-k.”

PRML 10.2.2 · the variational lower bound

The lower bound: a convergence test and a bug detector

Every variational method maximises the evidence lower bound ℒ(q): the expected log joint minus the expected log of q. It sits below ln p(X) by exactly KL(q ‖ p), the gap between q and the true posterior. For this model it falls apart into seven expectations, each available in closed form from the quantities the updates already compute. Writing it out is tedious, but it pays three times. First, it tells you when to stop, which here means when it changes by less than 10−7. Second, it checks your code: both VB steps maximise ℒ over their own factor exactly, so the bound can never go down. If it ever does, there is a bug in the maths or the code. Third, it approximates ln p(X) and so can compare models, which is the subject of the section on choosing K.

A stronger version of the second check: right after a VB-M step, nudge one updated quantity, say a mean mk, and recompute ℒ. The update is the best possible factor, so every nudge must lower the bound. And one exact test is available: with K = 1 there is nothing to factorise, q equals the true posterior, and ℒ must equal the log evidence, which for a single Gaussian with a Gaussian–Wishart prior has a closed form. Plant a bug and run: the bound starts to wobble downwards, sometimes only by a hair.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathcal L(q) = \mathbb E_q\Big[\ln \frac{p(\mathbf X, \mathbf Z, \boldsymbol\pi, \boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda)}{q(\mathbf Z, \boldsymbol\pi, \boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda)}\Big] \\ &\quad = \mathbb E[\ln p(\mathbf X \mid \mathbf Z, \boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda)] + \mathbb E[\ln p(\mathbf Z \mid \boldsymbol\pi)] \\ &\qquad + \mathbb E[\ln p(\boldsymbol\pi)] + \mathbb E[\ln p(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda)] \\ &\qquad - \mathbb E[\ln q(\mathbf Z)] - \mathbb E[\ln q(\boldsymbol\pi)] \\ &\qquad - \mathbb E[\ln q(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda)] \\ &\quad = \ln p(\mathbf X) - \mathrm{KL}\big(q \,\Vert\, p(\,\cdot \mid \mathbf X)\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the expectation is over everything q describes: a sum over the labels Z and integrals over π, μ and Λ. It splits into four expected log densities of the model (the data, the labels, the weights, the means and precisions), minus three expected log densities of the factors of q, which are their negative entropies. In the last line p(· | X) is the exact posterior over all the latent variables. It says why maximising ℒ is the right goal: ln p(X) does not depend on q, so raising ℒ shrinks the KL divergence.
Say it“L of q is the expected log joint minus the expected log q, which is log evidence minus the KL from q to the posterior.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb E[\ln p(\mathbf X \mid \mathbf Z, \boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda)] = \tfrac12 \textstyle\sum_k N_k\, e_k \\ &e_k = \ln \tilde\Lambda_k - D\beta_k^{-1} - D \ln 2\pi \\ &\quad - \nu_k \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf S_k \mathbf W_k) \\ &\quad - \nu_k (\bar{\mathbf x}_k - \mathbf m_k)^{\mathsf T} \mathbf W_k (\bar{\mathbf x}_k - \mathbf m_k) \\ &\mathbb E[\ln p(\mathbf Z \mid \boldsymbol\pi)] = \textstyle\sum_{n} \sum_{k} r_{nk} \ln \tilde\pi_k \\ &\mathbb E[\ln q(\mathbf Z)] = \textstyle\sum_{n} \sum_{k} r_{nk} \ln r_{nk} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the data term is each component’s expected Gaussian log density ek, counted once for each of its Nk effective points; the trace and the shift term are the expected squared distances written with the summary statistics. The label terms compare the expected log weights with the entropy of the responsibilities.
Say it“The data term sums N-k times the expected log Gaussian; the label terms are r times log pi-tilde and r times log r.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb E[\ln p(\boldsymbol\pi)] = \ln C(\boldsymbol\alpha_0) \\ &\qquad + (\alpha_0 - 1) \textstyle\sum_k \ln \tilde\pi_k \\ &\mathbb E[\ln q(\boldsymbol\pi)] = \ln C(\boldsymbol\alpha) \\ &\qquad + \textstyle\sum_k (\alpha_k - 1) \ln \tilde\pi_k \\ &\mathbb E[\ln q(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda)] = \textstyle\sum_k h_k \\ &h_k = \tfrac12 \ln \tilde\Lambda_k + \tfrac D2 \ln \frac{\beta_k}{2\pi} - \tfrac D2 \\ &\qquad - \mathrm H\big[q(\boldsymbol\Lambda_k)\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the weight terms are a Dirichlet log density averaged under the posterior Dirichlet. The last lines give minus the entropy of each Gaussian–Wishart factor, hk: a Gaussian part for the mean and the Wishart entropy H for the precision.
Say it“Log C plus alpha-minus-one times the sum of log pi-tilde, for the prior and for q; then the entropy of each Gaussian-Wishart.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb E[\ln p(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda)] = \tfrac12 \textstyle\sum_k g_k \\ &\qquad + K \ln B(\mathbf W_0, \nu_0) \\ &\qquad + \tfrac{\nu_0 - D - 1}{2} \textstyle\sum_k \ln \tilde\Lambda_k \\ &\qquad - \tfrac12 \textstyle\sum_k \nu_k \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf W_0^{-1} \mathbf W_k) \\ &g_k = D \ln \frac{\beta_0}{2\pi} + \ln \tilde\Lambda_k - \frac{D\beta_0}{\beta_k} \\ &\qquad - \beta_0 \nu_k (\mathbf m_k - \mathbf m_0)^{\mathsf T} \mathbf W_k (\mathbf m_k - \mathbf m_0) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the Gaussian–Wishart prior’s log density, averaged under each component’s posterior; gk is the part from the prior on the mean. B(W, ν) is the Wishart’s normalising constant. A posterior that has wandered far from the prior (mk far from m0, Wk very different from W0) pays here.
Say it“The expected log prior of the means and precisions: a Gaussian part, a Wishart part, and a trace.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathrm H[\boldsymbol\Lambda] = -\ln B(\mathbf W, \nu) \\ &\qquad - \tfrac{\nu - D - 1}{2} \ln \tilde\Lambda + \tfrac{\nu D}{2} \\ &\ln B(\mathbf W, \nu) = -\tfrac{\nu}{2} \ln |\mathbf W| - \tfrac{\nu D}{2} \ln 2 \\ &\qquad - \tfrac{D(D-1)}{4} \ln \pi \\ &\qquad - \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{D} \ln \Gamma\big(\tfrac{\nu + 1 - i}{2}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the entropy of a Wishart distribution and the log of its normaliser. These are the only places where the Gamma function (rather than digamma) appears.
Say it“The Wishart entropy is minus log B, minus nu minus D minus one over two times log Lambda-tilde, plus nu D over two.”

\[ \begin{aligned} &\ln p(\mathbf X \mid K{=}1) = -\tfrac{ND}{2} \ln \pi + \tfrac D2 \ln \tfrac{\beta_0}{\beta_N} \\ &\qquad + \ln \frac{\Gamma_D(\nu_N / 2)}{\Gamma_D(\nu_0 / 2)} \\ &\qquad + \tfrac{\nu_0}{2} \ln |\mathbf W_0^{-1}| - \tfrac{\nu_N}{2} \ln |\mathbf W_N^{-1}| \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the exact marginal likelihood of N points under one Gaussian with a Gaussian–Wishart prior (βN, νN, WN are the posterior values, the K = 1 case of the update above; ΓD is the multivariate Gamma function). It is a standard conjugate result, and it is what the K = 1 bound must reproduce.

PRML 10.2.3 · the predictive density

The predictive density: a mixture of Student t’s

To score a new point x̂ we want p(x̂ | X): the model’s density for it, averaged over everything we are unsure about, including its label and every parameter. Replace the true posterior by q and the integrals can be done exactly. The label sum gives a weighted sum over components; averaging πk over the Dirichlet gives the weight αk/α̂; and averaging a Gaussian over a Gaussian–Wishart posterior for its mean and precision gives a Student t, a bell with heavier tails. So the predictive density is a mixture of K Student t’s.

The heavy tails are the honest part. A component fitted to six points might be wider than it looks, and the t accounts for that by spreading some probability further out. As Nk grows, its degrees of freedom νk + 1 − D grow, the t tends to a Gaussian, and the predictive density tends to the plug-in mixture. The lab fits a VB mixture (K = 4, α0 = 0.01, and a weak prior: β0 = 0.01, ν0 = 1, W0−1 = 0.25, so a typical prior component has standard deviation 0.5) to N training points and scores 4,000 fresh test points from the same source, with the predictive density and with the plug-in Gaussian mixture of the same q. Slide N down to a handful of points and watch the plug-in lose on the test points it never expected.

\[ \begin{aligned} &p(\hat{\mathbf x} \mid \mathbf X) = \sum_{\hat{\mathbf z}} \iiint p(\hat{\mathbf x} \mid \hat{\mathbf z}, \boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda) \\ &\qquad \times p(\hat{\mathbf z} \mid \boldsymbol\pi)\, p(\boldsymbol\pi, \boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda \mid \mathbf X)\, d\boldsymbol\pi\, d\boldsymbol\mu\, d\boldsymbol\Lambda \\ &\quad \simeq \sum_{k=1}^{K} \iiint \pi_k\, \mathcal N\big(\hat{\mathbf x} \mid \boldsymbol\mu_k, \boldsymbol\Lambda_k^{-1}\big) \\ &\qquad \times q(\boldsymbol\pi)\, q(\boldsymbol\mu_k, \boldsymbol\Lambda_k)\, d\boldsymbol\pi\, d\boldsymbol\mu_k\, d\boldsymbol\Lambda_k \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first line is exact Bayesian prediction: sum over the new point’s label and integrate over the parameters. The second replaces the posterior by the variational one; summing out the label leaves one term per component, each needing only its own factor q(μk, Λk).
Say it“The predictive density sums, over components, pi-k times the Gaussian, averaged over q.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &p(\hat{\mathbf x} \mid \mathbf X) \simeq \frac{1}{\hat\alpha} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \alpha_k \\ &\qquad \times \mathrm{St}\big(\hat{\mathbf x} \mid \mathbf m_k, \mathbf L_k, \nu_k + 1 - D\big) \\ &\mathbf L_k = \frac{(\nu_k + 1 - D)\, \beta_k}{1 + \beta_k}\, \mathbf W_k \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: component k contributes a Student t centred at mk, with precision matrix Lk and νk + 1 − D degrees of freedom, weighted by its expected weight. The factor βk/(1 + βk) widens it for uncertainty in the mean; the low degrees of freedom fatten its tails for uncertainty in the precision.
Say it“A mixture of Student t's: weights alpha-k over alpha-hat, centres m-k, precisions L-k, nu-k plus one minus D degrees of freedom.”

PRML 10.2.4 · determining the number of components

How many components? Two ways to let the bound decide

The bound approximates ln p(X | K), the evidence for a model with K components, so the first way is to fit several K and compare. One correction is needed. Relabelling the components does not change the model, so the true posterior has K! identical copies of each mode. A factorised q can only sit on one of them, and the evidence it misses is roughly a factor of K!. When comparing K, use ℒ + ln K!. (Maximum likelihood cannot do this comparison at all: its log-likelihood keeps rising as K grows, as the dashed curve shows.)

The second way is cheaper: fit once with too many components and let the priors empty the ones that are not needed. With a small α0 the Dirichlet prefers sparse weights, so a component that loses its points has nothing to hold it: αk falls back to α0, its expected weight goes to nearly zero, and its factor q(μk, Λk) falls back to the prior. A related trick treats π as plain parameters and maximises the bound over them; their update is the average responsibility, and unused weights are driven to zero. Pick how many clusters the data really have, then compare the bound’s choice of K with how many components a single 10-component run keeps.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln p(\mathbf X \mid K) &\approx \mathcal L_K + \ln K! \\ \hat K &= \arg\max_K \big(\mathcal L_K + \ln K!\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: K! is the number of ways to relabel K components. Each relabelling of the posterior is equally good, q captures one, so adding ln K! approximately restores the probability of the others. That is an approximation that works when the modes are well separated; it overcounts when components overlap heavily or sit empty. So each ℒK here is fitted with α0 = 1, which keeps every component in use (with a tiny α0, empty components cost almost nothing and the ln K! bonus would reward them), and is the best of 4 random starts.
Say it“Log evidence for K is roughly the bound plus log K factorial.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &N_k \to 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \alpha_k \to \alpha_0 \\ &\qquad \mathbb E[\pi_k] \to \frac{\alpha_0}{K\alpha_0 + N} \\ &\qquad q(\boldsymbol\mu_k, \boldsymbol\Lambda_k) \to p(\boldsymbol\mu_k, \boldsymbol\Lambda_k) \\ &\text{type-II ML:}\quad \pi_k = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} r_{nk} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a component that owns no points keeps only its prior counts; with α0 = 0.001 and N = 200 its expected weight is about 5 × 10−6, so it no longer takes responsibility for anything. If instead π is a parameter chosen to maximise the bound (the last line), unused weights converge to zero outright.
Say it“An empty component falls back to its prior; or, as a point estimate, pi-k is the average responsibility.”

PRML 10.2.5 · induced factorisations

Induced factorisations: independence you get for free

We assumed only q(Z) q(π, μ, Λ), yet the optimal q(Z) came out as a product over points, and q(π, μ, Λ) as q(π) times one factor per component. These extra splits are induced factorisations: they come from the structure of the model, not from any assumption, so they cost no accuracy. They also make the updates cheap, because each point and each component can be handled on its own.

There is a clean test for them. Split the latent variables into three groups A, B and C, and suppose q is assumed to factorise as q(A, B) q(C). The optimal q(A, B) is the exponential of the log joint averaged over q(C), and it splits into q(A) q(B) when A and B are independent in the model given X and C. That is a question about the graph, and d-separation answers it, but only one way round: d-separation proves independence, and its failure does not prove dependence. Some independences come from the form of the distributions, not the arrows. Here the label zn switches on exactly one component, so the two components’ parameters never share a term of the log joint. That independence rests on the labels’ one-hot structure, and no graph can draw it. The lab therefore runs two tests: d-separation, and the direct one, which asks whether any term of the log joint still holds a variable from A and one from B once C is averaged out. The graph unrolls the mixture for three points and two components. Click latent nodes to move them between A, B and C, or pick a preset.

\[ \begin{aligned} &q(\mathbf A, \mathbf B, \mathbf C) = q(\mathbf A, \mathbf B)\, q(\mathbf C) \\ &\ln q^\star(\mathbf A, \mathbf B) \\ &\quad = \mathbb E_{\mathbf C}\big[\ln p(\mathbf X, \mathbf A, \mathbf B, \mathbf C)\big] + \text{const} \\ &\quad = \mathbb E_{\mathbf C}\big[\ln p(\mathbf A, \mathbf B \mid \mathbf X, \mathbf C)\big] + \text{const} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the mean-field update for the (A, B) factor. Writing the joint as p(A, B | X, C) times p(X, C) only moves terms that do not involve A or B into the constant.
Say it“Log q-star of A, B is the expected log of p of A, B given X and C, plus a constant.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf A \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathbf B \mid \mathbf X, \mathbf C \\ &\;\Longrightarrow\; \ln p(\mathbf A, \mathbf B \mid \mathbf X, \mathbf C) \\ &\qquad = \ln p(\mathbf A \mid \mathbf X, \mathbf C) + \ln p(\mathbf B \mid \mathbf X, \mathbf C) \\ &\;\Longrightarrow\; q^\star(\mathbf A, \mathbf B) = q^\star(\mathbf A)\, q^\star(\mathbf B) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: if A and B are conditionally independent given the data and C for every value of C, the log splits into an A part and a B part, and so does its average over q(C). Conditional independence can be read off the graph with d-separation. If they are not independent, the optimal q(A, B) generally keeps a dependence between them.
Say it“If A and B are independent given X and C, the optimal q of A, B splits into q of A times q of B.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\ln p(\mathbf X, \mathbf Z, \boldsymbol\pi, \boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Lambda) = \sum_{n} \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_k z_{nk} \ln \pi_k}_{\{\mathbf z_n,\ \boldsymbol\pi\}} \\ &\quad + \sum_{n} \sum_{k} \underbrace{z_{nk} \ln \mathcal N\big(\mathbf x_n \mid \boldsymbol\mu_k, \boldsymbol\Lambda_k^{-1}\big)}_{\{\mathbf z_n,\ \boldsymbol\mu_k,\ \boldsymbol\Lambda_k\}} \\ &\quad + \underbrace{\ln p(\boldsymbol\pi)}_{\{\boldsymbol\pi\}} + \sum_{k} \underbrace{\ln p(\boldsymbol\mu_k, \boldsymbol\Lambda_k)}_{\{\boldsymbol\mu_k,\ \boldsymbol\Lambda_k\}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the log joint is a sum of small terms, and the braces list the latent variables each term touches. Averaging over q(C) deletes C’s variables from every term. If no term is left holding both an A variable and a B variable, ln q*(A, B) is an A part plus a B part, and the factorisation is induced. Look at the second term: given zn, component k’s parameters appear only with zn, never with another component’s.
Say it“The log joint is a sum of terms; a split is induced when, after averaging out C, no term touches both A and B.”

The same experiments in Python

The script re-implements the updates with NumPy (same data transform, same prior, same random starts from the same generator), checks them against scikit-learn’s BayesianGaussianMixture started from identical responsibilities, checks the closed-form bound by Monte Carlo, checks K = 1 against the exact evidence, and checks the induced-factorisation verdicts with a second d-separation algorithm.

import sys, time, itertools, json
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import digamma, gammaln, multigammaln, logsumexp
from scipy import stats
from sklearn.mixture import BayesianGaussianMixture, GaussianMixture
T0 = time.time()
M32 = 0xFFFFFFFF
def rng(a):                                   # mulberry32, the generator the page uses
    st = [a & M32]
    def r():
        st[0] = (st[0] + 0x6D2B79F5) & M32; a = st[0]
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & M32
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & M32)) & M32) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M32) / 4294967296
    return r

# ---------- data: log spend, standardised ----------
raw = np.array(json.load(open(sys.argv[1]))["rows"], float)   # the 440 UCI rows, as embedded in the page
channel, region, spend = raw[:, 0].astype(int), raw[:, 1].astype(int), raw[:, 2:]
L = np.log(spend); X = (L - L.mean(0)) / L.std(0)
N, D = X.shape
def prior(a0):   # m0 = data mean, beta0 = 1, nu0 = D, W0^-1 = nu0 * sample covariance
    return dict(a0=a0, b0=1.0, m0=X.mean(0), n0=float(D), W0inv=D * np.cov(X.T))

def init_resp(K, seed):                       # nearest of K data points picked at random
    r = rng(seed); idx = list(range(N)); pick = []
    for k in range(K):
        j = k + int(r() * (N - k)); idx[k], idx[j] = idx[j], idx[k]; pick.append(idx[k])
    d = ((X[:, None, :] - X[pick][None]) ** 2).sum(2); R = np.zeros((N, K)); R[np.arange(N), d.argmin(1)] = 1
    return R

def stats_(R):                                # N_k, x-bar_k, S_k (10.51)-(10.53)
    Nk = R.sum(0); xb = np.where(Nk[:, None] > 1e-300, R.T @ X / np.maximum(Nk, 1e-300)[:, None], 0)
    Sk = np.array([((R[:, k, None] * (X - xb[k])).T @ (X - xb[k])) / Nk[k] if Nk[k] > 1e-300 else np.zeros((D, D)) for k in range(R.shape[1])])
    return Nk, xb, Sk

def m_step(R, P):                             # (10.57)-(10.63)
    Nk, xb, Sk = stats_(R); xb[Nk <= 1e-300] = P['m0']
    q = dict(a=P['a0'] + Nk, b=P['b0'] + Nk, nu=P['n0'] + Nk)
    q['m'] = (P['b0'] * P['m0'] + Nk[:, None] * xb) / q['b'][:, None]
    d = xb - P['m0']
    q['Winv'] = P['W0inv'] + Nk[:, None, None] * Sk + (P['b0'] * Nk / (P['b0'] + Nk))[:, None, None] * np.einsum('ki,kj->kij', d, d)
    q['W'] = np.linalg.inv(q['Winv']); q['ldW'] = -np.linalg.slogdet(q['Winv'])[1]
    q['lnpi'] = digamma(q['a']) - digamma(q['a'].sum())
    q['lnlam'] = digamma((q['nu'][:, None] - np.arange(D)) / 2).sum(1) + D * np.log(2) + q['ldW']
    return q

def e_step(q):                                # (10.46)-(10.49), (10.64)-(10.66)
    d = X[:, None, :] - q['m'][None]
    lr = q['lnpi'] + 0.5 * q['lnlam'] - 0.5 * D * np.log(2 * np.pi) - 0.5 * (D / q['b'] + q['nu'] * np.einsum('nki,kij,nkj->nk', d, q['W'], d))
    lr = lr - lr.max(1, keepdims=True); R = np.where(lr < -500, 0, np.exp(lr)); return R / R.sum(1, keepdims=True)

def lnB(ldW, nu): return -0.5 * nu * ldW - (0.5 * nu * D * np.log(2) + 0.25 * D * (D - 1) * np.log(np.pi) + gammaln((nu - np.arange(D)) / 2).sum())
def lnC(a): return gammaln(a.sum()) - gammaln(a).sum()

def bound(R, q, P):                           # (10.70)-(10.77)
    Nk, xb, Sk = stats_(R); K = len(Nk); W0 = np.linalg.inv(P['W0inv']); ldW0 = np.linalg.slogdet(W0)[1]
    t = 0.0
    for k in range(K):
        dx, dm, nu, W, ll = xb[k] - q['m'][k], q['m'][k] - P['m0'], q['nu'][k], q['W'][k], q['lnlam'][k]
        t += 0.5 * Nk[k] * (ll - D / q['b'][k] - nu * np.trace(Sk[k] @ W) - nu * dx @ W @ dx - D * np.log(2 * np.pi))
        t += 0.5 * (D * np.log(P['b0'] / (2 * np.pi)) + ll - D * P['b0'] / q['b'][k] - P['b0'] * nu * dm @ W @ dm) + lnB(ldW0, P['n0']) + 0.5 * (P['n0'] - D - 1) * ll - 0.5 * nu * np.trace(P['W0inv'] @ W)
        H = -lnB(q['ldW'][k], nu) - 0.5 * (nu - D - 1) * ll + 0.5 * nu * D
        t -= 0.5 * ll + 0.5 * D * np.log(q['b'][k] / (2 * np.pi)) - 0.5 * D - H
    with np.errstate(divide='ignore', invalid='ignore'):
        t += (R * q['lnpi']).sum() - np.where(R > 0, R * np.log(R), 0).sum()
    t += lnC(np.full(K, P['a0'])) + (P['a0'] - 1) * q['lnpi'].sum() - ((q['a'] - 1) * q['lnpi']).sum() - lnC(q['a'])
    return t

def vb(K, a0, seed, tol=1e-7, maxit=2000):    # the page's vbRun: init M-step, then (E, M, bound) until |dL| < tol
    P = prior(a0); R = init_resp(K, seed); q = m_step(R, P); Lb = bound(R, q, P); it = 0
    for it in range(1, maxit + 1):
        R = e_step(q); q = m_step(R, P); L1 = bound(R, q, P); dL = L1 - Lb; Lb = L1
        if abs(dL) < tol: break
    q['Epi'] = q['a'] / q['a'].sum()
    return dict(R=R, q=q, L=Lb, it=it, P=P, R0=init_resp(K, seed))

def em(K, seed, reg=1e-6, tol=1e-7, maxit=2000):   # maximum likelihood, the page's emRun
    R = init_resp(K, seed); prev = -np.inf; ll = None
    def M(R):
        Nk = R.sum(0); mu = R.T @ X / Nk[:, None]
        S = np.array([((R[:, k, None] * (X - mu[k])).T @ (X - mu[k])) / Nk[k] + reg * np.eye(D) for k in range(K)])
        return Nk / N, mu, S
    pi, mu, S = M(R); it = 0
    for it in range(1, maxit + 1):
        if (np.linalg.slogdet(S)[1] < 2 * D * np.log(1e-4)).any(): return dict(ll=np.inf, it=it - 1)
        lp = np.stack([np.log(pi[k]) + stats.multivariate_normal(mu[k], S[k]).logpdf(X) for k in range(K)], 1)
        ll = logsumexp(lp, 1).sum(); R = np.exp(lp - logsumexp(lp, 1, keepdims=True)); pi, mu, S = M(R)
        if abs(ll - prev) < tol: break
        prev = ll
    return dict(ll=ll, it=it, R0=init_resp(K, seed))

# ---------- 6. induced factorisations: moralised ancestral graph + term test ----------
lat = ['pi', 'z1', 'z2', 'z3', 'mu1', 'L1', 'mu2', 'L2']; obs = ['x1', 'x2', 'x3']
edges = [('pi', 'z' + n) for n in '123'] + [('z' + n, 'x' + n) for n in '123'] + [('L' + k, 'mu' + k) for k in '12'] + [(p + k, 'x' + n) for n in '123' for k in '12' for p in ('mu', 'L')]
terms = [{'pi'}] + [{'z' + n, 'pi'} for n in '123'] + [{'z' + n, 'mu' + k, 'L' + k} for n in '123' for k in '12'] + [{'mu' + k, 'L' + k} for k in '12']
def dsep(A, B, O):                            # Lauritzen: separation in the moral graph of the ancestral set
    anc, todo = set(), list(A | B | O)
    while todo:
        v = todo.pop()
        if v not in anc: anc.add(v); todo += [a for a, b in edges if b == v]
    und = {v: set() for v in anc}
    for a, b in edges:
        if a in anc and b in anc: und[a].add(b); und[b].add(a)
    for v in anc:
        ps = [a for a, b in edges if b == v]
        for p1, p2 in itertools.combinations(ps, 2): und[p1].add(p2); und[p2].add(p1)
    seen, todo = set(A), list(A)
    while todo:
        v = todo.pop()
        for w in und[v]:
            if w in O or w in seen: continue
            if w in B: return False
            seen.add(w); todo.append(w)
    return True
ind = ds = 0
for g in itertools.product('ABC', repeat=len(lat)):
    A = {v for v, c in zip(lat, g) if c == 'A'}; B = {v for v, c in zip(lat, g) if c == 'B'}; C = {v for v, c in zip(lat, g) if c == 'C'}
    if not A or not B: continue
    ok = not any((t - C) & A and (t - C) & B for t in terms)
    if ok: ind += 1; ds += dsep(A, B, C | set(obs))
    elif dsep(A, B, C | set(obs)): print("inconsistent split", g)

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • The Bayesian mixture puts a Dirichlet prior on the weights and a Gaussian–Wishart prior on each mean and precision; the single assumption q(Z) q(π, μ, Λ) gives closed-form updates.
  • Variational EM alternates responsibilities computed from expectations (ln π̃k, ln Λ̃k, the D/βk term) with conjugate updates that add Nk, x̄k and Sk to the prior’s counts.
  • The priors stop components collapsing onto single points, and with lots of data the updates reduce to EM’s.
  • The lower bound can never decrease, which makes it a convergence test and a bug detector; with ln K! added it compares numbers of components, and a small α0 switches unneeded components off in a single run.
  • The predictive density is a mixture of Student t’s, and factorisations beyond the one assumed are induced by the model: d-separation tells you which.

Check your understanding

Why does the variational responsibility use ψ(αk) − ψ(α̂) instead of ln(αk/α̂)?

Mean-field updates average the log joint, so they need E[ln πk] under the Dirichlet, which is ψ(αk) − ψ(α̂). ln(αk/α̂) is ln E[πk], the log of the average, which is larger (Jensen’s inequality). The difference is biggest for components with small counts, so it is one of the ways VB penalises components the data barely support.

A component owns a single data point. Why does EM’s variance go to zero while VB’s stays finite?

EM’s M-step sets the variance to the weighted scatter of the component’s points about their mean, which is zero for one point, and the likelihood rewards that without limit. VB’s expected covariance is Wk−1/νk, and Wk−1 always includes the prior’s W0−1, so it is at least W0−1/(ν0 + 1).

Your bound went down by 10−9 at iteration 40. Bug or not?

Look at the size relative to the bound. A change of 10−9 on a bound of size 103 is near the limit of double-precision rounding, so it is probably round-off. A clear, repeated fall well above that is a bug, because each correct VB step maximises the bound over its own factor and cannot lower it.

Why add ln K! to the bound when comparing different K, and when does that correction mislead?

The true posterior is unchanged by relabelling the components, so it has K! symmetric copies of each mode, while a factorised q covers only one. Adding ln K! approximately counts the copies q missed. It assumes the copies barely overlap. When components overlap heavily or some are empty, relabelled copies are nearly the same distribution, and adding ln K! overstates the evidence.

We assumed q(Z) q(π, μ, Λ). Why did q(μk, Λk) not split further into q(μk) q(Λk)?

Because μk and Λk are not independent given X and Z: there is a direct edge Λk → μk, and both are parents of the observed xn. So d-separation fails, nothing is induced, and the optimal factor keeps the Gaussian–Wishart coupling, in which the mean’s covariance depends on the precision.
