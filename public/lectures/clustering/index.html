Clustering

Finding groups that nobody labelled

Every model so far learned from an answer key. Clustering has none: just points, and the question “which of these belong together?” There is no single right answer, because there is no single meaning of “together”. Close to the same centre? Joined by a chain of neighbours? Drawn from the same bell curve? Each idea gives a different algorithm, and they disagree. On the right, pick an algorithm and watch it carve up the same 190 points.

The idea

Groups without an answer key

A shop with ten thousand customers can't design ten thousand offers, but it can design four, if it can find four kinds of customer. Nobody has labelled them; the groups have to come out of the data itself. That is unsupervised learning, and clustering is its most common form. To make “good groups” precise, the oldest approach gives each group a centre and charges every point the squared distance to its nearest centre. Good centres make the bill small. Click to drop centres on the points below, drag them around, and try to beat the computer.

\[ W = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\ \min_{k}\ \lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k \rVert^2 \]
Read it as: each point xi walks to its nearest centre μk and pays the squared distance. W, the within-cluster sum of squares (scikit-learn calls it inertia), is the total bill. Each centre's customers form its cluster. More centres always lower W, so W can compare placements for the same number of centres, but not choose how many.
Say it“W is the sum over all points of the squared distance to the nearest centre.”

The workhorse

k-means: assign, update, repeat

Finding the very best centres is a hard problem (NP-hard, in fact), but Stuart Lloyd's 1957 recipe gets a good answer fast. Start with k centres anywhere. Then repeat two steps: assign every point to its nearest centre, and update every centre to the mean of its points. Each step can only lower W, so the loop must stop, usually after a handful of rounds. Press Step and watch the stars walk; drag a star to restart from somewhere else.

\[ \text{assign:}\quad c_i = \arg\min_{k}\ \lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k \rVert^2 \]
Read it as: point i joins the cluster ci whose centre is nearest. With the centres fixed, this is the cheapest possible choice for every point, so W can only fall. The borders between clusters are straight lines, halfway between neighbouring centres.
Say it“c-i is the k that makes the squared distance from x-i to mu-k smallest.”
\[ \text{update:}\quad \boldsymbol{\mu}_k = \frac{1}{|C_k|}\sum_{i \in C_k} \mathbf{x}_i \]
Read it as: move each centre to the average of the points it owns. With the clusters fixed, the mean is the single point with the smallest total squared distance to them, so W falls again. This is why k-means uses squared distances.
Say it“mu-k is one over the size of cluster k, times the sum of its points.”

A known weakness

Bad starts and k-means++

Lloyd's loop always stops, but not always at the best answer. If two starting centres land in the same group, one of them may stay stuck there while another centre is left to cover two groups. That's a local minimum: no single assign or update step can fix it. The cure has two parts. Start more cleverly: k-means++ (Arthur & Vassilvitskii, 2007) picks each new starting centre with a probability that grows with its distance from the centres chosen so far. And start several times, keeping the run with the lowest W. Run 100 starts of each kind and compare.

\[ P(\text{pick } \mathbf{x}_i) =\frac{D(\mathbf{x}_i)^2}{\sum_{j} D(\mathbf{x}_j)^2} \]
Read it as: D(x) is the distance from x to the nearest centre picked so far. A point far from every existing centre has a big D(x)² and is likely to be chosen; a point next to one has almost no chance. The first centre is a point picked uniformly at random. Arthur and Vassilvitskii proved that this start alone, before Lloyd's loop even begins, has an expected W at most 8(ln k + 2) times the best possible.
Say it“The chance that x-i is the next centre is D of x-i squared, over the sum of all the D squareds.”

The hardest question

How many clusters? The elbow and the silhouette

W always falls as k grows (with one centre per point it reaches zero), so its value alone can't choose k. Two habits help. The elbow: plot W against k and look for the bend where extra centres stop paying off. The silhouette (Rousseeuw, 1987): for each point, compare how close it is to its own cluster with how close it is to the next-nearest one. Move the k slider, click points to see their silhouettes, then loosen the groups and watch both signals fade.

\[ \begin{aligned} a(i) &= \bar d(i, C_{\text{own}}) \\ b(i) &= \min_{C \ne C_{\text{own}}} \bar d(i, C) \\ s(i) &= \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max\{a(i),\, b(i)\}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: d̄(i, C) is the mean distance from point i to the points of cluster C (not counting i itself). So a(i) says how far i is from its own cluster-mates, and b(i) how far from the nearest other cluster. s(i) is near +1 when point i sits snugly inside its cluster, far from the rest; near 0 when it lies on a border; negative when it would fit better next door. The silhouette score is the average of s(i) over all points. Unlike W, it can go down when k grows, so you can pick the k with the highest score. Points alone in their cluster get s = 0.
Say it“s of i is b minus a, over the larger of a and b.”

A different idea

Hierarchical clustering: a family tree of points

Instead of fixing k first, build every clustering at once. Start with each point in a cluster of its own. Repeatedly merge the two closest clusters, until only one is left. The record of merges is a tree, the dendrogram: the height of each join says how far apart the two groups were. Cut the tree at any height and you get a clustering. The whole method rests on one choice: how to measure the distance between two groups of points, the linkage. Switch linkage, cut the tree, and drag points to see the tree rebuild.

\[ \begin{aligned} d_{\text{single}}(A,B) &= \min_{\mathbf{a},\,\mathbf{b}} \lVert \mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b} \rVert \\ d_{\text{complete}}(A,B) &= \max_{\mathbf{a},\,\mathbf{b}} \lVert \mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b} \rVert \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: here and on the right, a runs over the points of cluster A and b over the points of B. Single linkage measures the two closest members, so a chain of near neighbours can join anything (long, snaking clusters). Complete linkage measures the two farthest members, which favours compact, round groups.
Say it“Single: the smallest distance between a point of A and a point of B. Complete: the largest.”
\[ \begin{aligned} d_{\text{avg}}(A,B) &= \tfrac{1}{|A||B|}\textstyle\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\sum_{\mathbf{b}} \lVert \mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b} \rVert \\ d_{\text{Ward}}(A,B) &= \sqrt{\tfrac{2|A||B|}{|A|+|B|}}\, \lVert \boldsymbol{\mu}_A-\boldsymbol{\mu}_B \rVert \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: average linkage takes the mean of all the cross distances, a compromise. Ward's linkage (1963) is k-means' cousin: half its square, ½d², is exactly how much W grows when A and B merge, so Ward always makes the merge that adds the least spread. For two single points it is just their distance.
Say it“Average: the mean distance over all pairs. Ward: the distance between the centres, scaled by the root of two A B over A plus B.”

Density

DBSCAN: clusters as crowds

k-means looks for round groups around centres, so it slices a crescent in half. DBSCAN (Ester, Kriegel, Sander & Xu, 1996) has a different picture: a cluster is a crowd, a region where points are packed densely, separated from other crowds by emptier space. Draw a circle of radius ε around each point. A point with at least minPts points in its circle (itself included) is a core point. Core points that sit in each other's circles join the same cluster, and so do the non-core border points in their circles. Everything else is noise. You never choose k; it comes out. Set ε and minPts, and hover over points to see their circles.

\[ \begin{aligned} N_\varepsilon(\mathbf{x}) &= \{\, \mathbf{y} : \lVert \mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y} \rVert \le \varepsilon \,\} \\ \mathbf{x} \text{ is core} &\iff |N_\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})| \ge \text{minPts} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Nε(x) is x's neighbourhood, every point within distance ε (x included). Clusters grow outwards from core points through their neighbourhoods; a border point belongs to a cluster without extending it. Points in nobody's neighbourhood of a core point are labelled noise, which makes DBSCAN a natural outlier detector.
Say it“The epsilon-neighbourhood of x is every y within epsilon of x; x is a core point if that set has at least minPts points.”

Soft clusters

Gaussian mixtures: clusters as bell curves

k-means gives each point exactly one cluster and treats every cluster as a round ball of the same size. A Gaussian mixture model is more honest about both. It imagines the data was made by k bell curves, each with its own centre, its own shape and tilt (a covariance matrix), and its own share of the points. Fitting it is the EM algorithm (Dempster, Laird & Rubin, 1977), a soft version of k-means: the E-step works out how much each bell curve is responsible for each point, and the M-step refits each bell curve to the points, weighted by those responsibilities. Step through EM, and click a point to see its responsibilities.

\[ p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k) \]
Read it as: to make a point, first roll a K-sided die with probabilities πk (the mixing weights) to pick a bell curve, then draw the point from that bell curve, a Gaussian with centre μk and covariance Σk. The model's density is the weighted sum of the K bells.
Say it“p of x is the sum over k of pi-k times the normal density of x with mean mu-k and covariance sigma-k.”
\[ \text{E:}\quad r_{ik} = \frac{\pi_k\, \mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{x}_i)}{\sum_{j} \pi_j\, \mathcal{N}_j(\mathbf{x}_i)} \]
Read it as: Bayes' rule, writing Nk(x) for bell k's density N(x | μk, Σk). rik, the responsibility, is the probability that point i came from bell k, given where it is. Each point's responsibilities add up to 1. k-means would round them to 0 or 1.
Say it“r-i-k is pi-k times bell k's density at x-i, divided by the same for all the bells added up.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{M:}\quad N_k &= \textstyle\sum_i r_{ik}, \quad \pi_k = N_k / n \\ \boldsymbol{\mu}_k &= \tfrac{1}{N_k}\textstyle\sum_i r_{ik}\, \mathbf{x}_i \\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k &= \tfrac{1}{N_k}\textstyle\sum_i r_{ik}\, \mathbf{d}_{ik}\mathbf{d}_{ik}^{\top} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: dik = xi − μk is point i's offset from bell k's new centre. Nk is how many points bell k owns, counting fractions. Its weight is its share; its centre and covariance are the weighted mean and weighted spread of the points. Each E-then-M round never lowers the likelihood of the data.
Say it“N-k is the total responsibility of bell k; pi-k is N-k over n; mu-k and sigma-k are the responsibility-weighted mean and covariance.”

Playground

Four ideas of a cluster, side by side

Each algorithm carries its own idea of what a cluster is, and wins when the data matches that idea. Below, each dataset was generated with known groups, so we can score each answer against the truth with the adjusted Rand index (ARI): 1 for a perfect match, about 0 for a clustering no better than random. Each algorithm gets fair settings: k-means, hierarchical clustering and the mixture are told the true number of groups; DBSCAN gets an ε tuned for each dataset. Switch datasets and see which idea fits which shape.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{ARI} &= \frac{\sum_{ij}\binom{n_{ij}}{2} - E}{\tfrac12\big[\sum_i \binom{a_i}{2} + \sum_j \binom{b_j}{2}\big] - E} \\ E &= \frac{\sum_i \binom{a_i}{2}\,\sum_j \binom{b_j}{2}}{\binom{n}{2}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: count the pairs of points. nij is how many points are in true group i and found cluster j; ai and bj are the group and cluster sizes. The top counts pairs that both labellings put together, minus E, the number expected by pure chance; the bottom is the most that count could be. The cluster numbers themselves don't matter, only who is grouped with whom.
Say it“ARI is the together-pairs both agree on, minus what chance would give, over the maximum minus what chance would give.”

Real-world project

Project: segmenting a wholesaler's customers

A food wholesaler in Portugal recorded what each of its 440 client businesses spent in a year, in six categories: fresh food, milk products, grocery, frozen, detergents & paper, and delicatessen (Abreu, 2011; UCI repository “Wholesale customers”). The file also says each client's channel: 298 are hotels, restaurants and cafés (HoReCa) and 142 are retailers. We hide the channel from every algorithm and use it only at the end, to ask whether the segments found on their own mean something real. At the end, a second real dataset, Old Faithful's eruptions, shows a Gaussian mixture in the wild.

1 · Tame the skew

Spending is wildly lopsided: most clients spend a few thousand, a few spend a hundred times more. Distances on raw spend would be ruled by those few giants and by whichever category has the biggest numbers. So take logs (which turn “ten times more” into “a fixed step more”), then standardise each category to mean 0 and standard deviation 1. Switch category and scale to see the difference.

\[ z_{ij} = \frac{\ln x_{ij} - m_j}{s_j} \]
Read it as: xij is client i's spend in category j. Take its natural log, subtract that category's mean log spend mj, and divide by the standard deviation sj of the log spends. Every category now has the same weight in every distance.
Say it“z-i-j is log x-i-j minus m-j, over s-j.”

5 · Old Faithful: a mixture in the wild

The geyser Old Faithful in Yellowstone erupts every hour or so. R’s classic faithful data (Härdle, 1991) records 272 eruptions: how long each lasted, and the wait until the next one. The scatter shows two kinds of eruption, short ones followed by short waits and long ones followed by long waits, each a tilted cloud: a natural job for a two-component Gaussian mixture. EM is started from a deliberately crossed guess: each bell pairs one group's eruption length with the other group's waiting time. Run EM with both tolerances: EM can crawl for a while before it finds its way, and a loose stopping rule can mistake a slow stretch for the finish.

EM rounds
mean log-likelihood

Why two components and not three? More bells always fit the data at least as well, so, as with W, the likelihood alone can't choose. The Bayesian information criterion charges for every parameter; the lowest BIC wins.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{BIC} &= -2 \ln \hat{L} + p \ln n \\ p &= 6K - 1 \ \text{ in 2-D} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ln L̂ is the log-likelihood of all n points under the fitted model; p counts its free parameters: per bell, 2 for the centre and 3 for the covariance, plus K − 1 free mixing weights. Lower is better. The penalty p ln n grows with each bell, so a new bell must buy a real gain in fit.
Say it“BIC is minus two times the log-likelihood, plus the number of parameters times the log of n.”

The same project in scikit-learn

import numpy as np
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans, kmeans_plusplus, AgglomerativeClustering, DBSCAN
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score, adjusted_rand_score

D = np.loadtxt("wholesale.csv", delimiter=",", skiprows=1)      # UCI "Wholesale customers"
channel, spend = D[:, 0].astype(int), D[:, 2:]                   # channel 1 = hotel/restaurant/café, 2 = retail
Z = StandardScaler().fit_transform(np.log(spend))                 # tame the skew, then one scale for all six

for k in range(2, 9):
    _, start = kmeans_plusplus(Z, k, random_state=0)              # fixed starting centres; the page uses the same
    km = KMeans(k, init=Z[start], n_init=1).fit(Z)
    print(f"k-means k={k}: inertia {km.inertia_:7.2f}  silhouette {silhouette_score(Z, km.labels_):.4f}"
          f"  ARI vs channel {adjusted_rand_score(channel, km.labels_):.4f}  ({km.n_iter_} iterations)")
for k in (2, 3):
    w = AgglomerativeClustering(k, linkage="ward").fit(Z).labels_
    print(f"Ward     k={k}: sizes {np.bincount(w).tolist()}  silhouette {silhouette_score(Z, w):.4f}"
          f"  ARI vs channel {adjusted_rand_score(channel, w):.4f}")
db = DBSCAN(eps=1.5, min_samples=8).fit(Z).labels_
print(f"DBSCAN eps=1.5, min_samples=8: {db.max() + 1} cluster, {(db == -1).sum()} customers flagged as noise")

F = np.loadtxt("faithful.csv", delimiter=",", skiprows=1, usecols=(1, 2))   # eruption length, wait (minutes)
for tol in (1e-3, 1e-6):
    gm = GaussianMixture(2, means_init=[[2, 80], [4.5, 55]], weights_init=[0.5, 0.5],
                         precisions_init=[np.diag([1, 0.01])] * 2, tol=tol, max_iter=500).fit(F)
    print(f"EM from the crossed start, tol={tol:g}: {gm.n_iter_} iterations, mean log-likelihood {gm.score(F):.6f},"
          f" weights {gm.weights_.round(4).tolist()}, means {gm.means_.round(3).tolist()}")
for k in range(1, 5):
    gm = GaussianMixture(k, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(F)
    print(f"{k} component{'s' if k > 1 else ' '}: BIC {gm.bic(F):.2f}")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Clustering groups points with no labels, and there is no single right answer: each algorithm brings its own idea of what a cluster is.
  • k-means alternates assign-to-nearest and move-to-the-mean, lowering W every step; it finds round groups, needs k, and should be started several times with k-means++.
  • W always falls as k grows; choose k with the elbow, the silhouette score, BIC for mixtures, and above all with what the segments are for.
  • Hierarchical clustering records every merge in a dendrogram (the linkage decides the shapes); DBSCAN finds dense crowds of any shape and labels the rest as noise.
  • Gaussian mixtures give soft, elliptical clusters fitted by EM; like k-means, EM can stall at a poor start, so watch the tolerance and use several starts.

Check your understanding

Why can neither step of Lloyd's algorithm increase W?

The assign step gives every point its nearest centre, the cheapest choice with the centres fixed. The update step moves each centre to the mean of its points, and the mean minimises the sum of squared distances to a fixed set of points. Each step is a minimisation over one half of the unknowns, so W never goes up.

You run k-means twice on the same data and get different clusters. Is that a bug?

No. Different starting centres can lead to different local minima. Use k-means++ starts, run several times (scikit-learn's n_init), and keep the run with the lowest W; fix the random seed if you need reproducibility.

One feature is annual income in pounds, another is age in years. What happens if you cluster without scaling?

Distances are dominated by income, whose numbers are thousands of times larger, so the clusters are essentially income bands and age is ignored. Standardise (and for skewed amounts like spending, take logs first).

Your data are two interlocking crescents with a few stray points. Which method do you reach for, and why?

DBSCAN: it follows dense regions of any shape and labels stray points as noise. k-means and Ward look for compact blobs and cut the crescents; single linkage follows shapes but a stray point can chain two crescents together.

A Gaussian mixture says a point has responsibilities 0.55 and 0.45. What does k-means say about the same point, and which answer is more useful?

k-means just assigns it to one cluster, with no hint that it is a borderline case. The mixture's responsibilities say it is nearly a coin flip, which matters if decisions depend on it (target it with both offers, or flag it for review).
