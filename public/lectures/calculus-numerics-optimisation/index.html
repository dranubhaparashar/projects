Matrix Calculus, Numerics & Constraints

The maths every training loop runs on, and the ways a computer gets it wrong

Training a model means following derivatives downhill. This chapter collects the tools for that: gradients, Jacobians and Hessians of functions of many variables; the matrix identities that keep the algebra short; the numerical traps (overflow, underflow, ill-conditioning) that turn correct formulas into NaN; and the method of Lagrange multipliers for optimising when the answer must obey rules. In the figure, the coloured hill is a function to maximise, but you may only stand on the circle. Drag the white dot along the circle, and drag the star to move the hill. At the best point, the two arrows line up.

First derivatives

Gradients and Jacobians

A model’s loss depends on thousands or billions of parameters at once, so “the derivative” has to become a vector. Take a function f of a vector x = (x1, …, xn). Its partial derivative ∂f/∂xi is the slope when you nudge xi alone and hold every other coordinate still. Stack all n of them and you have the gradient ∇f. Its job is to answer one question: if I take a small step in direction u, how fast does f change? The answer is a dot product, and it says the gradient points straight uphill, at right angles to the contour lines.

Drag the dot, then turn the direction u. The lower plot shows the slope in every direction at once: a cosine wave whose peak is the length of the gradient.

\[ \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{pmatrix} \partial f/\partial x_1 \\ \vdots \\ \partial f/\partial x_n \end{pmatrix} \]
Read it as: the gradient is a column of n slopes, one per input. It lives in the same space as x, so you can draw it as an arrow at the point where you evaluated it.
Say it“Grad f is the vector of partial derivatives of f with respect to each x-i.”
\[ D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \mathbf{u}^{\top}\nabla f = \lVert\nabla f\rVert \cos\alpha \]
Read it as: for a unit direction u, the rate of change of f is the dot product of u with the gradient; α is the angle between them. It is largest (‖∇f‖) when u points along ∇f, zero along the contour (α = 90°), and most negative straight against it. That is why gradient descent steps along −∇f.
Say it“The slope along u is u transpose grad f, which is the length of the gradient times cos alpha.”

The Jacobian: a function’s local linear map

When the output is a vector too, as with a layer of a network that maps m inputs to n outputs, every output has its own gradient. Lay them out as rows and you get the Jacobian matrix J. Near any point the function behaves like multiplication by J: a tiny square is carried to (almost) a parallelogram, and the parallelogram’s area is the square’s area times |det J|. That factor is exactly what you need to change variables in an integral, and later in normalising flows. Drag the dot on the left, then shrink the square: the true image (filled) and the Jacobian’s parallelogram (dashed) become the same shape.

\[ \begin{aligned} J_{ij} &= \frac{\partial F_i}{\partial x_j} \\ F(\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\delta}) &\approx F(\mathbf{x}) + J\,\boldsymbol{\delta} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: row i of J is the gradient of output i; column j says how all outputs move when input j moves. For a small step δ, the change in the output is J times δ. For a scalar function, J is just the gradient laid on its side.
Say it“J-i-j is the partial of output i with respect to input j; F at x plus delta is about F of x plus J delta.”
\[ \operatorname{area}\big(F(R)\big) \approx \lvert\det J\rvert \cdot \operatorname{area}(R) \]
Read it as: a small region R is stretched by the local linear map J, so its area is scaled by |det J|. A determinant of 1 means area is preserved even when the shape is twisted; a sign change in det J would mean the map flips the region over.
Say it“The area of the image of R is about the absolute determinant of J times the area of R.”

Second derivatives

Curvature: the Hessian

The gradient tells you which way is downhill, not how far to go. For that you need to know how the slope itself changes: the Hessian H, the matrix of all second partial derivatives (the Jacobian of the gradient). Because the order of differentiation doesn’t matter for smooth functions, H is symmetric, so it has real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors. Each eigenvector is a direction, and its eigenvalue is the curvature along it: positive curves up, negative curves down. With the gradient and the Hessian you can write the best quadratic approximation of f around any point (the dashed contours below), and minimise that. Drag the dot, then take a gradient step or a Newton step, and watch the saddle and the banana.

\[ H_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j} \]
Read it as: entry (i, j) says how the slope in direction i changes as you move in direction j. Its eigenvalues are the curvatures along its eigenvectors; the second derivative along any unit direction u is u⊤Hu, a weighted average of them.
Say it“H-i-j is the second partial of f with respect to x-i and x-j.”
\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathbf{x}) \approx\ & f(\mathbf{x}_0) + \mathbf{g}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta} + \tfrac12\, \boldsymbol{\delta}^{\top} H\, \boldsymbol{\delta} \\ \boldsymbol{\delta} =\ & \mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the second-order Taylor expansion around x0, with g the gradient and H the Hessian there, and δ the step away from x0. The dashed contours in the plot are this quadratic. It is a good map near the dot and a poor one far away.
Say it“f of x is about f of x-nought, plus g transpose times the step, plus a half the step transpose H the step.”

\[ \epsilon^{*} = \frac{\mathbf{g}^{\top}\mathbf{g}}{\mathbf{g}^{\top} H\, \mathbf{g}} \quad (\text{when } \mathbf{g}^{\top}H\mathbf{g} > 0) \]
Read it as: put the step x = x0 − εg into the quadratic and minimise over ε. Strong curvature along the gradient means a short step. If the curvature along g is zero or negative, the quadratic keeps falling and suggests no finite step.
Say it“The best step size is g transpose g over g transpose H g.”
\[ \mathbf{x}^{*} = \mathbf{x}_0 - H^{-1}\mathbf{g} \]
Read it as: Newton’s method jumps straight to the stationary point of the quadratic model. On a true quadratic bowl that is the minimum in one step. But it goes to any stationary point: where H has a negative eigenvalue, it can jump to a saddle or a maximum. A stationary point is a minimum if every eigenvalue of H is positive, a maximum if all are negative, and a saddle if the signs are mixed.
Say it“x-star is x-nought minus H inverse g.”

Why a stretched bowl slows gradient descent

The ratio of the largest to the smallest curvature is the Hessian’s condition number κ. A fixed gradient step has to be short enough for the steepest direction (above 2/λmax it overshoots and diverges), so it crawls along the flattest one. Below is a quadratic bowl whose condition number you choose. Stretch the bowl and change the step size. Newton’s method uses H to undo the stretch and lands in one step.

\[ \begin{aligned} e_t &\le \left( \frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1} \right)^{t} e_0 \\ e_t &= \lVert \mathbf{x}_t - \mathbf{x}^{*} \rVert, \quad \kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: et is the distance to the minimum after t steps. On a quadratic with the best fixed step, ε = 2/(λmax + λmin), each step multiplies the distance to the minimum by at most (κ − 1)/(κ + 1). With κ = 1 (a round bowl) that is 0: one step. With κ = 100 it is 0.98, and you need hundreds of steps.
Say it“The error after t steps is at most kappa minus one over kappa plus one, to the power t, times the starting error.”

Matrix algebra

Matrix identities and matrix derivatives

Machine-learning formulas are written with matrices because it keeps them short: a whole dataset is a matrix X, a layer is a matrix W. A few rules let you rearrange them. A matrix A is an array with entries Aij (row i, column j); vectors are columns. Transposing reverses the order of a product, and so does inverting. A square matrix has an inverse only when its columns are linearly independent (none is a combination of the others); then its rank is full and its determinant is not zero.

\[ \begin{aligned} (AB)^{\top} &= B^{\top}A^{\top} \\ (AB)^{-1} &= B^{-1}A^{-1} \\ (A^{\top})^{-1} &= (A^{-1})^{\top} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: to undo “first B, then A” you undo A first and B second, like taking off shoes before socks. The same reversal happens with transposes. The third rule says the order of transposing and inverting doesn’t matter.
Say it“A B transpose is B transpose A transpose; A B inverse is B inverse A inverse.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &(P^{-1} + B^{\top}R^{-1}B)^{-1}B^{\top}R^{-1} \\ &\qquad = PB^{\top}(BPB^{\top} + R)^{-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the “push-through” identity. P is n×n and R is m×m; the left side inverts an n×n matrix, the right side an m×m one. It is how the Kalman filter and Gaussian processes move between the two forms of the same answer. Multiply both sides out on the left by (P−1 + B⊤R−1B) and on the right by (BPB⊤ + R) to check it.
Say it“P inverse plus B transpose R inverse B, all inverse, times B transpose R inverse, equals P B transpose times B P B transpose plus R, inverse.”

The most useful identity of the lot is the Woodbury identity. Suppose A is easy to invert (diagonal, say) and you add a low-rank correction UCV, where U is n×k with k much smaller than n. Woodbury replaces one big n×n inverse with one small k×k inverse. This is exactly the shape of a covariance matrix “noise plus a few factors”, and of ridge regression with few features and many data points. Choose n and k and solve the same system both ways.

\[ \begin{aligned} (A + UCV)^{-1} &= A^{-1} \\ &\quad - A^{-1}U\,K^{-1}\,VA^{-1} \\ K &= C^{-1} + VA^{-1}U \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: A is n×n, U is n×k, C is k×k and V is k×n. The only matrix inverted on the right besides A is K, which is just k×k. Here A is diagonal (inverting it is n divisions), C = I and V = U⊤, so we solve (A + UU⊤)x = b.
Say it“A plus U C V, inverse, is A inverse minus A inverse U K-inverse V A inverse, where K is C inverse plus V A inverse U.”

Derivatives with respect to vectors and matrices

Most gradients in machine learning follow from four rules. Each is ordinary calculus written for a whole vector at once: work out one component ∂/∂xi with sums, then recognise the result as a matrix product. Getting a transpose wrong is the classic bug, and the cure is a gradient check: compare your formula with a finite difference. The catch is the step size h. Too large and the difference is a poor approximation of the derivative (truncation error); too small and subtracting two nearly equal floating-point numbers wipes out the significant digits (round-off error). Pick a formula, then sweep h; try the tempting wrong gradient of x⊤Ax.

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\,\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{a} &= \mathbf{a} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\,\mathbf{x}^{\top}A\mathbf{x} &= (A + A^{\top})\mathbf{x} \\ \frac{\partial (AB)}{\partial x} &= \frac{\partial A}{\partial x}B + A\frac{\partial B}{\partial x} \\ \frac{\partial A^{-1}}{\partial x} &= -A^{-1}\frac{\partial A}{\partial x}A^{-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: line 1: a dot product’s gradient is the other vector. Line 2: a quadratic form’s gradient is (A + A⊤)x, which is 2Ax only when A is symmetric. Lines 3 and 4: the product rule (keep the order: matrices don’t commute), and the derivative of an inverse, which follows from differentiating AA−1 = I.
Say it“The derivative of x-transpose-a is a; of x-transpose-A-x is A plus A transpose, times x; of A inverse is minus A inverse, dA, A inverse.”
\[ \begin{aligned} D^{+}_i &= \frac{f(\mathbf{x}+h\mathbf{e}_i) - f(\mathbf{x})}{h} \\ \text{error} &\approx \tfrac{h}{2}\lvert f''\rvert + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{h}\lvert f\rvert \\ D^{\pm}_i &= \frac{f(\mathbf{x}+h\mathbf{e}_i) - f(\mathbf{x}-h\mathbf{e}_i)}{2h} \\ \text{error} &\approx \tfrac{h^2}{6}\lvert f'''\rvert + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{h}\lvert f\rvert \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: D+ is the forward difference, D± the central one; ei nudges only coordinate i; ε ≈ 2.2×10−16 is the float64 rounding unit. The first error term shrinks with h, the second grows as h shrinks, so there is a best h: near √ε ≈ 10−8 for forward differences and ε1/3 ≈ 10−5 for central ones (for functions and derivatives of size about 1).
Say it“Forward difference: f at x plus h e-i, minus f at x, over h. Central: f at x plus h minus f at x minus h, over two h.”

Two numbers per matrix

Traces and determinants

Two numbers summarise a square matrix. The trace is the sum of its diagonal entries; the determinant is the signed factor by which it scales area (volume, in more dimensions). Both are hidden inside the eigenvalues: the trace is their sum and the determinant their product. They turn up everywhere in probability, because a Gaussian’s density contains |Σ|, and its log-likelihood contains ln|Σ| and traces like tr(Σ−1S). Drag the tips of the two columns of A and watch the unit square become a parallelogram; push one column across the other to flip the sign.

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{tr}(AB) &= \operatorname{tr}(BA) \\ \operatorname{tr}(ABC) &= \operatorname{tr}(CAB) = \operatorname{tr}(BCA) \\ \operatorname{tr}(A) &= \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: you may rotate the factors inside a trace (cyclically), though not swap them freely. That rotation is the workhorse trick: a scalar like x⊤Ax equals tr(Axx⊤), which separates the matrix from the data.
Say it“Trace of A B equals trace of B A; the trace is the sum of the eigenvalues.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \lvert AB\rvert &= \lvert A\rvert\,\lvert B\rvert \\ \lvert A^{-1}\rvert &= 1/\lvert A\rvert \\ \lvert A\rvert &= \textstyle\prod_i \lambda_i \\ \lvert I_N + AB\rvert &= \lvert I_M + BA\rvert \\ \lvert I + \mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{\top}\rvert &= 1 + \mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{a} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: doing two maps in a row multiplies their area factors. A is N×M and B is M×N in the third line, so the two identity matrices have different sizes; when one of them is tiny the determinant becomes cheap. With M = 1 the N×N determinant collapses to a single dot product.
Say it“Det of A B is det A times det B; det of I plus a b-transpose is one plus b-transpose a.”

Derivatives of traces and determinants are what you need to fit a Gaussian’s covariance by maximum likelihood. They follow the same rule as before: write one entry with sums, then recognise the matrix. The log-determinant has an especially neat gradient, and the table in the lab checks it live for your A.

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial A}\operatorname{tr}(AB) &= B^{\top} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial A}\operatorname{tr}(A^{\top}B) &= B \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial A}\operatorname{tr}(ABA^{\top}) &= A(B + B^{\top}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ∂/∂A means the matrix of partial derivatives ∂/∂Aij, the same shape as A. tr(AB) = Σi,j AijBji, so the derivative with respect to Aij is Bji: the transpose. The third is the matrix version of “the derivative of a x² is 2ax”.
Say it“The derivative of trace A B with respect to A is B transpose.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\ln\lvert A\rvert &= \operatorname{tr}\!\Big(A^{-1}\frac{\partial A}{\partial x}\Big) \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial A}\ln\lvert A\rvert &= (A^{-1})^{\top} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the log-determinant changes, to first order, by the trace of A−1 times the change in A. Letting x be a single entry Aij gives the matrix form in the second line. In practice compute ln|A| as a sum of logs (of eigenvalues, or of a Cholesky factor’s diagonal): a 400×400 matrix 0.1·I has determinant 10−400, which is 0 in floating point, yet its log-determinant is a harmless −921.03.
Say it“The derivative of log det A with respect to A is A inverse, transposed.”
Numerical computation

Overflow, underflow and the log-sum-exp trick

A computer stores real numbers in a fixed number of bits, so it can only represent a finite set of them. Numbers too large become infinity (overflow); numbers too close to zero become exactly zero (underflow), and every result is rounded to the nearest representable value. Usually this is harmless. It stops being harmless when a formula exponentiates, because ex leaves the representable range quickly: in float64 at x ≈ 709.8, in float32 (the default on GPUs) at x ≈ 88.7.

The softmax, which turns scores into probabilities in every classifier and every language model, divides exponentials by their sum. Large scores make the naive formula compute ∞/∞ = NaN; very negative ones make it 0/0. The fix is to subtract the largest score first, which changes nothing mathematically. Scale and shift the four scores, switch to float32, and break the naive formula.

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{x})_i &= \frac{e^{x_i}}{\sum_j e^{x_j}} \\ &= \frac{e^{x_i - m}}{\sum_j e^{x_j - m}} \\ m &= \max_j x_j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: multiplying the top and bottom by e−m leaves the fraction unchanged. After the shift, the largest exponent is e0 = 1, so nothing overflows, and the denominator is at least 1, so it can’t underflow to 0.
Say it“Softmax of x, entry i, is e to the x-i over the sum of e to the x-j, and it is the same after subtracting the max from every x.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{LSE}(\mathbf{x}) &= \ln \textstyle\sum_j e^{x_j} \\ &= m + \ln \textstyle\sum_j e^{x_j - m} \\ m \le \operatorname{LSE}&(\mathbf{x}) \le m + \ln n \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the log-sum-exp is a smooth maximum: never below the largest score and never more than ln n above it. Computing it with the shift is the safe way to get log-probabilities: ln softmax(x)i = xi − LSE(x), which stays finite even when the probability itself underflows to 0.
Say it“Log-sum-exp of x is m plus the log of the sum of e to the x-j minus m; it lies between m and m plus log n.”

Multiplying many probabilities

The likelihood of a dataset is a product of one probability per example. With a few hundred examples the product falls below the smallest representable number and becomes exactly 0, and then every model looks equally bad. Sums of logs don’t have this problem, which is why every training loss is a log-likelihood. Raise n.

Numerical computation

Poor conditioning: when small errors grow

Every number in a computer carries a small error, from rounding or from measurement. A computation is well conditioned if small errors in its input cause small errors in its output, and poorly conditioned if they get amplified. For solving Ax = b, the amplification is set by A’s condition number. When A’s two columns point in almost the same direction, b barely pins down x: a tiny wobble in b sends x a long way along the nearly shared direction. Close the angle between the columns, then turn the direction of the error.

\[ \begin{aligned} \kappa(A) &= \frac{\sigma_{\max}}{\sigma_{\min}} \\ \frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{x}\rVert}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert} &\le \kappa(A)\,\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{b}\rVert}{\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: σmax and σmin are A’s largest and smallest singular values: the most and least it stretches any direction. For a symmetric matrix they are the largest and smallest |eigenvalue|, so κ = max|λi| / min|λj|. A relative error in b can come out of the solve up to κ times larger. A rule of thumb: you lose about log10 κ of the 16 significant digits float64 gives you.
Say it“Kappa of A is sigma-max over sigma-min; the relative error in x is at most kappa times the relative error in b.”

Constrained optimisation

Lagrange multipliers

Often the best point must also obey a rule: probabilities must add up to 1, a weight vector must have length 1, a budget must balance. Take the rule h(x) = c, a curve in the plane. Walk along it and watch f. Wherever the gradient of f has a component along the curve, you can still climb by walking that way. So at the best point, ∇f must be perpendicular to the curve, which is also the direction of ∇h. The two gradients are parallel, and the factor between them is the Lagrange multiplier λ. You saw this in the hero. Here is the part that makes λ useful: it is a price. It tells you how much the best value f* would improve if the rule were relaxed by one unit. Move the constraint level c, and compare λ with the slope of f*(c).

\[ \begin{aligned} L(\mathbf{x},\lambda) &= f(\mathbf{x}) + \lambda\,\big(c - h(\mathbf{x})\big) \\ \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} L = 0 &\iff \nabla f = \lambda\,\nabla h \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \lambda} = 0 &\iff h(\mathbf{x}) = c \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the Lagrangian L folds the constraint into the objective. Setting its derivatives to zero gives the “gradients are parallel” condition plus the constraint itself: n + 1 equations for the n unknowns in x and the one in λ. (Writing the constraint as g(x) = c − h(x) = 0 gives the textbook form L = f + λg.)
Say it“L of x and lambda is f of x plus lambda times c minus h of x; at the optimum grad f equals lambda grad h.”
\[ \frac{d f^{*}}{d c} = \lambda^{*} \]
Read it as: f*(c) is the best value achievable when the constraint level is c. Its slope is the multiplier at the optimum. A big λ means the constraint is costing you a lot; λ = 0 means it costs nothing (the unconstrained peak happens to lie on the curve). Economists call λ a shadow price.
Say it“The rate of change of the best value with c is the multiplier lambda-star.”

Constrained optimisation

Inequality constraints and the KKT conditions

Most real rules are inequalities: a weight must be non-negative, a dose must stay under a limit. An inequality constraint is either active, with the optimum pressed against the boundary, or inactive, with the optimum strictly inside where the rule doesn’t bind. If it is inactive, it is as if it weren’t there, and its multiplier is 0. If it is active, it behaves like an equality, except that its multiplier must have the right sign: the boundary may push the solution back into the allowed region, never pull it out. These conditions together are the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions. Below we minimise the distance to a target t subject to one or two constraints. Drag the target inside, outside and into the corner.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{\mathbf{x}}\ f(\mathbf{x}) \\ &\text{s.t.}\ \ g_i(\mathbf{x}) = 0,\ \ h_j(\mathbf{x}) \le 0 \\ &L(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\lambda,\boldsymbol\alpha) = f(\mathbf{x}) \\ &\qquad + \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i g_i(\mathbf{x}) + \sum_j \alpha_j h_j(\mathbf{x}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the generalised Lagrangian has one multiplier per constraint: λi for each equality (any sign) and αj ≥ 0 for each inequality. If x breaks a rule, the max over the multipliers of L is +∞; if it obeys them all, that max is just f(x). So minx maxλ, α≥0 L is the constrained problem itself.
Say it“L is f plus the sum of lambda-i g-i plus the sum of alpha-j h-j, with every alpha non-negative.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{(1)}\ \ \nabla f + \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i \nabla g_i \\ &\qquad + \textstyle\sum_j \alpha_j \nabla h_j = 0 \\ &\text{(2)}\ \ g_i(\mathbf{x}) = 0,\quad h_j(\mathbf{x}) \le 0 \\ &\text{(3)}\ \ \alpha_j \ge 0 \\ &\text{(4)}\ \ \alpha_j\, h_j(\mathbf{x}) = 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the four KKT conditions: (1) stationarity, (2) feasibility, (3) the right sign for every inequality multiplier, and (4) complementary slackness. The last says that for each inequality either the multiplier is 0 (inactive) or the constraint holds with equality (active), or both. Under mild regularity they are necessary at a local optimum; for convex problems like this one they are also sufficient. Bishop writes the mirror image, maximising f subject to g(x) ≥ 0 with L = f + λg and λ ≥ 0; the signs flip together and the content is the same.
Say it“Gradient of the Lagrangian is zero; the point is feasible; each alpha is at least zero; and each alpha times its h is zero.”

    Optimising over functions

    The calculus of variations

    Sometimes the unknown is not a vector but a whole function: the shape of a path, a probability density. A functional F[y] takes a function y(x) and returns a number, such as a length, a time or an entropy. To optimise it, perturb y by a small amount ε times any smooth bump η(x) and ask how F changes. The first-order change defines the functional derivative δF/δy(x), the infinite-dimensional cousin of a gradient: one “partial derivative” per point x. At an optimum it must vanish at every x, and that condition is a differential equation, the Euler–Lagrange equation.

    \[ \begin{aligned} F[y] &= \int G\big(y(x),\, y'(x),\, x\big)\, dx \\ F[y + \epsilon\eta] &= F[y] \\ &\quad + \epsilon \int \frac{\delta F}{\delta y(x)}\, \eta(x)\, dx \\ &\quad + O(\epsilon^2) \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: G scores each point of the curve from its height y and slope y′; F adds those scores up. Nudging the curve by εη changes F, to first order, by an integral of δF/δy against the nudge, just as a vector step changes f by the gradient dotted with the step.
    Say it“F of y plus epsilon eta is F of y plus epsilon times the integral of the functional derivative times eta, plus order epsilon squared.”
    \[ \frac{\delta F}{\delta y(x)} = \frac{\partial G}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial y'}\right) = 0 \]
    Read it as: the Euler–Lagrange equation, from integrating the y′ part by parts (the bump vanishes at the fixed end-points). If G does not involve y′ at all, it reduces to ∂G/∂y = 0 at each x separately: an ordinary equation per point, which is how maximum-entropy densities are found.
    Say it“Partial G by partial y, minus d by dx of partial G by partial y-prime, equals zero.”

    The fastest slide: a brachistochrone

    In 1696 Johann Bernoulli asked: along which curve does a bead slide, from rest and without friction, from one point to a lower one in the least time? The straight line is the shortest path but not the fastest, because a steep start builds up speed early. The functional is the travel time, and its Euler–Lagrange equation is solved by a cycloid, the curve traced by a point on a rolling wheel. Here the end is 2 m across and 1 m down, and the cycloid even dips below the finish before climbing back up. Drag the two handles to design your own track, then race it.

    \[ \begin{aligned} T[y] &= \int_0^{X} \sqrt{\frac{1 + y'(x)^2}{2\,g\,y(x)}}\; dx \\ \text{best } y{:}\quad & y\,\big(1 + y'^2\big) = 2R \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: y is the depth below the start, so energy conservation gives the speed √(2gy); arc length over speed, added up, is the time. Because G here does not depend on x, the Euler–Lagrange equation simplifies to the right-hand form, (second line) with a constant 2R; its solution is the cycloid x = R(φ − sin φ), y = R(1 − cos φ). The times below are exact for each track drawn as 400 short straight pieces (on each piece the acceleration is constant).
    Say it“T of y is the integral of the square root of one plus y-prime squared, over two g y; the best curve keeps y times one plus y-prime squared constant.”

    Maximum entropy: optimising a density

    The most important functional in machine learning is entropy. Among all densities that satisfy what you know (they integrate to 1, they have a given mean, perhaps a given variance), which one assumes the least beyond that? Maximise the entropy H[p] subject to the constraints, with one Lagrange multiplier per constraint. G = −p ln p contains no derivative of p, so the Euler–Lagrange equation holds point by point, and the answer is always an exponential of the constrained quantities. Only normalisation gives the uniform density; a fixed mean on [0, ∞) gives the exponential; a fixed mean and variance on the whole line gives the Gaussian. Here x lives on [0, 10], so the edges bend the answers. Pick the constraints and move the mean and spread; the multipliers are found by Newton’s method on the dual, the same solver as in the project.

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}[p] = &-\!\int p\ln p\,dx \\ &+ \lambda_0\Big(\int p\,dx - 1\Big) \\ &+ \lambda_1\Big(\int x\,p\,dx - \mu\Big) \\ &+ \lambda_2\Big(\int x^2 p\,dx - \mu^2 - \sigma^2\Big) \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: the entropy plus one multiplier per piece of knowledge: total probability 1, mean μ, and second moment μ² + σ² (which fixes the variance to σ²).
    Say it“Script L is minus the integral of p log p, plus lambda-nought times the normalisation constraint, plus lambda-one times the mean constraint, plus lambda-two times the second-moment constraint.”
    \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\delta \mathcal{L}}{\delta p(x)} &= -\ln p(x) - 1 \\ &\quad + \lambda_0 + \lambda_1 x + \lambda_2 x^2 = 0 \\ \Rightarrow\ p(x) &\propto e^{\lambda_1 x + \lambda_2 x^2} \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: setting the functional derivative to zero at every x gives a log-density that is linear in the constrained quantities. With λ2 < 0 it is a bell; with λ2 = 0 an exponential; with λ2 > 0 a valley that piles probability at both edges (that happens when you ask for more spread than the uniform has).
    Say it“The derivative of script L with respect to p of x is minus log p minus one plus lambda-nought plus lambda-one x plus lambda-two x squared; setting it to zero makes p an exponential of a quadratic.”

    Real-world project

    Project: the maximum-entropy diet

    In 1945 the economist George Stigler asked for the cheapest diet that meets every nutritional requirement. As a linear programme its answer sits at a corner of the feasible region, and corners use only a handful of foods (his used five, including evaporated milk and cabbage). Here is the opposite question: what is the most varied diet that meets the requirements? Treat the day’s food as a probability distribution p over foods, where pi is the share of the day’s grams that come from food i, and maximise its entropy subject to the nutrient rules. That puts every idea in this chapter to work: Lagrange and KKT multipliers for the rules, log-sum-exp for a stable softmax, and a Newton solver whose Hessian is a covariance matrix.

    The foods are 127 everyday items from the USDA’s FoodData Central (SR Legacy release, public domain): fruit, vegetables, pulses, grains, nuts, dairy, fish, meat and a few treats, with nutrients per 100 g. We fix the day at 1.5 kg of food and require exactly 2,000 kcal (an equality, so its multiplier may have either sign), plus the US daily values as floors and ceilings (inequalities, so their multipliers must be ≥ 0). Move any target and watch which rules bind.

    \[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{\mathbf{p}}\ -\textstyle\sum_i p_i \ln p_i \ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ \textstyle\sum_i p_i = 1 \\ &\textstyle\sum_i p_i\, s_{ki} \ge c_k \ \ \text{for each rule } k \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: ski is food i’s value for rule k, scaled so the target is 1: for a floor, the day’s intake of nutrient k if all 1.5 kg were food i, divided by the target; so ck = 1. For a ceiling, both sides get a minus sign, so “at most 1” becomes “at least −1” and ck = −1. The energy rule holds with equality, c = 1.
    Say it“Maximise the entropy of p, subject to p summing to one and each rule's average score being at least its c-k.”
    \[ \begin{aligned} p_i(\boldsymbol\mu) &= \frac{e^{\sum_k \mu_k s_{ki}}}{\sum_j e^{\sum_k \mu_k s_{kj}}} \\ D(\boldsymbol\mu) &= \operatorname{LSE}_i\Big(\textstyle\sum_k \mu_k s_{ki}\Big) \\ &\quad - \textstyle\sum_k \mu_k c_k \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: stationarity of the Lagrangian makes the diet a softmax of a linear score: each food’s log-share is the sum of its nutrient values weighted by the multipliers. Plugging that back in gives the dual function D, which we minimise over the multipliers (μk ≥ 0 for the inequalities, any sign for energy); its gradient is (average score − 1) and its Hessian is the covariance of the scores under p. Strong duality holds, so min D equals the maximum entropy, and dH*/dbk = −μk/bk for a floor bk: the multiplier is the entropy price of that rule.
    Say it“p-i is a softmax of mu dot s-i; the dual is the log-sum-exp of the scores minus the sum of mu-k c-k.”

    Why does the solver like spinach?

    Every food’s share is a softmax of its score Σkμkski, so you can read off exactly why a food is in or out: each active rule pushes it up or down in proportion to how much of that nutrient it carries per gram. Inactive rules have μ = 0 and push nothing.

    import json
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize
from scipy.special import logsumexp

F = json.load(open("x003-foods.json"))                 # 127 foods from USDA FoodData Central (SR Legacy), per 100 g
cols, foods = F["cols"], F["foods"]
name = [f[0] for f in foods]; group = [f[1] for f in foods]
per100 = {c: np.array([f[cols.index(c)] for f in foods], float) for c in cols[3:]}
M = 1500.0                                             # grams of food in the day; p[i] = share of those grams from food i
# nutrient, daily target, kind: "=" equality, ">=" floor, "<=" ceiling
T = [("kcal", 2000, "="), ("protein", 50, ">="), ("fibre", 28, ">="), ("calcium", 1300, ">="), ("iron", 18, ">="),
     ("potassium", 4700, ">="), ("vitc", 90, ">="), ("sodium", 2300, "<="), ("satfat", 20, "<=")]

def solve(mask, T):
    A = np.stack([M * per100[n][mask] / 100 for n, _, _ in T])          # day's intake if every gram were food i
    sgn = np.array([-1.0 if k == "<=" else 1.0 for _, _, k in T])
    S = sgn[:, None] * A / np.array([b for _, b, _ in T])[:, None]     # constraint k reads  E_p[S_k] >= sgn_k  (or =)
    c = sgn
    def dual(mu):                                                       # D(mu) = log Z(mu) - mu.c,  grad = E_p[S] - c
        z = mu @ S; L = logsumexp(z); p = np.exp(z - L)
        return L - mu @ c, S @ p - c
    bounds = [(None, None) if k == "=" else (0, None) for _, _, k in T]
    r = minimize(dual, np.zeros(len(T)), jac=True, method="L-BFGS-B", bounds=bounds,
                 options=dict(ftol=1e-15, gtol=1e-12, maxiter=10000))
    mu = r.x; z = mu @ S; p = np.exp(z - logsumexp(z))
    # independent check: maximise the entropy directly over the 127 shares with SLSQP
    n = mask.sum(); cons = [dict(type="eq", fun=lambda q: q.sum() - 1)]
    for k, (_, _, kind) in enumerate(T):
        cons.append(dict(type="eq" if kind == "=" else "ineq", fun=lambda q, k=k: S[k] @ q - c[k]))
    q = minimize(lambda q: np.sum(q * np.log(q)), np.full(n, 1 / n), jac=lambda q: np.log(q) + 1, method="SLSQP",
                 bounds=[(1e-12, 1)] * n, constraints=cons, options=dict(ftol=1e-15, maxiter=2000)).x
    return mu, p, A, q, r.fun

def report(label, mask, T=T):
    mu, p, A, q, Dval = solve(mask, T)
    H = -np.sum(p * np.log(p)); idx = np.flatnonzero(mask)
    print(f"{label}: {mask.sum()} foods   H* = {H:.6f} nats (dual {Dval:.6f}, SLSQP {-np.sum(q * np.log(q)):.6f})"
          f"   e^H = {np.exp(H):.2f} effective foods   max|p - p_SLSQP| = {np.abs(p - q).max():.1e}")
    for k, (n, b, kind) in enumerate(T):
        print(f"   {n:9s} {kind:2s} {b:5g}   intake {A[k] @ p:8.2f}   multiplier {mu[k]:+.5f}"
              + ("   active" if abs(mu[k]) > 1e-7 else ""))
    top = np.argsort(-p)[:6]
    print("   most grams:", ", ".join(f"{name[idx[i]]} {M * p[i]:.1f}" for i in top))

    What this is and isn’t. The maximum-entropy diet is the least committed distribution that meets the rules on average: read pi as how often a gram of your food is food i, not as a plate to cook tomorrow. Its entropy depends on how the food list is drawn up (split “cheese” into ten cheeses and cheese gains weight), so we use one entry per everyday food. Nutrient values the USDA never measured for a food (saturated fat in dates; fibre and vitamin C in prawns; vitamin C in dark chocolate) are set to 0, the usual reading of a blank as a trace amount. And this is an illustration of constrained optimisation, not dietary advice.

    Wrap-up

    Five lines to remember

    • The gradient points uphill and gives the slope in any direction as a dot product; the Jacobian is a function’s local linear map, and |det J| is how it scales area.
    • The Hessian holds the curvature: its eigenvalues classify stationary points, set the best step size, power Newton’s method, and their ratio, the condition number, sets how slowly gradient descent crawls.
    • A handful of identities (reversed products, Woodbury, cyclic traces, ∂ ln|A|/∂A = A−⊤) do most of the algebra; check every derived gradient against a finite difference with a sensible h.
    • Floats overflow, underflow and round: subtract the max before exponentiating (log-sum-exp), add logs instead of multiplying probabilities, and expect to lose about log10 κ digits when solving with a matrix.
    • At a constrained optimum the forces balance: ∇f is a combination of the constraint gradients, with Lagrange multipliers that are prices; inequalities add the KKT sign and complementary-slackness rules, and the same reasoning over functions gives the Euler–Lagrange equation and maximum-entropy distributions.

    Check your understanding

    At a point where ∇f = (3, 4), what is the largest slope in any direction, and what is the slope in direction u = (0, 1)?

    The largest slope is ‖∇f‖ = 5, in the direction (0.6, 0.8). Along (0, 1) it is u⊤∇f = 4.

    The Hessian at a stationary point has eigenvalues 2 and −0.5. What kind of point is it, and where would Newton’s method from nearby send you?

    Mixed signs: a saddle. Newton’s method jumps to the stationary point of the local quadratic, so near the saddle it heads to the saddle, not away from it. That is one reason pure Newton is rarely used to train networks.

    Why is the gradient of x⊤Ax not always 2Ax?

    Component i of x⊤Ax = Σjk xjAjkxk differentiates to Σk Aikxk + Σj xjAji, i.e. (A + A⊤)x. It equals 2Ax only when A = A⊤.

    Your softmax returns NaN for scores (1000, 999, 998). What happened and what is the fix?

    e1000 overflows to ∞ in float64, so the formula computes ∞/∞. Subtract the max first: the scores become (0, −1, −2) and the softmax is (0.665, 0.245, 0.090), unchanged mathematically.

    An inequality constraint has KKT multiplier α = 0 at the optimum. What does that tell you, and what would tightening it slightly do?

    By complementary slackness the constraint is inactive (or only just touching): the optimum doesn’t lean on it. To first order, tightening it slightly changes the optimal value by α times the change, which is 0, as long as it stays inactive.
