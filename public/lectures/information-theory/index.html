Information Theory

Entropy, KL divergence and mutual information

In 1948 Claude Shannon gave “information” a number: an event carries as many bits as it is surprising. Average that over everything that could happen and you get entropy, the shortest possible length of a message. The same arithmetic is inside every classifier’s loss, every language model and every zip file. In the figure, a model of English learned from Pride and Prejudice reads your text one letter at a time; each bar is as tall as that letter surprised it. Type anything in the box and switch the model: a better predictor means fewer bits.

The idea

Information is surprise

“The sun rose this morning” tells you almost nothing; “it snowed in the Sahara” tells you a lot. Shannon asked what a measure of information h(x) must look like if it depends only on how likely the event was. Three wishes pin it down. A certain event should carry nothing, so h = 0 when p = 1. Rarer events should carry more. And two independent events should carry the sum of their information, because learning both is learning one and then the other. Probabilities of independent events multiply; the only smooth function that turns products into sums is the logarithm. So information is the logarithm of 1/p.

The base only sets the unit. Base 2 gives bits (one fair coin flip is exactly one bit), base e gives nats, which make the calculus tidy and are what machine-learning libraries use. Slide the two probabilities and watch the surprises add while the probabilities multiply.

\[ h(x) = -\log_2 p(x) = \log_2 \frac{1}{p(x)} \]
Read it as: the information in seeing x is how many times you would have to halve your odds to get from “certain” down to p(x). p = ½ is 1 bit, p = ¼ is 2 bits, p = 1/1024 is 10 bits. Using ln instead of log2 gives nats; 1 nat = log2 e ≈ 1.443 bits.
Say it“h of x is minus log base two of p of x.”
\[ \begin{aligned} h(x,y) &= -\log_2 \big[p(x)\,p(y)\big] \\ &= h(x) + h(y) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for independent events the joint probability is a product, and the logarithm of a product is a sum. Two coin flips both landing heads (p = ¼) is 1 + 1 = 2 bits.
Say it“The information in x and y together is the information in x plus the information in y, when they are independent.”

Averaging

Entropy: the average surprise

Before you look, you don’t know which outcome you will get, but you do know how surprised you will be on average. That average is the entropy H, a property of the whole distribution rather than of one outcome. A distribution that piles everything on one outcome has low entropy: you can nearly guess the answer. A flat one has high entropy: every outcome is a surprise. For M outcomes the flat distribution is the worst case, at log2 M bits. When p(x) = 0 the term p log p is taken as 0, its limit, so impossible outcomes add nothing.

Below are eight outcomes, think of eight horses in a race. Drag the tops of the bars to reshape the distribution, or start from a preset. 2H is the perplexity: the number of equally likely outcomes that would be just as uncertain.

\[ \begin{aligned} H[x] &= -\sum_{x} p(x)\,\log_2 p(x) \\ &= \mathbb{E}\big[h(x)\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: weight each outcome’s surprise −log2 p(x) by how often it happens, and add up. Rare outcomes are very surprising but rarely seen, so they contribute little; the bulk of the entropy comes from outcomes that are neither certain nor rare.
Say it“H of x is minus the sum over x of p of x times log p of x: the expected surprise.”
\[ 0 \;\le\; H[x] \;\le\; \log_2 M \]
Read it as: entropy is zero only when one outcome is certain, and it reaches its ceiling, log2 M, only when all M outcomes are equally likely. The next section shows why the flat distribution wins.
Say it“Entropy lies between zero and log of the number of outcomes.”

With only two outcomes, say a biased coin that lands heads with probability p, entropy is a single hump. It is 1 bit for a fair coin and falls to 0 as the coin becomes certain either way. Move p: notice how flat the top is. A 70–30 coin still carries 0.88 bits per flip.

Where the formula comes from

Counting arrangements

The formula −Σ p ln p also falls out of plain counting, which is how it first appeared in physics. Put N numbered balls into M bins, ni of them in bin i. Many different orderings give the same counts: you may shuffle the balls inside each bin without changing anything. The number of distinct arrangements is the multiplicity W. For large N, Stirling’s approximation ln N! ≈ N ln N − N turns (1/N) ln W into exactly the entropy of the proportions pi = ni/N. Entropy is the log of the number of ways a distribution can happen, per ball.

Pick proportions and grow N: the per-ball log-count creeps up to H. Then compare the presets: the flat one has the most arrangements, which is the counting reason that the uniform distribution has maximum entropy.

\[ \begin{aligned} W &= \frac{N!}{\prod_i n_i!} \\ \frac{1}{N}\ln W &\to -\sum_i p_i \ln p_i \\ &\phantom{\to}\ \ \text{as } N\to\infty \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: N! orderings of the balls, divided by the ni! reshuffles inside each bin that change nothing. Take the logarithm, divide by N, and hold the proportions fixed while N grows: the result tends to the entropy in nats.
Say it“W is N factorial over the product of the n-i factorials; one over N log W tends to minus the sum of p-i log p-i.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde H &= -\sum_i p_i \ln p_i \\ &\quad + \lambda\Big(\sum_i p_i - 1\Big) \\ \frac{\partial \tilde H}{\partial p_i} &= -\ln p_i - 1 + \lambda = 0 \\ &\Rightarrow\; p_i = \frac{1}{M} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: to find the distribution with the most entropy, add a Lagrange multiplier λ to keep the probabilities summing to 1, and set the derivative to zero. Every pi must equal the same number, eλ−1, so all are 1/M, and the maximum entropy is ln M nats.
Say it“Maximise the entropy plus lambda times the sum of p minus one; the derivative vanishes only when every p-i is one over M.”

The coding view

Codes: compression is prediction

Suppose you must radio the winner of the eight-horse race to a friend, in binary. Give every horse a 3-bit label and each message costs 3 bits. But if horse A wins half the time, give it a 1-bit codeword and pay for that with longer words for the outsiders. A prefix code, in which no codeword is the start of another, can be decoded without separators. Shannon’s source coding theorem says no such code can average fewer than H bits per message, and that you can get within one bit of H. Huffman’s algorithm (1952) builds the best possible prefix code: repeatedly merge the two least likely items into one, until a single tree remains; the path to each leaf is its codeword.

The ideal codeword for x has length −log2 p(x): exactly its surprise. So to compress well you must predict well, and a model that predicts well can be turned into a compressor. Drag the bars at the bottom of the tree, and try coding blocks of several messages at once: the cost per message falls towards H.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell(x) &\approx -\log_2 p(x) \\ \bar L &= \sum_x p(x)\,\ell(x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: likely messages get short codewords, rare ones long codewords, each about as long as its surprise. The average length L̄ is what you pay per message in the long run. Huffman’s lengths are whole numbers, so they can only approximate −log2 p.
Say it“The length of x’s codeword is about minus log two of p of x; the average length is the sum of p of x times that length.”
\[ H[x] \;\le\; \frac{\bar L_k}{k} \;<\; H[x] + \frac{1}{k} \]
Read it as: code k independent messages at a time with a Huffman code over all Mk blocks. The cost per message, L̄k/k, can never beat the entropy, and the waste above it is less than 1/k bits. With k = 1 the waste is under a bit; with long blocks it vanishes.
Say it“Entropy is at most the block length over k, which is less than entropy plus one over k.”

Continuous variables

Differential entropy and the Gaussian

For a continuous variable, chop the line into bins of width Δ. Bin i has probability about p(xi)Δ, and the ordinary entropy of the bins splits into two parts: an integral that settles down, and a term −ln Δ that grows without limit. That makes sense: pinning down a real number exactly would take infinitely many bits. The settled part is the differential entropy h[x]. It measures spread, not information content in bits you could send, and unlike discrete entropy it can be negative: a density narrower than one unit has h < 0.

Which density is the most uncertain for a given spread? Fix the mean and the variance, maximise h with Lagrange multipliers, and the answer is the Gaussian. This is one reason Gaussians are a safe default: they assume nothing beyond the first two moments. Shrink the bin width Δ and watch the bins’ entropy run away while HΔ + ln Δ stays put; then switch shapes at the same σ.

\[ \begin{aligned} H_\Delta &= -\sum_i p(x_i)\Delta\,\ln\!\big(p(x_i)\Delta\big) \\ &\approx -\int p(x)\ln p(x)\,dx \;-\; \ln\Delta \\ h[x] &= -\int p(x)\,\ln p(x)\,dx \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the entropy of the binned variable equals the differential entropy h[x] minus ln Δ. Halve the bins and you need ln 2 more nats (one more bit) to say which bin you are in; the shape’s own contribution, h[x], does not change.
Say it“The binned entropy is about minus the integral of p log p, minus log delta.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_p\, h[x] \ \text{ given }\ \textstyle\int p = 1, \\ &\mathbb{E}[x] = \mu,\ \operatorname{Var}[x] = \sigma^2 \\ &\Rightarrow\ p(x) = \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu, \sigma^2) \\ &\Rightarrow\ h = \tfrac12 \ln\!\big(2\pi e\,\sigma^2\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with three Lagrange multipliers, the stationary point is p(x) ∝ exp(λ2x + λ3(x − μ)2), an exponential of a quadratic: a Gaussian. Its entropy grows with the log of σ and is negative when σ2 < 1/(2πe), that is σ < 0.242.
Say it“Of all densities with mean mu and variance sigma squared, the Gaussian has the largest entropy: one half log of two pi e sigma squared.”

Comparing distributions

KL divergence and Jensen’s inequality

Suppose the truth is p but you built your code (or your model) for q. Each message x now costs −log q(x) instead of −log p(x). The average extra cost is the Kullback–Leibler divergence KL(p‖q): how many extra nats (or bits) per message you waste by believing q when the world is p. It is zero exactly when q = p, and it is never negative. That “never” is a theorem, and its proof rests on Jensen’s inequality.

A function is convex if every chord lies on or above its curve. Jensen says that for convex f, the function of an average is at most the average of the function: f(E[x]) ≤ E[f(x)]. Drag the two points along the curve and move the weight: the chord’s point, the average of f, never dips below the curve, f of the average.

\[ \begin{aligned} &f\Big(\sum_i \lambda_i x_i\Big) \;\le\; \sum_i \lambda_i f(x_i) \\ &\lambda_i \ge 0,\ \ \sum_i \lambda_i = 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for a convex f, mixing the inputs first and then applying f gives no more than applying f first and then mixing. With the λs read as probabilities: f(E[x]) ≤ E[f(x)]. Equality needs all the xi to be equal (or f straight in between).
Say it“f of the weighted average is at most the weighted average of f.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{KL}(p\,\Vert\,q) &= \sum_x p(x) \ln\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \\ &= -\sum_x p(x)\ln\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} \\ &\ge -\ln \sum_x q(x) = 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the extra nats per message for coding p with q’s code. The second line is Jensen with the convex f = −ln, averaged under p: the average of −ln(q/p) is at least −ln of the average of q/p, and that average is Σ q = 1. So KL ≥ 0, with equality only when q = p.
Say it“KL of p from q is the sum of p log p over q; by Jensen it is at least minus log one, which is zero.”

Asymmetry in action

Which way round? Forward and reverse KL

Because KL is asymmetric, the direction you minimise matters, and it matters most when the model is too simple for the truth. Take a true distribution p with two humps and a model q that can only be one Gaussian. Minimising KL(p‖q) punishes q wherever p has mass and q has little, so q stretches to cover both humps, even if that puts its peak in the empty valley. Minimising KL(q‖p) punishes q wherever q has mass and p has little, so, once the humps are far enough apart, q hides inside one hump and ignores the other. (Closer together, ignoring half of p costs more than straddling a shallow valley, and the reverse fit also sits in the middle, only narrower.) The first behaviour is called mode-covering, the second mode-seeking. Maximum likelihood is the forward kind; variational inference (later in the book) uses the reverse kind.

Press both Fit buttons, then bring the humps closer and fit again. You can also steer q by hand. For the reverse fit, the hump q ends up in depends on where it starts.

\[ \begin{aligned} q^{*}_{\text{fwd}} &= \arg\min_q \mathrm{KL}(p\,\Vert\,q) \\ &= \arg\min_q \; -\mathbb{E}_{p}\big[\ln q(x)\big] \\ q^{*}_{\text{rev}} &= \arg\min_q \mathrm{KL}(q\,\Vert\,p) \\ &= \arg\min_q \; \mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\ln q(x) - \ln p(x)\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the forward fit averages over the truth: every place p visits must get decent probability from q. For a Gaussian q the answer is simply q’s mean and variance set to p’s (moment matching). The reverse fit averages over the model: q is free to ignore regions where it puts no mass, but must avoid putting mass where p is small.
Say it“Forward: choose q to minimise KL of p from q, the expected minus log q under p. Reverse: choose q to minimise KL of q from p, averaged under q itself.”

In every classifier

Cross-entropy: the classification loss

The cross-entropy H(p, q) is the average cost of coding the truth p with the model q: the entropy H(p) you could never avoid, plus the KL penalty for using the wrong model. When you train a model, p is fixed by the data, so minimising cross-entropy and minimising KL(p‖q) are the same thing.

In classification, the label is a one-hot p: probability 1 on the true class. H(p) = 0, every term but one drops out, and the loss is −ln q(true class): the model’s surprise at the right answer. Averaged over a training set this is the negative log-likelihood, so training with cross-entropy is maximum likelihood (chapter 0.7). A softmax turns the network’s scores z into q, and the gradient of the loss with respect to the scores is just q − p: prediction minus target. Move the three scores, pick the true class, and try label smoothing, which replaces the one-hot target with a slightly softened one.

\[ \begin{aligned} H(p, q) &= -\sum_x p(x)\ln q(x) \\ &= H(p) + \mathrm{KL}(p\,\Vert\,q) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the surprise of the model q, averaged over the truth p. Split −ln q into −ln p plus ln(p/q) and the two pieces are the entropy and the divergence. Since H(p) does not depend on q, the q that minimises cross-entropy is the q that minimises KL: q = p.
Say it“The cross-entropy of q relative to p is minus the sum of p log q, which is the entropy of p plus the KL divergence from p to q.”
\[ \begin{aligned} q_k &= \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} \\ \frac{\partial H(p,q)}{\partial z_k} &= q_k - p_k \\ -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n} \ln q(y_n \mid \mathbf{x}_n) &= H(\hat p, q) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the softmax turns scores into probabilities, and cross-entropy through a softmax has the simplest possible gradient: how much the model over- or under-predicts each class. Over a data set, the average loss is the cross-entropy between the empirical label distribution p̂ and the model, which is minus the log-likelihood divided by N.
Say it“q-k is e to the z-k over the sum of e to the z-j; the gradient of the loss in z-k is q-k minus p-k. The mean loss is minus the log-likelihood over N.”

Two variables

Conditional entropy and mutual information

With two variables, x and y, ask how uncertain y remains once you know x. That is the conditional entropy H[y|x]: the entropy of y within each value of x, averaged over x. The joint uncertainty splits neatly, by the chain rule: describe x, then describe y given x. The amount by which knowing x shrinks the uncertainty about y is the mutual information I[x, y]. It is symmetric (x tells you as much about y as y tells you about x), never negative, and zero exactly when the two are independent. It is also a KL divergence: how far the joint distribution is from the product of its marginals, the distribution it would have if x and y ignored each other.

Below, x is the weather and y is what a colleague brings to work. Turn up how closely their choice follows the weather, and change the climate. The bar diagram shows H[x] and H[y] overlapping by exactly I[x, y].

\[ \begin{aligned} H[y \mid x] &= \mathbb{E}_{x,y}\big[-\log_2 p(y \mid x)\big] \\ H[x, y] &= H[x] + H[y \mid x] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the surprise of y once x is known, −log2 p(y|x), averaged over the joint distribution; written out, −Σx,y p(x, y) log2 p(y|x). The chain rule follows from p(x, y) = p(x) p(y|x): take −log of both sides and average. Knowing more never hurts on average: H[y|x] ≤ H[y].
Say it“H of y given x is the expected value of minus log p of y given x; the joint entropy is H of x plus H of y given x.”
\[ \begin{aligned} I[x, y] &= \mathrm{KL}\big(p(x,y)\,\Vert\,p(x)p(y)\big) \\ &= H[y] - H[y \mid x] \\ &= H[x] - H[x \mid y] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: mutual information is the reduction in uncertainty about y from learning x, and the same number the other way round. As a KL divergence it is ≥ 0, and it is 0 only when p(x, y) = p(x)p(y). In Bayesian terms: x is data, y a hypothesis, and I is how much the data is expected to teach you.
Say it“The mutual information between x and y is the KL divergence of the joint from the product of the marginals: H of y minus H of y given x.”

For continuous variables the same definition works with densities. For a pair of Gaussians with correlation ρ it has a closed form, and it shows what correlation alone hides: as ρ approaches ±1 the information grows without bound, because one variable pins the other down to any precision. Slide ρ.

\[ \begin{aligned} I[x, y] &= h[x] + h[y] - h[x, y] \\ &= -\tfrac12 \ln\big(1 - \rho^2\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: using the Gaussian differential entropies from above: each marginal has ½ ln(2πeσ2), the pair has ½ ln((2πe)2|Σ|), and the difference leaves only the correlation. At ρ = 0 it is 0; at ρ = 0.9 it is 0.83 nats.
Say it“For two jointly Gaussian variables, the mutual information is minus a half log of one minus rho squared.”

Real-world project

Project: how much information is in a novel?

In 1951 Shannon estimated the entropy of printed English by asking people to guess the next letter of a text. We will do the measurement with counting instead, on four public-domain novels from Project Gutenberg: Pride and Prejudice (Austen, 1813), Moby-Dick (Melville, 1851), Frankenstein (Shelley, 1818) and Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland (Carroll, 1865). Each text runs from its first sentence to its last, with Gutenberg’s licence and front matter removed. As Shannon did, we reduce it to a 27-symbol alphabet: the letters a–z, lower-cased, plus one space for every run of spaces, punctuation and digits. The page holds the counts (letters, letter pairs at distances 1 to 12, letter triples, word frequencies) and computes every number below live. Pick a novel.

One letter at a time: entropy and a Huffman code

Count the 27 symbols, compute their entropy, and build a Huffman code for them. The space is the commonest symbol, then e and t. A fixed-length code for 27 symbols needs 5 bits (25 = 32); ASCII uses 8.

Better prediction, fewer bits: Shannon’s ladder

A letter is easier to predict once you know the one before it (after q, almost certainly u), and easier still given two. The conditional entropies Fk = H[xt | previous k−1 letters] fall step by step. But a model that counts triples in the very text it is scored on partly memorises it. The honest test is held-out text: learn the counts from the first 90% of the novel and pay, in bits, for coding the last 10% with them. That cost is a cross-entropy, and it is exactly what an arithmetic coder driven by the model would achieve. Unseen combinations need a little probability, so each count gets α added (additive smoothing). Move α and watch which bars respond.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{bits} &= -\tfrac{1}{M}\textstyle\sum_{t} \log_2 q\big(x_t \mid \text{ctx}_t\big) \\ \text{ctx}_t &= x_{t-k}\ldots x_{t-1} \\ q(c \mid \text{ctx}) &= \frac{n(\text{ctx}, c) + \alpha}{n(\text{ctx}) + 27\,\alpha} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the cost in bits per character: slide along the M held-out characters; at each one, ask the model how likely the actual next letter was given the k letters before it, and charge −log2 of that. The model’s probabilities are counts from the training part, each nudged by α so that no letter is ever impossible. This is the cross-entropy H(p̂test, q).
Say it“Bits per character is minus the average of log two q of each letter given its context, where q is the smoothed count of context-then-letter over the count of the context.”

How far does a letter remember? Mutual information with distance

Measure I[xt, xt+d], the mutual information between a letter and the one d places later, from the pair counts. Neighbours share most of a bit; by a word’s length the dependence has nearly gone, though never quite zero. The scale is logarithmic, so each grid line down is ten times less information. Slide d to see which pairs carry the information: the cells of the joint table with the largest share p(a, b) log2[p(a, b) / p(a)p(b)].

def normalise(raw, start, end):                 # 27 symbols: space and a-z
    t = raw[raw.index(start):raw.index(end, raw.index(start))]
    t = re.sub(r"\[Illustration[^\]]*\]", " ", t)
    t = "".join(c for c in unicodedata.normalize("NFKD", t) if not unicodedata.combining(c)).lower()
    t = re.sub(r"['‘’]", "", t)
    return re.sub(r"\s*\bthe end\b\s*$", "", re.sub(r"[^a-z]+", " ", t).strip())

def H(counts):                                   # entropy in bits of a table of counts
    p = np.asarray(counts, float).ravel(); p = p[p > 0] / p.sum()
    return -(p * np.log2(p)).sum()

def huffman_bits(counts):                        # average Huffman code length, bits per symbol
    h = [float(c) for c in counts if c > 0]; heapq.heapify(h); tot, cost = sum(h), 0.0
    while len(h) > 1:
        a, b = heapq.heappop(h), heapq.heappop(h); cost += a + b; heapq.heappush(h, a + b)
    return cost / tot

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Information is surprise: an outcome of probability p carries −log2 p bits (or −ln p nats), and surprises of independent events add.
  • Entropy H = −Σ p log p is the average surprise and the floor on average code length; it is largest for the uniform distribution, and for a fixed variance the Gaussian has the largest differential entropy.
  • KL(p‖q) = Σ p log(p/q) is the extra cost of believing q when the truth is p. Jensen’s inequality makes it ≥ 0; it is asymmetric, and the direction you minimise decides between covering every mode and hugging one.
  • Cross-entropy H(p, q) = H(p) + KL(p‖q). With one-hot labels it is −ln q(true class): training a classifier with it is maximum likelihood, and its gradient through a softmax is q − p.
  • Mutual information I = H[y] − H[y|x] = KL(p(x,y)‖p(x)p(y)) measures dependence of any shape. In real English, knowing the previous letter saves about 0.8 bits, and a model that predicts better compresses better: that is why a language model’s loss is quoted in bits per character.

Check your understanding

A die is loaded so that six comes up half the time and the other faces share the rest equally. What is its entropy in bits, and how does it compare with a fair die?

H = ½·1 + 5·(0.1·log2 10) = 0.5 + 1.661 = 2.161 bits. A fair die has log2 6 = 2.585 bits; the loaded die is more predictable, so it carries less information per roll.

Why can differential entropy be negative when discrete entropy cannot?

Discrete entropy averages −log p with p ≤ 1, so every term is ≥ 0. A density can exceed 1 (a narrow Gaussian peaks far above 1), so −ln p(x) can be negative over most of its mass. Differential entropy is the binned entropy with the diverging −ln Δ removed, so only differences between differential entropies have an absolute meaning.

Your classifier outputs q = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1) and the true class is the second. What is the cross-entropy loss, and what is the gradient on the scores?

Loss = −ln 0.2 = 1.609 nats (2.32 bits). The gradient is q − p = (0.7, 0.2 − 1, 0.1) = (0.7, −0.8, 0.1): gradient descent lowers the first and third scores and raises the second.

You fit a single Gaussian to a two-humped distribution by maximum likelihood. Does it sit on one hump or between them?

Between them. Maximum likelihood minimises the forward KL(p‖q), which is mode-covering: q matches p’s mean and variance, so with well-separated humps its peak sits in the valley. Minimising the reverse KL(q‖p) would pick one hump.

Two variables have zero correlation. Must their mutual information be zero?

No. Correlation only measures straight-line dependence. If y = x² with x symmetric around 0, the correlation is 0 but y is completely determined by x, so I[x, y] is large. Mutual information is zero only under full independence, p(x, y) = p(x)p(y).
