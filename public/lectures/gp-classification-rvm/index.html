GP Classification, ARD & the Relevance Vector Machine

One idea, a separate prior for every knob, gives you relevant inputs, honest probabilities and a handful of prototypes

Chapter 1.9b put a Gaussian process prior on a regression curve and learned its kernel from the evidence. This chapter pushes that idea three ways. Give every input its own length scale and the evidence switches off the useless ones. Pass the GP through a sigmoid and it classifies, with a probability that knows where the data runs out. Give every weight of a kernel model its own prior and almost all of them collapse to zero: what survives is the relevance vector machine, a kernel classifier as sparse as an SVM, usually sparser, that also returns probabilities. On the right, colour is the GP classifier’s probability, faded where it is unsure; the stars are the RVM’s relevance vectors. Drag any dot.

Relevance, learned

Automatic relevance determination

The squared-exponential kernel of chapter 1.9b measures distance with one ruler for every input. That is a strong assumption: with three inputs it says a step of 1 along a useless input matters exactly as much as a step of 1 along the input that drives everything. The fix, due to MacKay and Neal, is to give each input i its own precision ηi and to learn all of them by maximising the evidence, as you learned the length scale before.

The data below has three inputs. The target is t = sin(2x1) plus a little noise; x2 is a noisy copy of x1, so it carries some of the same information, and x3 is pure noise. Nobody tells the GP this. Press “Climb the evidence” and watch the three precisions separate. You can also set them by hand with the sliders and see what the evidence thinks of your choice.

\[ \begin{aligned} &C(\mathbf{x}_n,\mathbf{x}_m) = \theta_0\, e^{-\frac12 Q_{nm}} + \beta^{-1}\delta_{nm} \\ &Q_{nm} = \sum_{i=1}^{D}\eta_i\,(x_{ni}-x_{mi})^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Qnm is a weighted squared distance between the two inputs. θ0 sets how far the function swings; β−1 is the noise variance, added only where n = m. Each ηi says how fast the function changes along input i: its length scale is ℓi = 1/√ηi. As ηi → 0 the kernel stops looking at input i at all, so the function is flat along it: that input has been judged irrelevant.
Say it“C of x-n and x-m is theta-nought times e to the minus a half Q-n-m, plus the noise variance when n equals m; Q-n-m is the sum over inputs of eta-i times the squared gap.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\ln p(\mathbf{t}\mid\boldsymbol\theta) = -\tfrac12\ln|\mathbf{C}| \\ &\quad - \tfrac12\mathbf{t}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{t} - \tfrac N2\ln 2\pi \\ &\partial_j \ln p = -\tfrac12\operatorname{Tr}\big(\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{C}'_j\big) \\ &\quad + \tfrac12\,\mathbf{t}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{C}'_j\,\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{t} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the evidence from chapter 1.9b, now with D + 2 knobs. C′j is short for ∂C/∂θj and ∂j for ∂/∂θj. The second term rewards fitting the targets; the first charges for a flexible, high-volume prior. The gradient needs C−1 once and then one trace per knob, so climbing it is cheap. The climb below takes gradient steps on ln θ0, ln η1, ln η2, ln η3 and ln β−1 together.
Say it“Log evidence is minus a half log-det C, minus a half t-transpose C-inverse t, minus N over two log two pi; its slope in theta-j is minus a half the trace of C-inverse C-prime, plus a half t C-inverse C-prime C-inverse t.”

From regression to classes

Gaussian processes for classification: squash a random function

A GP produces real numbers, but a class label is 0 or 1. Logistic regression (chapter 1.3) solved the same problem for a straight line: compute a score a(x), then squash it with the sigmoid σ(a) = 1/(1 + e−a) into a probability. Do the same here, with the score drawn from a GP. The GP now describes the log-odds a(x) = ln[p/(1 − p)], a smooth random function; the sigmoid turns every draw into a smooth random probability curve; each label is then a coin flip with that probability.

Change the signal size and length scale, then draw new functions. A large signal pushes a(x) far from zero, so probabilities snap to almost 0 or 1: the prior believes in confident, near-deterministic classes. A small signal keeps every probability close to ½.

\[ \begin{aligned} &p(t\mid a) = \sigma(a)^{t}\,\big(1-\sigma(a)\big)^{1-t} \\ &a(\mathbf{x}) \sim \mathcal{GP}(0,\,k), \\ &C(\mathbf{x}_n,\mathbf{x}_m) = k(\mathbf{x}_n,\mathbf{x}_m) + \nu\,\delta_{nm} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: labels are t ∈ {0, 1}; the probability of t = 1 is σ(a). The latent a has a GP prior with kernel k. Unlike regression there is no noise on a, because the coin flip already supplies the randomness; ν is only a tiny constant on the diagonal that keeps C safely positive definite in floating point. Sampling the curves on the right uses ν = 10−8; the classifiers on this page never invert C, so they use ν = 0, as scikit-learn does.
Say it“The chance of label t given a is sigma of a to the t times one minus sigma of a to the one minus t; a is a Gaussian process with kernel k, plus nu on the diagonal.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &p(t_{N+1}=1 \mid \mathbf{t}_N) \\ &\quad = \int \sigma(a_{N+1})\; p(a_{N+1}\mid \mathbf{t}_N)\; \mathrm{d}a_{N+1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: to predict, average the sigmoid over everything the latent value at the new point might be, given the training labels. In regression both pieces were Gaussian and the answer came in closed form. Here the likelihood is a product of sigmoids, so p(aN+1 | tN) is not Gaussian and the integral has no formula. The next section replaces the posterior over the latent values by a Gaussian.
Say it“The predictive probability is the integral of sigma of a-N-plus-one, weighted by the posterior of a-N-plus-one given the training labels.”

Making it tractable

The Laplace approximation for GP classifiers

Chapter 1.3b met the Laplace approximation: find the peak of a posterior, measure how sharply it curves there, and stand a Gaussian in its place. Here the unknowns are the N latent values aN = (a(x1), …, a(xN)) at the training points. Their log posterior, up to a constant, is the GP prior plus the log-likelihood of the labels. It is concave, so it has one peak, and Newton’s method finds it in a handful of steps.

Below, dots on the top row have t = 1, dots on the bottom row t = 0. Press “Newton step” a few times and watch the latent values (top panel) settle; then click on the plot to add or erase points. Notice the gap between x = 5.5 and 8.3: the latent band widens there and the probability eases towards ½.

\[ \begin{aligned} \Psi(\mathbf{a}_N) &= \ln p(\mathbf{a}_N) + \ln p(\mathbf{t}_N\mid\mathbf{a}_N) \\ &= -\tfrac12\mathbf{a}_N^{\top}\mathbf{C}_N^{-1}\mathbf{a}_N + \mathbf{t}_N^{\top}\mathbf{a}_N \\ &\quad - \textstyle\sum_n \ln(1+e^{a_n}) + \text{const} \\ \nabla\Psi &= \mathbf{t}_N - \boldsymbol\sigma_N - \mathbf{C}_N^{-1}\mathbf{a}_N \\ \nabla\nabla\Psi &= -\mathbf{W}_N - \mathbf{C}_N^{-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Ψ is the log posterior of the latent values, minus a constant. The first term keeps a smooth (prior); the next two are the log-likelihood of the labels. σN holds σ(an), and WN is diagonal with σn(1 − σn), the same weights as in logistic regression’s IRLS. W is positive and C−1 is positive definite, so the Hessian is negative definite: Ψ is concave with a single peak.
Say it“Psi is minus a half a C-inverse a, plus t dot a, minus the sum of log one plus e to the a-n. Its gradient is t minus sigma minus C-inverse a; its Hessian is minus W minus C-inverse.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{a}^{\text{new}} = \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{W}\mathbf{C})^{-1}\mathbf{b} \\ &\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{t} - \boldsymbol\sigma + \mathbf{W}\mathbf{a} \\ &\mathbf{a}^\star = \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{t} - \boldsymbol\sigma^\star) \\ &q(\mathbf{a}) = \mathcal N\big(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{a}^\star,\ \mathbf{H}^{-1}\big) \\ &\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{W} + \mathbf{C}^{-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: (the subscript N is dropped from here on.) The first two lines are one Newton step, rearranged so that CN never has to be inverted. Repeat until nothing changes. At the peak the gradient is zero, which gives the third line: the mode is the kernel matrix times the residuals t − σ. The Laplace approximation is the Gaussian centred there whose precision is minus the Hessian. (The page computes the step through B = I + W½CW½, which is algebraically the same and numerically safer.)
Say it“New a is C times the inverse of I plus W C, times b, where b is t minus sigma plus W a. At the peak, a-star is C times t minus sigma-star, and q is a normal at a-star with precision W plus C-inverse.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mu(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{k}^{\top}(\mathbf{t} - \boldsymbol\sigma^\star) \\ &s^2(\mathbf{x}) = c - \mathbf{k}^{\top}(\mathbf{W}^{-1} + \mathbf{C})^{-1}\mathbf{k} \\ &p(t{=}1\mid\mathbf{x}) \approx \sigma\big(\kappa(s^2)\,\mu\big) \\ &\kappa(s^2) = (1 + \pi s^2/8)^{-1/2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: k holds the kernel between the new point and each training point, and c = k(x, x). The latent value at x is Gaussian with mean μ and variance s²: the mean is a kernel-weighted sum of the training residuals, the variance is the prior variance minus what the training points explain. Averaging the sigmoid over that Gaussian has no formula, but the probit trick from chapter 1.3b is very close: shrink μ by κ, which is near 1 when s² is small and smaller when the model is unsure.
Say it“Mu is k transpose times t minus sigma-star; s squared is c minus k transpose, W-inverse plus C, inverse, k. The probability is about sigma of kappa of s squared times mu.”

Hyperparameters

Learning the kernel of a GP classifier

The signal size and the length scale above were set by hand. In regression we chose them by maximising the evidence p(t | θ). For classification the evidence is the same awkward integral over the latent values, and Laplace answers it the same way it did in chapter 1.3b: the integral of a Gaussian bump is its height times its volume. That gives a formula you can evaluate for any θ, and a gradient you can climb.

The map shows the Laplace evidence for every signal size and length scale, for the points you have in the Laplace lab above. Click anywhere on the map to try those settings, or jump to the peak.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\ln p(\mathbf{t}\mid\boldsymbol\theta) \approx \Psi(\mathbf{a}^\star) \\ &\quad - \tfrac12\ln|\mathbf{W} + \mathbf{C}^{-1}| + \tfrac N2\ln 2\pi \\ &= \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_n \ln p(t_n\mid a^\star_n)}_{\text{fit}} \\ &\quad \underbrace{-\,\tfrac12\,\mathbf{a}^{\star\top}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{a}^\star}_{\text{smoothness}} \\ &\quad \underbrace{-\,\tfrac12\ln|\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{C}\mathbf{W}|}_{\text{Occam}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the log of the posterior’s height (Ψ at the peak, written out in full with its constants) plus the log of its volume (the −½ ln|H| term). Collecting the determinants turns it into three readable pieces: how well the latent values at the peak explain the labels; how far they had to stray from the prior; and an Occam penalty that grows when the data could pin down many independent directions of a flexible prior. At the peak a*⊤C−1a* = a*⊤(t − σ*), so not even this needs C−1.
Say it“Log evidence is about the sum of log p of t-n given a-star-n, minus a half a-star C-inverse a-star, minus a half log-det of I plus C W.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\partial_j \ln p = \tfrac12\,\mathbf{r}^{\top}\mathbf{C}'_j\,\mathbf{r} \\ &\quad - \tfrac12\operatorname{Tr}\big[(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{W}\mathbf{C})^{-1}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{C}'_j\big] \\ &\quad - \tfrac12\textstyle\sum_n v_n\, d_n\, \partial_j a^\star_n \\ &v_n = \big[(\mathbf{C}^{-1}+\mathbf{W})^{-1}\big]_{nn} \\ &d_n = \sigma^\star_n(1-\sigma^\star_n)(1-2\sigma^\star_n) \\ &\partial_j\mathbf{a}^\star = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{C}\mathbf{W})^{-1}\mathbf{C}'_j\,\mathbf{r} \\ &\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{t} - \boldsymbol\sigma^\star = \mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{a}^\star \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ∂j means ∂/∂θj and C′j = ∂C/∂θj. The first two lines are the explicit slope: what changes if the kernel moves while the peak a* stays put (the smoothness term and the Occam term each contribute one piece). But the peak itself moves with θ. Its effect on fit plus smoothness is zero, because that sum is Ψ, whose slope vanishes at its peak, so only the Occam term picks up the third, implicit line, through W, whose entries depend on a* (dn is the slope of Wnn). vn is simply the Laplace posterior variance of an. The sixth line, from differentiating a* = C(t − σ*), says how fast the peak moves. Every line is written here exactly as it comes out of differentiating ln|I + CW| and the peak condition directly; the finite-difference check in the running example confirms it.
Say it“The slope is a half r C-prime r, minus a half the trace of I plus W C, inverse, W C-prime, minus a half the sum of v-n d-n times the slope of a-star-n.”

Sparse Bayesian learning

The relevance vector machine for regression

An SVM’s prediction is a sum of kernel bumps centred on a few training points. That form is attractive, because prediction is cheap. But the SVM gives no probabilities, needs C (and ε for regression) set by cross-validation, and its support vectors grow in number with the data. Michael Tipping’s relevance vector machine (2001) keeps the form and makes it Bayesian: put one bump on every training point, give every weight its own prior precision αi, and learn all the αs from the evidence. This is ARD again, applied to weights instead of inputs, and it has a dramatic effect: most αi run off to infinity, which pins their weights at exactly zero.

Below are 40 noisy samples of sinc(x) = sin(x)/x with a Gaussian bump on each, plus a bias. Press “Re-estimate” a few times, then “Run”. The strip underneath shows each weight’s ln α; when a bar hits the top, that basis function is pruned.

\[ \begin{aligned} y(\mathbf{x}) &= \sum_{n=1}^{N} w_n\,k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_n) + b \\ p(t\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w},\beta) &= \mathcal N\big(t\mid y(\mathbf{x}),\ \beta^{-1}\big) \\ p(\mathbf{w}\mid\boldsymbol\alpha) &= \prod_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal N\big(w_i\mid 0,\ \alpha_i^{-1}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a linear model whose M = N + 1 basis functions are a kernel bump on each training point and a constant (the bias b is just one more weight). The kernel need not be positive definite, unlike the SVM’s. Each weight has its own Gaussian prior with precision αi: a huge αi says “this weight is zero”.
Say it“y of x is a weighted sum of kernels on the training points plus b; t is y plus Gaussian noise of variance one over beta; each weight w-i is normal with mean zero and variance one over alpha-i.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{m} = \beta\,\boldsymbol\Sigma\boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\mathbf{t} \\ &\boldsymbol\Sigma = \big(\mathbf{A} + \beta\boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\boldsymbol\Phi\big)^{-1} \\ &\gamma_i = 1 - \alpha_i\Sigma_{ii} \\ &\alpha_i^{\text{new}} = \gamma_i / m_i^2 \\ &(\beta^{\text{new}})^{-1} = \frac{\lVert\mathbf{t}-\boldsymbol\Phi\mathbf{m}\rVert^2}{N - \sum_i\gamma_i} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first line is Bayesian linear regression (chapter 1.2b) with A = diag(αi). Then each αi is re-estimated from the evidence, as in chapter 1.2c: γi ∈ [0, 1] measures how well the data determines wi. A weight the data can do without has mi → 0, so its αi grows; once it passes 105 (prior sd 0.003) we prune the basis function. Repeat until nothing moves.
Say it“m is beta Sigma Phi-transpose t, with Sigma the inverse of A plus beta Phi-transpose Phi. Gamma-i is one minus alpha-i Sigma-i-i; the new alpha-i is gamma-i over m-i squared; the new noise variance is the squared error over N minus the sum of gammas.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\ln p(\mathbf{t}\mid\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol\alpha,\beta) \\ &\quad = -\tfrac12\big[N\ln 2\pi + \ln|\mathbf{C}| \\ &\qquad + \mathbf{t}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{t}\big] \\ &\mathbf{C} = \beta^{-1}\mathbf{I} + \boldsymbol\Phi\mathbf{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol\Phi^{\top} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: integrate the weights out and the targets are jointly Gaussian with covariance C: noise plus one rank-one term αi−1φiφi⊤ per basis function, where φi is column i of Φ (basis function i evaluated at all N inputs). The re-estimation loop climbs this; each round can only raise it or leave it unchanged.
Say it“Log evidence is minus a half of N log two pi plus log-det C plus t C-inverse t, where C is noise plus Phi A-inverse Phi-transpose.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &p(t\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{t},\boldsymbol\alpha^\star,\beta^\star) \\ &\quad = \mathcal N\big(t\mid \mathbf{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}),\ \sigma^2(\mathbf{x})\big) \\ &\sigma^2(\mathbf{x}) = (\beta^\star)^{-1} \\ &\qquad + \boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\boldsymbol\Sigma\,\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: predict with the surviving basis functions only: the mean is a sum over the relevance vectors, the variance is noise plus weight uncertainty. A known flaw: with bumps as basis functions, φ(x) → 0 far from every relevance vector, so the variance falls back to bare noise exactly where the model knows least. A GP’s variance grows there instead. Move the probe outside the data to see it.
Say it“The prediction is normal with mean m dot phi of x and variance one over beta-star plus phi Sigma phi.”

Analysis of sparsity

Why the RVM is sparse

Why should maximising the evidence send weights to exactly zero, rather than just making them small? Pull one basis function φi out of the covariance C and look at the evidence as a function of its αi alone, everything else held fixed. The answer turns out to depend on just two numbers: how much φi overlaps with what the rest of the model already explains (its sparsity si), and how well it lines up with the part of t still unexplained (its quality qi).

The smallest case shows the geometry. With N = 2 data points, the targets t are one point in a 2-D “data space”, and so is the basis vector φ. The model says t ~ N(0, C): C draws an ellipse, the region where the model expects data. Adding φ with weight variance α−1 stretches the noise circle along φ. Drag the target t and the tip of φ: when φ points towards t, stretching helps; when it points away, stretching only spreads probability over places the data is not, and the evidence prefers α = ∞.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{C}_{-i} = \beta^{-1}\mathbf{I} + \textstyle\sum_{j\ne i}\alpha_j^{-1}\boldsymbol\varphi_j\boldsymbol\varphi_j^{\top} \\ &\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{C}_{-i} + \alpha_i^{-1}\boldsymbol\varphi_i\boldsymbol\varphi_i^{\top} \\ &s_i = \boldsymbol\varphi_i^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{-i}^{-1}\boldsymbol\varphi_i \\ &q_i = \boldsymbol\varphi_i^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{-i}^{-1}\mathbf{t} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: C−i is the covariance without basis function i; adding it back is a rank-one update. si is large when φi points somewhere the rest of the model does not already cover; qi is large when φi lines up with the targets, as seen through what the rest leaves unexplained. With N = 2 and nothing else in the model, C−i = β−1I, so s = β‖φ‖² and q = βφ⊤t.
Say it“C is C-minus-i plus phi-i phi-i-transpose over alpha-i. The sparsity s-i is phi-i C-minus-i-inverse phi-i; the quality q-i is phi-i C-minus-i-inverse t.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &L(\boldsymbol\alpha) = L(\boldsymbol\alpha_{-i}) + \lambda(\alpha_i) \\ &\lambda(\alpha_i) = \tfrac12\Big[\ln\alpha_i - \ln(\alpha_i+s_i) \\ &\qquad\qquad + \frac{q_i^2}{\alpha_i+s_i}\Big] \\ &\alpha_i^\star = \begin{cases} s_i^2/(q_i^2-s_i) & \text{if } q_i^2 > s_i \\ \infty & \text{if } q_i^2 \le s_i \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the log evidence L splits into a part without αi and a simple function λ of αi alone (the matrix determinant lemma and the Woodbury identity do the splitting). Setting dλ/dαi = 0 has a finite solution only if qi² > si: the alignment with the data must beat the overlap. Otherwise λ keeps rising as αi → ∞, and the best move is to remove the basis function completely. That is the source of exact sparsity.
Say it“L splits into L without alpha-i plus lambda of alpha-i, a half of log alpha minus log alpha plus s plus q squared over alpha plus s. The best alpha is s squared over q squared minus s if q squared beats s, and infinity otherwise.”

The fast sequential algorithm

The analysis also gives a much quicker way to train. The re-estimation loop above starts with all N + 1 basis functions and inverts an (N + 1) × (N + 1) matrix every round. Tipping and Faul (2003) start with none. Each step looks at every candidate, computes its si and qi from the current model, and makes the single move that raises the evidence most: add a missing function with qi² > si, re-estimate the α of one already in, or delete one whose qi² has dropped below si. The model never holds more than a few functions. Step through it; the bars show qi² − si for every candidate, so you can see who is knocking on the door.

\[ \begin{aligned} &S_i = \boldsymbol\varphi_i^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\boldsymbol\varphi_i, \quad Q_i = \boldsymbol\varphi_i^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{t} \\ &s_i = \frac{\alpha_i S_i}{\alpha_i - S_i}, \quad q_i = \frac{\alpha_i Q_i}{\alpha_i - S_i} \\ &\text{if } \alpha_i = \infty:\ s_i = S_i,\ q_i = Q_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: S and Q use the full current C, which only involves the functions in the model, so they are cheap. For a function already in the model, the formulas for s and q take its own contribution back out; for a function not in the model, C−i is simply C. In this demo the noise level is held at its true value, σ = 0.1, so the moves are easy to follow; the full algorithm re-estimates it as well.
Say it“S-i is phi-i C-inverse phi-i, Q-i is phi-i C-inverse t; then s-i is alpha-i S-i over alpha-i minus S-i, and q-i is alpha-i Q-i over the same.”

Classification

The RVM for classification

For classes, swap the Gaussian likelihood for a sigmoid, exactly as GP classification did. The weights then have no closed-form posterior, so Laplace steps in once more, this time for w rather than for latent values: find the most probable weights for the current α by Newton’s method (IRLS), fit a Gaussian there, and use its covariance to re-estimate every αi. The update has the same form as in regression.

The same data now goes to an RVM and to an SVM with the same RBF kernel and C = 1. Compare where their chosen points sit: the SVM’s support vectors line the boundary, while the RVM’s few relevance vectors sit back inside the classes, like prototypes. Click to add points, or change the kernel width.

\[ \begin{aligned} &y(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w}) = \sigma\big(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{x})\big) \\ &\ln p(\mathbf{w}\mid\mathbf{t},\boldsymbol\alpha) = \textstyle\sum_n\big\{t_n\ln y_n \\ &\qquad + (1-t_n)\ln(1-y_n)\big\} \\ &\qquad - \tfrac12\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w} + \text{const} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: logistic regression on N + 1 kernel basis functions, with an ARD prior on the weights. For fixed α this log posterior is concave, so IRLS finds its single peak w*; the page halves a Newton step if it ever overshoots.
Say it“y is sigma of w dot phi of x. The log posterior of w is the cross-entropy log-likelihood minus a half w-transpose A w.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{w}^\star = \mathbf{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}(\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{y}) \\ &\boldsymbol\Sigma = \big(\boldsymbol\Phi^{\top}\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol\Phi + \mathbf{A}\big)^{-1} \\ &p(\mathbf{t}\mid\boldsymbol\alpha) \approx p(\mathbf{t}\mid\mathbf{w}^\star)\,p(\mathbf{w}^\star\mid\boldsymbol\alpha) \\ &\qquad\times(2\pi)^{M/2}|\boldsymbol\Sigma|^{1/2} \\ &\alpha_i^{\text{new}} = \gamma_i / (w_i^\star)^2 \\ &\gamma_i = 1-\alpha_i\Sigma_{ii} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: at the peak the gradient Φ⊤(t − y) − Aw vanishes, giving the first formula; B is diagonal with yn(1 − yn). Laplace’s height-times-volume estimate of the evidence, differentiated in αi, gives the same update as regression. Alternate: IRLS for w*, then new αs, until they settle. For K > 2 classes use K weight vectors and a softmax; the Hessian then has size MK × MK.
Say it“w-star is A-inverse Phi-transpose times t minus y; Sigma is the inverse of Phi-transpose B Phi plus A. The evidence is about likelihood times prior at w-star times two pi to the M over two times root det Sigma, and alpha-i becomes gamma-i over w-star-i squared.”

Choosing

GP, SVM or RVM?

All three are kernel machines, and on clean, medium-sized tables they often reach similar accuracy. They differ in what they give you and what they cost. The GP keeps every training point and gives the best-calibrated uncertainty; the SVM keeps the points near the boundary and gives a score, not a probability; the RVM keeps a few prototypes and gives probabilities, but pays for it with a slow, non-convex training run and over-confidence far from the data.

Real-world project

Project: screening breast biopsies

In the early 1990s, William Wolberg, Nick Street and Olvi Mangasarian at the University of Wisconsin digitised images of fine-needle aspirates: a few cells drawn from a breast lump through a thin needle. For each image they traced the cell nuclei and measured ten properties (radius, texture, perimeter, area, smoothness, compactness, concavity, concave points, symmetry and fractal dimension), each summarised three ways: the mean over nuclei, its standard error, and the “worst” (mean of the three largest). That makes 30 features for each of 569 biopsies, 357 benign and 212 malignant, confirmed by later diagnosis. The data is on the UCI Machine Learning Repository under CC BY 4.0.

We shuffle once, learn from 400 biopsies and test on the other 169, with every feature standardised on the training set. Four kernel machines compete: a GP classifier with one shared length scale, a GP with ARD (one length scale per feature), an SVM and an RVM, the last two with the same RBF kernel, γ = 1/30, and C = 1 for the SVM. The page re-runs every fit itself. The one exception is the GP hyperparameters: searching 31 of them takes scikit-learn the better part of a minute, so they were found in Python and the page re-fits the Laplace posterior with them. import json, time, numpy as np
from sklearn.gaussian_process import GaussianProcessClassifier
from sklearn.gaussian_process.kernels import ConstantKernel as Const, RBF
from sklearn.svm import SVC

D = json.load(open("wdbc.json"))     # UCI WDBC: shuffled once, standardised on the 400 training rows
X, y, n = np.array(D["X"]), np.array(D["y"]), D["ntr"]           # y = 1 means malignant
Xtr, ytr, Xte, yte = X[:n], y[:n], X[n:], y[n:]
sig = lambda a: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-a))
logloss = lambda p, t: -np.mean(np.log(np.where(t == 1, p, 1 - p)))
rbf = lambda A, B, g: np.exp(-g * ((A[:, None, :] - B[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(-1))

def gp_probit(gpc, Xq):     # Laplace latent mean and variance, then p = sigma(kappa * mu)
    e = gpc.base_estimator_; Ks = e.kernel_(e.X_train_, Xq)
    mu = Ks.T @ (e.y_train_ - e.pi_)
    v = np.linalg.solve(e.L_, e.W_sr_[:, None] * Ks)
    s2 = e.kernel_.diag(Xq) - (v ** 2).sum(0)
    return sig(mu / np.sqrt(1 + np.pi * s2 / 8))

def rvm_classify(Phi, t, prune=1e5):   # Tipping's RVM: Laplace for w, then alpha_i = gamma_i / w_i^2
    keep = np.arange(Phi.shape[1]); alpha = np.ones(len(keep)); w = np.zeros(len(keep))
    obj = lambda w, a, A: np.sum(t * a - np.logaddexp(0, a)) - 0.5 * np.sum(A * w * w)
    for it in range(3000):
        P, A, wk = Phi[:, keep], alpha[keep], w[keep]
        for k in range(100):                        # Newton (IRLS) with step halving
            yy = sig(P @ wk); H = (P.T * (yy * (1 - yy))) @ P + np.diag(A)
            dw = np.linalg.solve(H, P.T @ (t - yy) - A * wk); st = 1.0; J = obj(wk, P @ wk, A)
            while obj(wk + st * dw, P @ (wk + st * dw), A) < J - 1e-12 and st > 1e-9: st /= 2
            wk = wk + st * dw
            if np.abs(st * dw).max() < 1e-8: break
        yy = sig(P @ wk); H = (P.T * (yy * (1 - yy))) @ P + np.diag(A)
        gamma = 1 - A * np.diag(np.linalg.inv(H)); new = gamma / wk ** 2
        change = np.abs(np.log(new) - np.log(A)).max()
        w[keep], alpha[keep] = wk, new; ok = new < prune; keep = keep[ok]
        if change < 1e-3 and ok.all(): break
    return keep, w, it + 1

g = 1 / 30                                        # kernel width shared by the SVM and the RVM
t0 = time.time(); gi = GaussianProcessClassifier(Const(1.0) * RBF(1.0), random_state=0).fit(Xtr, ytr); ti = time.time() - t0
t0 = time.time(); ga = GaussianProcessClassifier(Const(1.0) * RBF(np.ones(30)), random_state=0).fit(Xtr, ytr); ta = time.time() - t0
t0 = time.time(); svm = SVC(kernel="rbf", gamma=g, C=1).fit(Xtr, ytr); tsv = time.time() - t0
t0 = time.time(); keep, w, its = rvm_classify(np.c_[np.ones(n), rbf(Xtr, Xtr, g)], ytr.astype(float)); trv = time.time() - t0
p_rvm = sig(np.c_[np.ones(len(yte)), rbf(Xte, Xtr, g)][:, keep] @ w[keep])

print(f"GP isotropic : {gi.kernel_}   ln evidence {gi.log_marginal_likelihood_value_:.4f}   ({ti:.1f} s)")
print(f"GP with ARD  : ln evidence {ga.log_marginal_likelihood_value_:.4f}   ({ta:.1f} s)")
ls = ga.kernel_.k2.length_scale
print("  ARD keeps (length scale < 100):", ", ".join(f"{D['feats'][i]} {ls[i]:.1f}" for i in np.argsort(ls) if ls[i] < 100))
print(f"  ARD switches off {np.sum(ls >= 100)} of 30 features")
print(f"RVM          : {len(keep)} relevance vectors after {its} re-estimation rounds ({trv:.1f} s)")
print(f"SVM (C = 1)  : {svm.n_support_.sum()} support vectors ({tsv:.2f} s)")
print()

    acc = ((p > 0.5) == yte).mean() if p is not None else svm.score(Xte, yte)
    print(f"{name:14s}  {acc:.4f}     {f'{logloss(p, yte):.4f}' if p is not None else '   -  '}     {m}")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • ARD gives every input its own precision ηi and lets the evidence set them; an input whose ηi falls towards 0 has been switched off.
  • A GP classifier puts a GP on the log-odds and squashes it with a sigmoid; the posterior over latent values is not Gaussian, so Laplace fits one at the peak a* = C(t − σ*), found by a few Newton steps.
  • Its predictions shrink the latent mean by κ(s²), so probabilities fall back towards ½ where the data is thin, and the Laplace evidence (fit + smoothness + Occam) sets the kernel.
  • The RVM is ARD on the weights of a kernel model: re-estimating αi = γi/mi² drives most of them to infinity, leaving a few relevance vectors and real probabilities.
  • Sparsity is exact because the evidence for one basis function has a finite best α only if q² > s; on breast biopsies the RVM kept 10 training points where the SVM kept 102, at the same test accuracy.

Check your understanding

ARD sets η3 ≈ 10−9 for one input. What does the fitted GP do with that input, and could it still be useful?

The kernel ignores it: changing x3 by any realistic amount changes the exponent by nearly zero, so predictions are flat along it. It could still be useful in a different model or through an interaction the kernel family cannot express; ARD only says it does not help this GP explain these targets.

Why does a GP classifier need no noise term on the latent function, unlike GP regression?

The label noise is already in the likelihood: given a, the label is a Bernoulli draw with probability σ(a). Adding noise to a would only rescale the effective signal. The small ν on the diagonal is there for numerical safety, not as a model of noise.

At the mode, a* = C(t − σ*). What does that say about training points the classifier already fits well?

Their residuals tn − σ*n are near zero, so they contribute little to a* or to the prediction μ(x) = k⊤(t − σ*). Unlike the SVM, though, the contribution is small rather than exactly zero, so the GP is not sparse.

For one basis function you compute si = 4 and qi = 1.5. What does the evidence want, and what if qi were 3?

q² = 2.25 < s = 4, so λ(α) rises all the way to α = ∞: remove the function. With q = 3, q² = 9 > 4, so it keeps it with α* = s²/(q² − s) = 16/5 = 3.2.

An RVM regression is very confident far outside the training inputs. Why, and what would a GP do?

Every surviving basis function is a bump centred on a training point, so φ(x) → 0 far away and σ²(x) → β−1, the bare noise. A GP with the same kernel reverts to its prior there, so its variance grows back to θ0 + β−1.
