Gradient Boosting: Trees, Shrinkage & Interpretation

Gradient descent where every step is a small tree

Chapter 1.8a showed that boosting builds an additive model, one piece at a time. Here the pieces are small regression trees, and the recipe for choosing each one is gradient descent: work out which way the loss wants every prediction to move, then fit a tree that moves them that way. With a few dials set sensibly (tree size, learning rate, number of trees, a random half of the data per tree) this is one of the most reliable models for tabular data that we have. On the right, 100 shrunken trees fit a cloud of points; the faint curves show the model after 1, 3, 10 and 30 trees. Drag any dot and watch the whole sequence refit.

ESL 10.9

Boosting trees: a sum of step functions

A regression tree cuts the input space into J boxes, the regions R1, …, RJ, and predicts one constant γj in each. A boosted tree model is just a sum of M such trees. Because each tree is a step function, the sum is a finer step function, and with enough small steps it can follow almost any shape.

\[ \begin{aligned} T(x;\Theta) &= \sum_{j=1}^{J} \gamma_j\, I(x \in R_j) \\ f_M(x) &= \sum_{m=1}^{M} T(x;\Theta_m) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: I(x ∈ Rj) is 1 when x falls in box j and 0 otherwise, so a tree returns the γ of whichever box x lands in. Θ collects a tree's boxes and their values. The model adds up the outputs of M trees.
Say it“T of x is the sum over boxes of gamma-j times the indicator that x is in box j; f-M is the sum of M such trees.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\Theta_m &= \arg\min_{\Theta_m} \sum_{i=1}^{N} L\big(y_i,\ \hat y_i\big) \\ \hat y_i &= f_{m-1}(x_i) + T(x_i;\Theta_m) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: forward stagewise fitting (chapter 1.8a): freeze the trees you already have and choose only the next one, the tree that most reduces the loss when it is added on top.
Say it“Theta-m is the tree that minimises the total loss of f-m-minus-one plus that tree.”

That problem has two parts of very different difficulty. Finding the boxes is hard: there are astronomically many ways to cut the space, and a greedy search is the best we can do. Given the boxes, finding the values is easy: each γj is a one-number problem that only involves the points in its own box. For squared error the answer is the mean residual in the box, so boosting just fits a regression tree to the residuals. Press “Add the next tree” and watch each tree chase what the model still gets wrong.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\gamma_{jm} &= \arg\min_{\gamma} \sum_{x_i \in R_{jm}} L\big(y_i,\ f_i + \gamma\big) \\ f_i &= f_{m-1}(x_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: once tree m's boxes Rjm are fixed, choose the value in box j that best fixes the points inside it. Under squared error that is the average of their residuals yi − fm−1(xi); under absolute error, their median.
Say it“Gamma-j-m is the constant that minimises the loss of the points in box j when it is added to the current model.”

Other losses change only the line search inside each box. For two classes with labels ±1 and exponential loss, chapter 1.8a's AdaBoost, the per-box answer has a closed form too: half the log of the weighted vote inside the box, with weights wi = e−yifm−1(xi) that are large for points the model currently gets wrong. Finding the boxes for such losses is awkward, though, because the splitting rule has to be invented afresh for each loss. The next two sections remove that awkwardness.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\gamma_{jm} &= \tfrac12 \log \frac{W_{+}}{W_{-}} \\ W_{\pm} &= \sum_{x_i \in R_{jm},\; y_i = \pm 1} w_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: inside the box, compare the total weight of the +1 points with the total weight of the −1 points. If the +1 points outweigh them three to one, the box adds ½ log 3 ≈ 0.55 to the score; if the two sides balance, it adds nothing.
Say it“Gamma is a half times the log of the weight of the positives in the box over the weight of the negatives.”
Running example: a box holding positives of weight 0.9 + 0.6 = 1.5 and negatives of weight 0.3 + 0.2 = 0.5 gets γ = ½ log(1.5 / 0.5) = ½ log 3 = 0.549.
ESL 10.10 · 10.10.1

Steepest descent in function space

Forget trees for a moment. On the training set, a model is only a list of N numbers, its predictions f = (f(x1), …, f(xN)). The training loss is an ordinary function of those N numbers, so we can minimise it the way chapter 0's optimisation did: step downhill along the negative gradient, and pick the step length by a line search. Below, N = 2, so the whole “function” is a point in the plane. Drag the star (the targets y1, y2), change the loss, and step.

\[ \begin{aligned} L(\mathbf f) &= \sum_{i=1}^{N} L\big(y_i, f(x_i)\big) \\ g_{im} &= \left[\frac{\partial L(y_i, f(x_i))}{\partial f(x_i)}\right]_{f = f_{m-1}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the loss as a function of the N predictions. gim is how fast the loss rises as prediction i rises, measured at the current model. Each point's gradient only involves its own prediction.
Say it“g-i-m is the derivative of point i's loss with respect to its prediction, at the current model.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \rho_m &= \arg\min_{\rho}\ L(\mathbf f_{m-1} - \rho\, \mathbf g_m) \\ \mathbf f_m &= \mathbf f_{m-1} - \rho_m\, \mathbf g_m \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: move every prediction against its gradient, all by the same step ρm, chosen so the loss is as small as possible along that line. The sum of all steps is the model: fM = f0 − Σ ρmgm.
Say it“Rho-m minimises the loss along minus g; the new f is the old f minus rho-m times g-m.”
loss now
distance to targets

Notice what the minimum is: f(xi) = yi, the training targets themselves. Steepest descent in this space is perfectly happy to memorise the training set, and it says nothing at all about a new x, because the gradient only exists at the N training points. That is the problem the next section solves.

ESL 10.10.2

Gradient boosting: one recipe for any differentiable loss

Friedman's idea (2001) joins the two previous sections. The negative gradient −gm is the direction we want to move the predictions, but it is only known at the training points. So fit a regression tree to it: the tree's predictions point roughly the same way at the training points, and they are defined everywhere. The tree is grown by ordinary least squares on the pseudo-residuals rim, whatever the loss, and the loss returns only for the line search inside each box.

  1. Start with the best constant: f0(x) = argminγ Σi L(yi, γ).
  2. For m = 1 to M: compute the pseudo-residuals rim for every training point.
  3. Fit a regression tree with J leaves to the rim (squared-error splits), giving boxes Rjm.
  4. In each box, line-search the value γjm under the real loss, then add the tree: fm = fm−1 + Σj γjm I(x ∈ Rjm).
\[ \begin{aligned} r_{im} &= -\frac{\partial L(y_i, f_i)}{\partial f_i}\Big|_{f = f_{m-1}} \\ f_m(x) &= f_{m-1}(x) \\ &\quad + \sum_{j=1}^{J} \gamma_{jm}\, I(x \in R_{jm}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: fi is short for f(xi). The pseudo-residual is how much point i wants its prediction to go up. For squared error it is the ordinary residual yi − f(xi); for absolute error only its sign, ±1; for Huber loss the residual capped at ±δ. A single outlier therefore pulls on the tree as hard as any other point under absolute or Huber loss, and no harder.
Say it“r-i-m is minus the derivative of the loss at the current model. Add a tree whose boxes were fitted to the r's and whose values come from the line search.”

ESL 10.10.3 · modern libraries

Implementations: MART, XGBoost, LightGBM and histogram splits

The algorithm above, with squared-error trees and a learning rate, is Friedman's MART (multiple additive regression trees), the engine of R's gbm and scikit-learn's GradientBoostingRegressor. For K classes it grows K trees per round, one per class. Its two main tuning parameters are the tree size J and the number of rounds M. Two later libraries made it faster and a little more principled.

XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016) expands the loss to second order around the current model, using each point's gradient gi and curvature hi, and adds a penalty on the leaf values. That gives the leaf value and the split score in closed form, for any twice-differentiable loss. LightGBM (Ke et al., 2017) attacks the cost of finding splits: it buckets each feature into at most 255 bins, so a split search adds up gradients per bin and then tries only the bin edges, instead of every distinct value. It also grows trees leaf by leaf (always splitting the leaf that helps most), samples points with small gradients less often (GOSS), and bundles features that are rarely non-zero together. scikit-learn's HistGradientBoosting models copy the histogram idea.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Obj} &\approx \sum_{j}\Big[G_j w_j + \tfrac12 (H_j + \lambda) w_j^2\Big] \\ &\quad + \gamma J \\ w_j^\ast &= -\frac{G_j}{H_j + \lambda} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Gj and Hj add up the gradients gi and curvatures hi of the points in leaf j; wj is the leaf's value. Each leaf is a little parabola, minimised at w* = −G/(H + λ): a Newton step, shrunk towards 0 by the penalty λ. γ charges for every extra leaf.
Say it“The best leaf weight is minus G over H plus lambda.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Gain} = \tfrac12\Big[&\frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda} \\ &- \frac{(G_L+G_R)^2}{H_L+H_R+\lambda}\Big] - \gamma \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: how much the objective drops if one leaf is split into a left and a right part. For squared error, gi = f − yi and hi = 1, so H is a count and with λ = 0 the gain is half the drop in the sum of squared residuals, exactly the CART rule.
Say it“Gain is a half of G-L squared over H-L plus lambda, plus G-R squared over H-R plus lambda, minus the same for the parent, minus gamma.”

ESL 10.11

Right-sized trees: J sets the interaction order

Early boosting code grew each tree as large as possible and then pruned it, as if it were the final model. That wastes effort and, worse, gives the first trees far too many leaves. A better habit is to give every tree the same size J. What J controls becomes clear if you write the unknown function as an ANOVA expansion: main effects of single variables, plus effects of pairs, plus effects of triples, and so on.

\[ \begin{aligned} \eta(X) = &\sum_{j} \eta_j(X_j) \\ &+ \sum_{j \lt k} \eta_{jk}(X_j, X_k) \\ &+ \sum_{j \lt k \lt l} \eta_{jkl}(X_j, X_k, X_l) \\ &+ \cdots \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: any function splits into pieces that depend on one variable, then pieces that need two variables together (an interaction), and so on. A tree with J leaves makes at most J − 1 cuts along any path, so it can only build interactions of up to J − 1 variables. Stumps (J = 2) give a purely additive model: a sum of one-variable functions.
Say it“Eta of X is the sum of one-variable effects, plus two-variable effects, plus three-variable effects, and so on.”

Below, 400 noisy points come from a function of two inputs. The test error is measured against the true, noise-free surface on a 30 × 30 grid. Each tree size is boosted for 300 rounds with ν = 0.1. Switch the true function between additive and interaction and compare the tree sizes.

In practice, interactions of high order are rare and hard to estimate, so J between 4 and 8 works well for most problems and results are not very sensitive to the exact choice within that range. Use stumps when you want an additive, easily plotted model; go beyond 10 leaves only if a held-out set asks for it.

ESL 10.12 · 10.12.1 · 10.12.2

Regularisation: shrinkage and subsampling

The number of trees M is itself a regulariser: each tree reduces the training loss, so with enough of them the model memorises the data, and the test error turns back up. The usual answer is to watch a validation set and stop at its minimum. Two more dials make that minimum lower. Shrinkage scales each tree by a learning rate ν < 1, so the model takes many small steps instead of a few greedy ones; smaller ν needs more trees, and the two trade off. Subsampling (Friedman's stochastic gradient boosting) grows each tree on a random fraction η of the training points, often a half, drawn afresh every round: it is faster and, combined with shrinkage, usually more accurate.

\[ \begin{aligned} f_m(x) &= f_{m-1}(x) \\ &\quad + \nu \sum_{j=1}^{J} \gamma_{jm}\, I(x \in R_{jm}) \\ 0 &< \nu \le 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: compute tree m exactly as before, then add only a fraction ν of it. With subsampling, the boxes and the γs of tree m come from a random ηN of the points, but the model is updated for all of them.
Say it“The new model is the old one plus nu times the new tree.”

The data are ESL's “nested spheres”: ten standard normal inputs, and the class is 1 when the squared distance from the origin, ΣXj², exceeds its median, 9.34. There are 1,000 training and 2,000 test points; the loss is the binomial deviance. Try ν = 1, then ν = 0.1, then add subsampling; every setting you run stays on the chart in grey.

ESL 10.13 · 10.13.1

Interpretation I: relative importance of the inputs

A single tree is easy to read. Five hundred of them are not, so we summarise. Breiman's relative importance asks, for each input, how much all the splits on it reduced the squared error. Every internal node t of a tree splits on one variable v(t) and earns an improvement ît² (the drop in the sum of squared residuals). Add up the improvements credited to each variable, average over the trees, and take the square root. Scale so the biggest is 100.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal I_\ell^2(T) &= \sum_{t=1}^{J-1} \hat\imath_t^2\; I\big(v(t) = \ell\big) \\ \mathcal I_\ell^2 &= \frac1M \sum_{m=1}^{M} \mathcal I_\ell^2(T_m) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: in one tree, sum the squared-error improvements of the splits that used variable ℓ. Then average over the M trees. Averaging over many trees makes this far more stable than in a single tree, where a variable that just lost the split to a close rival scores zero.
Say it“The squared importance of variable ell is the average over trees of the improvements at nodes that split on ell.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal I_{\ell k}^2 &= \frac1M \sum_{m=1}^{M} \mathcal I_\ell^2(T_{km}) \\ \mathcal I_\ell^2 &= \frac1K \sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathcal I_{\ell k}^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with K classes there are K trees per round, one per class, so the same sum gives an importance for every pair of variable and class: which inputs separate class k from the rest. Their average over classes is the overall importance. The project's sex classifier shows these per class.
Say it“Importance of ell for class k averages over that class's trees; the overall importance averages over classes.”

Below, the truth is known: y = 1.8X1 + 1.2 sin(2X2) + 0.5X3 + noise. X4 is pure noise, and X5 is not used by the truth but is correlated with X1. ESL 10.13.2

Interpretation II: partial dependence plots

Importance says which inputs matter; a partial dependence plot says how. Pick a subset S of the inputs (one or two variables, so we can draw it) and call the rest C. Set XS to a value, keep every training point's own XC, predict, and average. Repeat for a grid of values. The result is the model's effect of XS after averaging over the other inputs.

\[ \bar f_S(X_S) = \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^{N} f\big(X_S,\ x_{iC}\big) \]
Read it as: the average prediction when XS is forced to a value and each training point keeps its other inputs. If f = h1(XS) + h2(XC), this recovers h1 up to a constant; if f = h1·h2, up to a scale factor.
Say it“f-bar-S at a value is the average of the model's predictions with X-S set to that value and the rest left as they are.”
\[ \tilde f_S(X_S) = \mathbb E\big[f(X_S, X_C) \mid X_S\big] \]
Read it as: a different, tempting curve: the average prediction among points that happen to have that XS. When XS and XC are correlated, it mixes in the effect of XC. It answers “what do we see?”, not “what does XS do?”.
Say it“f-tilde-S is the expected prediction given X-S.”

For trees there is also a fast shortcut that never passes the data through the model: run down each tree; at a split on a variable in S follow the branch the chosen value picks; at a split on a variable in C follow both branches, weighted by the share of training points that went each way. It equals the data average when XS is unrelated to XC inside each node, and drifts from it when they are correlated, because the shares were counted among points with their own XS. The project compares the two. Here, two inputs X1, X2 are standard normal with correlation ρ, and the model is 200 trees fitted to 600 points. Case studies: two-part models and many classes

ESL closes the chapter with real studies: California house values, fish catches off New Zealand, and the occupations of shopping-mall survey respondents. Two lessons in them reach far beyond those data sets.

Many zeros: split the question in two. In the fish study, many trawls catch none of the species being modelled. Squeezing “is it there?” and “how much, if so?” into one squared-error model blurs two different stories. Instead, fit one boosted model for presence, with the binomial deviance, and a second for the catch, using only the trawls that caught some. Their product is the expected catch, and each part gets its own importance scores and partial dependence plots.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E(Y \mid X) &= \underbrace{P(Y > 0 \mid X)}_{\text{presence model}} \\ &\quad\cdot \underbrace{\mathbb E(Y \mid Y > 0, X)}_{\text{catch model}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the expected catch equals the chance of catching any, times the typical catch when there is some. Each factor is a boosted model with its own loss: deviance on all trawls for the first, squared error on the non-zero trawls for the second.
Say it“The expected catch is the probability of a non-zero catch times the expected catch given that it is non-zero.”

The New Zealand survey data are not openly licensed, so this lab simulates a survey with the same shape: 2,000 trawls with a depth, a bottom temperature (colder in deeper water) and a towing speed. By construction, the species lives around 500 m, so presence depends on depth alone, while the size of a catch depends on temperature and speed. Each model has 200 trees with 4 leaves and ν = 0.1, fitted to 1,400 trawls and tested on 600. Switch the input and compare what each part has learned.

Many classes: one tree per class per round. The demographics study predicted occupation, nine classes, from thirteen survey answers. Gradient boosting handles K classes by keeping K score functions f1, …, fK, turned into probabilities by the softmax. Each round fits K trees, tree k to the pseudo-residuals I(y = k) − pk(x): +1 minus the probability for points of class k, minus the probability for the rest. ESL's study found some occupations easy to recognise (students and retirees among them) and others nearly hopeless, and the class-specific importances showed different questions mattering for different jobs. The project repeats this, at K = 3, on abalone.

\[ \begin{aligned} p_k(x) &= \frac{e^{f_k(x)}}{\sum_{l=1}^{K} e^{f_l(x)}} \\ r_{ikm} &= I(y_i = \mathcal G_k) - p_{k,m-1}(x_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: class k's probability is its exponentiated score over the total. Tree k of round m is fitted to how far each point's probability for class k falls short of the truth (1 for its own class, 0 otherwise). The scores are only defined up to a common constant, which the softmax ignores.
Say it“p-k is e to the f-k over the sum of e to the f-l; the residual for class k is the indicator of class k minus p-k.”
Real-world project

Project: how old is this abalone?

An abalone's age is read like a tree's: cut the shell, stain it, and count the rings under a microscope. It is slow, so Nash and colleagues (1994) asked whether simple measurements could stand in. Their data, from the Marine Resources Division in Tasmania and on the UCI repository (CC BY 4.0), describe 4,177 abalone: sex (male, female or infant), length, diameter and height in mm, and four weights in grams (whole, shucked meat, viscera, dried shell). Age is the ring count plus 1.5 years. We shuffle once (numpy seed 0), train on 3,133 and test on 1,044. The inputs are an infant flag and the seven measurements. import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingRegressor, GradientBoostingClassifier, HistGradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.inspection import partial_dependence

rows = [l.strip().split(",") for l in open("abalone.data") if l.strip()]       # UCI, 4,177 abalone
rows = [rows[i] for i in np.random.RandomState(0).permutation(len(rows))]
sex = np.array([r[0] for r in rows]); M = np.array([[float(v) for v in r[1:8]] for r in rows])
rings = np.array([int(r[8]) for r in rows])
X = np.column_stack([sex == "I", M]).astype(float)       # infant flag + 7 measurements
y = rings + 1.5                                          # age in years
tr, te = slice(0, 3133), slice(3133, None)
rmse = lambda f: np.sqrt(np.mean((y[te] - f) ** 2))

print(f"predict the mean        test RMSE = {rmse(y[tr].mean()):.4f}")
print(f"linear regression       test RMSE = {rmse(LinearRegression().fit(X[tr], y[tr]).predict(X[te])):.4f}")
for nu, depth in [(1, 3), (0.3, 3), (0.1, 1), (0.1, 2), (0.1, 3), (0.1, 5), (0.03, 3)]:
    g = GradientBoostingRegressor(learning_rate=nu, max_depth=depth, n_estimators=500, random_state=0)
    curve = [rmse(f) for f in g.fit(X[tr], y[tr]).staged_predict(X[te])]
    b = int(np.argmin(curve))
    print(f"GBM nu={nu:<4} depth={depth}   best M = {b + 1:3d}  test RMSE = {curve[b]:.4f}   at M=500: {curve[-1]:.4f}")
h = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(random_state=0).fit(X[tr], y[tr])
print(f"HistGradientBoosting    test RMSE = {rmse(h.predict(X[te])):.4f}   (LightGBM-style bins, defaults)")

g = GradientBoostingRegressor(learning_rate=0.1, max_depth=3, n_estimators=72, random_state=0).fit(X[tr], y[tr])
names = ["infant", "length", "diameter", "height", "whole wt", "shucked wt", "viscera wt", "shell wt"]
print("importance (share of squared improvement):", ", ".join(f"{n} {v:.3f}" for n, v in zip(names, g.feature_importances_)))
grid = np.array([0.05, 0.15, 0.25, 0.35, 0.45])          # shell weight / 200 g
pd_b = partial_dependence(g, X[tr], [7], custom_values={7: grid}, method="brute")["average"][0]
pd_r = partial_dependence(g, X[tr], [7], custom_values={7: grid}, method="recursion")["average"][0]
print("PDP shell wt, brute:    ", np.round(pd_b, 3))

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Gradient boosting is steepest descent on the training predictions, with each step replaced by a small regression tree fitted to the negative gradient, the pseudo-residuals.
  • The tree is always grown by least squares; the loss enters through the pseudo-residuals and the line search in each leaf. Absolute and Huber losses make it robust to outliers; deviance makes it a classifier.
  • Tree size J caps the interaction order at J − 1: stumps give an additive model, and J of 4 to 8 suits most problems.
  • Shrink every tree by ν ≤ 0.1, grow each on a random half of the data, and stop where a validation error bottoms out: small ν needs more trees but reaches a lower error.
  • Read a fitted model with relative importance (summed squared improvements) and partial dependence plots, remembering that correlated inputs share importance and that a PDP averages away interactions.

Check your understanding

Why fit a tree to the negative gradient instead of just taking the gradient step?

The gradient step changes the predictions only at the N training points; it defines nothing for a new x and, followed to the end, memorises the training targets. A tree fitted to the negative gradient points the same way at the training points but is defined everywhere, and its few leaves force it to generalise.

Under absolute-error loss, what are the pseudo-residuals, and what value does a leaf get?

The pseudo-residuals are sign(y − f), just +1 or −1, so the tree's boxes are chosen by where the model is too low or too high, not by how much. Each leaf's value is then the median of the actual residuals y − f in it.

A colleague boosts stumps on data where the effect of dose depends on age. What goes wrong?

Stumps build a purely additive model, a function of dose plus a function of age, so they cannot represent an effect of dose that changes with age. Trees with J ≥ 3 leaves can capture that two-variable interaction.

You halve the learning rate. Roughly what happens to the best number of trees and to the best test error?

The best M roughly doubles, since each tree now does about half as much. The best test error usually stays the same or improves slightly; it rarely gets worse. The cost is more trees to train and evaluate.

Two inputs are near-copies of each other. How does that affect relative importance and the partial dependence plot?

The trees split on either copy almost at random, so the importance is shared between them and each looks less important than the pair really is. The PDP of one copy forces values that rarely occur with the other copy, so it is extrapolating into regions with little data.
