Rollouts, MCTS & AlphaGo

Planning at decision time: spend your thinking on the position in front of you

Dyna plans in the background, improving a value table for every state it might ever meet. A game player cannot afford that: Connect Four has about 4.5 trillion positions. So it plans at the moment it must move, imagining thousands of futures that start from this position and keeping only what they say about the next move. That is the idea behind rollouts, Monte Carlo tree search and, with learned networks added, AlphaGo. Play Connect Four against the search: drop a disc by clicking a column of the board, and the bars show where the search spent its imagined games and how good each reply looked.

The idea · heuristic search

Planning at decision time

The last chapter used a model in the background: Dyna replays imagined transitions from anywhere, slowly improving a value table or policy for every state, and acting is just a lookup. There is a second way to use a model. Decision-time planning starts when a state St arrives, plans for that one state, picks an action and usually discards most of the work. All the computation is focused on the states that can actually follow from where you are, which matters when there are far too many states to cover in advance. The price is time: you must think before every move, so it suits games and tasks where a few seconds per decision is affordable.

The classic form is heuristic search. Grow a tree of possible continuations to some depth d, score the leaves with an approximate value function V̂ (the heuristic), and back the scores up to the root, averaging over chance outcomes and maximising over your own actions. The backed-up values are only used to choose the current action. Deeper search usually means better decisions, because the errors of V̂ are pushed further from the root. The gridworld below has a cup-shaped wall between the start S and the goal G. Its heuristic, “11 minus the straight-line distance (in steps) to G”, does not know about walls, so it pulls the agent straight into the cup. Raise the search depth until the arrows lead out of the cup, then add slip.

\[ \begin{aligned} V_0(s) &= \hat V(s) \\ V_d(s) &= \max_a \sum_{s'} p(s' \mid s,a) \\ &\quad \times \big[\, r(s,a,s') + \gamma\, V_{d-1}(s') \,\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a depth-0 search just trusts the heuristic. A depth-d search tries every action, averages over where the action might take you (weights p(s′ | s, a)), adds the reward and the depth-(d − 1) value of the next state, and keeps the best action. Terminal states (the goal and the pits) contribute their reward only. Here every step costs −1, reaching G pays +10, a pit costs −10, and γ = 1. The agent takes the action that wins the max at the root.
Say it“The depth-d value of s is the max over actions of the expected reward plus gamma times the depth-d-minus-one value of the next state, bottoming out at the heuristic.”

Online algorithms for MDPs

A tree of decisions and chances, and sampling it

Value iteration and policy iteration are offline: they solve the whole MDP before the agent takes a step. An online agent does its computation at each decision point, which is what the search above did. Its lookahead tree alternates two kinds of node: decision nodes, where the agent picks the best action (a max), and chance nodes, where the environment picks the next state (an average weighted by p(s′ | s, a)). This is the expectimax tree. Its size is the trouble: with |A| actions and up to b possible next states each, a depth-d tree has (|A| b)d leaves, and b can be the size of the whole state space.

Two remedies. A good leaf heuristic lets the tree stay shallow, and discounting means distant levels matter less. And chance nodes need not be enumerated: draw C sample next states from the model and average over those. Kearns, Mansour and Ng (2002) showed that this sparse sampling gives near-optimal decisions with a number of samples that depends on the horizon and the accuracy wanted, not on how many states exist. Sampling is also the step from here to Monte Carlo tree search, which was first analysed for MDPs (UCT, Kocsis and Szepesvári 2006) and handles chance nodes by sampling. Pick a cell, then switch to sampling and change C.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat Q_d(s,a) &= \frac1C \sum_{i=1}^{C} G_i \\ G_i &= r_i + \gamma\, \hat V_{d-1}(s'_i) \\ s'_i &\sim p(\cdot \mid s,a) \\ \text{exact:}\;\; & (|A|\,b)^d \text{ leaves} \\ \text{sampled:}\;\; & (|A|\,C)^d \text{ leaves} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: instead of the exact weighted sum over every next state, average over C states drawn from the model, each followed by its own sampled depth-(d − 1) subtree. The estimate is noisy, with error shrinking like 1/√C, but its cost no longer depends on b. In this gridworld b is only 3 (the intended move or a slip to either side), so sampling saves little here; in a problem where one action can lead to thousands of states it is the only option.
Say it“Q-hat is the average over C sampled next states of the reward plus gamma times the sampled value below; exact costs A-b to the d, sampled costs A-C to the d.”

Monte Carlo at decision time

Rollout algorithms

Heuristic search needs a value function. A rollout algorithm needs only a simulator and some policy π, the rollout policy. For each legal action a in the current state, play many simulated games that start with a and then follow π to the end, and average the results: that is a Monte Carlo estimate of qπ(s, a). Then play the action with the best average. Tesauro and Galperin (1997) did this for backgammon, rolling out dice sequences from the current position, and got a large improvement over the network that served as the rollout policy.

Why does it work? The policy improvement theorem from chapter 4 of Sutton and Barto: acting greedily with respect to qπ gives a policy at least as good as π. A rollout algorithm is one step of policy iteration, done only for the state you are in, with qπ estimated by simulation rather than computed. It does not learn anything that lasts; it improves π for this decision only. Below is a real Connect Four position from the UCI database (o, red, to move). Under each column is its true value with perfect play from both sides. Change the number of rollouts and the rollout policy (random, or “win if you can, else block, else random”), and see when the averages pick out a column that the database says is best.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat q(s,a) &= \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^{N} G_i \\ \text{SE} &= \hat\sigma / \sqrt N \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Gi is the result of the i-th simulated game that starts with move a and then follows π: 1 for a win, ½ for a draw, 0 for a loss, from the mover’s side. The average estimates qπ(s, a); its standard error shrinks like one over the square root of N, so four times the games halves the uncertainty.
Say it“q-hat of s, a is the average of N simulated returns; its standard error is sigma-hat over root N.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \pi'(s) &= \arg\max_a q_\pi(s,a) \\ \Rightarrow\quad v_{\pi'}(s) &\ge v_\pi(s) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the rollout player π′ picks the move with the best estimated value under π. If the estimates were exact, π′ would be at least as good as π everywhere, and strictly better wherever π was not already greedy. With noisy estimates the guarantee holds only approximately, which is why N matters.
Say it“Pi-prime picks the argmax of q-pi, so its value is at least pi's.”

The engine

Monte Carlo tree search, one iteration at a time

A rollout algorithm spends its games evenly: as many on an obviously terrible move as on the best one, and it never looks more carefully at the replies. Monte Carlo tree search (Coulom 2006; Kocsis and Szepesvári 2006) keeps a tree of the positions its simulations have passed through, with a visit count N and a total result W in every node, and uses them to steer the next simulation towards promising lines. Each iteration has four steps:

1 Selection. From the root, repeatedly step to the child with the best tree policy score (next section) until reaching a node that still has untried moves. 2 Expansion. Add one child for an untried move. 3 Simulation. From the new node, play a fast rollout policy (here uniformly random) to the end of the game. 4 Backup. Walk back up the path, adding 1 to every N and the result to every W, counted from the side of the player who made the move into that node. When time runs out, play the root move with the most visits. The tree grows unevenly, deep along good lines and thin elsewhere, and the root statistics turn into a sharper and sharper evaluation. The game is ordinary Connect Four; the position is the database position from the last section. Step through the four phases, then add iterations in bulk.

\[ \begin{aligned} N(v) &\leftarrow N(v) + 1 \\ W(v) &\leftarrow W(v) + z_{v} \\ Q(v) &= W(v) / N(v) \\ a^{\ast} &= \arg\max_{v} N(v) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first two lines are applied to every node v on the path of this iteration. zv is the simulation’s result for the player who moved into node v: 1 for a win, ½ for a draw, 0 for a loss. Because the players alternate, the same game counts as a win at one level and a loss at the next. Q(v) is the average result through v. The final move a* is the most visited child v of the root, which is more robust than the highest Q, since a rarely visited child can have a lucky average.
Say it“For every node on the path, add one to N and the result to W; Q is W over N, and the move played is the most visited child of the root.”

The tree policy

UCT: which branch deserves the next game?

Selection faces the explore–exploit dilemma of a multi-armed bandit at every node: play the child with the best average so far, or try a child whose average rests on few games and might be better? UCT (“upper confidence bounds applied to trees”, Kocsis and Szepesvári 2006) answers with the UCB1 rule of Auer, Cesa-Bianchi and Fischer (2002) at every node: add to each child’s average an exploration bonus that grows slowly with the parent’s visits and shrinks with the child’s own. A child that is never tried has an infinite bonus, so every move gets a first look. Kocsis and Szepesvári proved that, with rewards in [0, 1] and a suitable constant, the probability of choosing a wrong move at the root goes to zero as the number of iterations grows, so UCT converges to the minimax move. Russell and Norvig note the practical side: C = √2 is the theoretical default, and in practice it is tuned.

MCTS has two advantages over depth-limited alpha–beta search on games like Go. It needs no evaluation function, since the rollouts do the evaluating, and it copes with a large branching factor by focusing its games. Its weakness is the reverse: a single forced line (a trap several moves deep) can stay hidden when most random continuations from a move look fine. Many programs therefore cut rollouts short and score the position with an evaluation function, which is the road to AlphaGo. Slide the exploration constant C and watch the visits concentrate or spread.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{UCB1}(v) &= \underbrace{\frac{W(v)}{N(v)}}_{\text{exploit}} \\ &\quad + \underbrace{C\sqrt{\frac{\ln N(\text{parent})}{N(v)}}}_{\text{explore}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first term is the child’s average result so far. The second is large for children with few visits relative to their parent: it is the width of a confidence interval, so the rule plays the child whose optimistic value is highest. C trades the two: C = 0 is pure greed (and can lock onto an early lucky move), a large C spreads games almost evenly, like a rollout algorithm.
Say it“UCB1 is W over N plus C times the square root of log N-parent over N.”

Case study · Sutton & Barto 16.6.1

AlphaGo: networks that guide the search

Go was the game where search alone failed. A 19 × 19 board offers about 250 legal moves a turn over games of about 150 moves, and no one had written a good evaluation function for it. MCTS programs of the late 2000s reached strong amateur level and stalled. AlphaGo (Silver et al., 2016) kept MCTS and gave it learned knowledge in three places. A policy network pσ, a 13-layer convolutional network, was trained by supervised learning to predict expert moves from 30 million positions on the KGS Go server (57% accuracy); its probabilities became priors that steer selection towards plausible moves. A copy pρ was improved by policy-gradient reinforcement learning in self-play, then used to generate 30 million positions, each from a different game, on which a value network vθ learned to predict the winner. And a fast rollout policy, a linear model on local patterns, played the simulations. Sutton and Barto call the resulting search APV-MCTS.

At a new leaf, AlphaGo mixed two opinions: the value network’s prediction and the result of one fast rollout, weighted by a mixing parameter λ; the paper found λ = 0.5 played best. In October 2015 it beat the European champion Fan Hui 5–0, the first win by a program over a professional on a full board without handicap, and in March 2016 it beat Lee Sedol 4–1. Below is the same recipe at Connect Four scale, with honest substitutes: the value network is the small one trained in the project on the UCI database, the priors come from that network’s view one move ahead (there is no record of expert moves to learn from), and the rollouts use the win-or-block policy. Slide λ from network to rollouts.

\[ \begin{aligned} a &= \arg\max_a \big[\, Q + u \,\big] \\ u &= c\, P(s,a)\, \frac{\sqrt{N(s)}}{1 + N(s,a)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: at node s, pick the action with the largest Q(s, a) + u(s, a). The selection rule now has a prior P(s, a) from the policy network inside the exploration term (this “PUCT” form is the one AlphaGo Zero wrote down; AlphaGo’s bonus is also proportional to P/(1 + N)). A move the network likes gets explored early; a move it dislikes is tried only once its rivals have many visits. Values here run from −1 (loss) to +1 (win).
Say it“Pick the action maximising Q plus c times the prior times root N over one plus N of the action.”
\[ \begin{aligned} V(s_L) &= (1-\lambda)\, v_\theta(s_L) \\ &\quad + \lambda\, z_L \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: sL is the new leaf. vθ(sL) is the value network’s prediction; zL is the result (+1, 0 or −1) of one fast rollout from sL. λ = 0 trusts the network alone, λ = 1 is ordinary MCTS with rollouts. The mix is backed up the path like any simulation result.
Say it“The leaf value is one minus lambda times the network's value plus lambda times the rollout result.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta\sigma &\propto \nabla_\sigma \log p_\sigma(a \mid s) \\ \Delta\rho &\propto z_t\, \nabla_\rho \log p_\rho(a_t \mid s_t) \\ \Delta\theta &\propto \big(z - v_\theta(s)\big)\, \nabla_\theta v_\theta(s) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the three training signals, one per line: imitate the expert, reinforce in self-play, regress on the winner. Supervised learning raises the log-probability of the move the human played. Reinforcement learning raises the log-probability of every move the winner played (zt = +1) and lowers the loser’s (zt = −1). The value network is a regression of the final result z on the position, with one position per self-play game so that it cannot memorise whole games.
Say it“Sigma follows the gradient of the log-probability of the expert move; rho follows the outcome times that gradient; theta follows the error times the gradient of the value.”

Case study · Sutton & Barto 16.6.2

AlphaGo Zero: learning from nothing but self-play

AlphaGo Zero (Silver et al., 2017) threw away the human games, the hand-made input features and the rollouts. One deep residual network fθ(s) = (p, v) outputs both move probabilities and a value, from the raw stones alone. It learns by playing itself, and the trick is that MCTS is a policy improvement operator. In every position of a self-play game it runs a search guided by the current network (1,600 simulations per move) and turns the root visit counts into a target policy π, which is sharper and better than the raw p. When the game ends, every position is labelled with the final result z. The network is then trained so that p moves towards π and v towards z: search improves the policy, and the network learns to imitate the improved policy, so the next search starts from a better guess. This is generalised policy iteration with MCTS as the improvement step. After about 4.9 million self-play games in three days it beat the version that had defeated Lee Sedol, 100 games to 0.

The lab runs the same loop on tic-tac-toe, small enough to learn in your browser in a second. The “network” is a lookup table holding nine move logits and one value per position, every entry starting at zero (so the first policy is uniform and every value is 0). Each self-play move runs 25 PUCT simulations guided by the table, with Dirichlet noise at the root and moves sampled from π for the first three moves; after each game, every position takes one gradient step on the loss below. Like AlphaGo Zero, each position also teaches its 8 rotations and reflections. The referee is exact: tic-tac-toe is solved by full minimax over its 5,478 positions, a draw with best play. Play some self-play games and watch the table learn to hold a perfect player to a draw.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell(\theta) &= (z - v)^2 - \boldsymbol\pi^{\top}\log \mathbf p \\ &\quad + c\,\Vert\theta\Vert^2 \\ \pi_a &\propto N(s,a)^{1/\tau} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the value v should predict the game’s result z (squared error); the policy p should match the search’s visit distribution π (cross-entropy); c‖θ‖² is weight decay (the table here uses none). τ is a temperature: τ = 1 makes π proportional to the visit counts, τ → 0 puts all its weight on the most visited move. For a table, one gradient step moves the position’s value a fraction αv of the way to z and adds α(π − p) to its logits.
Say it“The loss is z minus v squared, minus pi transpose log p, plus c times the squared weights; pi is proportional to the visit counts to the power one over tau.”

Summary of chapter 8 and Part I

The dimensions of reinforcement learning methods

Chapter 8 of Sutton and Barto ends by showing that planning and learning are the same activity seen twice: both estimate value functions by backing up values from possible futures, and they differ only in whether the experience is real or simulated. Background planning (Dyna, prioritised sweeping) and decision-time planning (heuristic search, rollouts, MCTS) differ in when and where the updates happen. The book then closes Part I with a map. Every tabular method so far shares three ideas: estimate value functions, back up values along actual or possible state trajectories, and follow generalised policy iteration. They differ along two main dimensions. Width: a sample update follows one sampled next state; an expected update averages over all of them. Depth: how far the update looks before it bootstraps on an estimate, from one step to the end of the episode. The four corners are TD(0), dynamic programming, Monte Carlo, and exhaustive search.

A third dimension is on-policy versus off-policy: learning the value of the policy you follow, or of another one. The book lists more: the definition of return (episodic or continuing, discounted or not), action values versus state values versus afterstate values, how actions are selected for exploration, synchronous versus asynchronous updates, real versus simulated experience, which states get updated, when updates are made (during action selection or in the background), and how long updated values are kept (MCTS keeps them only for the current decision). Choose a width and a depth to see which method sits there and the shape of its backup.

\[ \begin{aligned} G_{t:t+n} &= \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \gamma^{k} R_{t+k+1} \\ &\quad + \gamma^{n}\, V(S_{t+n}) \\ V(s) &\leftarrow \sum_a \pi(a\mid s)\, q(s,a) \\ q(s,a) &= \sum_{s',r} p(s',r\mid s,a) \\ &\quad \times \big[r + \gamma V(s')\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first line is a sample update of depth n, the second an expected update of depth 1. Along the depth axis, an n-step sample update adds n real or simulated rewards and then bootstraps on the estimate n steps on (n = 1 is TD(0); n to the end of the episode is Monte Carlo, with no bootstrapping). Along the width axis, the expected update replaces the single sample with the full average over actions and next states (DP). Doing the expected update to full depth enumerates the whole tree: exhaustive search.
Say it“The n-step return is the discounted sum of n rewards plus gamma to the n times the value n steps on; the expected update averages over every action and next state.”

Real-world project

Project: Connect Four against perfect-play labels

Connect Four is solved: with perfect play the first player wins, provided the first disc goes in the centre column (James D. Allen and Victor Allis, independently, 1988). John Tromp’s Connect-4 opening database on the UCI Machine Learning Repository (CC BY 4.0) holds all 67,557 legal positions after 8 moves (4 discs each, x to move) in which neither player has won and the next move is not forced, each labelled with its game-theoretic result for x, the first player. This page embeds all of them, packed into three bytes each. That gives search something rare: a ground truth. For 6,853 positions with 7 discs (o to move), every legal reply leads to a database position, so the true value of every move is known. 200 of them, drawn at random, form the benchmark used throughout this page: does a planner pick a move that is optimal with perfect play?

The value network of the AlphaGo section is trained here, offline in Python because it takes a minute: a multilayer perceptron with 84 inputs (one per square for each player), 64 ReLU units and 3 outputs (x wins, draw, o wins), 5,635 weights in all, fitted by scikit-learn on 57,424 positions and their mirror images. The 10,133 test positions include every child of the 200 benchmark positions, so no benchmark answer was ever seen in training. The browser loads the weights (rounded to 6 significant digits) and recomputes everything else itself.

M32 = 0xFFFFFFFF
class Mulberry32:
    def __init__(self, a): self.a = a & M32
    def __call__(self):
        a = (self.a + 0x6D2B79F5) & M32; self.a = a
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & M32
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & M32)) & M32) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M32) / 4294967296
D4 = ((1, 0), (0, 1), (1, 1), (1, -1))
def line(b, c, r, p):
    for dc, dr in D4:
        k = 1; cc, rr = c + dc, r + dr
        while 0 <= cc < 7 and 0 <= rr < 6 and b[cc * 6 + rr] == p: k += 1; cc += dc; rr += dr
        cc, rr = c - dc, r - dr
        while 0 <= cc < 7 and 0 <= rr < 6 and b[cc * 6 + rr] == p: k += 1; cc -= dc; rr -= dr
        if k >= 4: return True
    return False
class St:
    __slots__ = ('b', 'h', 'p', 'n')
    def __init__(s, b, h, p, n): s.b, s.h, s.p, s.n = b, h, p, n
    def clone(s): return St(s.b[:], s.h[:], s.p, s.n)
def from_cells(cells):
    b = list(cells); h = [sum(1 for r in range(6) if b[c * 6 + r]) for c in range(7)]; n = sum(h)
    return St(b, h, 2 if n % 2 else 1, n)
def legal(s): return [c for c in range(7) if s.h[c] < 6]
def wins(s, c, p):

    if r >= 6: return False
    s.b[c * 6 + r] = p; w = line(s.b, c, r, p); s.b[c * 6 + r] = 0; return w
def play(s, c):
    r = s.h[c]; p = s.p; s.b[c * 6 + r] = p; s.h[c] = r + 1; s.n += 1; s.p = 3 - p
    return 1 if line(s.b, c, r, p) else 0
def pol_rand(s, L, rng): return L[int(rng() * len(L))]
def pol_wb(s, L, rng):
    for c in L:
        if wins(s, c, s.p): return c
    for c in L:
        if wins(s, c, 3 - s.p): return c

def playout(s, rng, pol):
    while True:
        L = legal(s)
        if not L: return 0
        p = s.p

def flat_mc(s0, N, rng, pol):

    for a in legal(s0):
        g = 0.0

        out.append((a, g / N))

def uct(s0, iters, c, rng, pol):
    root = UNode(-1, 3 - s0.p, -1, s0)
    for _ in range(iters):
        s = s0.clone(); node = root; path = [node]
        while not node.untried and node.kids:
            lnN = math.log(node.N); best = None; bv = -math.inf
            for k in node.kids:
                u = k.W / k.N + c * math.sqrt(lnN / k.N)
                if u > bv: bv = u; best = k
            play(s, best.mv); node = best; path.append(node)
        if node.term == -1 and node.untried:
            i = int(rng() * len(node.untried)); m = node.untried.pop(i); pj = s.p; w = play(s, m)
            k = UNode(m, pj, pj if w else (0 if s.n == 42 else -1), s); node.kids.append(k); node = k; path.append(k)
        winner = node.term if node.term != -1 else playout(s, rng, pol)
        for n in path:
            n.N += 1
            if winner == 0: n.W += 0.5
            elif winner == n.pj: n.W += 1
    return root

class Net:
    def __init__(s, d): s.H, s.W1, s.b1, s.W2, s.b2 = d['H'], d['W1'], d['b1'], d['W2'], d['b2']

def p_expand(net, node, s, beta):
    me = s.p; u = []
    for m in legal(s):
        r = s.h[m]; s.b[m * 6 + r] = me; w = line(s.b, m, r, me)
        if w: val = 1
        elif s.n + 1 == 42: val = 0
        else: val = net.v(s.b, me)
        s.b[m * 6 + r] = 0; u.append((m, me if w else (0 if s.n + 1 == 42 else -1), val))
    mx = max(x[2] for x in u); e = [math.exp(beta * (x[2] - mx)) for x in u]; Z = 0.0
    for x in e: Z += x

def puct(net, s0, sims, cp, lam, beta, rng, pol):
    root = PNode(-1, 3 - s0.p, -1, 1.0); p_expand(net, root, s0.clone(), beta); root.N = 1
    for _ in range(sims):
        s = s0.clone(); node = root; path = [node]
        while node.kids is not None and node.term == -1:
            sq = math.sqrt(node.N); best = None; bv = -math.inf
            for k in node.kids:
                q = k.W / k.N if k.N else 0
                u = q + cp * k.P * sq / (1 + k.N)
                if u > bv: bv = u; best = k
            play(s, best.mv); node = best; path.append(node)
        if node.term != -1: v = 0 if node.term == 0 else 1
        else:
            vn = net.v(s.b, node.pj)
            if lam > 0:
                z0 = playout(s.clone(), rng, pol); z = 0 if z0 == 0 else (1 if z0 == node.pj else -1)
                v = (1 - lam) * vn + lam * z
            else: v = vn
            p_expand(net, node, s, beta)
        sg = 1
        for n in reversed(path): n.N += 1; n.W += sg * v; sg = -sg
    return root

The engine, the random number generator (mulberry32), the UCT and PUCT searches and the network’s forward pass are written twice, once in the page’s JavaScript and once in Python (mcts.py, a line-by-line port), with the same tie-breaking. The browser’s run reproduces Python’s move on every one of the 200 positions for every planner, and its test accuracy to every printed digit; the table shows both. Three honest caveats. First, 200 positions give a standard error of about 3 points, so planners within 5 points of each other are not clearly different. Second, the labels are about perfect play to the end of the game, a much higher bar than beating a human; a move that the database marks as a draw rather than a win may still win against any real opponent. Third, the network only ever saw positions with exactly 8 discs: it is used in-distribution one move below the benchmark root, and out of distribution deeper in the tree, which is one reason mixing in rollouts can help.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Decision-time planning spends all its computation on the current state: grow a lookahead from here, back up values, act, and throw most of the work away. Heuristic search does this with a value function at the leaves; deeper trees correct a poor heuristic.
  • Online MDP planners alternate max (decision) and average (chance) nodes; sampling the chance nodes makes the cost independent of the number of states, at the price of noise that shrinks like 1/√C.
  • A rollout algorithm is one step of policy iteration for the current state: estimate qπ(s, a) by simulation and act greedily, so it is at least as good as its rollout policy when the estimates are accurate.
  • MCTS repeats select, expand, simulate, back up; UCT selects by an upper confidence bound, W/N + C√(ln Nparent / N), so the tree grows deep where it matters and the move played is the most visited.
  • AlphaGo added a policy prior, a value network and fast rollouts to MCTS; AlphaGo Zero dropped human data and rollouts and used MCTS as a policy-improvement operator inside self-play, training the network to predict the search’s visits and the game’s winner.

Check your understanding

When would you choose background planning (Dyna) over decision-time planning (MCTS)?

When decisions must be instant (a lookup, not a search), when the same states recur so that stored values pay off, or when there is no time to think per step, as in fast control loops. Decision-time planning wins when the state space is too big to cover, a few seconds per decision are affordable, and a model can be simulated cheaply, as in board games.

Depth-d heuristic search with V̂ at the leaves gives the same values as what other algorithm, run for d sweeps?

Value iteration started from V̂: each extra level of the tree is one more Bellman optimality backup. The difference is coverage: the search does those backups only for the states reachable from the current state within d steps, and recomputes them at every decision.

Why does MCTS play the most visited root move rather than the one with the highest Q?

A move with few visits can have a high average by luck. UCT sends more visits to moves that keep looking good, so the visit count is a more stable vote; and if one child had the best Q but few visits, the search would normally have kept visiting it.

In UCB1, what happens with C = 0, and with a very large C?

With C = 0 the search is greedy: after one lucky early result it can keep descending into the same child and never give the others a fair hearing. With a very large C the bonus dominates and visits are spread almost evenly, which is close to a plain rollout algorithm and wastes games on bad moves.

What does AlphaGo Zero use as the training target for its policy head, and why is it better than the network’s own output?

The visit distribution π of the MCTS run from that position. The search looked ahead with the network’s own value estimates, so π is an improved policy, a policy-improvement step; training p towards π lets the next search start from a stronger prior, as in generalised policy iteration.
