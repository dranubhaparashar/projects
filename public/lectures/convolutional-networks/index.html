Convolutional Neural Networks

Small filters, slid across the image, stacked deep

A convolutional network looks at an image through a small window. A filter, here a 5×5 grid of learned weights, slides to every position, and at each one it writes down how strongly the patch underneath matches its pattern. The result is a feature map: a new image showing where the pattern occurs. The same 26 numbers do the work at all 784 positions. On the right, move your pointer over the digit to slide the window yourself, or pick another of the eight filters.

The problem

Why images break dense networks

A plain neural network like the one in chapter 2.2 reads an image by flattening it into one long vector and connecting every pixel to every hidden unit. That is fine for a tiny 8×8 digit. For real images it fails twice over. First, too many weights: a dense layer needs one weight for every pair of input number and unit, so a single layer on a phone photo needs millions. Second, no sense of place: to a dense layer, pixel 300 and its neighbour 301 are as unrelated as pixel 300 and pixel 7. Move a digit two pixels to the right and its vector looks like a different picture, so the network must learn the same stroke separately at every position.

A convolutional layer fixes both at once. It connects each output to a small neighbourhood (local connectivity) and uses the same weights at every position (weight sharing). Slide the digit: its raw pixels stop matching the original, yet the filter’s feature map simply slides along with it.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{dense:}\quad & (H\,W\,C + 1)\; n \\ \text{conv:}\quad & (k^2\,C + 1)\; n \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: an image H pixels high, W wide, with C colour channels, feeding n units. Each dense unit has a weight for every one of the H·W·C input numbers, plus a bias. Each convolutional filter has only k×k weights per channel, plus a bias, whatever the image size, because it is reused at every position.
Say it“Dense: H W C plus one, times n. Convolutional: k squared C plus one, times n.”
\[ \cos(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \frac{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x}'}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\;\lVert\mathbf{x}'\rVert} \]
Read it as: treat each image as a vector of 784 pixel values. The cosine is 1 when the two vectors point the same way (same picture, maybe brighter) and 0 when no inked pixel of one overlaps an inked pixel of the other. It is what a dense layer “sees” of the similarity.
Say it“The cosine of x and x-prime is their dot product over the product of their lengths.”

The operation

Convolution, one window at a time

A convolution needs two things: an input grid and a small grid of weights called the kernel (or filter). Lay the kernel over the top-left corner of the input, multiply each pair of overlapping numbers, and add the products up. That is one output number. Slide one step right and repeat; at the end of a row, go down one. The outputs form a new grid, smaller by k−1 in each direction. Click any output cell to see its sum, or press Step. Click kernel cells to change their weights, and click input cells to paint.

\[ \begin{aligned} &(X \star K)[i,j] \\ &\quad = \sum_{u,v=0}^{k-1} X[i+u,\,j+v]\, K[u,v] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for output position (i, j), put the k×k kernel over the patch whose top-left corner is (i, j), multiply overlapping numbers and add. The output is large when the patch looks like the kernel: big pixels under positive weights, small pixels under negative ones. Strictly this is cross-correlation; a mathematician’s convolution flips the kernel first. Deep-learning libraries skip the flip and still call it convolution: the kernel is learned, so a flipped kernel is just a different set of weights.
Say it“Output i, j is the sum over u and v of X at i plus u, j plus v, times K at u, v.”

What a filter sees

Feature maps: where the pattern is

Run a kernel over a real digit and the output is a feature map: a picture of where the kernel’s pattern occurs. An edge kernel lights up along one side of every stroke; a blur kernel softens the whole digit. A layer then adds a bias and applies a ReLU, which keeps only the positive evidence. The bias works as a threshold: a negative bias silences weak matches. We pad the digit with a ring of zeros so the map stays 28×28. Pick a kernel, then move the bias; hover over the map to read any position.

\[ \begin{aligned} z_{ij} &= b + \sum_{u,v} K_{uv}\, X_{i+u,\,j+v}\\ a_{ij} &= \operatorname{ReLU}(z_{ij}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same sliding sum as before, plus one bias b shared by every position, then clipped at zero by ReLU(z) = max(0, z). A position lights up only if its patch matches the kernel by more than −b. Pixels run from 0 (paper) to 1 (ink).
Say it“a at i, j is ReLU of b plus the sum of K times X over the window.”

Bookkeeping

Padding, stride and output size

Two knobs control the shape of the output. Padding p adds rings of zeros around the input, so the window can sit over the border and the map does not shrink. Stride s is the step size: with s = 2 the window jumps two pixels at a time and the map comes out about half as wide. Every architecture diagram is this formula applied over and over. Change the four numbers and watch the window walk.

\[ n_{\text{out}} = \left\lfloor \frac{n + 2p - k}{s} \right\rfloor + 1 \]
Read it as: the padded input is n + 2p wide. A k-wide window has n + 2p − k steps of room to move; taking them s at a time gives ⌊(n + 2p − k)/s⌋ moves, plus the starting position. The floor ⌊ ⌋ means a window that would hang over the far edge is dropped. With s = 1, “same” padding p = (k − 1)/2 keeps nout = n.
Say it“n-out is the floor of n plus two p minus k, over s, plus one.”

Shrinking

Pooling: keep the strongest signal

After a convolution, neighbouring positions usually say much the same thing. A pooling layer cuts the feature map into small blocks and keeps one number per block: the largest (max pooling) or the mean (average pooling). The map shrinks four-fold, the next layer becomes four times cheaper, and its windows now cover twice the distance in the original image. Pooling has no weights to learn. It also buys a little tolerance to small shifts: if the strongest response moves within its block, the pooled map does not change. Shift the digit a pixel or two and compare the similarity before and after pooling.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{max:}\ \ y_{ij} &= \max_{u,v \in \{0,1\}} a_{2i+u,\,2j+v}\\ \text{average:}\ \ y_{ij} &= \tfrac14 \sum_{u,v \in \{0,1\}} a_{2i+u,\,2j+v} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: output cell (i, j) looks at the 2×2 block that starts at row 2i, column 2j of the feature map a. Max pooling reports whether the feature occurred somewhere in the block; average pooling reports how much of the block it filled. In backpropagation, max pooling passes the gradient only to the winning position.
Say it“y at i, j is the max of a over the two-by-two block at two i, two j.”

Stacking

Channels and the growing receptive field

A real layer has many filters, and each produces its own feature map; the maps are stacked as channels. The next layer’s filters then reach across all of those channels at once, so a second-layer filter can say “a top edge here and a left edge just below”: a corner. Each layer also sees further. A unit in layer one sees a 3×3 patch; a unit above it sees 3×3 of those, which is 5×5 pixels; after a pooling layer the view grows twice as fast. That patch is the unit’s receptive field. Build a stack and watch the centre unit’s view spread over the digit.

\[ \begin{aligned} z_f[i,j] &= b_f + \sum_{c=1}^{C_{\text{in}}} \sum_{u,v}\\ &\quad W_{f,c}[u,v]\, x_c[i+u,\,j+v] \\ \text{params} &= (k^2\, C_{\text{in}} + 1)\; C_{\text{out}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: filter f is really a k×k×Cin block of weights. It slides over the map in two directions only, but sums over every input channel. A layer with Cout filters makes Cout output channels.
Say it“z-f is b-f plus the sum over channels c and offsets u, v of W f c times x c. Parameters: k squared C-in plus one, times C-out.”
\[ \begin{aligned} r_\ell &= r_{\ell-1} + (k_\ell - 1)\; j_{\ell-1} \\ j_\ell &= j_{\ell-1}\; s_\ell, \qquad r_0 = j_0 = 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: r is the width of the patch of input pixels one unit can see; j (the jump) is how many input pixels apart two neighbouring units are. A k×k layer widens the view by k − 1 steps of the current jump. A stride or a pool multiplies the jump, so every layer after it widens the view faster.
Say it“r-l is r of l minus one, plus k-l minus one times the jump; the jump is multiplied by each stride.”

History

Classic architectures, from LeNet to ResNet

Every famous CNN is the same few layers in a different order. Yann LeCun’s LeNet-5 (1998) was the heart of a system that read millions of handwritten cheques a day for American banks. AlexNet (2012) scaled the idea up with GPUs, ReLUs and dropout and won the ImageNet challenge by a huge margin, starting the deep-learning boom. VGG-16 (2014) showed that stacks of plain 3×3 convolutions, deep enough, beat clever large kernels. ResNet (2015) added shortcut connections and trained networks of 50, 101 and 152 layers. The pattern never changes: the maps shrink while the channels multiply. Pick an architecture and drag through its layers; every count is computed from the layer list.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{multiply-adds} &= H_{\text{out}}\, W_{\text{out}}\\ &\quad \times k^2\, C_{\text{in}} \times C_{\text{out}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: every one of the Hout×Wout output positions of every one of the Cout filters costs k²Cin multiplications (each followed by an addition). Weights are reused at every position, so a conv layer can have few weights yet cost a lot of arithmetic. A fully connected layer is the case Hout = Wout = k = 1.
Say it“Multiply-adds equal H-out W-out, times k squared C-in, times C-out.”

Going very deep

Residual connections: a highway for the gradient

If depth helps, why not 100 layers? In 2015 Kaiming He and colleagues found that simply stacking more layers made things worse: on CIFAR-10 a plain 56-layer network had higher training error than a 20-layer one, even though it could in principle copy the 20-layer net and leave its extra layers as do-nothing identities. The gradient is part of the story. Backpropagation multiplies one Jacobian per layer, and a product of many factors shrinks or explodes unless each one is almost perfectly scaled. Their fix: let every block learn only a change to its input, and add the input back. Turn the depth and the weight scale and compare how much gradient reaches the first layer.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{plain:}\quad & \mathbf{h}_{\ell+1} = F_\ell(\mathbf{h}_\ell) \\ \text{residual:}\quad & \mathbf{h}_{\ell+1} = \mathbf{h}_\ell + F_\ell(\mathbf{h}_\ell) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the residual block keeps a copy of its input and adds what the layers compute. If the best thing a block can do is nothing, it only has to learn F = 0, which is easy; a plain block would have to learn the identity, which is not. In this lab Fℓ(h) = Wℓ ReLU(h) on 32 numbers. In the residual stack each branch is scaled by 1/√L so the signal stays bounded at any depth; in real ResNets batch norm has a similar effect, shrinking each branch relative to the shortcut by roughly the square root of the depth (De & Smith, 2020).
Say it“Plain: h next is F of h. Residual: h next is h plus F of h.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{plain:}\ \ \mathbf{g}_0 &= \Big(\prod_{\ell} J_\ell\Big)^{\!\top} \mathbf{g}_L\\ \text{residual:}\ \ \mathbf{g}_0 &= \Big(\prod_{\ell} \big(I + J_\ell\big)\Big)^{\!\top} \mathbf{g}_L \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: gℓ = ∂ℒ/∂hℓ is the gradient of the loss at layer ℓ, and Jℓ is the Jacobian of block ℓ, how its output moves when its input moves. A plain stack multiplies L of them, so if each shrinks the gradient by 20%, after 40 layers almost nothing is left (0.840 ≈ 0.0001). In the residual stack every factor contains the identity I: expanding the product gives a direct path from the loss to h0 that passes through no weights at all.
Say it“Plain: g-zero is the product of the Jacobians, transposed, times g-L. Residual: the same with identity plus each Jacobian.”

Real-world project

Project: a CNN that reads your handwriting

MNIST is the data set LeCun, Cortes and Burges built in the 1990s from handwriting collected by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology: 70,000 digits written by Census Bureau employees and high-school students, each size-normalised to fit a 20×20 box and centred in a 28×28 image. The standard split is 60,000 for training and 10,000 for testing, written by different people. We downloaded it from OpenML (mnist_784) and trained the small network below offline, in plain numpy: backpropagation written by hand, the Adam optimiser, 8 passes over the training set, each digit randomly shifted by up to 2 pixels. Its weights are embedded in this page, and everything below runs that network in your browser, in plain JavaScript.

The same network in numpy

import json, numpy as np
from numpy.lib.stride_tricks import sliding_window_view
from sklearn.datasets import fetch_openml

w = json.load(open("cn_weights.json"))                          # the weights this page uses
W1 = np.array(w["W1"]).reshape(8, 1, 5, 5);  b1 = np.array(w["b1"])
W2 = np.array(w["W2"]).reshape(16, 8, 3, 3); b2 = np.array(w["b2"])
W3 = np.array(w["W3"]).reshape(10, 784);     b3 = np.array(w["b3"])

def conv(x, W, b, pad):                     # x: (C, H, W) -> (F, H, W), stride 1, "same" padding
    k = W.shape[-1]
    win = sliding_window_view(np.pad(x, ((0, 0), (pad, pad), (pad, pad))), (k, k), axis=(1, 2))
    return np.einsum("chwuv,fcuv->fhw", win, W) + b[:, None, None]

def pool(a):                                # 2x2 max pooling
    C, H, W = a.shape
    return a.reshape(C, H // 2, 2, W // 2, 2).max(axis=(2, 4))

def forward(img):                           # img: 28x28 uint8
    x = img.reshape(1, 28, 28) / 255.0
    p1 = pool(np.maximum(conv(x, W1, b1, 2), 0))     # 8 maps, 14x14
    p2 = pool(np.maximum(conv(p1, W2, b2, 1), 0))    # 16 maps, 7x7
    return W3 @ p2.ravel() + b3                      # 10 logits

X, y = fetch_openml("mnist_784", version=1, return_X_y=True, as_frame=False)
Xte, yte = X[60000:].astype(np.uint8), y[60000:].astype(int)   # the standard 10,000 test digits
logits = np.array([forward(im) for im in Xte])
pred = logits.argmax(1)
cm = np.zeros((10, 10), int); np.add.at(cm, (yte, pred), 1)
print("parameters:", sum(a.size for a in (W1, b1, W2, b2, W3, b3)))
print(f"test accuracy (10,000 images): {(pred == yte).mean():.4f}")
print(f"test accuracy (first 500):     {(pred[:500] == yte[:500]).mean():.4f}")
print("per-digit accuracy:", " ".join(f"{d}:{cm[d, d] / cm[d].sum():.3f}" for d in range(10)))
z = logits[0] - logits[0].max(); p = np.exp(z) / np.exp(z).sum()
print("test image 1 (a", yte[0], ") logits:", np.round(logits[0], 4))
print("                 P(7) =", round(p[7], 6))

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A convolution slides one small kernel across the image and takes a weighted sum at every position. Local connections and shared weights mean the parameter count does not depend on the image size, and a pattern learned in one place is detected everywhere.
  • Each filter produces a feature map, a picture of where its pattern occurs; a bias and a ReLU decide where it counts. Many filters give many channels, and the next layer combines all of them.
  • Shapes follow nout = ⌊(n + 2p − k)/s⌋ + 1. Pooling shrinks maps and tolerates small shifts; stacking layers widens the receptive field, faster after every stride or pool.
  • From LeNet to AlexNet, VGG and ResNet the recipe is the same: maps shrink while channels grow. Fully connected layers can hold most of the weights; convolutions do most of the arithmetic.
  • Residual connections h + F(h) give the gradient an identity path through every block, which made networks of 50 to 150 layers trainable. A 9,226-weight CNN already reads MNIST digits correctly about 99 times in 100.

Check your understanding

A 3×3 convolution with 64 filters reads a 128-channel input. How many parameters does it have, and what happens to that number if the image doubles in size?

(3×3×128 + 1) × 64 = 73,792. It does not change with the image size: the same filters are reused at every position. Only the amount of arithmetic grows, four-fold for an image twice as wide and high.

A 32×32 input goes through a 5×5 convolution with padding 2 and stride 2. How big is the output?

⌊(32 + 4 − 5)/2⌋ + 1 = ⌊15.5⌋ + 1 = 16, so 16×16.

Why does VGG use two stacked 3×3 convolutions instead of one 5×5?

They see the same 5×5 receptive field (r = 1 + 2 + 2), but with C channels in and out they need 2×9C² = 18C² weights instead of 25C², and they put an extra ReLU in between, so the stack can represent more complicated patterns.

Max pooling has no weights. What does backpropagation do when it reaches a max-pooling layer?

Each block’s gradient goes entirely to the position that held the maximum; the other three positions get zero, because nudging them slightly does not change the output.

Why does a residual block h + F(h) make a very deep network easier to train?

Its Jacobian is I + JF, so the gradient always has a direct identity path back to earlier layers instead of a long product that can vanish or explode. And a block that is not needed only has to learn F ≈ 0, so adding blocks should not make the training error worse.
