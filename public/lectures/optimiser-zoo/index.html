The Optimiser Zoo & Optimisation Strategies

Many ways down the same hill

Chapter 2.3 gave you SGD, momentum and Adam. They are three animals in a much larger zoo. Some members look ahead before they step, some give every weight its own learning rate, some average where they have been, and some change the problem itself: they pretrain, redesign the network, or start with an easier version of the task. On the right, seven optimisers leave the same starting point on the same landscape. Drag the star to move the start, and slide the step count to replay the race.

Look before you leap

Nesterov momentum

Plain momentum keeps a running velocity v and moves along it. Its weakness is timing: it measures the gradient where it stands, then adds the old velocity on top, so when the velocity is already carrying it past the valley floor it notices too late and overshoots. Yurii Nesterov's 1983 fix, brought to deep learning by Sutskever and colleagues in 2013, changes one thing. First let the velocity carry you to a look-ahead point, measure the gradient there, and correct with that. If the look-ahead point is already uphill on the far side, the correction brakes before the overshoot happens.

The lab is a long, narrow bowl: steep across (curvature 1), gentle along (curvature 1/κ). Both methods use the same η and β. At β = 0.9 the orange heavy-ball path swings across the valley while the red Nesterov path settles. Slide β and η to find where each one wins (and where each one breaks), then press the button for the textbook-best settings.

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde\theta &= \theta - \eta\beta\, v \\ v &\leftarrow \beta\, v + \nabla L(\tilde\theta) \\ \theta &\leftarrow \theta - \eta\, v \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: θ̃ is where the old velocity alone would take you. The new velocity is the old one shrunk by β, plus the gradient measured at that look-ahead point. Then step along it. Delete the tilde (measure the gradient at θ) and you are back to the plain momentum of chapter 2.3.
Say it“Peek ahead to theta minus eta beta v; v becomes beta v plus the gradient at the peek; theta moves by minus eta v.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{momentum:} \\ &\quad z^2 - (1+\beta-\eta\lambda)\,z + \beta = 0 \\ &\text{Nesterov:} \\ &\quad z^2 - (1+\beta)(1-\eta\lambda)\,z \\ &\qquad + \beta(1-\eta\lambda) = 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: on a bowl, each direction with curvature λ evolves on its own, and every step multiplies its error by a root z of these equations. The largest |z| over all directions, the convergence factor ρ, sets the speed: the error shrinks like ρt. Nesterov's extra factor (1 − ηλ) damps the steep directions, where plain momentum rings.
Say it“For momentum, z squared minus one plus beta minus eta lambda times z, plus beta, equals zero; Nesterov multiplies both lower terms by one minus eta lambda.”

One honest caveat. On a smooth convex problem with exact gradients, Nesterov provably improves the rate: the gap to the minimum value falls like 1/t² after t steps, rather than 1/t for gradient descent. With the noisy mini-batch gradients of deep learning that guarantee is gone: the noise sets the pace, not the curvature. In practice the look-ahead still tends to help a little, and it costs nothing extra, so many frameworks offer it as a switch (nesterov=True).

A learning rate per weight

AdaGrad: shrink the steps that have been big

One learning rate for millions of weights is a compromise. A weight whose gradient is large must take small steps or it overshoots; a weight whose gradient is tiny then crawls. Duchi, Hazan and Singer's AdaGrad (2011) gives every weight its own rate: divide its step by the square root of all the squared gradients it has seen so far. Weights with a history of big gradients slow down; weights that rarely receive a gradient (a rare word, a rare feature) keep a big step for when they do.

Below, the bowl is 100 times steeper across than along. Gradient descent cannot raise η past the steep direction's limit, so it crawls along the floor. Raise the SGD learning rate until it starts to bounce, then compare with AdaGrad, and watch the lower plot: each coordinate's effective step size.

\[ \begin{aligned} r &\leftarrow r + g\odot g \\ \theta &\leftarrow \theta - \frac{\eta}{\delta + \sqrt{r}} \odot g \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: g is the gradient and ⊙ multiplies element by element, so every weight keeps its own running total r of squared gradients. Its step is η divided by the root of that total (δ ≈ 10−7 only prevents dividing by zero). The first step is η times the sign of g, whatever its size.
Say it“r adds g squared, element by element; theta moves by eta over delta plus root r, times g.”
\[ \begin{aligned} g_t = g \ \Rightarrow\ \sqrt{r_t} &= |g|\sqrt{t} \\ \text{step}_t &\approx \frac{\eta}{\sqrt t} \\ \textstyle\sum_{s\le t} \text{step}_s &\approx 2\eta\sqrt t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: if a weight keeps seeing the same gradient, its accumulated total grows like t, its step shrinks like 1/√t, and the distance it can travel grows only like √t. That is good for convex problems, where you want to slow down as you arrive, and a handicap for deep networks, which have a long way to travel.
Say it“With a constant gradient, root r grows like root t, so each step is about eta over root t.”

Forget the distant past

RMSProp, and how it grows into Adam

AdaGrad never forgets. One steep stretch early in training inflates r for good, and every later step is small, even after the landscape has turned gentle. Geoffrey Hinton's RMSProp (from his 2012 Coursera lectures) swaps the running total for a running average that fades: each new squared gradient gets weight 1 − ρ, and the old memory is shrunk by ρ. The step then tracks the size of recent gradients, about the last 1/(1 − ρ) of them.

The lab is a walk down a steep cliff onto a long, gently sloping plain, with the lowest point far away at x = 8. All three walkers use the same η. Drag the step slider and see who reaches the bottom, then shorten RMSProp's memory with ρ.

\[ \begin{aligned} r &\leftarrow \rho\, r + (1-\rho)\, g\odot g \\ \theta &\leftarrow \theta - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\delta + r}} \odot g \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: r is now an exponentially weighted average of recent squared gradients, so √r is a running estimate of a gradient's typical size, its root mean square. Dividing by it makes the step about η whatever the gradient's scale, and the estimate adapts as the landscape changes. Typical ρ is 0.9.
Say it“r becomes rho r plus one minus rho times g squared; theta moves by eta over root of delta plus r, times g.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde\theta &= \theta - \eta\beta\, v,\quad g = \nabla L(\tilde\theta) \\ r &\leftarrow \rho\, r + (1-\rho)\, g\odot g \\ v &\leftarrow \beta\, v + g \,/ \sqrt{\delta + r} \\ \theta &\leftarrow \theta - \eta\, v \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: RMSProp with Nesterov momentum. Measure the gradient at the look-ahead point, rescale it by its recent size, and feed the rescaled gradient into a velocity. The project below races it as a seventh optimiser.
Say it“Peek ahead, take the gradient there, update r, add the rescaled gradient to beta v, step by minus eta v.”

Adam = RMSProp + momentum + a correction

Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015), the default of chapter 2.3, keeps two fading averages: m of the gradients (momentum, in RMSProp's units) and s of the squared gradients (RMSProp's r). Both start at zero, so for the first few dozen steps they are biased towards zero. Dividing by 1 − βt removes that bias. The lab feeds Adam a gradient of a fixed typical size and plots how big each step is, as a multiple of η. Switch the correction off and look at the early steps.

\[ \begin{aligned} m &\leftarrow \beta_1 m + (1-\beta_1)\,g \\ s &\leftarrow \beta_2 s + (1-\beta_2)\,g\odot g \\ \hat m &= \frac{m}{1-\beta_1^{\,t}}, \quad \hat s = \frac{s}{1-\beta_2^{\,t}} \\ \theta &\leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat s}+\delta} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: after t steps the weights on past gradients in m add up to 1 − β1t, not 1, because the average started from zero. Dividing by that sum turns m into a proper average. Without it, s (with β2 = 0.999) is far more shrunk than m in the early steps, and the ratio m/√s comes out several times too large.
Say it“m and s are fading averages of g and g squared; divide each by one minus beta to the t; step by eta times m-hat over root s-hat.”

Which animal?

Choosing an optimiser

There is no winner. Schaul, Antonoglou and Silver (2014) built a suite of small test problems and found that the adaptive family (RMSProp, AdaDelta, Adam and relatives) was the most robust across them, but no method won everywhere. Later studies disagree on the details: Wilson et al. (2017) found that well-tuned SGD often generalised better than adaptive methods, while Choi et al. (2019) found that once every setting is tuned, the more general optimisers (Adam includes momentum as a special case) never did worse. They agree that tuning matters as much as the choice. What visibly separates the animals is how forgiving they are: how wide the range of learning rates is that gives a good result.

The lab runs all seven optimisers for 100 steps at 61 learning rates from 10−4 to 10 and plots the final loss. Switch problems and compare the widths of the dips. Click a name to hide or show it.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{forgiveness} = \log_{10}\frac{\eta_{\text{hi}}}{\eta_{\text{lo}}}, \\ &\text{where } L_{100}(\eta) \le \tau \\ &\text{for all } \eta_{\text{lo}} \le \eta \le \eta_{\text{hi}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: L100(η) is the loss after 100 steps with learning rate η, and τ is a target (here 10−3 above the minimum). Forgiveness is the width, in powers of ten, of the widest unbroken band of learning rates that reach the target. A width of 2 means the good rates span a factor of 100.
Say it“Forgiveness is log ten of eta-high over eta-low, the widest band of learning rates whose loss after a hundred steps is below tau.”

A practical rule: start with Adam at η = 10−3 to get something working, then tune η over a log grid. If you are reproducing a known recipe, use the optimiser it used: settings that were tuned for one optimiser rarely transfer to another. Goodfellow and colleagues make the same point more bluntly: in practice the choice often comes down to which optimiser the user knows how to tune.

Strategies, 1

Coordinate descent: one variable at a time

Sometimes the easiest move is to stop moving everything at once. Coordinate descent picks one variable, solves for its best value with all the others frozen, then moves to the next, and sweeps round. Block coordinate descent does the same with groups of variables. It shines when each small problem has a cheap exact answer: the lasso (each weight is a soft-threshold, chapter 1.2b), k-means (assign points, then move centres), or sparse coding, where the loss ‖X − WH‖² plus a sparsity penalty is not convex in W and H together but is convex in each one when the other is held fixed.

It fails when variables are strongly coupled: the value one variable wants depends on the other, so each sweep fixes only part of the error. The lab's bowl has a coupling ρ between x and y. Drag the coupling towards ±1 and count the sweeps; drag the star to move the start.

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x,y) &= \tfrac12\left(x^2 + y^2\right) + \rho\, x y \\ x &\leftarrow \arg\min_x f = -\rho\, y \\ y &\leftarrow \arg\min_y f = -\rho\, x \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: setting ∂f/∂x = x + ρy to zero gives x's best value for the current y, and likewise for y. Each update is exact, a jump straight to the floor of a one-dimensional slice, so the path is a staircase of right angles.
Say it“f is a half of x squared plus y squared plus rho x y; set x to minus rho y, then y to minus rho x.”
\[ \begin{aligned} y_{k+1} &= \rho^2\, y_k \\ f_{k+1} &\approx \rho^4 f_k \\ \text{sweeps} &\approx \frac{\ln 10^{-6}}{\ln \rho^4} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: one sweep (an x-update then a y-update) multiplies the error in y by ρ², and f, which is quadratic in the error, by ρ4. At ρ = 0.5 the error halves twice per sweep; at ρ = 0.99, a sweep removes only 2% of it.
Say it“Each sweep multiplies y by rho squared, and f by rho to the fourth.”

Strategies, 2

Polyak averaging: report the average, not the last step

With a constant learning rate and noisy gradients, SGD never settles. It reaches the bottom of the bowl and then rattles around it, the noise kicking it one way, the gradient pulling it back. The individual points are poor estimates of the minimum, but their average sits much closer, because the kicks in opposite directions cancel. Polyak and Juditsky (1992) proved that, for well-behaved convex problems, averaging the iterates achieves the best possible accuracy in the long run. For deep networks, where early points come from a different part of a non-convex landscape, the usual form is an exponential moving average (EMA) that forgets old points.

Raise the noise and compare the three distances from the true minimum (the cross). Then make the learning rate small, and see why averaging matters less when the steps themselves are tiny.

\[ \bar\theta_t = \frac1t \sum_{i=1}^{t} \theta_i \]
Read it as: keep training as normal, but alongside it keep the plain average of every point visited. Use that average, not θt, as the answer. Its weakness: the early points, far from the minimum, never stop counting.
Say it“Theta-bar t is one over t times the sum of all the thetas so far.”
\[ \hat\theta_t = \alpha\,\hat\theta_{t-1} + (1-\alpha)\,\theta_t \]
Read it as: the EMA shrinks its memory by α each step and mixes in the newest point. It effectively averages the last 1/(1 − α) points: α = 0.98 means about 50. The project below keeps such an average of the network's weights (as many image and diffusion models do) and tests it alongside the raw weights.
Say it“Theta-hat t is alpha theta-hat t minus one plus one minus alpha theta t.”

Strategies, 3

Supervised pretraining: build deep from shallow

If a deep model is hard to train directly, train a simpler one first and use it as the starting point. The oldest form, greedy layer-wise supervised pretraining (Bengio et al., 2007), trains a network with one hidden layer, then freezes that layer, stacks a second one on top with a fresh output, trains the new layer, and so on. Each stage is a shallow problem that gradient descent can solve. A last fine-tuning pass trains all layers together, starting from the stack. Related ideas: FitNets (Romero et al., 2015) train a thin, deep student by asking its middle layer to predict a wide teacher's middle layer, and transfer learning (chapter 2.9) starts from a network pretrained on a big related task.

Below, a stack of sigmoid layers, 12 units each, must separate two rings of points. Started from small random weights, a deep sigmoid stack sits on a plateau: the signal fades through every layer and the gradient that reaches the bottom is too weak to move it. Press Train, then change the depth.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{stage } k: \ \min_{W_k,\,U_k}\ L\big(U_k\, h_k\big) \\ &\quad h_k = \sigma\big(W_k h_{k-1}\big), \ \ W_{1..k-1} \text{ frozen} \\ &\text{finally: } \min_{W_{1..K},\,U_K} L, \ \text{from the stack} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: at stage k only the newest layer Wk and a temporary read-out Uk are trained, on top of the frozen layers below. Each stage starts from layers that already carry useful features, so its gradient is not lost in a deep stack of random layers. The last line is ordinary training, from a much better starting point.
Say it“At stage k, train the new layer and a read-out with the layers below frozen; at the end, train everything from the stacked network.”

Strategies, 4

Designing models that are easy to optimise

A better optimiser is one route; a model that is easier to optimise is often a better one. Most of the advances that let networks go deep were changes to the model, not to the optimiser: activations that do not saturate (ReLU, maxout), initialisations that keep the signal's size steady (chapter 2.3), skip connections that give the gradient a short path to every layer (ResNets, He et al., 2016), and auxiliary heads that attach an extra loss to a middle layer so that early layers receive a strong, direct gradient (as in GoogLeNet, Szegedy et al., 2015).

The lab builds a random stack of layers, 24 units wide, feeds a batch through it, and measures how large the gradient is at each layer at initialisation. Try sigmoid at depth 30, then switch on skip connections.

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_1} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_D} \prod_{l=1}^{D-1} J_l \\ J_l &= \frac{\partial h_{l+1}}{\partial h_l} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: backpropagation multiplies one Jacobian per layer. If each Jl shrinks vectors (a sigmoid's slope is at most 1/4), the product shrinks exponentially with depth and the first layers barely learn.
Say it“The gradient at the first layer is the gradient at the top times the product of every layer's Jacobian.”
\[ \begin{aligned} h_{l+1} &= h_l + F_l(h_l) \\ \Rightarrow\ J_l &= I + \frac{\partial F_l}{\partial h_l} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with a skip connection, each layer only adds a correction Fl to its input. Its Jacobian is the identity plus a small term, so the product no longer collapses: some of the gradient always flows straight through. In the lab each branch's weights start scaled by 1/√D, a common recipe that keeps the sum from growing.
Say it“h l plus one is h l plus F of h l, so its Jacobian is the identity plus the Jacobian of F.”

Strategies, 5

Continuation and curriculum: solve an easier problem first

A continuation method replaces the hard objective by a sequence of easier ones that slowly turn into it. The classic recipe blurs the loss: average J over a cloud of points around θ. Blurring erases small wiggles (and the local minima they create) while keeping the broad shape. Solve the most blurred version, use its answer to start the next, less blurred one, and finish on the true loss. The hope is that the broad shape points towards a good region.

Below, plain gradient descent from the start point (the star) is trapped by the first dip it meets. Continuation runs six rounds with the blur shrinking to zero. Move the start, then try the narrow-well landscape, where the best minimum is a deep, narrow hole that blurring erases.

\[ \begin{aligned} J_\sigma(\theta) &= \mathbb{E}_{\theta' \sim \mathcal N(\theta,\,\sigma^2)}\big[ J(\theta') \big] \\ \sigma_0 &> \sigma_1 > \dots > \sigma_K = 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Jσ is J smoothed by a Gaussian of width σ. Minimise Jσ0 first, then use the answer as the start for Jσ1, and so on, until σ = 0 and you are minimising J itself.
Say it“J-sigma of theta is the expected value of J at theta-prime, drawn from a normal around theta with spread sigma.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\big[\cos(k\theta')\big] &= e^{-k^2\sigma^2/2}\cos(k\theta) \\ \mathbb{E}\big[\theta'^2\big] &= \theta^2 + \sigma^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: blurring keeps each wave in place but shrinks its height by e−k²σ²/2. Fast wiggles (large k) vanish first; the slow bowl survives. These two facts give the lab's blurred curves exactly, with no sampling.
Say it“The blurred cosine is e to the minus k squared sigma squared over two, times the cosine.”

Curriculum learning is continuation over the data

Bengio and colleagues (2009) pointed out that teaching people and animals starts with easy examples, and framed curriculum learning as a continuation method: the early objectives weight easy examples heavily, and later ones bring in the hard examples until every example counts equally. Zaremba and Sutskever (2014) found that a stochastic curriculum, mixing a share of examples from the full range at every stage, worked better than a strict easy-to-hard order when teaching networks to evaluate short programs. Whether a curriculum helps depends on the task and on how “easy” is measured, and the project below tests one honestly.

\[ \begin{aligned} J^{(i)}(\theta) &= \frac{\sum_{n} w^{(i)}_n\, \ell_n(\theta)}{\sum_n w^{(i)}_n} \\ w^{(i)}_n &= 1 \text{ if } n \in E_i, \text{ else } 0 \\ E_i &= \text{the easiest } p_i N \text{ examples} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: stage i trains on the easiest fraction pi of the data, and pi grows to 1. In the project, p goes 40%, 55%, 70%, 85%, then 100% from the fifth epoch on, with “easiest” measured by how close an image is to its own class's average image compared with the nearest other class's.
Say it“Stage i's loss is the average loss over the easiest p-i fraction of the examples.”
Real-world project

Project: seven optimisers and a curriculum on KMNIST

Kuzushiji-MNIST (Clanuwat et al., 2018; licence CC BY-SA 4.0) is a drop-in replacement for MNIST built from cursive Japanese characters (kuzushiji) in pre-modern books. It has 10 classes, each a hiragana character listed in the dataset's class map (shown with its Unicode code point below), and its handwriting varies far more than MNIST's digits, so it is a harder test. We take a balanced sample: 3,000 training images, 1,000 validation images (both from the official training set) and 1,000 test images (from the official test set), shrunk to 14×14 pixels with 16 grey levels. The network is 196 inputs, 64 ReLU units, 10 outputs: 13,258 weights.

Every optimiser starts from the same random weights and sees the same mini-batches of 50 in the same order, for 10 epochs (600 steps). Each one's learning rate was picked from a grid of nine by its validation accuracy (map below). The race runs live in your browser. Press Run if it hasn't started.

def mulberry32(seed):                       # the page's random numbers, so both sides start from identical weights
    st = [seed & 0xFFFFFFFF]
    def draw(n):
        a = (st[0] + 0x6D2B79F5 * np.arange(1, n + 1, dtype=np.uint64)) & 0xFFFFFFFF; st[0] = int(a[-1]); M = np.uint64(0xFFFFFFFF)

        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M).astype(np.float64) / 4294967296.0
    return draw
def gauss(draw, n): u = draw(2 * n); return np.sqrt(-2 * np.log(u[0::2])) * np.cos(2 * np.pi * u[1::2])
def shuffle(draw, idx):
    idx = list(idx); u = draw(len(idx) - 1)
    for k, i in enumerate(range(len(idx) - 1, 0, -1)): j = int(u[k] * (i + 1)); idx[i], idx[j] = idx[j], idx[i]
    return idx

S = [196 * 64, 64, 64 * 10, 10]; C = np.cumsum([0] + S)          # 196-64-10 ReLU network, one flat parameter vector
def parts(th): return [th[C[i]:C[i + 1]] for i in range(4)]
def forward(th, X):
    W1, b1, W2, b2 = parts(th); Z = X @ W1.reshape(196, 64) + b1; H = np.maximum(Z, 0); O = H @ W2.reshape(64, 10) + b2
    E = np.exp(O - O.max(1, keepdims=True)); return Z, H, E / E.sum(1, keepdims=True)
def lossgrad(th, X, y):
    Z, H, P = forward(th, X); B = len(y); dO = P.copy(); dO[np.arange(B), y] -= 1; dO /= B
    dZ = (dO @ parts(th)[2].reshape(64, 10).T) * (Z > 0)
    return -np.log(P[np.arange(B), y]).mean(), np.concatenate([(X.T @ dZ).ravel(), dZ.sum(0), (H.T @ dO).ravel(), dO.sum(0)])
def evaluate(th, X, y):
    P = forward(th, X)[2]; return -np.log(np.maximum(P[np.arange(len(y)), y], 1e-300)).mean(), (P.argmax(1) == y).mean()

def step(o, th, data):                      # the seven optimisers; beta = rho = 0.9, Adam (0.9, 0.999)
    k, lr, v, r = o["kind"], o["lr"], o["v"], o["r"]; o["t"] += 1
    L, g = lossgrad(th - lr * 0.9 * v if k in ("nag", "rmsnag") else th, *data)
    if k == "sgd": th -= lr * g
    elif k in ("mom", "nag"): v[:] = 0.9 * v + g; th -= lr * v
    elif k == "adagrad": r += g * g; th -= lr * g / (1e-7 + np.sqrt(r))
    elif k == "rmsprop": r[:] = 0.9 * r + (1 - 0.9) * g * g; th -= lr * g / np.sqrt(1e-6 + r)
    elif k == "rmsnag": r[:] = 0.9 * r + (1 - 0.9) * g * g; v[:] = 0.9 * v + g / np.sqrt(1e-6 + r); th -= lr * v
    elif k == "adam":
        v[:] = 0.9 * v + (1 - 0.9) * g; r[:] = 0.999 * r + (1 - 0.999) * g * g
        th -= lr * (v / (1 - 0.9 ** o["t"])) / (np.sqrt(r / (1 - 0.999 ** o["t"])) + 1e-8)
    return L

def train(kind, lr, Xe, ye, pools=None, seed=11, epochs=10, B=50, ema=0.98):
    d = mulberry32(7); th = np.zeros(C[-1]); th[C[0]:C[1]] = np.sqrt(2 / 196) * gauss(d, S[0]); th[C[2]:C[3]] = np.sqrt(2 / 64) * gauss(d, S[2])
    o = {"kind": kind, "lr": lr, "t": 0, "v": np.zeros(C[-1]), "r": np.zeros(C[-1])}; draw = mulberry32(seed); av = None
    rec = {"batch": [], "epoch": [], "ema": []}; N = len(y)
    for e in range(epochs):
        pool = list(range(N)) if pools is None else pools(e); order = []
        while len(order) < N: order += shuffle(draw, pool)
        for s in range(N // B):
            ib = order[s * B:(s + 1) * B]; L = step(o, th, (X[ib], y[ib]))
            av = th.copy() if av is None else ema * av + (1 - ema) * th
            if not np.isfinite(L): return None
            rec["batch"].append(float(L))
        rec["epoch"].append([*evaluate(th, X, y), *evaluate(th, Xe, ye)]); rec["ema"].append(list(evaluate(av, Xe, ye)))
    return rec

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Nesterov momentum measures the gradient at the look-ahead point θ − ηβv, which brakes before an overshoot; on smooth convex problems it is provably faster, with noisy gradients the gain is small.
  • AdaGrad divides each weight's step by the root of its total squared gradient, so its steps shrink forever; RMSProp uses a fading average instead, and Adam adds momentum and a bias correction for the zero start.
  • Tuned well, the optimisers end within a few points of each other (on KMNIST, 76–80%); what differs is how wide the band of good learning rates is, so tune η on a log grid whatever you choose.
  • Strategies that change the problem often beat a cleverer optimiser: coordinate descent when one-variable updates are exact and coupling is weak, averaged weights for a cheap boost, pretraining, ReLUs, skip connections and auxiliary heads for depth.
  • Continuation and curricula solve an easier problem first; they can find better minima, but blurring can also erase the best one, and on KMNIST an easy-first curriculum did not help.

Check your understanding

Momentum and Nesterov momentum use the same η and β. What exactly is different, and why does it help on a narrow valley?

Only where the gradient is measured: momentum at θ, Nesterov at θ − ηβv, where the velocity is about to carry it. In a narrow valley the velocity across the valley would overshoot; at the look-ahead point the gradient already points back, so the correction comes one step earlier and the swinging dies out faster.

A weight has received the gradient 0.5 at each of its first 100 AdaGrad steps. What is its effective learning rate now, if η = 0.1?

r = 100 × 0.25 = 25, √r = 5, so the effective rate is 0.1/5 = 0.02 (ignoring the tiny δ) and the step is 0.02 × 0.5 = 0.01, exactly η/√100.

Why does Adam need a bias correction but RMSProp, as usually written, does not have one?

Both averages start at zero. Adam divides m by √s, and with β2 = 0.999 the average s is far more shrunk towards zero early on than m with β1 = 0.9, so the uncorrected ratio is several times too large. Dividing each by 1 − βt fixes both. Plain RMSProp's ρ = 0.9 forgets its zero start within a few dozen steps, and its early steps are merely a little large.

Your SGD run with a constant learning rate has plateaued with a noisy loss. Name a strategy from this chapter that improves the final model for free, and why it works.

Averaging the weights (Polyak averaging, or an exponential moving average). The iterates rattle around the minimum; their average cancels much of the noise and lands closer to the bottom. It costs one extra copy of the weights and no extra gradients.

When does blurring the loss (continuation) lead you to the wrong minimum?

When the best minimum is narrow: blurring flattens it away, and the blurred loss's minimum is in the broad basin instead. As the blur shrinks you stay in that basin. Continuation favours wide basins, which is often, but not always, what you want.
