Causal Networks

Seeing that it rained is not the same as making it rain

A Bayesian network says what goes with what. A causal network also says what would happen if you reached in and changed something. The two questions can have opposite answers. In the figure, 300 simulated people who exercise more have higher cholesterol, because older people both exercise more and have higher cholesterol; yet exercise lowers cholesterol for everyone. This chapter gives you the tools to tell the two apart: the do-operator, the adjustment formula and the back-door criterion, tested at the end on a real job-training experiment. Correlation and causation

Seeing is not doing

Statistics answers questions of the form “among the cases where X took this value, how was Y distributed?” That is seeing: P(y | x). Decisions need something else: “if I set X to this value, how will Y be distributed?” That is doing, written P(y | do(x)). The two agree only when nothing but X itself links X to Y.

Three things can make X and Y move together without X causing Y. A common cause Z can drive both (age drives exercise and cholesterol). The arrow can point the other way (Y causes X). Or the data can be selected on a common effect of the two (studying only hospital patients links diseases that each put people in hospital). Chance adds a fourth, which more data cures; the other three it does not.

Take the simplest common cause: Z pushes X with strength a and Y with strength b, and X pushes Y with strength c, all with unit-variance noise. Watching gives the slope below; setting X at random breaks the link from Z and gives back c. Drag a and b until the watched slope has the wrong sign, then switch to the experiment.

\[ \begin{aligned} Z &= U_Z \\ X &= aZ + U_X \\ Y &= cX + bZ + U_Y \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: three structural equations, one per variable, each with its own independent standard-normal noise U. Each says how a variable is made, not merely how it correlates. The causal effect of X on Y is c: raise X by one and Y rises by c, whatever Z is.
Say it“Z is noise; X is a times Z plus noise; Y is c times X plus b times Z plus noise.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \tfrac{d}{dx}\,E[Y \mid X = x] &= c + \frac{ab}{a^2 + 1} \\ \tfrac{d}{dx}\,E[Y \mid do(X = x)] &= c \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: watching, a higher X also signals a higher Z (by a/(a² + 1) per unit of X), and that Z adds b to Y on its own. Doing, X is set from outside, so it carries no news about Z and only the direct c remains. The extra term is the confounding bias.
Say it“The watched slope is c plus a b over a-squared plus one; the slope under intervention is just c.”

Simpson’s paradox

Simpson’s paradox: kidney stones

In a 1986 study of kidney-stone treatments, open surgery (A) beat a keyhole method (B) for small stones and for large stones, yet B had the better recovery rate overall. Nothing is wrong with the arithmetic. Doctors gave the harder cases, large stones, to open surgery far more often, and large stones recover less whatever you do. So the pooled rate for A is an average over mostly hard cases, and the pooled rate for B over mostly easy ones.

Which answer should a patient believe? The numbers alone cannot say. If stone size is a cause of the treatment choice (it was: it was known before treatment), it opens a back door and you should compare like with like, then average the strata with the stone mix of the whole population. If instead Z were something the treatment causes, adjusting for it would hide part of the treatment’s effect and the pooled comparison would be the right one. Switch the story and watch which bar the graph tells you to trust; then move the case mix to see how much imbalance it takes to flip the sign.

\[ P(r \mid A) = \sum_z P(r \mid A, z)\, P(z \mid A) \]
Read it as: the pooled recovery rate for A averages the stratum rates using the stone mix of the patients who got A. Two treatments given to different mixes are not being compared on the same patients.
Say it“Recovery given A is the sum over z of recovery given A and z, times z given A.”
\[ P(r \mid do(A)) = \sum_z P(r \mid A, z)\, P(z) \]
Read it as: if everyone were given A, the stone mix would be the population’s, P(z), not A’s. Same stratum rates, different weights: this is the adjustment formula, valid when Z is a cause of the treatment choice (sections below make this precise).
Say it“Recovery if we do A is the sum over z of recovery given A and z, times the probability of z.”

AIMA 13.5 · causal networks

Arrows as mechanisms: causal Bayesian networks

In chapter 6.1 an arrow only had to make the factorisation work, and many different graphs could encode the same distribution. A causal network asks more: an arrow from A to B means that A is among the things B “listens to”. Each node then stands for a mechanism, a structural equation that makes its value from its parents and some private noise, and its table P(xi | pai) describes that mechanism. Mechanisms are autonomous: you can change one (repair a sprinkler, ban smoking) without touching the others.

That extra commitment is not visible in data. Fire and smoke make the point. Fire → Smoke and Smoke → Fire factorise exactly the same joint distribution: Bayes’ rule turns one set of tables into the other. But only one of them is how the world works, and they disagree the moment you act. Pick an intervention, compare the two predictions, then move P(fire) and see which graph needs only one table changed.

\[ \begin{aligned} X_i &= f_i(\mathrm{Pa}_i,\, U_i), \\ &U_1, \dots, U_n \ \text{independent} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a structural causal model: every variable is a function of its direct causes and an independent noise term. Integrating out the noise turns each equation into the table P(xi | pai), so a structural model always defines a Bayesian network over the same graph.
Say it“Each X-i is f-i of its parents and its own noise U-i, the noises independent.”
\[ \begin{aligned} P(f, s) &= P(f)\,P(s \mid f) \\ &= P(s)\,P(f \mid s) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: both orders of the chain rule are true of any distribution, so both graphs fit any data on fire and smoke perfectly. Choosing between them needs causal knowledge or an experiment, not more observations.
Say it“P of f and s is P of f times P of s given f, which is also P of s times P of f given s.”

AIMA 13.5.1 · representing actions

The do-operator: surgery on the graph

How should a causal network represent an action such as “turn the sprinkler on”? Not as evidence. Seeing the sprinkler on is informative about the weather, because sprinklers tend to run on clear days. Turning it on yourself is not: the weather did not decide it, you did. Judea Pearl’s answer is the do-operator. To compute the effect of do(Xj = x), replace Xj’s structural equation by the constant x, which in the graph means deleting every arrow into Xj, and leave every other mechanism exactly as it was. Then reason in the cut network as usual.

In probability terms, the joint distribution of the cut network is the original product of tables with Xj’s own factor removed: the truncated factorisation. Its parents keep their old distribution, its descendants respond, and nothing flows back up. Click the nodes to switch between seeing and doing, and compare what happens to Cloudy and Rain.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{before: } X_j = f_j(\mathrm{Pa}_j, U_j) \\ &\text{after } do(X_j = x'_j)\text{: } X_j = x'_j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: an intervention is a minimal surgery on the model: one equation is overwritten by a constant, all others are kept. Because the new Xj no longer depends on its parents, the arrows into it vanish from the graph.
Say it“Doing X-j equals x-j-prime replaces its equation with the constant x-j-prime.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &P(x_1, \dots, x_n \mid do(x'_j)) \\ &\quad = \prod_{i \ne j} P(x_i \mid \mathrm{pa}_i) \ \text{ if } x_j = x'_j, \\ &\quad = 0 \ \text{ otherwise} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the probability of a whole world after the action is the usual product of tables with the factor for Xj left out; worlds that disagree with the action get probability zero. Every query about the action is answered by summing this, just like ordinary inference.
Say it“After doing x-j-prime, a world’s probability is the product of all the other nodes’ tables, or zero if it contradicts the action.”

AIMA 13.5.1 · from doing back to seeing

The adjustment formula

The truncated factorisation needs the full causal model. Often you have only observational data and the graph. The good news: when the parents Z of X are all observed, the effect of doing X can be computed from seeing alone. Start from the truncated product, divide out X’s own factor, sum over everything except Y, and the answer collapses to a formula that uses only conditional probabilities you can estimate from data.

The lab is a small clinic. Severe cases (Z) get the treatment (X) more often, with confounding strength k, and recover (Y) less often; the treatment itself adds e to everyone’s chance of recovery. Each sample is a fresh set of patient records. Raise k and watch the naive comparison drift away from the truth while the adjusted one stays on it; then raise N: more data narrows both histograms but moves neither.

\[ \begin{aligned} P(y \mid do(x)) &= \sum_{z} \frac{P(x, y, z)}{P(x \mid z)} \\ &= \sum_{z} P(y \mid x, z)\, P(z) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with Z the parents of X, the cut network’s product equals the original joint divided by X’s factor P(x | z), which gives the first line (the inverse-probability form). Writing P(x, y, z) = P(y | x, z) P(x | z) P(z) cancels the denominator and gives the second (the adjustment form): compare like with like inside each z, then average with the population’s P(z).
Say it“P of y given do x is the sum over z of P of y given x and z, times P of z.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\mathrm{ACE}} = \sum_z \hat P(z)\,\big[ &\hat P(y \mid 1, z) \\ - &\hat P(y \mid 0, z) \big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the average causal effect, P(y | do(1)) − P(y | do(0)), estimated by plugging sample frequencies into the adjustment form. Both forms need positivity: every stratum z must contain treated and untreated cases, or P(y | x, z) is undefined. With sample frequencies, the two forms give the same number to the last digit.
Say it“The estimated effect sums, over z, the share of z times the treated-minus-untreated recovery rate inside z.”

AIMA 13.5.2 · the back-door criterion

The back-door criterion

Adjusting for the parents of X always works, but parents may be unmeasured, and other sets may be cheaper. Which sets Z are safe? Pearl’s back-door criterion answers from the graph alone. A back-door path is any path between X and Y that starts with an arrow into X; such paths carry association that is not causation. Z must close all of them, using the usual rules from chapter 6.2 (a chain or fork is blocked by conditioning on its middle node, a collider is blocked unless it or a descendant is conditioned on), and Z must not contain anything X causes.

The graph below is a standard trap. C is a confounder, so it must be in Z. But C is also a collider between A and B, so conditioning on C alone opens the path X ← A → C ← B → Y. M is a mediator and S a common effect of X and Y; both are descendants of X. Every number on the right is exact: the true effect comes from the cut network, the estimate from the adjustment formula applied to the observational joint. Click nodes into Z until the error is exactly zero, then test all 32 sets at once.

\[ \begin{aligned} &Z \text{ is a back-door set for } (X, Y) \text{ if:} \\ &\text{(i) no node in } Z \text{ is a descendant} \\ &\quad \text{of } X, \text{ and} \\ &\text{(ii) } Z \text{ blocks every path between} \\ &\quad X \text{ and } Y \text{ with an arrow into } X. \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: condition (ii) removes all spurious association; condition (i) makes sure you do not block or distort the causal paths themselves. The parents of X always satisfy both, which is the adjustment formula of the previous section.
Say it“Z is a back-door set if it holds no descendant of X and blocks every path into X’s back door.”
\[ P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_{z} P(y \mid x, z)\, P(z) \]
Read it as: for any back-door set Z, the same adjustment formula holds, with z running over all joint values of the variables in Z. When Z fails the criterion the formula still returns a number, just not the causal one.
Say it“For a back-door set Z, P of y given do x is the sum over z of P of y given x and z, times P of z.”

Three remarks. First, the criterion is sufficient, not necessary: when no back-door set exists, the effect may still be identifiable by other routes (next sections), and Pearl’s three rules of the do-calculus are complete for deciding when an effect can be computed from observational data. Second, everything rests on the graph being right, including the claim that no unmeasured variable opens a back door. Third, “adjust for everything measured before treatment” is not the same rule: the next section shows a pre-treatment variable that must be left out.

Using the criterion · regression

Good and bad controls

In practice adjustment is usually done by putting variables into a regression. In a linear model with Gaussian noise, the coefficient on X in a least-squares fit of Y on X and Z is exactly the back-door-adjusted effect when {Z} is a back-door set, so the criterion tells you which regressors are safe. Six small worlds, each with a true total effect of X on Y equal to 1, show the patterns every analyst should recognise. Step through the six graphs and predict, before looking, whether adding Z helps or hurts.

\[ Y = \alpha + \beta X + \gamma Z + \varepsilon \]
Read it as: regress Y on X with Z as a control. If {Z} satisfies the back-door criterion and the model is linear, the least-squares β̂ is an unbiased estimate of the causal effect of X. If Z is a mediator, a collider, or opens an M-shaped path, β̂ is biased however large the sample.
Say it“Y equals alpha plus beta X plus gamma Z plus noise; beta is the effect only if Z is a back-door set.”

When the back door is locked

Front doors and instruments, in brief

Sometimes the confounder is never measured, so no back-door set exists. Two classic escapes remain. The front-door criterion works when the whole effect of X passes through a measured mediator M that the hidden confounder does not touch, and nothing confounds X and M. Pearl’s textbook illustration: a hidden genotype might cause both smoking and cancer, but if smoking harms only through tar in the lungs, the effect is still identifiable. Chain two adjustments: the effect of X on M (no back door, since X’s confounding does not reach M), and the effect of M on Y (whose back door through X is closed by adjusting for X). Raise the hidden confounding: the naive difference moves, the front door does not.

\[ \begin{aligned} P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_m &P(m \mid x) \\ \times &\sum_{x'} P(y \mid m, x')\, P(x') \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the outer sum is how smoking changes tar; the inner sum is how tar changes cancer, adjusted for smoking status x′ to close the back door M ← X ← U → Y. Every term is observable; the hidden U never appears.
Say it“Sum over m of P of m given x, times the sum over x-prime of P of y given m and x-prime, times P of x-prime.”

An instrumental variable Z is a variable that moves X (relevance), affects Y only through X (exclusion), and shares no hidden cause with Y (independence): a lottery for training places, or distance to a college. In a linear model the effect is the ratio of two covariances, each free of the hidden confounder. Without linearity an instrument alone generally gives only bounds, or, under an extra monotonicity assumption, the effect among those whose X the instrument actually moves. Weaken the instrument and watch the 200 IV estimates scatter.

\[ \hat\beta_{\mathrm{IV}} = \frac{\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(Z, Y)}{\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(Z, X)} \]
Read it as: how much Y moves with the instrument, divided by how much X moves with it. Because Z touches Y only through X, the ratio is the effect of X. When Z barely moves X (a weak instrument, first-stage F well below 10), the denominator is mostly noise and the ratio becomes unstable.
Say it“Beta-hat IV is the covariance of Z with Y over the covariance of Z with X.”

Real-world project

Project: did job training pay? Back-door adjustment on the NSW experiment

In the mid-1970s the US National Supported Work Demonstration (NSW) offered disadvantaged workers subsidised jobs for up to 18 months, and chose who got a place by lottery. The lottery makes the comparison of trainees with rejected applicants a randomised experiment: for the 185 trained men and 260 controls in the Dehejia–Wahba subsample, trainees earned about $1,794 more in 1978. Robert LaLonde (1986) then asked an uncomfortable question: if we had no lottery, and compared the trainees with ordinary men from national surveys (the PSID and the CPS), would the standard methods find that answer? Mostly they did not.

The causal story is a back-door graph. Age, schooling, race, marriage, degree and, above all, earnings in 1974 and 1975 influenced who enrolled and also influence 1978 earnings. If those eight covariates form a back-door set (no unmeasured cause of both enrolment and earnings), the effect is identified. The quantity the experiment estimates is the effect on the men who trained, so the back-door formula is weighted by the trainees’ covariate mix, P(z | trained), rather than the population’s. The data (Dehejia & Wahba 1999, from LaLonde’s files) are distributed by Rajeev Dehejia at users.nber.org/~rdehejia/nswdata2.html for attributable non-commercial use (CC BY-NC); this page embeds all 18,927 rows, with earnings rounded to whole dollars.

1 · Who are we comparing?

The survey men are older, better schooled, far more often married, and earned roughly seven to twelve times as much in the two years before the programme. Comparing raw 1978 earnings mostly measures those differences.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{ATT} = \sum_z P(z \mid T{=}1)\,\big[ &E(Y \mid T{=}1, z) \\ - &E(Y \mid T{=}0, z) \big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the back-door formula for the effect on the treated: inside each cell z of the covariates, compare trainees with controls; average the gaps with the trainees’ own mix. Cells with no control cannot be used (positivity), so those trainees drop out.
Say it“The effect on the treated sums, over z, the trainees’ share in z times the trained-minus-control gap in z.”
\[ w_i = \frac{e(z_i)}{1 - e(z_i)}, \qquad e(z) = P(T{=}1 \mid z) \]
Read it as: with eight covariates the cells are too many and too thin, so model the propensity score e(z) with a logistic regression and weight each control by its odds of having trained. Weighted controls then mimic the trainees’ covariate mix; Rosenbaum and Rubin showed that adjusting for e(z) suffices whenever adjusting for z does.
Say it“Each control’s weight is its propensity over one minus its propensity.”

How the three estimators use Z

Back-door strata cut each covariate into a few bands (age under 20, 20–24, 25–34, 35+; schooling under 9, 9–11, 12+ years; earnings zero, up to $5,000, more) and apply the ATT formula cell by cell. Regression adjustment fits 1978 earnings on a training indicator plus the covariates (age and schooling with squares, earnings in thousands with an indicator for zero earnings) and reads off the training coefficient; it assumes the same effect for everyone and extrapolates where there are no comparable controls. Propensity weighting fits a logistic model of training on the same covariate terms and weights controls by e/(1 − e).

3 · Did the weights balance the covariates?

A standardised difference near 0 means the weighted controls look like the trainees on that covariate. Balance on what you measured is checkable; the absence of unmeasured back doors is not.

The same analysis in Python

One script reproduces every lab and the project: it uses the same random-number generator as the page for the simulations, exact enumeration for the networks, and numpy, scikit-learn and the same embedded data for the project, including a bootstrap the browser does not attempt.

# Python reference for chapter 6.20 (causal networks). Reproduces every number the page prints.
import json, sys, math, itertools, time
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression, LogisticRegression
S = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "./"
M32 = 0xFFFFFFFF
def rng(a):                                    # mulberry32, the generator the page uses
    st = [a & M32]
    def r():
        st[0] = (st[0] + 0x6D2B79F5) & M32; a = st[0]
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & M32
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & M32)) & M32) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M32) / 4294967296
    return r
def gauss(r):                                  # Box-Muller, as gaussOf() on the page
    u = 0.0
    while u == 0.0: u = r()
    return math.sqrt(-2 * math.log(u)) * math.cos(2 * math.pi * r())
def slope(x, y): x, y = np.asarray(x), np.asarray(y); return float(np.cov(x, y, bias=True)[0, 1] / np.var(x))
res = {}; T0 = time.time()

# ---------- hero: exercise and cholesterol (simulated) ----------
r = rng(47); E, C, ER, CR = [], [], [], []
for i in range(300):
    g = i % 5; age = 25 + 10 * g + (r() * 8 - 4)
    ex = max(0.1, 2 + 0.08 * (age - 25) + 0.6 * gauss(r)); e2 = gauss(r); exr = max(0.1, 3.75 + 1.05 * gauss(r))
    E.append(ex); C.append(150 + 1.5 * (age - 25) - 7 * ex + 6 * e2); ER.append(exr); CR.append(150 + 1.5 * (age - 25) - 7 * exr + 6 * e2)
within = [slope(E[g::5], C[g::5]) for g in range(5)]
res["hero"] = dict(see=slope(E, C), do=slope(ER, CR), within=within)
print(f"hero (simulated, 300 people): slope seen {res['hero']['see']:.4f}, slope under do(exercise) {res['hero']['do']:.4f}, within-decade slopes " + " ".join(f"{w:.3f}" for w in within))

# ---------- seeing vs doing, linear-Gaussian ----------
a, b, c = 1.2, 1.5, 0.5
r = rng(11); X, Y, XD, YD = [], [], [], []
for i in range(400):
    z = gauss(r); ex = gauss(r); ey = gauss(r); exd = gauss(r)
    x = a * z + ex; xd = math.sqrt(a * a + 1) * exd
    X.append(x); Y.append(c * x + b * z + ey); XD.append(xd); YD.append(c * xd + b * z + ey)
res["why"] = dict(exact=c + a * b / (a * a + 1), see=slope(X, Y), do=slope(XD, YD))
print(f"seeing vs doing (a={a}, b={b}, c={c}): exact observed slope {res['why']['exact']:.6f}; 400 samples: seen {res['why']['see']:.4f}, doing {res['why']['do']:.4f}")

# ---------- Simpson's paradox: kidney stones (Charig et al. 1986) ----------
K = {"A": {"small": (81, 87), "large": (192, 263)}, "B": {"small": (234, 270), "large": (55, 80)}}
nz = {z: K["A"][z][1] + K["B"][z][1] for z in ("small", "large")}; N = sum(nz.values())
kid = {}
for t in "AB":
    pooled = sum(K[t][z][0] for z in nz) / sum(K[t][z][1] for z in nz)
    adj = sum(K[t][z][0] / K[t][z][1] * nz[z] / N for z in nz)
    kid[t] = dict(pooled=pooled, adj=adj, small=K[t]["small"][0] / K[t]["small"][1], large=K[t]["large"][0] / K[t]["large"][1])
    print(f"kidney stones, treatment {t}: small {kid[t]['small']:.4f}, large {kid[t]['large']:.4f}, pooled {pooled:.4f}, back-door adjusted {adj:.4f}")
res["kidney"] = kid

# ---------- fire and smoke: two graphs, one joint ----------
pf, psf, psn = 0.1, 0.9, 0.05
ps = pf * psf + (1 - pf) * psn; pfs = pf * psf / ps; pfn = pf * (1 - psf) / (1 - ps)
print(f"fire/smoke: P(smoke) {ps:.4f}, P(fire|smoke) {pfs:.6f}, P(fire|no smoke) {pfn:.6f}; true P(fire|do(smoke)) {pf:.4f}; the reversed graph would say {pfs:.6f}")
res["fire"] = dict(ps=ps, pfs=pfs, pfn=pfn)

# ---------- garden network: seeing vs doing by enumeration ----------
GN = ["Cloudy", "Sprinkler", "Rain", "Wet grass", "Slippery"]; GP = [[], [0], [0], [1, 2], [3]]
GC = [[0.5], [0.5, 0.1], [0.2, 0.8], [0.01, 0.9, 0.9, 0.99], [0.05, 0.7]]   # P(node=1 | parents), index = sum parent_k * 2^k
def joint(par, cpt, n, do=None):
    do = do or {}; P = {}
    for w in itertools.product([0, 1], repeat=n):
        p = 1.0
        for i in range(n):
            if i in do: p *= 1.0 if w[i] == do[i] else 0.0; continue
            idx = sum(w[q] << k for k, q in enumerate(par[i])); q1 = cpt[i][idx]; p *= q1 if w[i] else 1 - q1
        P[w] = p

def adj_world(k, e, n, seed):

    for i in range(n):

def fd(s, t):

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Seeing and doing are different questions: P(y | x) describes the cases where X happened to be x; P(y | do(x)) describes the world after you make X equal x. Common causes, reversed arrows and selection all separate them.
  • A causal network reads each arrow as a direct cause and each table as an autonomous mechanism. Graphs that fit the data equally well can disagree about every intervention.
  • An action do(X = x) cuts the arrows into X and keeps every other mechanism: the truncated factorisation drops X’s own factor from the product.
  • If Z satisfies the back-door criterion (no descendant of X, every back-door path blocked), then P(y | do(x)) = Σz P(y | x, z) P(z): causal effects from observational data, given the graph.
  • Not every variable is a safe control: mediators, colliders and M-shaped paths add bias. When the back door is locked, a front-door mediator or an instrument may still identify the effect.

Check your understanding

In the garden network, why does turning the sprinkler on leave P(Rain) at 0.5 while seeing it on lowers P(Rain) to 0.3?

Seeing the sprinkler on is evidence that the day is not cloudy (the sprinkler is used mostly on clear days), and clear days rarely bring rain, so belief flows from Sprinkler up to Cloudy and down to Rain. Turning it on yourself cuts the arrow from Cloudy, so the sprinkler’s state carries no information about the weather, and Rain keeps its prior.

Why is {C} alone not a valid adjustment set in the seven-node graph, even though C is a confounder?

Conditioning on C blocks X ← C → Y and X ← A → C → Y, but C is a collider on X ← A → C ← B → Y, and conditioning on a collider opens that path. Adding A or B closes it again, which is why {A, C} and {B, C} work.

The kidney-stone counts are fixed. What would make the pooled comparison, not the stratified one, the right answer?

A different causal graph with the same numbers: if Z were caused by the treatment (a mediator) instead of causing the treatment choice, adjusting for it would remove part of the effect. With no other confounding, the pooled comparison would then be the causal one. The data cannot distinguish the two stories; knowledge of the timing and the mechanism can.

Why might adjusting for a variable measured before treatment still introduce bias?

If it is a collider of two hidden causes, one affecting X and one affecting Y (the M-bias graph), the path through it is blocked as it stands; conditioning on it opens the path. Being pre-treatment rules out condition (i) failing, but not condition (ii).

With the PSID controls, why does the exact-strata estimate keep only about half of the trainees?

With eight covariates cut into bands there are thousands of cells, and many cells that hold a trainee hold no PSID control: young, unmarried, low-earning men are rare in that survey. The back-door formula needs P(y | x, z) for both arms, so those cells, and their trainees, cannot be used, a failure of positivity. The propensity score avoids empty cells by modelling one number instead of thousands of cells, but its estimate still depends on the few controls that resemble the trainees.
