Sparse Models: Lasso Paths, LARS & the Group Lasso

One knob, every model from empty to full

Chapter 1.2 met the lasso as one model with one λ. This chapter looks at the whole family at once. Turn λ from huge to zero and the coefficients do not wander about: they move along straight lines, bending only where a variable joins or leaves. That path can be computed exactly, crawled along in tiny steps, or traced by coordinate descent, and the same idea extends to groups, neighbours and classifiers. On the right is the full lasso path for ten diabetes measurements. Drag across the figure to slide the budget and watch variables switch on one at a time.

The idea

Three ways to shrink a coefficient

Best-subset selection, ridge and the lasso all trade fit for simplicity, but each treats a coefficient differently. The cleanest way to see the difference is the textbook special case where the inputs are orthonormal (uncorrelated, unit length). Then each method takes the ordinary least-squares estimate β̂j and transforms it on its own: best subset keeps it or kills it, ridge scales it down, and the lasso slides it toward zero and stops at zero. Move β̂ and λ and compare the three outputs.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{subset: } \hat\beta_j\,\mathbb{1}\big(|\hat\beta_j| > \sqrt{2\lambda}\,\big) \\ &\text{ridge: } \hat\beta_j \,/\, (1+\lambda) \\ &\text{lasso: } \operatorname{sign}(\hat\beta_j)\,\big(|\hat\beta_j| - \lambda\big)_+ \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: hard threshold, proportional shrinkage, soft threshold. Each line is the minimiser of ½(b − β̂j)² plus a penalty on b: λ for being non-zero at all (best subset), ½λb² (ridge), or λ|b| (lasso). (x)+ means max(x, 0). Hard thresholding jumps; ridge never reaches zero; soft thresholding is continuous and reaches zero, at the price of shifting every survivor toward zero by λ.
Say it“Best subset keeps beta-hat if its size beats root two lambda; ridge divides it by one plus lambda; the lasso subtracts lambda from its size and clips at zero.”

lasso

With correlated inputs there is no formula, but there is a picture. Least squares has elliptical contours of equal error around β̂. A penalty is the same as a budget: keep the coefficients inside a ball. The answer is where the smallest ellipse first touches the ball. The lasso's ball is a diamond with corners on the axes, so the touch often happens at a corner, where one coefficient is exactly zero. A circle has no corners. Bridge penalties |β|q with q < 1 have sharper, non-convex corners; the elastic net rounds the diamond's edges but keeps its corners. Drag the star (the least-squares estimate), change the ball and shrink the budget.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\tilde\beta = \arg\min_\beta \ \textstyle\sum_i \big(y_i - x_i^{\mathsf T}\beta\big)^2 \\ &\text{s.t.}\ \ \textstyle\sum_j |\beta_j|^q \le t^q \\ &\text{or}\ \ \textstyle\sum_j \big(\alpha\beta_j^2 + (1-\alpha)|\beta_j|\big) \\ &\qquad \le \alpha t^2 + (1-\alpha)t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: minimise the squared error while keeping the coefficients inside a ball that crosses each axis at ±t. q = 2 is ridge, q = 1 the lasso, q < 1 a non-convex bridge that selects more aggressively but has local traps, and the second budget is the elastic net, a blend of ridge and lasso. Only q ≤ 1 balls, and the elastic net, have corners on the axes, which is what makes exact zeros possible; q = 1 is the only one that is both cornered and convex.
Say it“Beta-tilde minimises the residual sum of squares subject to the sum of absolute betas to the q being at most t.”

An algorithm

Least angle regression: a fair race

Forward stepwise regression grabs the best variable and fits it fully, which is greedy. Least angle regression (LAR; Efron, Hastie, Johnstone & Tibshirani, 2004) is more even-handed. Standardise the inputs and start with every coefficient at zero. Find the input most correlated with the residual and move its coefficient toward its least-squares value, but only until some other input has caught up in correlation. Then move both together, in the direction that keeps their correlations tied and falling, until a third catches up, and so on. After p steps you arrive at the full least-squares fit. Step through the race on the diabetes data: the active variables' correlations fall together along one line, and each newcomer joins exactly when it touches that line.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\beta_{\mathcal A}(\gamma) = \beta_{\mathcal A} + \gamma\, d_{\mathcal A}, \\ &d_{\mathcal A} = \big(X_{\mathcal A}^{\mathsf T} X_{\mathcal A}\big)^{-1} s_{\mathcal A} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: 𝒜 is the active set, s𝒜 the signs of their correlations with the residual. Moving along d𝒜 lowers every active correlation xjTr by exactly γ, so they stay tied: the fitted vector heads along the direction that makes equal angles with every active input, hence “least angle”.
Say it“The active coefficients move by gamma times d, where d is X-A-transpose-X-A inverse times the sign vector.”
\[ \gamma = \min_{j\notin\mathcal A}{}^{+} \Big\{ \frac{C - c_j}{1 - a_j},\ \frac{C + c_j}{1 + a_j} \Big\} \]
Read it as: C is the shared correlation of the active inputs, cj = xjTr an inactive input's correlation, and aj = xjTX𝒜d𝒜 how fast it changes. The step stops at the first γ (the smallest positive one, min+) at which some outsider's |cj| has risen or fallen to meet C.
Say it“Gamma is the smallest positive value of C minus c-j over one minus a-j, or C plus c-j over one plus a-j, over the inactive inputs.”

The path

The lasso path: LAR with one extra rule

LAR's path and the lasso's path agree almost everywhere, and the reason is the lasso's optimality conditions. At a lasso solution every active input has correlation with the residual exactly ±nλ, with the same sign as its coefficient, and every inactive input has smaller correlation. That is LAR's tie, read backwards. The one place they part company is when an active coefficient shrinks to zero: LAR lets it cross and change sign, which breaks the sign rule. The lasso modification drops the variable at that moment, recomputes the direction, and carries on. On the diabetes data that happens once, to s3 (HDL cholesterol). Set λ between 0.062 and 0.104 and compare the two methods in the table.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\hat\beta_j \ne 0:\ \ \tfrac1n\, x_j^{\mathsf T} r = \lambda\,\operatorname{sign}(\hat\beta_j) \\ &\hat\beta_j = 0:\ \ \tfrac1n\, \big|x_j^{\mathsf T} r\big| \le \lambda \\ &\text{where } r = y - X\hat\beta \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: r is the residual. These are the lasso's Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions for minimising (1/2n)‖y − Xβ‖² + λ‖β‖1. Active inputs are tied at λ with the right sign; the rest sit below. Any β meeting both lines is a lasso solution.
Say it“For an active j, one over n times x-j-transpose the residual equals lambda times the sign of beta-j; for an inactive j its size is at most lambda.”
\[ \frac{d\hat\beta_{\mathcal A}}{d\lambda} = -\,n\,\big(X_{\mathcal A}^{\mathsf T}X_{\mathcal A}\big)^{-1} s_{\mathcal A} \]
Read it as: solve the first KKT line for β𝒜: β𝒜 = (X𝒜TX𝒜)−1(X𝒜Ty − nλs𝒜). It is linear in λ, so while the active set and signs stay fixed the path is a straight line. It only bends at a knot, where the set changes.
Say it“The slope of the active coefficients in lambda is minus n times X-A-transpose-X-A inverse times the signs: a constant.”
non-zero coefficients
Cp estimate of error

Two more facts make the path useful. First, degrees of freedom: although the lasso chooses its variables adaptively, the number of non-zero coefficients is an unbiased estimate of its effective degrees of freedom (Zou, Hastie & Tibshirani, 2007), and after k LAR steps the fit has about k. That justifies the Cp score in the panel. Second, piecewise linearity is not special to squared error. Rosset & Zhu (2007) showed that any loss that is piecewise quadratic (squared error, the Huber loss, the hinge loss) paired with a piecewise-linear penalty (L1) has a piecewise-linear path, so a LAR-like algorithm can trace it exactly.

\[ \begin{aligned} &C_p = \frac{\mathrm{RSS}(\hat\beta_\lambda)}{\hat\sigma^2} - n + 2\,\mathrm{df} \\ &\widehat{\mathrm{df}}(\hat\beta_\lambda) = \big|\{j : \hat\beta_j \ne 0\}\big| \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Cp estimates prediction error in units of the noise variance: training error, plus a charge of 2 for every degree of freedom spent. σ̂² comes from the full least-squares fit. Smaller is better.
Say it“C-p is the residual sum of squares over sigma-hat squared, minus n, plus twice the degrees of freedom, which for the lasso is the number of non-zero coefficients.”
Small steps

Forward stagewise: crawling along the path

There is an even simpler way to trace a path. Incremental forward stagewise regression (FSε) finds the input most correlated with the residual and nudges its coefficient by a tiny ε in the right direction. That's it; repeat thousands of times. It never fits anything fully, so a variable that is only briefly the best gets only a little weight. As ε → 0 the crawl traces a path that matches the lasso's whenever every lasso coefficient moves monotonically; where a lasso coefficient turns back toward zero, the infinitesimal crawl, FS0, follows a slightly different “monotone lasso” path instead. Pick an ε and press Crawl, then compare with the dashed lasso path.

\[ \begin{aligned} & j^\star = \arg\max_j \big|x_j^{\mathsf T} r\big| \\ & \delta = \varepsilon\,\operatorname{sign}\!\big(x_{j^\star}^{\mathsf T} r\big) \\ & \beta_{j^\star} \leftarrow \beta_{j^\star} + \delta \\ & r \leftarrow r - \delta\, x_{j^\star} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: pick the input that best explains what is left, move its coefficient by δ = ±ε toward agreeing with the residual, subtract that sliver of fit from the residual, repeat. All inputs are standardised, so the correlations are comparable.
Say it“Find j-star with the largest absolute correlation with r; add epsilon times its sign to beta j-star and take the same sliver out of r.”

Now replace the ten measurements by a huge dictionary of trees: every possible split of the data. Forward stagewise over that dictionary is exactly boosting with shrinkage: at each step fit the best small tree to the residual and add only ε of it. That is why boosting with a small learning rate behaves like an L1-penalised fit in the space of all trees: it walks along an approximate lasso path, adding complexity slowly (Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman, §16.2). Watch boosting with stumps build a fit of disease progression from body-mass index, one sliver at a time.

\[ \begin{aligned} &(\beta^\star, k^\star) \\ &\quad = \arg\min_{\beta,\,k} \textstyle\sum_i \big(r_i - \beta\, T_k(x_i)\big)^2 \\ &\alpha_{k^\star} \leftarrow \alpha_{k^\star} + \varepsilon\,\operatorname{sign}(\beta^\star) \\ &f(x) = \textstyle\sum_k \alpha_k T_k(x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each Tk is a tree from the dictionary (here, a stump: ±1 on either side of one split of body-mass index). Pick the one that best fits the residual and add ε to its coefficient. After M steps ‖α‖1 ≤ Mε: the step count plays the role of the lasso's budget.
Say it“Find the tree and coefficient that best fit the residual; add epsilon times that coefficient's sign to the tree's weight.”

The workhorse

Pathwise coordinate descent

LAR is exact but needs a matrix solve per knot; for millions of inputs the method of choice is far humbler. Coordinate descent fixes every coefficient but one and solves for that one exactly, which for the lasso is just the soft threshold from the first section applied to a partial residual. Sweep over the coordinates until nothing moves. Two tricks make it fast (Friedman, Hastie, Höfling & Tibshirani, 2007): compute a whole decreasing grid of λ, each fit warm-started from the last, and sweep mostly over the active variables. Press One update and watch the iterate move parallel to an axis each time; then switch to the strongly correlated pair.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\hat\beta_j \leftarrow \frac{S\big(z_j,\ \lambda\big)}{\tfrac1n\, x_j^{\mathsf T} x_j} \\ &z_j = \tfrac1n\, x_j^{\mathsf T} r^{(j)} \\ &r^{(j)} = y - \textstyle\sum_{k\ne j} x_k \hat\beta_k \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: r(j) is the residual with input j's own contribution put back, and zj its covariance with xj. Regressing it on xj alone is a one-dimensional problem, and its lasso solution is the soft threshold S(z, λ) = sign(z)(|z| − λ)+. With standardised inputs the denominator is 1.
Say it“Beta-j becomes the soft threshold at lambda of one over n x-j-transpose the partial residual, divided by one over n x-j-transpose x-j.”

Relatives

The Dantzig selector and the group lasso

Candès & Tao (2007) turned the lasso's KKT inequality into the whole problem. The Dantzig selector asks for the smallest L1 norm among all coefficient vectors whose correlations with the residual are all at most s. It is a linear program. Since the lasso solution at λ = s satisfies the same bound, the Dantzig selector's L1 norm is never larger. But it no longer cares about the residual sum of squares itself, only about the worst correlation, and its path can jump about: variables may enter and leave abruptly, and with correlated inputs it can choose oddly. Slide s and compare the two paths.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_\beta \ \lVert\beta\rVert_1 \\ &\text{s.t.}\ \ \tfrac1n\big\lVert X^{\mathsf T}(y - X\beta)\big\rVert_\infty \le s \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ‖v‖∞ is the largest absolute entry of v. Keep every input's correlation with the residual at or below s, and among all such β take the one with the smallest total absolute size. Written with β = u − v, u, v ≥ 0, it is a linear program; this page solves it with a small simplex method.
Say it“Minimise the L1 norm of beta, subject to the largest absolute correlation between an input and the residual being at most s.”

Sometimes inputs come in natural groups: the dummy variables of one categorical feature, the measurements of one organ, the expansions of one input. Selecting half a group is meaningless. The group lasso (Yuan & Lin, 2006) penalises each group's Euclidean length instead of each coefficient's size, so a whole group enters or leaves together. Inside a group the penalty behaves like ridge; across groups, like the lasso. Below, the ten diabetes inputs are grouped as demographics (age, sex), body (bmi, bp), the four cholesterol measures (s1–s4), triglycerides (s5) and glucose (s6). Switch between the lasso and the group lasso.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_\beta \ \frac{1}{2n}\Big\lVert y - \sum_{\ell=1}^{L} X_\ell\beta_\ell \Big\rVert^2 \\ &\quad + \lambda \sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \sqrt{p_\ell}\;\lVert \beta_\ell \rVert_2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: group ℓ has pℓ coefficients βℓ. ‖βℓ‖2 is not squared, so it has a corner at zero for the group as a whole (whole groups can vanish) but is smooth inside (members shrink together). √pℓ stops big groups from being favoured. Each update shrinks a group's vector toward zero by a common factor, or sets it exactly to zero.
Say it“Minimise the squared error over two n plus lambda times the sum over groups of root p-ell times the length of that group's coefficients.”

Strengths and flaws

What the lasso gets right, and what it doesn't

The soft threshold shifts every survivor toward zero by λ, so the lasso is biased on exactly the coefficients it keeps. To kill the noise variables, λ must be large; but then the real effects are shrunk too, and choosing λ by prediction error tends to keep too many variables to compensate. Remedies keep the lasso's selection and undo its shrinkage. The relaxed lasso (Meinshausen, 2007) refits least squares on the selected variables and blends: φ = 1 is the lasso, φ = 0 an unshrunk refit. The adaptive lasso (Zou, 2006) gives each coefficient its own penalty weight, small for inputs that look big in a first-pass fit. Concave penalties such as SCAD and MC+ do the same by flattening the penalty for large coefficients. Whether the lasso picks exactly the right variables also depends on the design: if a noise input is too well explained by the true ones (the irrepresentable condition fails), no λ recovers the truth. Slide λ and φ on a simulated problem where we know the answer.

\[ \hat\beta^{\text{relax}} = \phi\,\hat\beta^{\text{lasso}}_\lambda + (1-\phi)\,\hat\beta^{\text{LS}}_{\mathcal A_\lambda} \]
Read it as: 𝒜λ is the lasso's active set at λ, and β̂LS𝒜 the ordinary least-squares fit using only those inputs. φ between 0 and 1 blends shrunk and unshrunk.
Say it“The relaxed estimate is phi times the lasso plus one minus phi times least squares on the lasso's chosen inputs.”
\[ \min_\beta \ \tfrac{1}{2n}\lVert y - X\beta\rVert^2 + \lambda \sum_j \frac{|\beta_j|}{|\hat\beta_j^{\text{LS}}|} \]
Read it as: the adaptive lasso: each coefficient's penalty is divided by the size of its least-squares estimate, so inputs that look strong are barely penalised and weak ones heavily. Under mild conditions it selects the right variables as n grows, which the plain lasso need not.
Say it“Minimise the squared error plus lambda times the sum of each absolute beta divided by its least-squares size.”

The bet on sparsity

If the lasso is biased, why use it? Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman's answer (§16.2.2) is a bet. If the truth is sparse, with a few large effects among many inputs, an L1 method can find them and does far better than ridge. If the truth is dense, with many small effects, nothing does well with modest data: there is too little information to estimate a hundred effects from fifty rows. So betting on sparsity loses little when it is wrong and wins a lot when it is right. Classification

L1-penalised logistic regression

Nothing about the L1 penalty is specific to squared error. Add it to logistic regression's log-likelihood and you get a classifier that selects features, the standard tool when there are more measurements than patients: gene-expression arrays, or the protein mass spectra in which every one of thousands of mass-to-charge readings is a candidate biomarker (Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman, §18.4). The fit is coordinate descent again, wrapped in the iteratively reweighted least-squares loop that fits ordinary logistic regression. Here: 569 breast-tumour samples taken by fine-needle aspiration, 30 measurements of cell nuclei from digitised images, 400 used for training. Lower λ and watch features join, the test accuracy settle and the margin climb.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{\beta_0,\beta}\ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \Big[ y_i\eta_i - \log\!\big(1 + e^{\eta_i}\big) \Big] \\ &\quad - \lambda \lVert\beta\rVert_1, \qquad \eta_i = \beta_0 + x_i^{\mathsf T}\beta \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the average log-likelihood of logistic regression (y = 1 for malignant), with ηi the linear score, minus an L1 charge on every coefficient except the intercept. The solution satisfies xjT(y − p)/n = λ sign(βj) for active features, where p are the fitted probabilities: the same tie as the lasso, with y − p as the residual. The path is no longer exactly piecewise linear, but it is close.
Say it“Maximise the average log-likelihood minus lambda times the L1 norm of beta.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &m(\beta) = \min_i \ \frac{\tilde y_i\,\big(\beta_0 + x_i^{\mathsf T}\beta\big)}{\lVert\beta\rVert_1} \\ &\tilde y_i = \pm 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the L1 margin: the worst-classified training point's signed score, measured against the L1 size of β. Rosset, Zhu & Hastie (2004) showed that as λ → 0 on separable data, the normalised L1 logistic path tends to the classifier with the largest possible m, and that boosting's ε-steps head the same way in tree space.
Say it“The margin of beta is the smallest y-tilde times score, divided by the L1 norm of beta.”

Ordered features

The fused lasso: when neighbours should agree

Some features have a natural order: positions along a chromosome, wavelengths of a spectrum, days of a series. Then we expect neighbouring coefficients to be similar, and the plain lasso, which treats features as strangers, ignores that. The fused lasso (Tibshirani, Saunders, Rosset, Zhu & Knight, 2005) adds a second L1 penalty on the differences between neighbours, so the solution is sparse and piecewise constant. Its simplest form denoises a signal directly, as in copy-number data from comparative genomic hybridisation (CGH), where segments of a chromosome are gained or lost. The data below are simulated in that style. Turn up the fusion λ2 and watch noise collapse into flat segments.

\[ \begin{aligned} \min_\beta \ &\frac12\sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - \beta_i)^2 \\ &+ \lambda_1 \sum_{i=1}^N |\beta_i| \\ &+ \lambda_2 \sum_{i=2}^{N} |\beta_i - \beta_{i-1}| \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: one coefficient per position, fitted to the noisy reading yi. λ1 pulls levels to exactly zero (no change in copy number); λ2 charges for every jump, so the fit is flat until a jump pays for itself. The exact solution: denoise with λ2 alone (an O(N) algorithm by Condat, 2013, runs here), then soft-threshold every level by λ1.
Say it“Minimise half the squared misfit plus lambda-one times the sum of absolute levels plus lambda-two times the sum of absolute jumps between neighbours.”

The same penalty works inside a regression: when the inputs are ordered, put the fusion penalty on the coefficients. That is how Tibshirani and colleagues analysed protein mass spectra, where neighbouring mass-to-charge readings belong to the same peak. The project below does exactly this with infrared spectra of meat.

\[ \begin{aligned} \min_\beta \ &\frac{1}{2n}\lVert y - X\beta\rVert^2 \\ &+ \lambda_1 \sum_j |\beta_j| \\ &+ \lambda_2 \sum_{j\ge2} |\beta_j - \beta_{j-1}| \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the lasso plus a charge for every change between neighbouring coefficients. The solver used here is accelerated proximal gradient: a gradient step on the squared error, then the exact fused-lasso denoiser above applied to the coefficient vector.
Say it“Minimise the squared error over two n, plus lambda-one times the L1 norm of beta, plus lambda-two times the total absolute change between neighbouring coefficients.”
Real-world project

Project: which wavelengths reveal the fat in meat?

A Tecator Infratec analyser shines near-infrared light (850–1050 nm) through a sample of finely chopped meat and records how much is absorbed at 100 wavelengths, every 2 nm. Chemistry in the lab then measured each sample's fat content. The data (StatLib, public domain with Tecator's permission note) has 215 samples, already split by its authors: 129 for training (C), 43 for monitoring (M) and 43 for testing (T). We fit on C, choose λ on M and report T once. Neighbouring wavelengths are almost perfectly correlated (above 0.99), which is exactly the setting where the lasso's choices become fragile and the fused lasso's bands become attractive. Each spectrum can be used raw or after standard normal variate (SNV) scaling, which removes each sample's overall baseline and scale (light scattering differs from sample to sample). The same project in scikit-learn

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import lars_path, Ridge, LinearRegression

rows = open("tecator").read().split("\n")[137:]                  # StatLib file, after its header
D = np.array([float(t) for r in rows for t in r.split()]).reshape(-1, 125)
A, fat = D[:215, :100], D[:215, 123]                              # 100 absorbances, fat %
C, M, T = slice(0, 129), slice(129, 172), slice(172, 215)         # the file's own split

def fused(X, y, l1, l2, rho=0.1, iters=20000):                   # ADMM for the fused lasso
    n, p = X.shape; Dm = np.vstack([np.eye(p), np.diff(np.eye(p), axis=0)])
    Qi = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X / n + rho * Dm.T @ Dm); q = X.T @ y / n
    z = u = np.zeros(2 * p - 1); thr = np.r_[np.full(p, l1), np.full(p - 1, l2)] / rho
    for _ in range(iters):
        b = Qi @ (q + rho * Dm.T @ (z - u)); v = Dm @ b + u
        z = np.sign(v) * np.maximum(np.abs(v) - thr, 0); u = v - z
    return z[:p]

for name, X in [("raw", A), ("SNV", (A - A.mean(1, keepdims=True)) / A.std(1, keepdims=True))]:
    mu, sd = X[C].mean(0), X[C].std(0); Z = (X - mu) / sd; y0 = fat[C].mean()
    rmse = lambda b, S: np.sqrt(np.mean((Z[S] @ b + y0 - fat[S]) ** 2))
    alphas, _, coefs = lars_path(Z[C], fat[C] - y0, method="lasso")       # the exact lasso path
    grid = alphas[0] * 1e-4 ** (np.arange(60) / 59)
    path = np.array([[np.interp(-g, -alphas, coefs[j]) for j in range(100)] for g in grid])
    ...                                    # ridge, relaxed lasso, fused grid, OLS: same recipe
    print(f"{name}: lasso path has {len(alphas)} knots; best on M at lambda/lambda_max = ...")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Best subset hard-thresholds, ridge scales, the lasso soft-thresholds: it is the only one of the three that is continuous and also sets coefficients exactly to zero, because its constraint ball has corners.
  • The lasso path is piecewise linear in λ. LAR finds its knots exactly, moving the active coefficients so their correlations with the residual stay tied; the lasso modification drops a variable whose coefficient hits zero.
  • Forward stagewise with tiny ε crawls along (nearly) the same path, and the same crawl over a dictionary of trees is boosting with shrinkage. Coordinate descent with warm starts is the fast general-purpose solver.
  • The family shares the trick: the Dantzig selector bounds the correlations directly, the group lasso selects whole groups, the fused lasso makes ordered coefficients piecewise constant, and L1 logistic regression selects features for classification.
  • The lasso is biased on what it keeps and fragile among near-twin inputs; relaxed or adaptive versions fix the bias. Bet on sparsity anyway: when the truth is dense, nothing does well; when it is sparse, L1 wins.

Check your understanding

With orthonormal inputs and λ = 1, what do best subset, ridge and the lasso give for β̂ = 1.2?

Best subset: 1.2 is below √2 ≈ 1.41, so 0. Ridge: 1.2/2 = 0.6. Lasso: 1.2 − 1 = 0.2. Only the lasso keeps a small, shrunk coefficient.

Why do all active correlations fall at the same rate in LAR?

The direction d𝒜 = (X𝒜TX𝒜)−1s𝒜 is built so that X𝒜TX𝒜d𝒜 = s𝒜: moving γ along it changes every active correlation by exactly γ in the direction that shrinks its size.

When do the LAR and lasso paths differ, and which rule fixes it?

When an active coefficient reaches zero. LAR lets it change sign while its correlation keeps the old sign, which violates the lasso's KKT conditions. The lasso modification removes the variable from the active set at that point and recomputes the direction.

The Dantzig selector at s and the lasso at λ = s: which has the smaller L1 norm, and why?

The Dantzig selector (or a tie). The lasso solution satisfies the constraint |xjTr|/n ≤ s by its KKT conditions, so it is a feasible point; the Dantzig selector is the feasible point with the smallest L1 norm.

Your spectra have 100 nearly identical neighbouring channels, and the lasso picks 12 scattered ones. Name two penalties that would give a more stable answer.

The fused lasso (penalise differences between neighbouring coefficients, giving bands), or the elastic net (an added ridge term spreads weight across correlated channels). A group lasso over pre-defined bands would also work.
