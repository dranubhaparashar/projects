Memory for Agents

A model remembers nothing between calls; an agent remembers whatever it puts back in the context

A language model is stateless. Each call starts from a blank slate, and everything the agent “remembers” has to be pasted back into the prompt, within a fixed budget of tokens. So memory is an engineering choice: what to keep word for word, what to compress, what to file away and look up, and what to forget. Every cell in the figure is one turn of a real five-month conversation, 19 sessions from May to October 2023, sized by its length in tokens. The idea

The context is the only memory

Chat with an assistant for an hour and it seems to remember you. It does not: the model’s weights are fixed, and between two calls nothing about you is kept inside it. What looks like memory is the program around the model sending the whole conversation again, every time. The agent-design literature (Sumers et al., 2024) calls the prompt the agent’s working memory; anything kept outside it, a log of past episodes or a list of facts, is long-term memory, and it only helps when something brings it back into the prompt.

Re-sending everything has two costs. Each call carries the whole history, so the total number of tokens processed grows with the square of the conversation’s length. And every model has a hard ceiling, its context window: GPT-2, whose tokenizer this page uses, could read 1,024 tokens. Slide through the conversation and change the ceiling to see when the history stops fitting.

\[ \begin{aligned} n_{\text{call}}(T) &= \sum_{t=1}^{T} n_t \\ N_{\text{total}}(T) &= \sum_{u=1}^{T} n_{\text{call}}(u) \\ &\approx \bar n\,\frac{T(T+1)}{2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: nt is the number of tokens in turn t (plus a one-line heading for each session). If the agent calls the model at every turn and sends the full history, call T carries all T turns so far. Add up every call and you get Ntotal: with an average turn of n̄ tokens that is about n̄ × T(T + 1)/2, which grows with T². Doubling the conversation roughly quadruples the bill.
Say it“The call at turn T carries the sum of all the turns so far; the total over the conversation is about n-bar times T times T plus one, over two.”

The unit of the budget

Counting in tokens: byte-pair encoding

A context budget is counted in tokens, not words or characters, so a memory system has to count them exactly. Most models cut text with byte-pair encoding (BPE; Sennrich, Haddow & Birch, 2016). The text is first split into word-like pieces, then every piece starts as a sequence of single bytes, and a fixed, learned list of merges is applied: at each step, the adjacent pair that ranks highest on the list (the pair that was most frequent in the tokenizer’s training text) is fused into one token. Common words end up as one token; rare names, numbers and emoji break into several. This page runs the real GPT-2 tokenizer (Radford et al., 2019): its 256 byte tokens and all 50,000 merges, loaded into the page and applied in your browser. Type anything, then step through the merges one by one.

\[ \begin{aligned} (a,b)^\star &= \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{(a,b)\ \text{adjacent}} \text{rank}(a,b) \\ \text{chars/token} &= n_{\text{chars}} / n_{\text{tok}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: among all neighbouring pairs of tokens in a piece, find the one with the smallest rank in the merge list (rank 0 was learned first), replace every occurrence of it by the merged token, and repeat until no neighbouring pair is on the list. The second line is the usual rule of thumb in reverse: English prose runs at roughly four characters per token, but the real count is the only one the model’s limit respects.

Policy 1 · keep the recent past

The sliding window: keep the most recent turns

The simplest memory is a sliding window: when the history is too long, drop the oldest turns until it fits. It is what most chat front-ends do, and it is often right: the last few turns are usually what the next reply depends on. Its failure is just as plain: anything said before the window is gone, however important. The question set measures this directly. Each of the 150 questions was written by the LoCoMo authors together with the turns that contain its answer (its evidence), so we can ask, for any memory policy, whether all of a question’s evidence made it into the context. A model may also fail to use a fact that is in a long context, especially one buried in the middle (Liu et al., 2024); recall here is the ceiling, what the best possible reader could use. Slide the budget and pick questions from early and late sessions.

\[ \begin{aligned} s^\star(B) &= \min\big\{ s : c(s) \le B \big\} \\ c(s) &= \textstyle\sum_{t=s}^{T} n_t + h(s) \\ \text{recall}(B) &= \frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q\in Q} \mathbb{1}\big[E_q \subseteq \text{ctx}\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: c(s) is the cost of keeping turns s to T: their tokens plus h(s), the headings of the sessions they span. The window starts at the earliest turn s☆ whose cost fits in B tokens. A question q counts as recalled only if its whole evidence set Eq is in the context (ctx); a multi-hop question needs every piece. Recall is the share of the 150 questions for which that holds.
Say it“The window starts at the earliest turn whose suffix fits in B; recall is the share of questions whose evidence is all inside.”

Policy 2 · compress the past

Rolling summaries: squeeze old turns into fewer tokens

Instead of dropping old turns, compress them. A rolling summary keeps the recent turns verbatim and a summary of everything older, refreshed as each session ends: the new summary is made from the old summary plus the session that just finished (Wang et al., 2023; MemGPT, Packer et al., 2023, does something similar when its context fills up). The budget is split between the two: M tokens for the summary, the rest for the window. Two summarisers are compared here. Pick whole turns runs in your browser: it keeps the most informative turns, scored by the rarity of their words, and drops the rest. LLM-written facts uses the 184 short observations the LoCoMo authors generated with an LLM, one sentence each, each linked to the turn it came from; here the same rule picks which facts to keep. Compare the two summarisers at the same budget, then change the share given to the summary.

\[ \begin{aligned} S_j &= \text{Compress}_M\big(S_{j-1} \cup D_j\big) \\ \text{context} &= S \;\cup\; W_{B-M} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Dj is what session j adds (turns, or facts); Sj is the summary after it. CompressM keeps the most informative items, best first, skipping any that no longer fit in M tokens. At question time the context is the summary of everything before the window plus the window itself, W, which gets the remaining B − M tokens. The compression ratio ρ is tokens in over tokens out.
Say it“The new summary is the old summary plus the new session, compressed to M tokens; the context is the summary plus a window of B minus M.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{info}(x) &= \sum_{t \in \text{terms}(x)} \text{idf}(t) \\ \text{idf}(t) &= \ln\frac{1+N}{1+n_t} + 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the information score of a turn or fact x adds up the idf of its distinct content words (lower-cased, stop words removed, plurals stemmed). N is the number of items and nt how many contain term t, so words that appear everywhere (“thanks”, the speakers’ names) count little and rare ones (“Sweden”, “clarinet”) count a lot. It is a crude, transparent stand-in for an LLM judging what is worth keeping.
Say it“Info of x is the sum of the idf of its terms; idf is the log of one plus N over one plus n-t, plus one.”

Policy 3 · file everything, look it up

Vector-store memory: write everything, retrieve what is relevant

A window and a summary decide what to keep before anyone knows the next question. A vector store postpones the decision. Every turn is written to an external store as a vector; when a question arrives, it is turned into a vector too, and the k most similar memories are pasted into the context. This is retrieval-augmented generation (chapter 4.9) pointed at the agent’s own past: episodic memory if the store holds raw turns, semantic memory if it holds extracted facts. Production systems embed text with a neural encoder; this page uses TF-IDF vectors, built in your browser, which match words rather than meaning. Every memory keeps its session heading, so the model can still see the date. Pick a question and move k; then switch the store from turns to facts.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf x_i &= \frac{\mathbf{tf}_i \odot \mathbf{idf}}{\Vert \mathbf{tf}_i \odot \mathbf{idf} \Vert} \\ \cos(q, i) &= \mathbf x_q \cdot \mathbf x_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each memory i becomes a vector with one entry per term: how often the term occurs (tf), times its idf, scaled to length 1. The question gets the same treatment, and because both have length 1 their dot product is the cosine of the angle between them: 1 for the same words in the same proportions, 0 for no word in common. The store returns the k memories with the largest cosine.
Say it“x-i is tf times idf, normalised to unit length; similarity is the dot product of the question vector and the memory vector.”

Retrieval, refined

Recency, importance and relevance

Similarity is not the only reason to recall something. The “generative agents” of Park et al. (2023), simulated villagers who planned parties and remembered gossip, scored every memory on three things and added them up: relevance to the current situation, importance (an LLM rated each memory from 1, mundane, to 10, poignant) and recency, which decays exponentially with the time since the memory was last used. Each part is rescaled to 0–1 across all memories before the weighted sum. Here relevance is the TF-IDF cosine, importance is the information score from above (standing in for the LLM’s rating), and time is real: the sessions’ dates, with questions asked after the last one. Move the three weights and the decay, and watch which memories rise.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{score}(m) = {} & \alpha_{\text{rel}}\,\widehat{\cos}(q,m) \\ & + \alpha_{\text{imp}}\,\widehat{\text{info}}(m) \\ & + \alpha_{\text{rec}}\,\widehat{\gamma^{\,\Delta t_m}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Δtm is the number of days since memory m was made; γ < 1 is the decay per day, so γΔt shrinks the older the memory is. The hat means min–max scaled over all 419 memories, so 1 is the best memory on that criterion and 0 the worst. The αs say how much each criterion counts; the top k by score go into the context.
Say it“The score is alpha-rel times scaled relevance plus alpha-imp times scaled importance plus alpha-rec times scaled gamma to the delta-t.”

Policy 4 · forgetting on purpose

Forgetting on purpose

A store that keeps everything forever is not free. It costs storage and search time, every irrelevant memory is a distractor that can push a relevant one out of the top k, stale facts (“I live in Sweden”, two moves ago) contradict new ones, and people have a right to have their data erased. So memory systems forget deliberately, with a capacity N and an eviction rule. First in, first out drops the oldest; least recently used drops what has not been recalled for longest. MemoryBank (Zhong et al., 2024) borrows Ebbinghaus’s forgetting curve: a memory’s retention fades exponentially, and each time it is recalled it is refreshed and fades more slowly. Or simply evict the least informative memory. In this simulation the turns arrive in order; before each is stored, it is used as a cue, and the 3 most similar memories count as recalled. A write filter can also refuse to store low-information turns at all. Shrink the store and race the four rules.

\[ \begin{aligned} R_m(t) &= e^{-(t - t_m)/S_m} \\ \text{on recall:}\quad t_m &\leftarrow t \\ S_m &\leftarrow S_m + 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: tm is when memory m was last written or recalled, in days, and Sm its strength, 1 when it is new. Its retention R starts at 1 and decays with time; a recall resets the clock and adds 1 to the strength, so the next decay is slower. When the store holds more than N memories, the one with the lowest retention is evicted. (FIFO evicts the smallest write time, LRU the smallest tm.)
Say it“Retention is e to the minus time since last use over strength; each recall resets the time and adds one to the strength.”

Real-world project

Project: a memory simulator for a five-month conversation

The conversations come from LoCoMo (Maharana et al., 2024), a public benchmark for very long-term conversational memory released by Snap Research under CC BY-NC 4.0. Each of its ten conversations follows two people over months of sessions; the dialogues were generated by LLM agents with given personas and life events, then checked and edited by human annotators, who also wrote the questions and marked their evidence turns. Photos shared in the chat appear as their captions. This page uses two of the ten: Caroline & Melanie (conv-26, used in every lab above) and Jon & Gina (conv-30). Adversarial questions (category 5, designed to have no answer) and two questions with no evidence are left out. Every token below is counted by the GPT-2 tokenizer in your browser; every number is recomputed live and checked against a separate Python implementation.

2 · The race: six memories, one budget

Each policy gets the same budget B for its memory, in GPT-2 tokens, session headings included. Recent window: the newest turns. Rolling summary + window: half the budget for a rolling summary that picks whole turns, half for the window. Rolling fact summary + window: the same with the LLM-written facts. Vector store: the most similar turns (or facts) to the question until the budget is full. Window + vector store: half the budget for the newest turns, the rest filled from older turns by similarity.

import json, re, math, datetime as dt, numpy as np
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
L = json.load(open("mm_dl/locomo10.json", encoding="utf8"))
enc = json.load(open("mm_dl/encoder.json", encoding="utf8"))
ranks = {tuple(l.split()): i for i, l in enumerate(open("mm_dl/vocab.bpe", encoding="utf8").read().split("\n")[1:-1])}
stop = set(json.load(open("rg-stop.json", encoding="utf8")))

def bytes_to_unicode():                       # GPT-2's byte <-> printable-character table
    bs = list(range(33, 127)) + list(range(161, 173)) + list(range(174, 256)); cs = bs[:]; n = 0
    for b in range(256):
        if b not in bs: bs.append(b); cs.append(256 + n); n += 1
    return dict(zip(bs, map(chr, cs)))
B2U = bytes_to_unicode()
PAT = re.compile(r"""'s|'t|'re|'ve|'m|'ll|'d| ?[^\W\d_]+| ?\d+| ?(?:[^\s\w]|_)+|\s+(?!\S)|\s+""")
cache = {}
def bpe(piece):                               # the merge loop of OpenAI's encoder.py
    if piece in cache: return cache[piece]
    w = [B2U[b] for b in piece.encode("utf8")]
    while len(w) > 1:
        pair = min(zip(w, w[1:]), key=lambda p: ranks.get(p, 1e18))
        if pair not in ranks: break
        out, i = [], 0
        while i < len(w):
            if i < len(w) - 1 and (w[i], w[i + 1]) == pair: out.append(w[i] + w[i + 1]); i += 2
            else: out.append(w[i]); i += 1
        w = out
    cache[piece] = w; return w
def encode(t): return [enc[x] for p in PAT.findall(t) for x in bpe(p)]
assert encode("Hello world") == [15496, 995]

def stem(w):                                  # Harman S-stemmer, as in chapter 4.9
    if len(w) <= 3: return w
    if w.endswith("ies") and not w.endswith(("eies", "aies")): return w[:-3] + "y"
    if w.endswith("es") and not w.endswith(("aes", "ees", "oes")): return w[:-1]
    if w.endswith("s") and not w.endswith(("us", "ss")): return w[:-1]
    return w
def terms(t): return [stem(w) for w in re.findall(r"[^\W_]+", t.lower()) if w not in stop and not (len(w) == 1 and w.isalpha())]
norm = lambda s: re.sub(r"\s+", " ", str(s)).strip()
def days(s):
    h, m, ap, d, mon, y = re.match(r"(\d+):(\d+) (am|pm) on (\d+) (\w+), (\d+)", s).groups()
    t = dt.datetime(int(y), dt.datetime.strptime(mon, "%B").month, int(d), int(h) % 12 + (12 if ap == "pm" else 0), int(m))
    return (t - dt.datetime(1970, 1, 1)).total_seconds() / 86400

def build(c):
    cv = c["conversation"]; names = [cv["speaker_a"], cv["speaker_b"]]
    keys = sorted((k for k in cv if re.fullmatch(r"session_\d+", k)), key=lambda k: int(k.split("_")[1]))
    T, hdr = [], []
    for s, k in enumerate(keys):
        hdr.append(len(encode(f"## Session {s + 1} ({cv[k + '_date_time']})\n"))); t0 = days(cv[k + "_date_time"])
        for j, x in enumerate(cv[k]):
            cap = f" [shares {norm(x['blip_caption'])}]" if x.get("blip_caption") else ""
            line = f"{x['speaker']}: {norm(x['text'])}{cap}\n"
            T.append(dict(k=len(T), s=s, id=f"{s + 1}:{j + 1}", line=line, n=len(encode(line)), time=t0 + j / 1440))
    by = {t["id"]: t["k"] for t in T}
    ev = lambda e: [by[x.lstrip("D")] for x in re.split(r"[\s;,]+", " ".join(e if isinstance(e, list) else [e])) if x and x.lstrip("D") in by]
    F = []
    for s, k in enumerate(keys):
        for sp, lst in c["observation"].get(k + "_observation", {}).items():
            for text, e in lst:
                e = ev(e)
                if e: F.append(dict(k=len(F), s=s, line=f"- {norm(text)}\n", ev=e, last=max(e))); F[-1]["n"] = len(encode(F[-1]["line"]))
    Q = [dict(q=norm(q["question"]), ev=ev(q["evidence"]), cat=q["category"]) for q in c["qa"] if q["category"] != 5]
    Q = [q for q in Q if q["ev"]]
    for items, name in ((T, "vt"), (F, "vf")):  # TF-IDF store: smooth idf, l2 norm
        V = TfidfVectorizer(tokenizer=terms, lowercase=False, token_pattern=None).fit([x["line"] for x in items])
        X = V.transform([x["line"] for x in items]); idf = V.idf_
        for i, x in enumerate(items): x["imp"] = idf[X[i].indices].sum()
        for q in Q: q[name] = (X @ V.transform([q["q"]]).T).toarray().ravel()
        if name == "vt": XT = X
    return dict(T=T, F=F, Q=Q, hdr=hdr, XT=XT)

def fill(C, order, B, st=None):               # greedy: skip what does not fit; a session heading is paid once
    used, sess, picked = (st["used"], set(st["sess"]), list(st["picked"])) if st else (0, set(), [])
    for it in order:
        c = it["n"] + (0 if it["s"] in sess else C["hdr"][it["s"]])
        if used + c <= B: used += c; sess.add(it["s"]); picked.append(it)
    return dict(used=used, sess=sess, picked=picked)
def window(C, B):                             # newest first, stop at the first turn that does not fit
    used, sess, picked, start = 0, set(), [], len(C["T"])
    for it in reversed(C["T"]):
        c = it["n"] + (0 if it["s"] in sess else C["hdr"][it["s"]])
        if used + c > B: break
        used += c; sess.add(it["s"]); picked.append(it); start = it["k"]
    return dict(used=used, sess=sess, picked=picked, start=start)
def rolling(C, B, M, kind):
    w = window(C, B - M); S = []
    for s in range(len(C["hdr"])):
        fresh = [f for f in C["F"] if f["s"] == s and f["last"] < w["start"]] if kind == "facts" else [t for t in C["T"] if t["s"] == s and t["k"] < w["start"]]
        if fresh: S = fill(C, sorted(S + fresh, key=lambda x: (-x["imp"], -x["k"])), M)["picked"]
    return w["picked"] + S
order = lambda sc: np.lexsort((np.arange(len(sc)), -sc))
def policy(C, name, q, B):
    if name == "window": return window(C, B)["picked"]
    if name == "sumT": return rolling(C, B, B // 2, "turns")
    if name == "sumF": return rolling(C, B, B // 2, "facts")
    if name == "vecT": return fill(C, [C["T"][i] for i in order(q["vt"])], B)["picked"]
    if name == "vecF": return fill(C, [C["F"][i] for i in order(q["vf"])], B)["picked"]
    w = window(C, B // 2)                     # "hyb": window + vector store over older turns
    return fill(C, [C["T"][i] for i in order(q["vt"]) if i < w["start"]], B, w)["picked"]
def recalled(q, items):
    cov = {e for it in items for e in it.get("ev", [it["k"]])}
    return all(e in cov for e in q["ev"])

def forget(C, pol, N, theta=0, r=3, K=10):    # a store with room for N turns, written turn by turn
    T = C["T"]; P = (C["XT"] @ C["XT"].T).toarray(); store = []
    for i, t in enumerate(T):
        if store and r:
            sc = [P[i, m["k"]] for m in store]
            for j in sorted((j for j in range(len(store)) if sc[j] > 0), key=lambda j: (-sc[j], store[j]["k"]))[:r]:
                store[j]["last"] = t["time"]; store[j]["S"] += 1
        if t["imp"] >= theta: store.append(dict(k=i, last=t["time"], S=1))
        while len(store) > N:
            key = {"fifo": lambda m: (m["k"],), "lru": lambda m: (m["last"], m["k"]),
                   "decay": lambda m: (math.exp(-(t["time"] - m["last"]) / m["S"]), m["k"]),
                   "imp": lambda m: (T[m["k"]]["imp"], m["k"])}[pol]
            store.remove(min(store, key=key))
    kept = {m["k"] for m in store}
    stored = sum(all(e in kept for e in q["ev"]) for q in C["Q"])
    top = sum(all(e in set([i for i in order(q["vt"]) if i in kept][:K]) for e in q["ev"]) for q in C["Q"])
    return stored, top

for ci in (0, 1):

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A model is stateless: an agent’s memory is whatever it puts back into the context, and re-sending everything costs tokens that grow with the square of the conversation.
  • Budgets are counted in tokens, so count them with the model’s own tokenizer; BPE makes common words cheap and names, numbers and emoji dear.
  • A sliding window is perfect for the recent past and blind to everything before it; a summary buys more past per token, and how much depends on how well the summariser compresses.
  • A vector store keeps everything and decides at question time; it wins on facts from long ago, but only if the question shares enough with the memory to find it.
  • Forget on purpose, with a rule you have measured: on this conversation, keeping the most informative memories beats keeping the most recent or the most reused.

Check your understanding

A chatbot “remembers” your name from earlier in the chat. Where is that memory stored?

In the prompt. The application re-sends the earlier turns (or a summary, or retrieved notes) with every call; the model’s weights do not change between calls. Remove the name from what is sent and the model no longer knows it.

Why does re-sending the full history make a long conversation so expensive?

Call T carries all T turns so far, so the total over a conversation of T turns is about n̄ × T(T + 1)/2 tokens: quadratic in T. Doubling the length roughly quadruples the tokens processed, even before the context limit is hit.

Why is “a token is about four characters” not good enough for a memory system?

Because the limit is hard and the ratio varies: names, numbers, code, emoji and non-English text use many more tokens per character. A memory that overshoots its budget gets truncated by the API, often silently and from the wrong end. Counting with the model’s own tokenizer gives the exact figure.

A rolling summary that keeps whole turns barely beats a plain window here, while LLM-written facts do much better. Why?

Keeping whole turns compresses only by dropping turns, and the most informative turns are also the longest, so few fit. Rewriting a turn into a one-sentence fact keeps its point at a fraction of the tokens, so many more facts fit in the same M. The price is that facts the extractor never wrote down are lost for good.

Why might adding a recency term to retrieval hurt on LoCoMo but help in a coding assistant?

LoCoMo asks about events spread over five months, so a bias towards recent memories pushes old evidence out of the top k. A coding assistant mostly needs what happened in the last few steps, where recency is a good prior. The right weights depend on what questions the agent will face, so measure them.
