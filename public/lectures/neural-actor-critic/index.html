The Brain’s Actor‑Critic

Hedonistic neurons, three-factor synapses, and addiction as a prediction error that refuses to go away

The previous chapter argued that the phasic firing of dopamine neurons looks like a TD error. This one asks what the rest of the circuit would have to be for that to make sense, and finds an answer that was written down before the dopamine data arrived: an actor-critic, with the critic in the ventral striatum, the actor in the dorsal striatum, dopamine broadcasting one error to both, and the weights stored at cortico-striatal synapses that learn by a three-factor rule. The circuit · 15.7

The actor-critic in the basal ganglia

An actor-critic is two learners sharing one teacher. The critic maintains a value estimate v̂(s, w) and, after every transition, computes the TD error δ = R + γv̂(S′) − v̂(S): the difference between what just happened plus what is now expected and what was expected a moment ago. The actor maintains a policy π(a | s, θ) and nudges the probability of whatever it just did up when δ is positive and down when it is negative. The critic uses the same δ to correct itself. Neither learner needs to know the other’s internals; the only thing passed between them is one scalar per time step. (The policy-gradient chapter derives the method; here we take it as given and ask where it would live.)

The proposal, due to Barto (1995) and to Houk, Adams and Barto (1995), is that the striatum, the input stage of the basal ganglia, holds both learners. Cortical neurons carry the state: a pattern of activity over tens of thousands of axons, the feature vector x(s). They synapse massively onto striatal medium spiny neurons. Spiny neurons of the ventral striatum (the nucleus accumbens and its neighbours) play the critic, pooling cortical input into a value; spiny neurons of the dorsal striatum play the actor, and their output, via the pallidum and thalamus, biases which movement or plan the cortex commits to. The dopamine neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta and the ventral tegmental area receive value-related input from the ventral striatum and reward-related input from elsewhere, and their phasic output is δ. Each dopamine axon branches into an enormous tree and releases onto hundreds of thousands of cortico-striatal synapses at once: the ideal anatomy for a broadcast of one number to every weight. The weights themselves are the strengths of those cortico-striatal synapses, whose plasticity is known to depend on dopamine.

The order of events is worth knowing. The actor-critic architecture dates from Barto, Sutton and Anderson’s 1983 pole-balancer, where the critic was called the adaptive critic element and the actor the associative search element. The basal-ganglia mapping was written down in 1995, in two chapters of the same MIT Press volume on basal-ganglia models, at a time when Schultz’s recordings from dopamine neurons were known but had not yet been read as a prediction error; Montague, Dayan and Sejnowski’s TD interpretation of those recordings came in 1996 and Schultz, Dayan and Montague’s synthesis in 1997. So the circuit was proposed first and the signal was identified afterwards, which is the right way round for a theory.

Three features of the mapping do real work. First, the critic’s output must reach the dopamine neurons with a minus sign and the next value with a plus sign and a delay; striatal projections to the dopamine neurons are inhibitory, which gives the first half for free, while the second needs a timed pathway that is still argued over. Second, dopamine must change synapses that were active before it arrived, which forces an eligibility trace at the synapse (section 4). Third, actor synapses and critic synapses need traces of different kinds, because the actor’s learning rule depends on what the postsynaptic neuron did and the critic’s does not (section 2). What the mapping predicts, and what was found

A mapping earns its keep by predicting things that were not used to build it. The actor-critic mapping was proposed in 1995 on anatomical and computational grounds; most of the tests came later.

  • Dopamine should look like δIt should burst for unpredicted reward, stay flat for predicted reward, dip when a predicted reward is omitted, and move backwards to the earliest predictive cue as learning proceeds. This is the reward-prediction-error story of the previous chapter, and it was the first and still the strongest piece of evidence.
  • The critic should learn without actions; the actor only with themO’Doherty and colleagues (2004) scanned people in two versions of a task with the same cues and rewards, one in which they chose and one in which they only watched. A prediction-error signal appeared in the ventral striatum in both versions, and in the dorsal striatum only when choices were being learned. That is the actor-critic split drawn on a human brain.
  • Cortico-striatal plasticity should need dopamine, and need it lateReynolds, Hyland and Wickens (2001) found that potentiation of cortico-striatal synapses in the rat depended on dopamine, and that its size predicted how fast the animal learned to self-stimulate. Yagishita and colleagues (2014) then showed the timing: dopamine must arrive within about two seconds after the pre-post pairing, which is what an eligibility trace requires (section 4).
  • Ventral and dorsal lesions should break different thingsDamage to the ventral striatum impairs learning driven by Pavlovian cue values (conditioned reinforcement, Pavlovian-instrumental transfer) while leaving simple stimulus-response learning intact; damage to the dorsolateral striatum does the reverse, preventing the formation of habits (Yin and Knowlton, 2006). The pattern fits a critic that values and an actor that acts, with the qualification of section 9 that the dorsal striatum is itself divided between habit and goal-directed control.
Learning rules · 15.8

Actor and critic learning rules with eligibility traces

Write both learners as linear-in-features units sitting on the same cortical input. The critic is one unit with weights w; the actor is a set of units, one per action, with weights θa, and its policy is a softmax over the preferences h(s, a) = θa⊤x(s). Both learn with eligibility traces: a vector, one entry per weight, that is bumped whenever the weight’s input is active and otherwise decays by γλ each step. When δ arrives, every weight moves in proportion to δ times its own trace. That is TD(λ) for the critic and the eligibility-trace form of the policy-gradient actor for the actor; the eligibility-traces chapter explains why the trace is the right memory of the past.

The difference between the two traces is the point of the section. The critic’s trace increment is just the input, x(St): a synapse becomes eligible because its presynaptic axon fired, regardless of what the critic unit itself did. Sutton and Barto call this a non-contingent eligibility trace. The actor’s increment is the gradient of the log-probability of the action taken, and for a softmax unit that is x(St) times (1 if this unit’s action was chosen, else 0) minus the probability it would have been chosen: presynaptic activity times (postsynaptic activity minus its expectation). A synapse onto the actor becomes eligible only when its axon fired and the postsynaptic neuron did something other than what it usually does. That is a contingent trace, and it is what lets a shared scalar δ teach each actor unit something specific: “you fired more than usual and things went better than expected, so fire more next time.”

The timing matters as much as the sign. The reward that justifies a choice arrives after the choice, often by a second or more; the postsynaptic spike that must be credited is long over. Without a memory at the synapse, dopamine could only ever strengthen whatever happened to be active at the moment it arrived. The trace is that memory: it rises at the moment of pre-and-post activity and decays over a timescale set by γλ (in the algorithm) or by the kinetics of a molecular tag (in the synapse). One update, by hand, through the brain

Take the smallest circuit that has everything: two cortical features, one critic unit, two actor units (“left” and “right”), one dopamine neuron. The animal is in a state where both cortical axons are active, x(St) = (1, 1); it chooses left; it lands in a state where only the second axon is active, x(St+1) = (0, 1), and receives a reward of 1. The critic’s synapses are w = (0.4, 0.2); the left unit’s are θL = (0.3, −0.1) and the right unit’s θR = (0.1, 0.2). Discount γ = 0.9, trace decay λ = 0.8, both step sizes 0.1. The critic’s trace from the previous step was zw = (0.5, 0); the actor’s traces were zero (this was the first choice of the episode). Follow the six steps, then change the numbers underneath.

The update, step by step

  1. 1 · What the critic predicted, before and after
    \( \hat v(S_t) = 0.4\cdot 1 + 0.2\cdot 1 = 0.6, \qquad \hat v(S_{t+1}) = 0.4\cdot 0 + 0.2\cdot 1 = 0.2 \)
    how exactly?
    The critic unit’s firing rate is the weighted sum of its inputs. In St both axons fire, so both synapses contribute: 0.4 + 0.2. In St+1 only the second axon fires, so only the 0.2 synapse contributes. In the brain these are two successive firing rates of the ventral striatal population, a moment apart.
  2. 2 · The dopamine signal
    \( \delta = R + \gamma\,\hat v(S_{t+1}) - \hat v(S_t) = 1 + 0.9\times 0.2 - 0.6 = 0.58 \)
    how exactly?
    What happened (reward 1) plus what is still expected (0.9 × 0.2 = 0.18) comes to 1.18; what was expected a step ago was 0.6. The surplus, 0.58, is a burst of dopamine above baseline. Had the reward been 0.42 the burst would have been zero: fully predicted, nothing to learn.
  3. 3 · The critic’s eligibility trace
    \( \mathbf z^{\mathbf w} = \gamma\lambda\,\mathbf z^{\mathbf w}_{\text{prev}} + \mathbf x(S_t) = 0.72\,(0.5,\,0) + (1,\,1) = (1.36,\;1) \)
    how exactly?
    γλ = 0.9 × 0.8 = 0.72. The old trace (0.5, 0) decays to (0.36, 0), and both synapses are bumped by 1 because both axons fired in St. Synapse 1 is more eligible than synapse 2 because it was also active a step earlier. Note that the critic unit’s own output played no part: this is the non-contingent trace.
  4. 4 · The critic’s synapses change
    \( \Delta\mathbf w = \alpha\,\delta\,\mathbf z^{\mathbf w} = 0.1\times 0.58\times(1.36,\;1) = (0.0789,\;0.0580) \)
    how exactly?
    0.058 × 1.36 = 0.0789 and 0.058 × 1 = 0.0580, so w becomes (0.4789, 0.2580). The first cortico-striatal synapse is potentiated more because it carried a bigger trace, not because it mattered more for this reward. Predicted value of St is now 0.7369: the critic has moved 0.1369 of the way toward the 1.18 it should have said.
  5. 5 · The actor’s choice and its contingent trace
    \( h_L = 0.2,\ h_R = 0.3,\quad \pi(L) = \tfrac{1}{1 + e^{0.1}} = 0.4750, \quad \nabla_{\theta_L}\ln\pi(L) = (1 - 0.4750)(1,1) = (0.525,\,0.525) \)
    how exactly?
    Preferences: hL = 0.3 − 0.1 = 0.2 and hR = 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.3, so right was slightly favoured and left was the less likely choice (probability 0.475). The animal chose left anyway. The left unit’s trace increment is (fired = 1, minus expected 0.475) × input = 0.525 on each of its synapses. The right unit did not fire but was expected to with probability 0.525, so its increment is (0 − 0.525) × (1, 1) = (−0.525, −0.525). The previous traces were zero, so these increments are the traces.
  6. 6 · The actor’s synapses change
    \( \Delta\boldsymbol\theta_L = 0.1\times 0.58\times(0.525,\,0.525) = (0.0304,\,0.0304), \quad \Delta\boldsymbol\theta_R = (-0.0304,\,-0.0304) \)
    how exactly?
    The same dopamine burst, 0.58, reaches both actor populations. On the left unit it meets a positive trace and potentiates: θL = (0.3304, −0.0696). On the right unit it meets a negative trace and depresses: θR = (0.0696, 0.1696). New preferences in St: hL = 0.2609, hR = 0.2391, so π(L) rises from 0.475 to 0.5054. One better-than-expected outcome has flipped a slight aversion into a slight preference.

Reading the same six lines as physiology. Two successive readings of a ventral striatal firing rate (step 1) are compared, with the reward, at the dopamine neurons (step 2). A burst follows. In the ventral striatum it finds synapses tagged by recent cortical input, with the tag strongest where input has been sustained (step 3), and strengthens them in proportion (step 4). In the dorsal striatum it finds a different kind of tag: on the population that fired against the odds, a positive tag; on the population that stayed silent when it was expected to fire, a negative one (step 5). The one burst potentiates the first and depresses the second (step 6). No synapse needed to know what the other synapses were doing; the only shared information was the burst.

The softmax over two units is a logistic unit, so this calculation is also the Bernoulli-logistic unit of Williams (1992) that reappears in sections 5 and 6: output 1 with probability σ(hL − hR), trace increment (output − probability) × input.

Had the animal chosen right instead

contingency The right unit’s trace would be (1 − 0.525)(1, 1) = (0.475, 0.475) and the left unit’s (−0.475, −0.475): smaller marks, because right was the expected choice. The same burst of 0.58 (if the same reward followed) would then move π(R) from 0.525 to 0.550. An expected action that pays earns less credit than a surprising one that pays; that is the (Y − p) factor at work.

Had there been no actor at all

critic only Steps 1 to 4 are a complete Pavlovian learner: cue values updated by dopamine with non-contingent traces, the TD model of conditioning of the psychology chapter. Everything the actor adds is steps 5 and 6, and the only extra ingredient they need is a postsynaptic term in the trace.

Synapses · three-factor rules

Three-factor rules and dopamine-gated STDP

Hebb’s rule has two factors: a synapse strengthens when the presynaptic and the postsynaptic neuron are active together. Its modern, spike-level form is spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP): if the presynaptic spike arrives a few tens of milliseconds before the postsynaptic spike the synapse potentiates, and if it arrives just after it depresses, with the size of the change falling off exponentially with the gap. Two-factor rules can learn correlations and nothing else. They have no way to know whether the correlation was worth learning, and no way to learn from a reward that arrives a second after the spikes.

A three-factor rule adds a neuromodulator. The pairing of pre- and postsynaptic activity does not change the synapse directly; it sets a short-lived eligibility trace at the synapse (a “flag” or “tag”), which decays over a second or so. The weight changes only if the third factor, a pulse of dopamine (or another modulator), arrives while the flag is still up, and it changes by the product of the two. Frémaux and Gerstner (2016) review this family and show that neuromodulated STDP is its natural spiking form: the STDP window decides the sign and size of the flag, the flag decays, and the modulator reads it out. Izhikevich (2007) had shown earlier that such a mechanism solves what he called the distal-reward problem: with thousands of synapses active every second, a dopamine pulse arriving late can still credit the right ones, because only the ones whose pre-post pairing was recent and in the right order carry a flag.

This is the actor’s rule of section 2 drawn at the level of a single synapse. Pre-before-post is “this input helped make the neuron fire”, the flag is the contingent eligibility trace, and dopamine is δ. The experimental support is direct in the striatum: Reynolds, Hyland and Wickens (2001) showed that potentiation of cortico-striatal synapses requires dopamine, and Yagishita and colleagues (2014) showed in spiny neurons of the nucleus accumbens that a dopamine pulse enlarges a stimulated spine only if it arrives in a window of roughly 0.3 to 2 seconds after the glutamate-plus-spike pairing; earlier or later, nothing. That window is the lifetime of the flag. Move the pre-post gap, the dopamine delay and the flag’s time constant and read off the resulting weight change.

\[ \begin{aligned} W(\Delta t) &= \begin{cases} A_+\,e^{-\Delta t/\tau_+} & \Delta t > 0 \ \ (\text{pre before post}) \\ -A_-\,e^{\,\Delta t/\tau_-} & \Delta t < 0 \ \ (\text{post before pre}) \end{cases} \\ \frac{de}{dt} &= -\frac{e}{\tau_e} + W(\Delta t)\,\text{(at each pre-post pairing)} \\ \frac{dw}{dt} &= \eta\,M(t)\,e(t), \qquad M(t) = \text{dopamine above baseline} \approx \delta \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: W is the STDP window: a positive bump of height A+ for pre-then-post pairs that fades with time constant τ+ (about 15 to 20 ms), a negative one for post-then-pre. A pairing does not touch the weight; it adds W(Δt) to the flag e, which otherwise decays with time constant τe (hundreds of milliseconds to a few seconds). The weight changes at rate η times the modulator times the flag, so the total change from one pairing and one dopamine pulse at delay d is η × W(Δt) × e−d/τe × (pulse size). Three factors: pre (the axon’s spike), post (the neuron’s spike, through W), modulator (M).

From spikes back to the actor’s rule

The STDP window is a convenient way to write “pre caused post”, but the actor of section 2 needs something slightly different: pre times (post minus what post was expected to be). Frémaux and Gerstner show that this is exactly what falls out when the policy-gradient derivation is redone for a neuron that emits spikes stochastically. If the neuron fires as a Poisson process whose rate ρ(t) is a smooth function of its membrane potential, the gradient of the log-likelihood of its spike train with respect to a synaptic weight is the input’s trace times the difference between the spikes that actually occurred and the rate that was expected. The eligibility trace of a spiking actor unit is therefore

\[ \frac{de_{ij}}{dt} = -\frac{e_{ij}}{\tau_e} + \underbrace{\big(Y_i(t) - \rho_i(t)\big)}_{\text{post: spikes} - \text{expected rate}}\;\underbrace{\frac{\rho_i'(u_i)}{\rho_i(u_i)}\,\big(\epsilon * X_j\big)(t)}_{\text{pre: filtered input spikes}}, \qquad \frac{dw_{ij}}{dt} = \eta\,\delta(t)\,e_{ij}(t) \]
Read it as: Yi(t) is the postsynaptic spike train (a sum of impulses), ρi(t) the instantaneous firing rate the neuron would have at its current potential ui, and (ε * Xj) the presynaptic spikes filtered through the shape of a postsynaptic potential. A spike where the rate was low (fired against the odds) leaves a positive flag; a stretch of silence where the rate was high leaves a negative one. This is (Y − p)x of the by-hand calculation, written for spikes, with the STDP-like timing dependence coming from the filter ε. The modulator δ(t) reads the flag out exactly as before.
Say it“The flag is the postsynaptic surprise, spikes minus expected rate, times the filtered presynaptic input; dopamine times the flag changes the weight.”

What the three-factor picture buys

  • A synapse can learn from a reward that arrives after the spikes are over, because the flag outlives the spikes
  • One broadcast scalar can teach millions of synapses different things, because each flag is local and specific
  • The actor’s contingent trace (pre × post-dependent sign) and the critic’s non-contingent trace (pre only) are both three-factor rules with different second factors
  • Negative δ (a dip in dopamine) reverses the sign of learning at every flagged synapse at once

What it leaves open

  • The measured flag lifetime (about a second) is shorter than many of the delays animals bridge; bootstrapping, not the flag, has to carry credit over longer gaps
  • Dopamine release is not one number: it differs across striatal regions, and acts through at least two receptor families with opposite effects on plasticity (the direct and indirect pathways)
  • Tonic dopamine, acetylcholine and serotonin all modulate the same synapses, so “the third factor” is really several
  • The critic’s trace should not depend on the postsynaptic spike, yet most striatal plasticity data require one
Single units · 15.9

Klopf’s hedonistic neurons

Long before anyone recorded a dopamine neuron, Harry Klopf asked a different question: what if a neuron is not a passive summing device but a goal-seeking agent? In The Hedonistic Neuron (1982), building on reports from the early 1970s, he proposed that each neuron seeks its own excitation and avoids its own inhibition, treating depolarisation as reward and hyperpolarisation as punishment. The mechanism he proposed is the one this chapter has been circling. When a neuron fires, the synapses that drove it become eligible for change for a short time. If, during that time, the neuron receives more excitation, its eligible excitatory synapses are strengthened; if it receives inhibition, they are weakened. The neuron thereby learns to fire in the circumstances that have led, shortly afterwards, to its being excited. A brain, in this view, is a society of self-interested units, and intelligent behaviour is what their collective self-interest produces.

Two parts of the idea became permanent. The first is the eligibility trace itself: Klopf’s synaptic eligibility is the origin of the traces in TD(λ) and in every actor-critic since. The second is that the trace is contingent on the neuron’s own firing: unlike Hebb’s rule, the synapse is marked by what the postsynaptic cell did, so the unit can learn from the consequences of its own actions. Barto, Sutton and Anderson (1983) turned this into the associative search element, and Barto and Anandan (1985) into the associative reward-penalty unit (AR-P): a noisy threshold unit that is told, after each output, whether the outcome was good or bad, and that moves its weights by reward × (what it did − what it was expected to do) × input. The third part of Klopf’s idea, that a neuron’s own excitation is its reward, did not survive: a cell’s depolarisation is not a reliable signal that the animal is better off. The modern version keeps the hedonist’s rule and replaces its private reward with a public one, a neuromodulator that reports how the whole animal is doing.

The right panel is a team: N hedonistic units each emit one bit, the team’s joint output is an N-bit word, and the reward is shared. \[ \begin{aligned} p_t &= \sigma(\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf x_t), \qquad Y_t \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p_t) \\ \mathbf e_t &= (Y_t - p_t)\,\mathbf x_t \qquad\text{(eligibility: contingent on the unit's own output)} \\ \mathbf w &\leftarrow \mathbf w + \alpha\,(R_t - \bar R)\,\mathbf e_t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the unit fires with a probability that is a logistic function of its weighted input. After it fires (or not), its synapses are marked by (fired − expected) × input: a synapse on an active axon is marked positively if the unit fired against the odds and negatively if it stayed silent against the odds. The reward, relative to a running average R̄ that plays the critic’s part, then multiplies the mark. The expected change equals α times the gradient of the expected reward: the unit climbs its own reward hill by trial and error. Klopf’s original used the unit’s later excitation for R; the AR-P unit used an external ±1.
Say it“Mark each synapse by output minus its probability times the input; then move it by the reward minus baseline times the mark.”

What the single unit shows. The two patterns share their middle input, so the shared synapse can carry no information about which pattern is on; the unit has to raise the weight on the first input, lower the weight on the third, and let the bias settle wherever it balances the two. Nobody tells it any of this. It fires or not, is told good or bad, and the (Y − p)x mark makes the correction land on the right synapses: when it fires for pattern A and is rewarded, the active synapses (first, middle, bias) are strengthened; when it fires for pattern B and is punished, the active synapses (middle, third, bias) are weakened; the middle synapse is pushed both ways and ends up near zero. That is the whole of Klopf’s mechanism and the whole of the actor’s, in four weights. The running-average baseline is doing the critic’s job in miniature: early on every outcome is a surprise, later only mistakes are.

What survived from Klopf

  • Eligibility traces as a synaptic memory that bridges the delay to reinforcement
  • Eligibility contingent on the neuron’s own firing, so a unit learns from the consequences of its actions
  • Neurons as agents: the unit of learning has a goal, not just a rule
  • Heterostasis over homeostasis: a neuron (and an animal) seeks more of something, rather than a set point

What did not

  • The neuron’s own excitation as its reward: depolarisation is not a signal that the animal is doing well
  • Learning without a critic: a raw reward is too noisy and too delayed; the modern unit subtracts a prediction, which is what the ventral striatum supplies
  • Purely local reward: the teams of the next section need a shared, external signal, which is what dopamine provides
Teams · 15.10

Collective reinforcement learning and structural credit assignment

Put many hedonistic units together, give them one shared reward and no other communication, and you have a team in the sense of game theory: agents with identical interests who cannot talk. Each unit faces a problem the single unit did not. When the reward comes, who earned it? The unit that fired against the odds might have caused the good outcome, or a teammate might have, or the two might have cancelled. This is the structural credit assignment problem (as opposed to the temporal one, which is about when the useful action happened and is handled by traces and bootstrapping). Backpropagation solves it by sending each unit a private, computed error. A team solves it statistically: each unit varies its own output, keeps a record of how it deviated from its expectation, and lets the shared reward tell it, over many trials, whether its deviations tend to coincide with good outcomes. Teammates’ variations are just noise that averages out, slowly.

Williams (1992) proved the fact that makes this more than wishful thinking. Take any feedforward network of Bernoulli-logistic units, hidden layers included, and let every unit update by the rule of the previous section, Δw = α(R − b)(Y − p)x, using only its own input, its own output and the shared reward. Then the expected change in the whole weight vector is proportional to the gradient of the expected reward with respect to those weights. The team, without anyone computing a gradient, performs stochastic gradient ascent on its collective objective. The algorithm is REINFORCE; the baseline b can be anything that does not depend on the current output, and a running average of reward is a good choice because it removes the part of the update that is pure noise. Barto (1985) had already shown such teams learning non-linearly separable tasks, with AR-P units as hidden units, years before the proof.

The price is variance. Each unit’s update is a one-sample estimate of its share of the gradient, and the noise in that estimate grows with the number of teammates exploring at the same time, so learning time grows quickly with team size: the all-or-nothing curve in the previous section is the textbook case, because a unit that flips its bit correctly still gets no reward unless every other unit happened to be right too. The figure below is a two-input, two-hidden, one-output network of such units learning XOR (the parity of two bits) from a single global ±1 reward. A single unit cannot represent XOR at all; the team of three can, and finds it, but only if its hidden units explore and the baseline is sensible. Switch the task to three-bit parity and watch what one more input does to the learning time.

\[ \mathbb E\big[\Delta w_{ij}\big] = \alpha\,\frac{\partial\,\mathbb E[R]}{\partial w_{ij}} \qquad\text{for every weight of every unit, when}\quad \Delta w_{ij} = \alpha\,(R - b)\,(Y_i - p_i)\,x_{j} \]
Read it as: Williams’s REINFORCE result. Unit i has output Yi, firing probability pi and inputs xj (which, for a hidden unit, are the network’s inputs, and for the output unit are the hidden units’ outputs). Every unit uses the same rule with the same R and the same baseline b. Averaged over the randomness in all the outputs, the step points up the gradient of expected reward. The proof is the log-derivative trick applied to the joint probability of all the units’ outputs, which factorises, so each unit’s share of the gradient involves only its own (Yi − pi)xj.
Say it“Each unit’s reward-times-deviation step is, on average, its share of the gradient of the team’s expected reward.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_{\mathbf w}\,\mathbb E[R] &= \sum_{\mathbf y} R(\mathbf y)\,\nabla_{\mathbf w} P(\mathbf y) = \sum_{\mathbf y} R(\mathbf y)\,P(\mathbf y)\,\nabla_{\mathbf w}\ln P(\mathbf y) = \mathbb E\big[R\,\nabla_{\mathbf w}\ln P(\mathbf Y)\big] \\ \ln P(\mathbf y) &= \sum_i \ln P_i(y_i \mid \text{inputs of } i), \qquad \frac{\partial \ln P_i}{\partial w_{ij}} = (y_i - p_i)\,x_j \quad\text{for a logistic unit} \\ \mathbb E\big[b\,\nabla_{\mathbf w}\ln P(\mathbf Y)\big] &= b\,\nabla_{\mathbf w}\sum_{\mathbf y} P(\mathbf y) = b\,\nabla_{\mathbf w} 1 = \mathbf 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the proof in three lines. First, the gradient of an expectation over outputs y becomes an expectation of R times the gradient of the log-probability (multiply and divide by P). Second, because every unit draws its output independently given its inputs, the joint log-probability is a sum over units, and only unit i’s term depends on wij; for a logistic unit that term’s derivative is (yi − pi)xj. Third, subtracting any baseline b that does not depend on the current outputs changes nothing in expectation, since the probabilities sum to one. Hidden units need no special treatment: their “inputs of i” are the network inputs, the output unit’s are the hidden outputs, and the sum covers both layers. What the proof does not say is how large the variance is, and that is what team size controls.
Say it“Gradient of expected reward equals expected reward times gradient of log-probability; the log-probability of the team is the sum of the units’; a baseline drops out.”

Against backpropagation. Backprop gives each hidden unit an exact, private error by running the network’s weights backwards, which requires a second pathway carrying the transposed weights (the weight-transport problem that makes it biologically awkward). A team needs no backward pass and no transposed weights: a scalar reward, local traces and stochastic units are enough, and every one of those exists in the striatum. What it pays is time. For a network with K stochastic units the variance of each unit’s gradient estimate grows roughly with K, because the other K − 1 units’ exploration enters its reward as noise, so trials to learn grow at least linearly, and often much faster, with the size of the team. Modern “node perturbation” methods are the same idea with Gaussian noise instead of Bernoulli outputs, and they scale the same way. The brain’s answer, if it uses this, is probably to keep the teams small: an actor whose units are whole populations with correlated noise is a team of a few, not of millions.

Three ways to cut the variance

  • Subtract a predictionThe baseline b removes the part of R that every unit would have received regardless of what it did. The best constant baseline is close to the expected reward; a learned, state-dependent baseline is a critic, and then (R − b) is δ. The actor-critic is REINFORCE with the best available baseline, and that is why the two halves of section 1 belong together.
  • Explore less at onceNoise from teammates is the problem, so correlate it or reduce it: let a few units explore while the rest hold still, or make whole populations share one source of noise so that a “team” of a million neurons behaves like a team of a handful. Population-level variability in motor cortex has been read this way.
  • Give each unit more of its own signalScoring each bit separately in the previous section is the extreme case: a private reward per unit. Backpropagation is the other extreme, a private gradient. In between lie schemes that broadcast a few scalars to a few groups, which is what multiple dopamine populations with different inputs could provide (section 9).

Planning · 15.11

Model-based methods in the brain

Everything so far has been model-free: the critic caches values, the actor caches preferences, and neither knows what any action actually does. The psychology chapter showed that animals also behave in a model-based way: devalue a food after training and a rat with moderate training stops pressing for it immediately, without ever pressing and finding it worthless, which a cached value cannot explain. The neural story has the same two halves. Lesion and inactivation work (Yin and Knowlton, 2006; Balleine and O’Doherty, 2010) places habitual, model-free control in the dorsolateral striatum and goal-directed, model-based control in the dorsomedial striatum together with prefrontal and orbitofrontal cortex, with the two systems competing for the same motor output. The actor-critic of this chapter is the habit half.

Where would a model live, and how would it be used? The best candidate is the hippocampus. Its place cells fire in sequence as an animal runs a track, and during pauses they fire the same sequences again, compressed about twenty-fold, in both directions. Foster and Wilson (2006) found reverse replay of a just-run path at the moment the rat reached its reward, which is exactly the order in which a value-learning algorithm would want to revisit the states: start at the reward, work backwards, and let each state learn from the one after it in a single sweep. Johnson and Redish (2007) found forward sweeps at choice points, the place-cell representation running ahead of the animal down one arm and then the other, which looks like looking ahead with a model. Pfeiffer and Foster (2013) found forward sequences that traced paths to a remembered goal before the animal moved. In the language of the Dyna chapter, reverse replay is a form of prioritised sweeping, and forward sweeps are a form of rollout planning; Mattar and Daw (2018) showed that a single rule, replay whatever experience would most improve the policy, predicts when each kind occurs.

Humans show the mixture directly. In the two-step task of Daw and colleagues (2011), choices reflect a blend of cached values and a learned transition model, the blend differs between people, and prediction-error signals in the striatum carry model-based information, so even the “dopamine = model-free δ” story needs a footnote. Read it as: reverse replay as an algorithm. The run visited s0, …, sN−1 and ended with reward. Replaying it backwards applies the ordinary one-step TD update to each state in turn, but because the successor was updated a moment earlier in the same sweep, the reward’s value propagates the whole length of the path in one pass. Forward TD(0) needs N runs for the same spread; TD(λ) gets most of it in one run but with traces that fade with distance. Dyna does this with remembered transitions in any order; prioritised sweeping does it in roughly this order, largest error first.

A failure mode · 15.12

Addiction as a TD failure mode

Cocaine and amphetamine act on the dopamine system directly, by blocking the transporter that clears dopamine from the synapse or by forcing its release. Whatever else they do, they produce a surge of striatal dopamine at the moment they are taken. If phasic dopamine is the brain’s δ, that surge is a prediction error that is not about any prediction: the critic cannot have been wrong by that amount, because the surge does not come from the comparison circuit at all. A. David Redish (2004) asked what a TD learner would do with such a signal, and the answer is the cleanest account of why drugs are different from food.

His model is one line. The drug contributes a term D(S) to the TD error that cannot be cancelled by prediction: δ = max(R + γV(S′) − V(S) + D(S), D(S)). With a natural reward, as V(S) rises to match what follows, the bracket goes to zero and learning stops; the value settles at the reward. With the drug, δ is never less than D, so every visit to the drug state adds at least αD to its value, forever, and TD propagates that growing value to every cue and action that leads there. Three consequences follow, and all three are real features of addiction. First, the drug option comes to be chosen over any natural alternative, because its value keeps rising while theirs are bounded. Second, the choice becomes inelastic: a cost c subtracted from a bounded value will eventually exceed it, so demand for a natural reward falls as its price rises, but a cost subtracted from a value that grows without bound is eventually irrelevant. Third, the cues that predict the drug acquire a value out of all proportion to anything they predict, which is at least a candidate for craving.

What does the model say about stopping? Withhold the drug and δ = R + γV(S′) − V(S) with no D: a negative error, and the value falls by a factor (1 − α) per unwarranted visit. So the pure model does extinguish, only from a far greater height and so more slowly, and this is one of its known shortcomings (section 9): real drug-seeking relapses after months of abstinence in a way that a decayed value does not predict. What the model gets right is a different kind of failure to extinguish. A punishment delivered together with the drug cannot teach anything, because δ = max(· + D, D) is at least D however large the cost: the value keeps rising through the punishment. And a prediction can never catch up with the drug, so the learning that normally stops when a reward is fully expected never stops. \[ \delta_t = \max\!\Big( R_{t+1} + \gamma V(S_{t+1}) - V(S_t) + D(S_t),\;\; D(S_t) \Big), \qquad V(S_t) \leftarrow V(S_t) + \alpha\,\delta_t \]
Read it as: D(S) is the pharmacological dopamine surge, present only in states where the drug is taken (D = 0 elsewhere, where the rule is ordinary TD). The max says the surge can add to a prediction error but can never be subtracted from: however high V(S) has climbed, the drug still produces at least D. For the drug state itself, with nothing after it, V grows by at least αD every visit: after n visits V ≥ nαD. A natural reward R in the same place gives δ = R − V, and V stops at R. The chooser in Redish’s simulations was a softmax over the values of the options; the project uses an actor fed with the same δ.

What this does and does not show. The arithmetic is a statement about one learning rule, not about people. Caveats

What the models leave out

The actor-critic mapping and Redish’s addiction model are computational hypotheses, and they are valuable in the way good hypotheses are: they make specific claims that experiments can embarrass. Several have been. None of the following undoes the chapter, but a reader who repeats “dopamine is the TD error and addiction is a runaway TD error” without them is repeating a slogan rather than a theory.

  • Dopamine is not one signalSome dopamine neurons respond to aversive events or to salience rather than value; dopamine release differs between striatal regions receiving the same spikes; slow tonic dopamine seems to set response vigour (Niv, Daw, Joel and Dayan, 2007) rather than report errors; and dopamine ramps up as a rat approaches a goal (Howe and colleagues, 2013), which a pure phasic δ does not predict unless the value function has a particular shape (the previous chapter works through the ramp question). The “one scalar broadcast to everyone” of the schematic is a first approximation.
  • The striatum is not two boxesVentral, dorsomedial and dorsolateral striatum form a gradient, connected by spiralling striato-nigro-striatal loops (Haber and colleagues, 2000) in which each region’s output influences the dopamine input to the next. That is a richer architecture than one critic feeding one actor, and it has been read as a chain in which control migrates dorsally as a behaviour becomes a habit, which is also how the addiction literature (Everitt and Robbins, 2005) describes the transition from drug use to compulsion.
  • Two kinds of spiny neuronStriatal neurons expressing D1 receptors (the direct, “Go” pathway) are potentiated by a dopamine burst, while D2 neurons (the indirect, “NoGo” pathway) learn mostly from dips. Frank (2005) built an actor from these two populations and explained why Parkinson’s patients, on and off medication, learn differently from positive and negative outcomes. A single actor population with signed updates is a simplification of that.
  • The error may not be a scalarDabney and colleagues (2020) found that individual dopamine neurons have different reversal points, some reporting errors relative to optimistic and some relative to pessimistic predictions, so that the population encodes a distribution of returns rather than their mean (see the distributional section of the previous chapter). The critic of this chapter would be the average of such a population.
  • Where does γV(S′) − V(S) come from?The critic must report its value to the dopamine neurons with a negative sign and, a moment later, the next value with a positive sign. Striatal projections to the dopamine neurons are inhibitory, so the first half is anatomically easy; the delayed, positively signed second half has several proposed routes and none is settled. Proposals in the Houk, Adams and Barto tradition use a fast and a slow pathway whose difference approximates the time derivative.
  • Addiction is more than a runaway valueRedish’s model does not say why only some users become addicted, predicts extinction when the drug is withheld, has no role for withdrawal, stress or the opponent processes of Koob and Le Moal (2008), and does not distinguish wanting from liking as incentive-sensitisation theory does (Robinson and Berridge, 1993). Redish, Jensen and Johnson (2008) replaced the single mechanism with a catalogue of ten vulnerabilities in the decision process, of which the non-compensable δ is one. The lesson of section 8 stands: a reward that cannot be predicted away breaks a TD learner; the claim that this is the mechanism of addiction does not.
  • Replay is not freeReverse replay in the figure of section 7 costs one sweep per run; real replay occupies the hippocampus for hundreds of milliseconds at a time, during rest and during sleep, and the same sequences reappear during slow-wave sleep, where they have been linked to consolidation rather than to immediate valuation. Which replays are for planning, which for learning values and which for memory is not settled, and a model that uses them all for TD updates is choosing one reading.
  • TimescalesSynaptic flags last about a second; dopamine arrives within a second or two of an event; yet animals learn associations across delays of many seconds and plans spanning minutes. In the algorithm, bootstrapping carries credit across gaps that traces cannot; in the brain, that means the critic’s predictions, and possibly hippocampal replay, do work that no single synapse can.

What would strengthen the mapping

  • A demonstrated pathway that delivers +γV(S′) to dopamine neurons a step after −V(S)
  • Eligibility traces of different kinds, contingent in dorsal and non-contingent in ventral striatum, measured at the synapse
  • Optogenetic dopamine bursts that stand in for δ and produce the learning the rule predicts, including the depression after dips (several such experiments now exist)

What would weaken it

  • Dopamine transients that carry no prediction-error structure in a task where the actor-critic requires one
  • Cortico-striatal plasticity that ignores dopamine timing in behaving animals
  • Action learning in the dorsal striatum that proceeds normally when the ventral critic is silenced, under conditions where the model says the actor needs its δ

Project: a brain-shaped actor-critic that can be given a drug

Build the circuit of section 1 as a program and let it find its way around a small arena. Each cell of the arena is a cortical feature (a one-hot vector, as if a different cortical ensemble fired in each place). One critic unit, the ventral striatum, reads the cell through weights w. Four actor units, the dorsal striatum, read it through weights θ and compete through a softmax for the move: up, right, down or left. One dopamine signal δ is computed from the reward and the critic’s two successive outputs and is broadcast to every synapse, where it meets the eligibility traces of section 2: non-contingent on the critic’s synapses, contingent on the actor’s. The arena has two goals on the far side of a pillar: A at the top pays 1, B at the bottom pays 0.8, and the two cells before A are a toll gate that can be made to cost something.

Two copies of the agent run side by side on identical random numbers, so for as long as they make the same choices they live the same life. For the healthy copy, A delivers an ordinary reward. For the drug copy, A delivers Redish’s non-compensable term D instead of a reward, with the same nominal size. Three phases follow: 150 episodes with the toll switched off, 200 with the toll on, and 150 in which A delivers nothing at all. Two behavioural questions are being asked. Does a cost make the agent switch to the cheaper goal (sensitivity to cost)? When A stops paying, how long does the agent keep going there (perseveration)? Press play to watch the two agents learn episode by episode, or run all 500 at once; then change the toll, the drug strength and the traces.

Six experiments to run

  1. Find the price. Set the toll to 0 and run: both agents prefer A, which pays more. Raise the toll in steps of 0.05 and find the smallest toll at which the healthy agent spends most of the second half of phase 2 at B. Check it against the discounted returns: with γ = 0.95 and eight steps to either goal, A is worth about 0.70 minus roughly 1.5 tolls, B about 0.56.
  2. Make the drug weak. Set D to 0.25, a quarter of the natural reward. The drug agent still ends up at A: what matters is not the size of D but that it never goes away. How many episodes does it take before its value of the start cell passes the healthy agent’s?
  3. Watch extinction do its work, and not do it. In phase 3 the drug agent’s V(start) collapses within a few dozen visits, exactly as the pure model says it should, and the table shows it still ending at A almost every time. Why? Look at the actor’s arrows: the critic has extinguished, but δ is now near zero along the whole path, so the actor has nothing to learn from and keeps its enormous preferences. This is the model’s version of a habit that outlives its reason.
  4. Turn the traces off. Set λ to 0 and run. Learning in phase 1 is slower for both agents and the drug agent’s values stay more concentrated near A. With λ = 0.95 the value of the drug reaches the start cell in a handful of episodes.
  5. Change the seed. With seed 7 or 12 the healthy agent spends longer than usual exploring in phase 1. Does the toll-phase result change? The 20-seed averages in the Python output below say how much of what you see is this particular life.
  6. Make the toll ruinous. Set the toll to 0.6 so that reaching A costs more than A pays, even for the healthy agent on a good day. The healthy agent leaves. The drug agent pays 1.2 per episode for a “reward” of 0 and never stops. Then cut D to 0.25 and repeat.

import math
import numpy as np

# ---------- the page's random-number generator, bit for bit ----------
def mulberry32(a):
    def nxt():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return nxt

# ---------- the arena: 7 x 5 cells, start on the left, two goals on the right ----------
W, H = 7, 5
START, GOAL_A, GOAL_B = (0, 2), (6, 0), (6, 4)          # A: natural reward 1 or the drug; B: natural reward 0.6
WALLS = {(3, 1), (3, 2), (3, 3)}                         # a pillar, so the two routes are separate
TOLL = {(4, 0), (5, 0)}                                  # entering either top-row cell before A costs c
MOVES = [(0, -1), (1, 0), (0, 1), (-1, 0)]               # up, right, down, left
R_A, R_B, GAMMA, LAMBDA, MAX_STEPS = 1.0, 0.8, 0.95, 0.8, 80
PHASES = [(0, 150, 'free'), (150, 350, 'toll'), (350, 500, 'withheld')]

def step(s, a):
    nx, ny = s[0] + MOVES[a][0], s[1] + MOVES[a][1]
    if 0 <= nx < W and 0 <= ny < H and (nx, ny) not in WALLS:
        return (nx, ny)
    return s

def run(drug, seed=3, alpha_w=0.2, alpha_th=0.2, D=1.0, toll=0.45, n_episodes=500, record=False):
    """One brain-shaped actor-critic: tabular 'cortical' features, a critic unit (ventral striatum),
    four actor units (dorsal striatum), one dopamine signal delta, eligibility traces at every synapse.
    drug=True turns goal A's reward into Redish's non-compensable dopamine term D."""
    rng = mulberry32(seed)
    w = np.zeros((W, H))                                  # critic weights, one per cortical feature
    th = np.zeros((W, H, 4))                              # actor weights
    ends, tolls, steps, vstart = [], [], [], []
    for ep in range(n_episodes):
        phase = next(name for lo, hi, name in PHASES if lo <= ep < hi)
        zw, zt = np.zeros((W, H)), np.zeros((W, H, 4))
        s, paid, t, end = START, 0.0, 0, None
        while True:
            h = th[s]; p = np.exp(h - h.max()); p /= p.sum()
            u, a, acc = rng(), 3, 0.0
            for k in range(4):
                acc += p[k]
                if u < acc:
                    a = k; break
            s2 = step(s, a); t += 1
            r, d_term = 0.0, 0.0
            if s2 in TOLL and phase != 'free':
                r -= toll; paid += toll
            terminal = False
            if s2 == GOAL_A:
                terminal, end = True, 'A'
                if phase != 'withheld':
                    if drug: d_term = D
                    else: r += R_A
            elif s2 == GOAL_B:
                terminal, end = True, 'B'; r += R_B
            elif t >= MAX_STEPS:
                terminal, end = True, 'timeout'
            v2 = 0.0 if terminal else w[s2]
            delta = r + GAMMA * v2 - w[s]
            if d_term > 0:
                delta = max(delta + d_term, d_term)       # Redish (2004): the drug term cannot be predicted away
            zw *= GAMMA * LAMBDA; zw[s] += 1.0
            zt *= GAMMA * LAMBDA; zt[s] -= p; zt[s][a] += 1.0
            w += alpha_w * delta * zw
            th += alpha_th * delta * zt
            s = s2
            if terminal: break
        ends.append(end); tolls.append(paid); steps.append(t); vstart.append(w[START])
    return dict(w=w, th=th, ends=ends, tolls=tolls, steps=steps, vstart=vstart)

def summarise(res):
    out = {}
    for lo, hi, name in PHASES:
        e = res['ends'][lo:hi]
        out[name] = dict(pA=e.count('A') / len(e), pB=e.count('B') / len(e), timeout=e.count('timeout') / len(e),
                         toll=float(np.mean(res['tolls'][lo:hi])), steps=float(np.mean(res['steps'][lo:hi])),
                         v_end=float(res['vstart'][hi - 1]))
    return out

Wrap-up · 15.13

Five lines to remember

  • The actor-critic has a plausible home: cortex supplies the features, ventral striatum is the critic, dorsal striatum the actor, dopamine neurons compute δ and broadcast it, and cortico-striatal synapses are the weights. Every arrow is a real projection; whether the brain uses them this way is the hypothesis.
  • Both learners move each weight by αδ times an eligibility trace. The critic’s trace is presynaptic activity alone (non-contingent); the actor’s is presynaptic activity times (postsynaptic activity minus its expectation), which is what lets one shared scalar teach each action unit something different.
  • At the synapse this is a three-factor rule: a pre-post pairing (with STDP deciding the sign) sets a flag that decays over about a second, and dopamine arriving while the flag is up changes the weight by the product. The flag is Klopf’s eligibility, and his hedonistic neuron, with a public reward in place of its private excitation, is the actor unit.
  • A team of such units sharing one reward climbs the gradient of expected reward (Williams’s REINFORCE), so no backward pass is needed, but every teammate’s exploration is noise to every other, so learning time grows fast with team size: the structural credit assignment problem, paid for in variance.
  • A reward the critic cannot predict away breaks TD: Redish’s δ = max(· + D, D) makes drug values grow without bound, so the drug is chosen over anything, costs stop mattering and punishment paired with the drug teaches nothing. It is one mechanism among several, not the theory of addiction; it does predict extinction when the drug is withheld, which real addicts often do not show.

Check your understanding

Why must the actor’s eligibility trace depend on the postsynaptic neuron’s activity when the critic’s need not?

The critic is a predictor: a weight should change according to how much its input contributed to the prediction, which is just the input, so the trace is x(S). The actor is a chooser: the same δ has to make the action that was taken more likely and the others less, and the only local information that distinguishes them is whether each unit fired. The factor (1[A = a] − π(a|S)) is the gradient of the log-probability and is positive only for the unit that fired; without it every actor unit would be strengthened equally by a good outcome and nothing about the choice would change.

In the by-hand example the dopamine burst was 0.58. What single change to the reward would have produced a dip instead, and what would the dip have done to the two actor units?

Any reward below 0.42 makes δ negative (the prediction was 0.6, the continuation was worth 0.18). A dip meets the same traces as a burst with the opposite sign: the left unit’s synapses, flagged positively because it fired against the odds, are depressed, and the right unit’s, flagged negatively, are potentiated, so the choice of left becomes less likely next time.

Dopamine arriving 1 second after a pre-post pairing changes a spiny neuron’s synapse; dopamine arriving 1 second before does not. What in the three-factor rule gives that asymmetry?

The flag is set by the pairing and decays afterwards; before the pairing there is no flag, so the product M(t)e(t) is zero however much dopamine there is. The weight can only change when the modulator finds a flag already up, which means after the pairing and within the flag’s lifetime. This is also why the window in Yagishita’s experiments closes after a couple of seconds.

A team of eight Bernoulli units must output a fixed 8-bit word for an all-or-nothing reward. Why does the REINFORCE theorem not make this easy, even though it guarantees the team climbs the expected-reward gradient?

The theorem is about the expected update. The actual update of unit i is (R − b)(Yi − pi)x, and R is 1 only on the rare trials when all eight bits are right (1 in 256 at the start). On every other trial the unit receives the same reward whatever it did, so its update is pure noise around zero. The gradient is correct but tiny relative to its variance, and the variance comes from the teammates; scoring each bit separately removes that dependence and the team learns in a few dozen trials.

In Redish’s model, why does a cost delivered together with the drug fail to reduce the drug state’s value, while the same cost delivered with a natural reward does?

With the natural reward δ = (R − c) − V, so the value settles at R − c and falls when c rises. With the drug δ = max((−c) + γV′ − V + D, D) ≥ D > 0 for any c: the cost can lower the first argument of the max but never the second, so every visit still raises the value by at least αD. The cost is simply not learned about in that state.

O’Doherty and colleagues found prediction-error signals in the ventral striatum whether or not the subject was choosing, and in the dorsal striatum only when choosing. Why is this evidence for an actor-critic rather than for a single value learner?

A single learner that predicts rewards from cues would show the same error signal in the same place whether actions mattered or not. An actor-critic predicts two different things: the critic must learn in both conditions (cue values are needed either way), but the actor has nothing to learn when no choices are made, so its region should be silent in the passive condition. The dissociation is what the two-learner architecture predicts and what the one-learner architecture does not.

In the project, the drug agent’s value of the start cell collapses to near zero in phase 3, yet it keeps walking to A. What does that say about the relationship between the critic and the actor?

The actor only changes when δ is non-zero. Once the critic has learned that the path to A is worth nothing (and the tolls are fully predicted), δ along that path is close to zero, so the actor’s weights stop changing, and they were left enormous by the drug phase. The policy has become independent of the value that built it: a habit in the strict sense. The healthy agent did switch because its preferences were modest and the negative errors during the toll phase were enough to reverse them while the critic was still learning.
