NMF, Archetypes & Sparse Coding

Explaining data as a sum of a few parts

PCA explains data with a handful of directions, but its directions mix positive and negative values, so they look like ghosts rather than pieces. Forbid the negatives and the factors turn into parts that add up. Then go one step further and let each example use only a few atoms out of many: that is sparse coding, the idea behind learned image dictionaries and many early deep features. On the right, the page has just split 150 toy faces into 8 non-negative parts, with nobody telling it what an eye is. Touch any face to see the parts that add up to it.

The idea

Parts, not ghosts

Stack the data in a matrix X with one example per row: 150 faces, each a row of 256 pixel intensities. Three classic ways to summarise X with k building blocks all have the same shape, X ≈ WH. The k rows of H are the building blocks (each one an image), and row i of W says how much of each block face i uses. They differ only in what they allow W and H to be.

PCA lets both be anything, so the blocks are orthogonal directions that add and cancel: half of their ink is negative. Vector quantisation (k-means) lets each face use exactly one block, so the blocks are whole prototype faces. Non-negative matrix factorisation (NMF; Paatero & Tapper 1994, Lee & Seung 1999) forbids negative numbers anywhere. Nothing can be cancelled, only added, so the cheapest way to build many faces is from parts. Switch between the three methods and move k.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{X} \approx \mathbf{W}\mathbf{H} \\ &x_{ij} \approx \textstyle\sum_{a=1}^{k} w_{ia}\, h_{aj} \\ &w_{ia} \ge 0,\ \ h_{aj} \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: X is N×p (examples by features), W is N×k (how much of each block), H is k×p (the blocks themselves). Pixel j of face i is a weighted sum of pixel j of every block. With every number non-negative, each block can only ever add ink.
Say it“X is approximately W times H; x-i-j is the sum over a of w-i-a times h-a-j, with every w and every h at least zero.”

Fitting

Fitting NMF: multiplicative updates

NMF needs a measure of misfit. The obvious one is squared error. Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman instead treat each xij as a Poisson count with mean (WH)ij, which suits word counts and photon counts; maximising that likelihood is the same as minimising the generalised Kullback–Leibler divergence between X and WH. Neither problem is convex in W and H together, but each is convex in W alone and in H alone, so we alternate.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{\mathbf{W},\mathbf{H}\,\ge\,0}\ \lVert \mathbf{X}-\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H}\rVert_F^2 \\ &= \textstyle\sum_{i,j}\big(x_{ij}-(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H})_{ij}\big)^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: add up the squared gap at every entry of the matrix; F is for Frobenius, the matrix version of the Euclidean length.
Say it“Minimise, over non-negative W and H, the squared Frobenius norm of X minus W H.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{\mathbf{W},\mathbf{H}\,\ge\,0}\ L, \\ &L = \textstyle\sum_{i,j}\big[x_{ij}\log(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H})_{ij} \\ &\qquad - (\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H})_{ij}\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the Poisson log-likelihood of X when xij has mean (WH)ij, dropping the log xij! terms that don’t depend on W or H. Large counts are allowed to miss by more, so rare small entries are not drowned out by big ones.
Say it“Maximise L, the sum over i and j of x-i-j times log of W H i-j, minus W H i-j.”

Lee and Seung (2001) found update rules that never leave the non-negative orthant and never make the objective worse: multiply each entry by a ratio of two non-negative numbers. The ratio is 1 exactly when the gradient for that entry is zero, so a fixed point is a stationary point. Step the updates and watch the parts emerge from noise; then try the Poisson loss, and HALS, a faster solver that updates one whole block at a time.

\[ \begin{aligned} h_{aj} &\leftarrow h_{aj}\,\frac{(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{X})_{aj}}{(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H})_{aj}} \\ w_{ia} &\leftarrow w_{ia}\,\frac{(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{H}^{\top})_{ia}}{(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{\top})_{ia}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: squared error. This is gradient descent on haj with its own step size haj/(W⊤WH)aj, chosen so the subtraction becomes a multiplication. Something that starts positive stays positive; an exact zero stays zero for ever.
Say it“h-a-j becomes h-a-j times W-transpose X over W-transpose W H, entry a-j; and likewise for w.”
\[ \begin{aligned} w_{ia} &\leftarrow w_{ia}\,\frac{\sum_j h_{aj}\,x_{ij}/(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H})_{ij}}{\sum_j h_{aj}} \\ h_{aj} &\leftarrow h_{aj}\,\frac{\sum_i w_{ia}\,x_{ij}/(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H})_{ij}}{\sum_i w_{ia}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Poisson (KL) loss. xij/(WH)ij is how far each entry is under- or over-predicted; the update scales an entry up when the entries it feeds are under-predicted on average, and down when they are over-predicted.
Say it“w-i-a becomes w-i-a times the h-weighted average of x over W H.”

A warning

Many right answers: the cone

Even a perfect fit need not be unique. In two dimensions it is easy to see why. Every row of X is a point; NMF with k = 2 picks two non-negative vectors h1, h2 (the rows of H) and writes each point as wi1h1 + wi2h2 with non-negative weights. The points it can build exactly are those inside the cone between h1 and h2. Any cone that contains the data, and stays in the positive quadrant, gives zero error (Donoho & Stodden 2004). Drag the tips of h1 and h2, then try both snap buttons.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{H} &= (\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{H}) \\ \text{whenever } &\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Q} \ge 0,\ \mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{H} \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: slip any invertible k×k matrix Q and its inverse between W and H and the product is unchanged. If both new factors are still non-negative, NMF can’t tell the two answers apart. Q can be a simple rescaling (always allowed, so NMF fixes a scale by convention) or a genuine change of parts, like moving the cone’s edges.
Say it“X equals W H equals W Q times Q-inverse H, whenever both are non-negative.”
points outside the cone
‖X − WH‖²

So NMF’s parts are only as meaningful as the data allows. They are well defined when, for each part, some examples contain that part and almost nothing else (the toy faces have that property), and slippery otherwise. Extra preferences pin the answer down: sparse weights, or the next idea, which ties the parts to the data itself.

Extremes

Archetypal analysis: the corners of the cloud

Cutler and Breiman (1994) asked for prototypes that are real mixtures of data and that span the data from the outside. Each archetype must be a convex combination of data points (so it sits inside the cloud’s hull), and each data point is approximated by a convex combination of archetypes (so it is a blend of extremes). The best archetypes end up on the convex hull, at the cloud’s corners. k-means does the opposite: its prototypes are averages, so they sit in the middle of their clusters. Change k, drag points out of the cloud, and switch to k-means to compare.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{\mathbf{W},\,\mathbf{B}}\ \lVert \mathbf{X} - \mathbf{W}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{X}\rVert_F^2 \\ &\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{X} \\ &w_{ia} \ge 0,\ \textstyle\sum_a w_{ia} = 1 \\ &b_{aj} \ge 0,\ \textstyle\sum_j b_{aj} = 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: H = BX: archetype a is the B-weighted average of the data points (row a of B is a recipe of non-negative weights summing to 1). Row i of W is a recipe of non-negative weights, summing to 1, for mixing the archetypes into point i. A point inside the archetypes’ polygon is reproduced exactly; a point outside is replaced by the nearest point of the polygon. k-means is the special case where each row of W has a single 1.
Say it“Minimise the squared norm of X minus W B X, where the rows of W and the rows of B are non-negative and sum to one.”
residual sum of squares
points rebuilt exactly

The fitting alternates two convex steps, as for NMF. With the archetypes fixed, each point’s weights solve a small least-squares problem on the simplex. With the weights fixed, the best archetypes are found by least squares and then pulled back inside the hull, which is again a simplex-constrained least squares, now over data points. As k grows the archetypes walk around the hull; with as many archetypes as hull vertices, every point is rebuilt exactly.

Sparsity

Sparse representations and the L1 penalty

From here on we follow Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville and look at one example at a time. Write a signal x (a column of p numbers) as x ≈ Dh, where the columns of D are atoms and h is the code. (Their book calls the dictionary W; we use D so it is not confused with NMF’s weights.) When D has more atoms than x has entries, infinitely many codes rebuild x perfectly, and we need a preference. The preference that has proved most useful is sparsity: as few non-zero entries in h as possible.

Note what is sparse. The lasso of chapters 1.2 and 1.2b penalised parameters so that a model uses few inputs. Here the parameters D may be dense; it is the representation h of each example that is mostly zeros. The tool is the same, a penalty added to the training objective, now applied to activations.

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde J(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}) &= J(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}) \\ &\quad + \alpha\,\Omega(\mathbf{h}) \\ \Omega(\mathbf{h}) &= \lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert_1 = \textstyle\sum_i |h_i| \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: keep whatever loss J you had and add α times a penalty on the representation, not on the weights. The L1 norm is the usual choice; a Student-t prior, a KL penalty pulling each unit’s average activation towards a small target, or a hard cap on how many units fire are others.
Say it“J-tilde is J plus alpha times Omega of h, and Omega of h is the sum of the absolute values of h.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{\mathbf{h}}\ \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{D}\mathbf{h}\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert_1 \\ &h_j \leftarrow S_\lambda\big(\mathbf{d}_j^{\top}\mathbf{r}_{-j}\big)\,/\,\lVert\mathbf{d}_j\rVert^2 \\ &S_\lambda(z) = \operatorname{sign}(z)\max(|z|-\lambda,0) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the lasso again, with the code as the unknown. r−j is what is left of x after every atom except j has done its part. Atom j’s coefficient is its correlation with that remainder, shrunk towards zero by λ and set to exactly zero if the correlation is smaller than λ. Cycling through j until nothing changes is coordinate descent.
Say it“h-j becomes the soft threshold at lambda of d-j dot the partial residual, over the squared length of d-j.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{h}^{*} &= \arg\min_{\mathbf{h}}\ \lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{D}\mathbf{h}\rVert^2 \\ &\text{subject to}\ \ \lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert_0 \le k \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the L0 “norm” counts non-zeros, so this asks for the best code using at most k atoms. Searching all subsets is hopeless, so orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP-k) builds the set greedily: add the atom most correlated with the current residual, refit all chosen atoms by least squares, repeat k times. OMP-1 is a very cheap feature extractor that worked surprisingly well in Coates & Ng’s (2011) experiments.
Say it“h-star is the h minimising the squared error, subject to h having at most k non-zero entries.”

Learning the atoms

Sparse coding and dictionary learning

So far the dictionary was given. Sparse coding (Olshausen & Field 1996) learns it, by treating x as generated from a few active causes. It is a linear factor model, like probabilistic PCA and ICA, but with a prior that puts a sharp peak at zero, so most causes are silent for any one example.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(h_i) &= \tfrac{\lambda}{4}\,e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}|h_i|} \\ p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{h}) &= \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{x};\ \mathbf{D}\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{b},\ \beta^{-1}\mathbf{I}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each cause hi is drawn independently from a Laplace distribution (a two-sided exponential, scale 2/λ); the signal is the atoms weighted by the causes, plus Gaussian noise of precision β.
Say it“p of h-i is lambda over four, e to the minus lambda over two times the absolute value of h-i. x given h is normal with mean D h plus b and variance one over beta.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{h}^{*} &= f(\mathbf{x}) \\ &= \arg\min_{\mathbf{h}}\ \lambda\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert_1 \\ &\qquad + \beta\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{D}\mathbf{h}\rVert^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the encoder is not a formula but an optimisation: the most probable code given x (the next section shows why). With β = ½ it is exactly the lasso above, which is the setting used in this lab.
Say it“h-star, f of x, is the h minimising lambda times the L1 norm of h plus beta times the squared error.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \min_{\mathbf{D},\ \mathbf{h}_1,\dots,\mathbf{h}_N}\ &\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Big(\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{x}_n-\mathbf{D}\mathbf{h}_n\rVert^2 \\ &\quad + \lambda\lVert\mathbf{h}_n\rVert_1\Big) \\ \text{s.t.}\ \ &\lVert\mathbf{d}_a\rVert \le 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: learning alternates. Fix D and code every example (a lasso each); fix the codes and refit D by least squares, then rescale each atom to length 1. The length cap matters: without it, doubling an atom and halving its coefficient fits equally well and halves the penalty, so the atoms grow for ever while the codes shrink towards nothing.
Say it“Minimise over D and all the codes the sum of half the squared error plus lambda times the L1 norm, with every atom of length at most one.”

Sparse coding’s encoder is non-parametric: an optimisation run afresh for every input. That costs time, and it makes it awkward to back-propagate through the encoder, but it also means the code is always the best one for the current dictionary, which tends to generalise better than a learned encoder that might have fitted its training set badly. As a generator it is weak: the Laplace prior almost never produces an exact zero, so a sampled code switches on every atom a little, and samples smear across the plane instead of following the arms. Spike-and-slab priors, which really do output zeros, fix this.

Inference

MAP inference: why the code is an optimisation

With a Laplace prior and a Gaussian likelihood the posterior p(h | x) has no tidy form, and it is not factorial: the causes are dependent once x is observed. If two atoms point in similar directions, either one could explain x, so evidence for one is evidence against the other. That is explaining away. Rather than represent the whole posterior, sparse coding keeps a single point, its peak: the maximum a posteriori (MAP) code. Close the angle between the atoms and watch the posterior tilt into a ridge; then raise λ and see the peak land exactly on an axis.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{h}^{*} &= \arg\max_{\mathbf{h}}\ p(\mathbf{h}\mid\mathbf{x}) \\ &= \arg\max_{\mathbf{h}}\,\log p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{h}) + \log p(\mathbf{h}) \\ &= \arg\min_{\mathbf{h}}\ \beta\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{D}\mathbf{h}\rVert^2 \\ &\qquad + \lambda\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert_1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Bayes’ rule makes the posterior proportional to likelihood times prior, and the evidence p(x) does not depend on h. Take minus the log: the Gaussian gives (β/2)‖x − Dh‖², the Laplace prior gives (λ/2)‖h‖1; doubling both gives the sparse-coding objective. The L1 term comes from the prior, which is why MAP codes have exact zeros.
Say it“h-star is the arg-max of the posterior, which is the arg-max of log-likelihood plus log-prior, which is the arg-min of beta times the squared error plus lambda times the L1 norm.”

MAP also fits the lower-bound picture of chapter 1.12a (EM). The evidence lower bound (ELBO) holds for any distribution q(h); choose q to be a spike at a single point μ. The bound’s first term becomes log p(μ, x), and maximising over μ is MAP inference. The second term, the entropy of a spike, is minus infinity, so strictly the bound is useless; in practice we simply drop that constant and keep the useful part. Learning by alternating MAP codes with updates of D is then exactly the dictionary learning above, including the need for a length cap on the atoms.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta},q) &= \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{h}\sim q}\big[\log p(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{x})\big] \\ &\quad + \mathcal{H}(q) \\ q(\mathbf{h}) &= \delta(\mathbf{h}-\boldsymbol{\mu}) \ \Rightarrow \\ \boldsymbol{\mu}^{*} &= \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\ \log p(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{x}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the ELBO is the expected log joint plus the entropy of q. With all of q’s mass on μ, the expectation is just log p(μ, x), and the best μ is the posterior’s peak, since p(μ, x) = p(μ | x) p(x).
Say it“The ELBO is the expectation under q of log p of h and x, plus the entropy of q. If q is a Dirac delta at mu, the best mu maximises log p of h equals mu and x.”
Fast encoders

Predictive sparse decomposition

Running an optimiser for every input is slow. Kavukcuoglu, Ranzato and LeCun (2008) proposed predictive sparse decomposition (PSD): train a small feed-forward encoder f(x) to predict the sparse code, alongside the dictionary. Training still runs the optimiser, but the code is now pulled towards the encoder’s guess as well. After training, f(x) alone gives a good code in one pass, worth a number of optimiser steps, and it is a natural starting point if you want the exact code. PSD layers were used to pre-train convolutional networks for object recognition, and the idea of learning to approximate inference grew into LISTA and amortised inference in VAEs.

\[ \begin{aligned} \min_{\mathbf{h},\,\mathbf{D},\,f}\ &\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{D}\mathbf{h}\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert_1 \\ &+ \gamma\lVert\mathbf{h}-f(\mathbf{x})\rVert^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: three terms: rebuild x from the code, keep the code sparse, and keep the code close to what the encoder predicts. Training alternates: start h at f(x), minimise over h, then take gradient steps on D (rebuild better) and on f (predict that h better).
Say it“Minimise over h, D and f: the squared reconstruction error, plus lambda times the L1 norm of h, plus gamma times the squared distance between h and f of x.”
\[ f(\mathbf{x}) = S_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}\big) \]
Read it as: the encoder in this lab: a linear map U, a bias c, then the soft threshold S of the sparse section with a learned threshold θa per code unit, so it can output exact zeros. That is one step of the ISTA optimiser with learned weights, the form Gregor & LeCun (2010) unrolled into LISTA; the original PSD paper used g⊙tanh(Ux + c), which cannot output zeros. Here it has 3×2 + 3 + 3 = 12 numbers.
Say it“f of x is the soft threshold at theta of U x plus c.”

Real-world project

Project: topics in Wikipedia, atoms in Old Master portraits

Two real data sets, one for each half of the chapter. First, 68 English Wikipedia articles (text licensed CC BY-SA), ten each on planets and stars, musical instruments, infectious diseases, team sports, programming languages and food, plus eight “bridge” articles that straddle two fields, such as Computer music and Bioinformatics. Each article was cut to its first 2,500 words and turned into counts of 1,500 vocabulary words (English stop words removed; a word must appear in at least 3 and at most half of the articles). The page builds the tf–idf matrix and runs NMF on it live, from the same starting values as the Python reference.

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde x_{dt} &= \big(1+\log c_{dt}\big)\Big(1+\log\tfrac{1+N}{1+n_t}\Big) \\ x_{dt} &= \tilde x_{dt}\,/\,\lVert \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_d \rVert \quad (c_{dt}>0) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: cdt is how often word t occurs in article d. A damped count (the log stops one word repeated 80 times from dominating) times an inverse document frequency (rare words count more: nt of the N = 68 articles contain t); then each article’s row is divided by its length, so every row has length 1. Every entry is non-negative, so NMF applies.
Say it“x-d-t is one plus log of the count, times one plus log of N plus one over n-t plus one, divided by the row’s length.”

Atoms from portraits

Second, 20 portraits from the Metropolitan Museum of Art’s Open Access collection (public domain, CC0): self-portraits and portraits from the 1440s to the 1890s, from Florentine profiles to Rembrandt and Degas. Each was converted to grey and cropped to 192×240 pixels. From 17 of them, 47,141 overlapping 8×8 patches (each with its mean brightness removed) were used to learn a dictionary of 128 atoms with scikit-learn’s online dictionary learning (Mairal et al. 2010); that offline step takes about 20 seconds and its atoms are embedded in the page. The other three portraits are held out. Below, the page codes every 8×8 block of a held-out portrait live with OMP, using k atoms per block plus the block’s mean, and compares that with the same budget spent on PCA directions. The same project in scikit-learn

import json, glob, re, numpy as np
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer, TfidfTransformer
from sklearn.decomposition import NMF, MiniBatchDictionaryLearning, SparseCoder, PCA

# ---- Part 1: topics in 68 Wikipedia articles (CC BY-SA), fetched with the MediaWiki API ----
texts, titles = [], []
for f in sorted(glob.glob("wiki/doc_*.json")):
    page = list(json.load(open(f, encoding="utf8"))["query"]["pages"].values())[0]
    body = re.split(r"\n==+ *(See also|References|Notes|External links|Further reading|Bibliography"
                    r"|Sources|Citations|Footnotes) *==+", page["extract"])[0]
    body = re.sub(r"==+[^=]+==+", " ", body)
    texts.append(" ".join(body.split()[:2500])); titles.append(page["title"])
cv = CountVectorizer(stop_words="english", token_pattern=r"(?u)\b[a-zA-Z][a-zA-Z]+\b",
                     min_df=3, max_df=0.5, max_features=1500)
counts = cv.fit_transform(texts); vocab = cv.get_feature_names_out()
X = TfidfTransformer(sublinear_tf=True).fit_transform(counts).toarray()     # 68 x 1500, rows unit length

def u(t):   # the page's integer hash, so both sides start NMF from the same W0, H0
    t = (t * 2654435761) & 0xffffffff; t ^= t >> 15; t = (t * 2246822519) & 0xffffffff; t ^= t >> 13
    return t / 4294967296
def start(X, k):
    n, m = X.shape; s = np.sqrt(X.mean() / k)
    return (s * (0.05 + np.array([u(1 + i) for i in range(n * k)]))).reshape(n, k), \
           (s * (0.05 + np.array([u(1000003 + i) for i in range(k * m)]))).reshape(k, m)

print(f"X: {X.shape[0]} articles x {X.shape[1]} words, {counts.nnz} non-zero counts")
for loss in ["frobenius", "kullback-leibler"]:
    W0, H0 = start(X, 6)
    nmf = NMF(n_components=6, init="custom", solver="mu", beta_loss=loss, max_iter=300, tol=0)
    W = nmf.fit_transform(X, W=W0, H=H0); H = nmf.components_
    print(f"\nNMF k=6, {loss}, 300 updates: reconstruction_err_ = {nmf.reconstruction_err_:.6f}")
    if loss == "frobenius":
        for a in range(6): print(f"  topic {a+1}:", " ".join(vocab[np.argsort(-H[a])[:7]]))
        share = W * np.linalg.norm(H, axis=1); share /= share.sum(1, keepdims=True)
        for d in [60, 61, 65]:
            print(f"  {titles[d]:19s}", " ".join(f"{v:.2f}" for v in share[d]))

# ---- Part 2: an 8x8 patch dictionary from Met Open Access portraits (CC0) ----
TEST = [437397, 436158, 437859]            # Rembrandt, Degas, Florentine profile: held out
def load(i): return np.fromfile(f"met/img_{i}.g8", np.uint8).reshape(240, 192) / 255   # greyscale 192x240
def patches(a, step):
    return np.array([a[r:r+8, c:c+8].ravel() for r in range(0, 233, step) for c in range(0, 185, step)])
ids = sorted(int(f[-9:-3]) for f in glob.glob("met/img_*.g8"))
P = np.vstack([patches(load(i), 4) for i in ids if i not in TEST]); P -= P.mean(1, keepdims=True)
dl = MiniBatchDictionaryLearning(n_components=128, alpha=0.1, batch_size=256, max_iter=30, random_state=0).fit(P)
D = dl.components_; pca = PCA(n_components=64).fit(P); V = pca.components_
print(f"\ndictionary: 128 atoms learned from {len(P)} patches of {len(ids) - 3} portraits")
print("PSNR (dB) on held-out portraits, k numbers per 8x8 patch (plus its mean):")
for i in TEST:
    Y = patches(load(i), 8); dc = Y.mean(1, keepdims=True); Yc = Y - dc
    row = []
    for k in [1, 2, 4, 8]:

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • PCA, k-means, NMF, archetypal analysis and sparse coding all write X ≈ WH; the constraints on W and H decide whether the blocks are ghosts, prototypes, parts, extremes or atoms.
  • NMF forbids negatives, so blocks can only add: parts and topics emerge. Fit it by multiplicative updates (squared error or Poisson/KL) or by HALS; expect local optima and, when parts are not separable, many equally good answers (the cone).
  • Archetypes are convex mixtures of data that mix back into the data: they sit on the convex hull, the opposite of k-means’ central prototypes.
  • Sparse coding puts the penalty on the representation: the code is the MAP estimate under a Laplace prior, a lasso solved for every input, and the dictionary needs a length cap or it grows for ever.
  • MAP keeps the posterior’s peak, which is exactly sparse, and ignores its spread and the explaining-away tilt; PSD trains a fast encoder to predict those codes, trading a little accuracy for one-pass inference.

Check your understanding

Why do NMF’s blocks look like parts while PCA’s look like ghosts?

PCA’s blocks are orthogonal and can have negative entries, so a face is built by adding and cancelling global patterns. NMF allows only non-negative blocks and weights; nothing can be cancelled, so the economical solution is localised pieces that are switched on and off.

A multiplicative update leaves an entry of H at exactly zero. Can a later update bring it back?

No. The update multiplies the current value by a ratio, and zero times anything is zero. That is why multiplicative updates start from strictly positive values, and why scikit-learn sets tiny values to zero only for losses where that improves stability.

Your 2-D data all lie between the rays at 30° and 60°. Give two different exact NMF factorisations with k = 2.

Use h1, h2 along the 30° and 60° rays, or along the x and y axes. Both cones contain every point, so each point is a non-negative mix of either pair and the error is zero in both cases.

In dictionary learning, what goes wrong if the atoms’ lengths are not constrained?

Multiplying an atom by c and dividing its coefficients by c leaves Dh unchanged but divides the L1 penalty by c. The optimiser keeps doing this: atoms grow, codes shrink towards zero, and the sparsity penalty stops meaning anything.

Why is a MAP code from a Laplace prior often exactly sparse, while the posterior mean never is?

The log-prior −(λ/2)|hi| has a kink at zero, so the peak of the posterior can sit exactly on an axis. The posterior mean averages over the whole posterior, which has positive density on both sides of zero, so it is pulled off the axis.
