Representation Learning

Pretraining, semi-supervised and multitask learning: what pictures without labels can teach a network

Labels are expensive; raw data is nearly free. A network that first learns to describe its inputs, from unlabelled examples alone, can then learn a task from very few labels. The idea

What makes a representation good?

The same information can be written down in many ways, and some ways make a job easy while others make it hard. Looking up a word in a dictionary is quick because the entries are sorted; the same entries in random order hold exactly the same information and are nearly useless. In machine learning the written-down form of an input is its representation h(x), and the job is usually a simple model on top of it, often a linear classifier. A good representation is one that makes the next learning step easy: fewer labels, a simpler model, better generalisation.

Every deep network already learns one. Trained with labels, its last hidden layer becomes a description in which the classes can be split by the softmax on top, a linear model. This chapter is about learning such descriptions on purpose, and especially about learning them from data that has no labels. First, a picture of why it matters. Below, two classes lie on two noisy rings. Switch between three descriptions of the same points and watch the same logistic regression fail or succeed.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(y = 1 \mid \mathbf x) &= \sigma\big(\mathbf w^\top \mathbf h(\mathbf x) + b\big) \\ \sigma(z) &= \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the classifier never sees x itself, only its description h(x). It weighs the description’s entries, adds a bias, and squashes the total into a probability. Its decision boundary w⊤h + b = 0 is a straight line in h-space; what that line looks like back in x-space depends entirely on h. Here h is either (x₁, x₂), the polar pair (r, θ) with θ rescaled to [−1, 1), or the squares (x₁², x₂²). The weights are fitted by Newton’s method on the log-loss with a small penalty 10−3‖w‖².
Say it“The probability of class one is the sigmoid of w transpose h of x, plus b.”

Pretraining · 2006

Greedy layer-wise unsupervised pretraining

In 2006 deep networks were hard to train from random weights. Hinton, Osindero and Teh, and soon Bengio and colleagues and Ranzato and colleagues, found a way round it: build the network one layer at a time, each trained as a small unsupervised model of the layer below. Train an autoencoder on the pixels; keep its encoder as layer 1. Feed every image through layer 1 and train a second autoencoder on those codes; keep its encoder as layer 2. Repeat. It is greedy because each layer is optimised on its own, given the ones beneath it, and never revisited; layer-wise because it proceeds a layer at a time; unsupervised because no labels are used; and pretraining because it is only the first stage. In the second stage, fine-tuning, a classifier is put on top and the whole stack is trained with labels, starting from the pretrained weights. (Alternatively the stack is frozen and only the classifier on top is trained.)

Here the layers are denoising autoencoders (Vincent et al., 2008): each must rebuild its clean input from a copy with a share of the entries set to zero, which stops it from merely copying. Train layer 1 a few epochs on 4,000 unlabelled characters, then stack layer 2, and watch the filters change from noise to strokes.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf h^{(k)} &= f_k\big(\mathbf h^{(k-1)}\big) \\ &= \tanh\big(W_k^\top \mathbf h^{(k-1)} + \mathbf b_k\big) \\ \theta_k &= \arg\min_{\theta}\; \frac1N \sum_{i} L\big(\mathbf h_i^{(k-1)},\, \hat{\mathbf h}_i\big) \\ \hat{\mathbf h}_i &= g_k\big(f_k(\tilde{\mathbf h}_i^{(k-1)})\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: h(0) is the image x. Layer k turns the code below it into a new code with weights Wk and biases bk. Its parameters θk (encoder fk and a throw-away decoder gk) are chosen so that the decoder’s output ĥi, computed from the encoding of a corrupted copy h̃(k−1), rebuilds the clean code h(k−1). Layers below k are frozen while layer k trains.
Say it“Each layer's parameters minimise the average loss between the clean input and the decoding of the encoded, corrupted input.”
\[ \begin{aligned} L_1(\mathbf x, \mathbf z) &= \sum_{j=1}^{196} \big[\ln(1 + e^{z_j}) - x_j z_j\big] \\ L_2(\mathbf h, \mathbf r) &= \tfrac12 \Vert \mathbf h - \mathbf r \Vert^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: layer 1 rebuilds pixels in [0, 1], so its decoder outputs logits z and pays the cross-entropy of a sigmoid output, written in a numerically safe form: it is smallest when sigmoid(zj) equals xj. Layer 2 rebuilds tanh codes in [−1, 1], so it pays half the squared error. Both are reported per image.
Say it“Layer one pays the sigmoid cross-entropy summed over pixels; layer two pays half the squared distance.”

Why it works, and when it doesn’t

When does unsupervised pretraining help?

Pretraining is thought to help in two ways. First, as a starting point: the pretrained weights put the network in a region of parameter space that gradient descent would rarely reach from random weights, and fine-tuning stays nearby. Erhan and colleagues (2010) found that pretrained networks end up computing functions that are more alike from run to run, and generalise better, much as a regulariser would; but unlike weight decay, how strongly it regularises cannot be dialled. Second, as knowledge of the inputs: a description that is good for rebuilding x may also be good for predicting y, if the two depend on the same underlying structure. Nothing guarantees that: a feature can be essential for reconstruction and useless for the label, and vice versa.

So pretraining helps most when labelled examples are few and unlabelled ones plentiful, when the raw representation is poor (one-hot words, where every pair of words is equally far apart, gained enormously from learned word embeddings), and when the function to be learned is complicated. It helps less, or not at all, as labels become plentiful. Since about 2012, ReLUs, dropout, later batch normalisation, and large labelled sets have let vision networks train well from scratch, and greedy pretraining fell out of use there; its heir is today’s self-supervised pretraining of language and vision models, which is not greedy but keeps the same two-stage idea. The cleanest way to see the effect is to strip the classifier down to the bare minimum, nearest neighbour, so that only the description matters. Slide the label budget and compare raw pixels with the learned code.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat y(\mathbf x) &= y_{i^\ast} \\ i^\ast &= \arg\min_{i \in \mathcal L}\; \big\Vert \mathbf h(\mathbf x) - \mathbf h(\mathbf x_i) \big\Vert^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ℒ is the set of labelled training characters. A test character gets the label of the labelled character nearest to it, where “near” is measured in the representation h: the 196 pixels, or the 64-number code of the stack pretrained on 60,000 unlabelled characters (the hero’s weights). There is nothing to fit, so any difference between the two columns is the representation’s doing.

Using unlabelled data directly

Semi-supervised learning: when p(x) knows about y

In semi-supervised learning a few examples come with labels, drawn from p(x, y), and many come without, drawn from p(x). Pretraining is one way to use the unlabelled ones; another is to fit a single model to both at once, sharing parameters between a model of p(x) and the classifier p(y | x) and trading their two losses off with a weight. Both rest on the same bet: that the structure of p(x) says something about p(y | x). A common form of the bet is the cluster assumption: examples in the same dense clump share a label, so the decision boundary should run through empty space.

When is the bet good? Think about what causes the data. If the label is one of the causes of x (the writer meant to write this character, and the pixels follow), then p(x) is a mixture of one component per label, and learning p(x) well means discovering the labels’ clumps. If instead y is unrelated to how x is generated, p(x) carries no information about it, and unlabelled data can only mislead. The ideal representation separates the underlying causes of x, so that whichever one is y can be read off easily. Below, a two-class Gaussian model is fitted by expectation–maximisation to a handful of labelled points and many unlabelled ones. Try both worlds, and slide the weight α given to the unlabelled points.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell(\theta) = &\sum_{(\mathbf x, y) \in \mathcal L} \ln p_\theta(\mathbf x, y) \\ &+ \alpha \sum_{\mathbf x \in \mathcal U} \ln \sum_{y} p_\theta(\mathbf x, y) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ℒ holds the labelled points and U the unlabelled ones. The model is p(x, y) = πy N(x; μy, Σ): a share, a centre and a covariance (shared by the classes). Labelled points count with their known class; unlabelled points count through the total probability of either class, weighted by α. With α = 0 this is ordinary supervised fitting. EM climbs it (together with the weak prior on Σ described on the right): guess each unlabelled point’s class probabilities, refit π, μ and Σ with those as weights, repeat.
Say it“The log-likelihood is the sum over labelled points of log p of x and y, plus alpha times the sum over unlabelled points of log of the sum over y.”
\[ p(y \mid \mathbf x) = \frac{p(\mathbf x \mid y)\, p(y)}{\sum_{y'} p(\mathbf x \mid y')\, p(y')} \]
Read it as: the classifier is not fitted separately; it falls out of the model of how x is generated. That is why unlabelled x can move it: anything that changes the class-conditional densities p(x | y) moves the boundary. When y really is a cause of x, the clumps of p(x) are the p(x | y), and the boundary lands in the gap between them. (To keep a covariance estimable from one point per class, Σ carries a weak prior worth one extra point of spread 0.05 in every direction.)
Say it“The probability of y given x is p of x given y times p of y, over the same summed over all classes.”

Sharing between tasks

Multitask learning: one trunk, many heads

Labels for one task are scarce, but there may be several related tasks, each with a few labels of its own. Multitask learning (Caruana, 1997) trains them together: the lower layers, the trunk, are shared and receive gradient from every task; each task keeps a small head of its own. The shared parameters are estimated from all the examples pooled, so they are pinned down far better than any one task could manage, and the per-task heads have few parameters left to fit. The bet is that the tasks depend on a common set of underlying factors. If they do not, sharing hurts: negative transfer.

Multitask learning sits beside its sequential cousin, transfer learning (the previous chapter): there, a trunk trained on one task is handed to another; here, the tasks train side by side. Russell and Norvig describe both as ways to reuse what one task taught for another, and the two are often combined: a large network pretrained on general data, then fine-tuned on several targets at once. Below are up to 24 small regression tasks. Each is a different mix of the same two hidden curves, sampled at only a few noisy points. Fit them one at a time, then with a shared trunk, and change how many tasks there are and how much they really share.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat f_t(x) &= \mathbf b_t^\top A^\top \boldsymbol\phi(x) \\ \min_{A,\,B}\;& \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(y_{ti} - \hat f_t(x_{ti})\big)^2 \\ &+ \lambda\big(\Vert A\Vert_F^2 + \Vert B\Vert_F^2\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: φ(x) is a fixed set of 12 bumps (11 Gaussian bumps across [−1, 1] and a constant). The trunk A (12 × K) turns them into K shared features A⊤φ(x); task t’s head bt mixes those K features. Every task’s error pushes on A, so A is learned from all T × n points. Fitting each task alone means a full 12-weight ridge regression per task from its own n points, with the same λ = 0.1. The shared fit alternates: heads given the trunk, then the trunk given the heads, 100 rounds, each an exact ridge solve.
Say it“Task t's prediction is its head b-t dotted with the shared features A transpose phi of x; minimise the total squared error over all tasks plus lambda times the size of A and B.”

Why codes beat categories

Distributed representations: many features at once

There are two ways to describe something with n features. A symbolic or one-hot description switches on exactly one of them: “this is prototype 3”. Clustering, nearest-prototype methods, the leaves of a decision tree and n-gram tables all work this way, and n features can tell apart only n kinds of input, each learned from the examples that fall in it. A distributed description switches on many features together: “round, open at the top, with a hook”. Each feature splits the input space in two, and n of them together split it into far more regions than n, each named by its pattern of ons and offs.

That is a statistical bargain. Each feature is learned from all the examples on either side of its boundary, not just the ones in one cell, so a distributed model can generalise to regions it has never seen an example from, by recombining features it has. The price is an assumption: that the world really can be described by features that vary somewhat independently. A deep network’s hidden layer is a distributed code; so is the 64-number code of the hero. Drag the lines, change their number, and switch to prototypes to count the regions.

\[ R(n, d) = \sum_{j=0}^{d} \binom{n}{j} \]
Read it as: n hyperplanes in d dimensions, in general position, cut space into at most R(n, d) regions. In the plane (d = 2) that is 1 + n + n(n − 1)/2, which grows like n²; in d dimensions it grows like nd, and once d ≥ n it is the full 2n. The model needs only n(d + 1) numbers to place its n hyperplanes, while n prototypes, with n × d numbers, give exactly n regions.
Say it“R of n and d is the sum, for j from zero to d, of n choose j.”

What to ask of a representation

Clues to the underlying causes

The ideal representation would disentangle the causes behind the data: one group of features for the writer’s intended character, another for stroke thickness, another for slant, so that any task needing one of them finds it ready to read. But a learner given only x has no way to know which of the endless possible descriptions is the “right” one; no algorithm is best for every possible world. What it needs are clues: general assumptions about how the world tends to be, built into the model or its training objective. Most of this book’s regularisers are one or more of these.

Clues also decide what counts as salient. An autoencoder trained on squared pixel error spends its capacity on whatever covers many pixels; a small object that matters enormously for a task, a ball in a game scene, say, covers few pixels and may simply be left out. Change the loss (a learned discriminator, as in adversarial training, or a supervised signal) and a different set of factors becomes worth keeping.

One clue in action. The two sensors below each record a blend of a slow cause and a fast one. Principal component analysis looks for the direction of greatest variance, and so picks whichever blend is loudest. Slow feature analysis (Wiskott & Sejnowski, 2002) instead asks for the combination that changes least from one moment to the next, the temporal-coherence clue. Make the fast cause loud, and change the angle between the two causes.

\[ \begin{aligned} \min_{\mathbf w}\;\; & \mathbb E_t\big[(\mathbf w^\top \mathbf x_{t+1} - \mathbf w^\top \mathbf x_t)^2\big] \\ \text{subject to}\;\; & \mathbb E_t[\mathbf w^\top \mathbf x_t] = 0 \\ \text{and}\;\; & \mathbb E_t[(\mathbf w^\top \mathbf x_t)^2] = 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: among all ways of combining the sensors into one signal with mean 0 and variance 1, pick the one whose step-to-step change is smallest. The constraint stops the trivial answer w = 0. It is solved exactly: whiten x (so that every direction has variance 1), then take the direction in which the differences xt+1 − xt have the least variance. PCA instead maximises E[(u⊤x)²] with ‖u‖ = 1.
Say it“Minimise the expected squared change of w transpose x from one step to the next, keeping its mean at zero and its variance at one.”

Real-world project

Project: reading Kuzushiji with 1% of the labels

Kuzushiji-MNIST (KMNIST; Clanuwat et al., 2018) has 70,000 handwritten characters of ten classes, cut from scanned pre-modern Japanese books: 60,000 for training and 10,000 for testing. It is harder than the familiar digits: a class can gather several historical ways of writing the same character, which can look quite different. The ten classes, as named in the dataset’s own class map, are . Every image here is shrunk from 28 × 28 to 14 × 14 pixels by averaging 2 × 2 blocks, and stored at 16 grey levels.

The question is the one this chapter keeps asking: given only 1% of the labels (60 per class), how much do unlabelled characters help, and by which route? Five networks with the same shape, 196 → 128 → 64 → 10 with tanh units, are trained with the same Adam settings (learning rate 10−3, batches of 50, 30 epochs) and the same starting weights wherever they start from scratch:

  1. Scratch: the 600 labelled characters only.
  2. Pretrain + fine-tune: layers 1 and 2 are first trained as a stack of denoising autoencoders (8 epochs each, 25% of inputs masked) on all the training characters, labels unused; then the whole network is fine-tuned on the 600.
  3. Pretrain, frozen: the same stack, kept fixed; only the 64 → 10 classifier is trained (learning rate 10−2), a linear probe.
  4. Multitask: from scratch, but layer 1 also feeds a decoder that must rebuild 50 unlabelled characters at every step, so every step pays the loss below.
  5. Pretrain + multitask: both: start from the stack and keep its decoder’s reconstruction task during fine-tuning.
\[ \begin{aligned} J(\theta) = &\frac{1}{|\mathcal B|} \sum_{(\mathbf x, y) \in \mathcal B} -\ln p_\theta(y \mid \mathbf x) \\ &+ \frac{\lambda}{|\mathcal B_u|} \sum_{\mathbf x \in \mathcal B_u} L_1\big(\mathbf x, g(\mathbf h^{(1)})\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each step takes a batch ℬ of 50 labelled characters and a batch ℬu of 50 characters drawn at random from the training pool, labels ignored. The first term is the usual classification loss; the second asks layer 1’s code to stay good enough for the decoder g to rebuild the image, with the reconstruction cross-entropy L₁ from the greedy section. The two tasks share layer 1, the trunk; the classifier and the decoder are their heads. λ sets how much the reconstruction task counts.
Say it“J is the average classification loss on the labelled batch plus lambda times the average reconstruction loss on the unlabelled batch.”

"""Shared numpy code for chapter x064 (representation learning). Every random number comes from mulberry32
streams, generated in bulk, so the page's JavaScript can repeat each run draw for draw."""
import numpy as np
M32 = np.uint64(0xFFFFFFFF)
class Stream:
    def __init__(s, seed): s.seed, s.k = seed & 0xFFFFFFFF, 0
    def uniform(s, n):
        j = np.arange(s.k + 1, s.k + 1 + n, dtype=np.uint64); s.k += n
        a = (np.uint64(s.seed) + j * np.uint64(0x6D2B79F5)) & M32
        t = ((a ^ (a >> np.uint64(15))) * (a | np.uint64(1))) & M32
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> np.uint64(7))) * (t | np.uint64(61))) & M32)) & M32) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> np.uint64(14))) & M32).astype(np.float64) / 4294967296.0
    def gauss(s, shape):
        n = int(np.prod(shape)); d = s.uniform(2 * n); u, v = d[0::2], d[1::2]
        assert (u > 0).all()
        return (np.sqrt(-2 * np.log(u)) * np.cos(2 * np.pi * v)).reshape(shape)
    def perm(s, n):
        p = list(range(n)); d = s.uniform(n - 1)
        for idx, i in enumerate(range(n - 1, 0, -1)):
            j = int(d[idx] * (i + 1)); p[i], p[j] = p[j], p[i]
        return np.array(p)
    def ints(s, n, hi): return (s.uniform(n) * hi).astype(np.int64)

sig = lambda t: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-t))
class Adam:
    def __init__(s, P, lr): s.P, s.lr, s.m, s.v, s.b1, s.b2 = P, lr, [np.zeros_like(p) for p in P], [np.zeros_like(p) for p in P], 1.0, 1.0
    def step(s, G):
        s.b1 *= 0.9; s.b2 *= 0.999
        for p, g, m, v in zip(s.P, G, s.m, s.v):
            m[...] = 0.9 * m + 0.1 * g; v[...] = 0.999 * v + 0.001 * (g * g)
            p -= s.lr * (m / (1 - s.b1)) / (np.sqrt(v / (1 - s.b2)) + 1e-8)

def init_w(st, a, b): return st.gauss((a, b)) / np.sqrt(a)

def dae(X, H, out, epochs, seed, noise=0.25, lr=2e-3, bs=50, log=None):
    """one denoising autoencoder layer: tanh encoder, sigmoid+cross-entropy ("sig") or linear+squared-error ("lin") decoder"""
    sI, sO, sN = Stream(seed), Stream(seed + 1), Stream(seed + 2); D = X.shape[1]
    W, b = init_w(sI, D, H), np.zeros(H); V, c = init_w(sI, H, D), np.zeros(D); A = Adam([W, b, V, c], lr); hist = []
    for e in range(epochs):
        for i in sO.perm(len(X)).reshape(-1, bs):
            x = X[i]; xin = x * (sN.uniform(x.size).reshape(x.shape) >= noise)
            h = np.tanh(xin @ W + b); r = h @ V + c
            if out == "sig": r = sig(r)
            dr = (r - x) / bs; dh = (dr @ V.T) * (1 - h * h)
            A.step([xin.T @ dh, dh.sum(0), h.T @ dr, dr.sum(0)])
        hist.append(recon_loss(X, W, b, V, c, out))
        if log: log(f"  layer {D}->{H} epoch {e + 1}: loss {hist[-1]:.4f}")
    return dict(W=W, b=b, V=V, c=c, hist=hist)

def recon_loss(X, W, b, V, c, out):
    r = np.tanh(X @ W + b) @ V + c
    if out == "sig":
        return float(np.mean(np.sum(np.logaddexp(0, r) - X * r, 1)))   # cross-entropy with logits, nats per image
    return float(np.mean(0.5 * np.sum((r - X) ** 2, 1)))

def fwd(P, x):
    h1 = np.tanh(x @ P[0] + P[1]); h2 = np.tanh(h1 @ P[2] + P[3]); s = h2 @ P[4] + P[5]
    s = s - s.max(1, keepdims=True); p = np.exp(s); p /= p.sum(1, keepdims=True); return h1, h2, p
def acc(P, X, y): return float(np.mean(fwd(P, X)[2].argmax(1) == y))

def new_net(seed, H1, H2, pre=None):
    st = Stream(seed)
    P = [init_w(st, 196, H1), np.zeros(H1), init_w(st, H1, H2), np.zeros(H2)]
    head = [init_w(st, H2, 10), np.zeros(10)]
    if pre is not None: P = [pre[0].copy(), pre[1].copy(), pre[2].copy(), pre[3].copy()]
    return P + head

def train(P, XL, yL, epochs, seed, lr=1e-3, bs=50, lam=0.0, dec=None, XU=None, bu=50, freeze=False, test=None):
    """cross-entropy on the labelled set; with lam > 0 also lam x reconstruction cross-entropy of unlabelled images from h1"""
    sO, sU = Stream(seed), Stream(seed + 1); bs = min(bs, len(XL))
    params = P + (dec if lam else []); A = Adam(params, lr); curve = []
    for e in range(epochs):
        for i in sO.perm(len(XL)).reshape(-1, bs):
            x, y = XL[i], yL[i]; h1, h2, p = fwd(P, x); ds = p.copy(); ds[np.arange(len(i)), y] -= 1; ds /= len(i)
            G = [None] * 6; G[4] = h2.T @ ds; G[5] = ds.sum(0); dh2 = (ds @ P[4].T) * (1 - h2 * h2)
            G[2] = h1.T @ dh2; G[3] = dh2.sum(0); dh1 = (dh2 @ P[2].T) * (1 - h1 * h1); G[0] = x.T @ dh1; G[1] = dh1.sum(0)
            if freeze: G[0], G[1], G[2], G[3] = G[0] * 0, G[1] * 0, G[2] * 0, G[3] * 0
            if lam:
                u = XU[sU.ints(bu, len(XU))]; hu = np.tanh(u @ P[0] + P[1]); r = sig(hu @ dec[0] + dec[1]); dr = lam * (r - u) / bu
                dhu = (dr @ dec[0].T) * (1 - hu * hu); G[0] = G[0] + u.T @ dhu; G[1] = G[1] + dhu.sum(0)
                G = G + [hu.T @ dr, dr.sum(0)]
            A.step(G)
        if test is not None: curve.append(acc(P, *test))
    return P, curve

def unpack4(b64, n):
    import base64
    raw = np.frombuffer(base64.b64decode(b64), dtype=np.uint8)
    q = np.empty(raw.size * 2, np.uint8); q[0::2] = raw >> 4; q[1::2] = raw & 15
    return q[: n * 196].reshape(n, 196)
def pack4(Q):
    import base64
    f = Q.reshape(-1); f = np.concatenate([f, np.zeros(f.size % 2, np.uint8)])
    return base64.b64encode(((f[0::2] << 4) | f[1::2]).astype(np.uint8)).decode()

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A representation is good when it makes the next learning step easy; every deep network learns one, and representation learning sets out to learn it on purpose, often from unlabelled data.
  • Greedy layer-wise pretraining trains a stack of unsupervised layers one at a time, then fine-tunes the whole stack with labels; it helps most when labels are few, as a starting point that regularises and as knowledge of p(x).
  • Unlabelled data helps only if p(x) carries information about p(y | x), as when y is one of the causes of x; when the label cuts across the data’s structure, semi-supervised learning can make things worse.
  • Multitask learning shares a trunk between tasks so that every task’s examples train it; it pays off when the tasks share underlying factors, and causes negative transfer when they do not.
  • Distributed codes name exponentially many regions with few parameters; which factors a representation keeps depends on the clues built into it: smoothness, sparsity, slowness, shared factors, depth and more.

Check your understanding

Why is greedy layer-wise pretraining called “greedy”, and what does fine-tuning add?

Each layer is trained to do well on its own local objective (rebuilding the layer below), with the lower layers frozen and without looking ahead to what the layers above or the final task will need. Fine-tuning then trains all layers jointly on the real objective, so the layers can adjust to one another and to the labels.

You have 2 million labelled images and 5 million unlabelled ones. Would you expect unsupervised pretraining of the old greedy kind to help much?

Probably little. Pretraining helps most when labelled data is scarce relative to the model’s needs; with millions of labels, modern networks train well from scratch, and the full-scale curve in the “when” section shows the gap shrinking as the label budget grows. Self-supervised pretraining at very large scale can still help, but that is a different regime.

Give a case where unlabelled data should not help a classifier, and say why.

When the label is unrelated to how x is generated, for instance when it cuts straight across the clumps of p(x), as in the second world of the semi-supervised lab. Then learning p(x) better tells you nothing about where the class boundary lies, and a model that ties p(y | x) to p(x) is pulled towards the wrong boundary.

Ten tasks each have 6 examples. Why can a shared trunk beat ten separate models, and when can it lose?

The trunk’s parameters are estimated from all 60 examples, and each head then has only K weights to fit from its 6 examples, rather than the full model. It loses when the tasks do not share the factors the trunk is forced to use (negative transfer), or when the trunk is too narrow to hold the factors they do share.

How many regions can 8 lines make in the plane, and how many can 8 prototypes make? What does that say about generalisation?

Up to 1 + 8 + 28 = 37 regions for lines, exactly 8 for prototypes. A distributed code can give distinct responses to many more regions than it has features, and each feature is learned from examples on both sides of it, so it can respond sensibly in regions where no training example fell.
