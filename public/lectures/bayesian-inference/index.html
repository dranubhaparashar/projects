Bayesian Inference & Conjugate Priors

Beliefs that update, one observation at a time

Maximum likelihood hands you one number. Bayesian inference hands you a whole distribution of belief over the unknown, and every new observation reshapes it. For the right choice of prior the reshaping is pure bookkeeping: you add counts. On the right is a coin with a hidden bias. The idea

Bayes' rule for an unknown parameter

In everyday statistics a probability is a long-run frequency: toss a coin a million times and count. But we also use probability for things that happen once, or that are simply unknown: whether this coin is bent, what tomorrow's click rate will be. The Bayesian view treats probability as a degree of belief and lets us put a distribution on a parameter itself. Before any data, the prior p(θ) says which values of θ we find plausible. The data's likelihood p(D | θ) says how well each value explains what we saw. Bayes' rule multiplies the two and rescales, giving the posterior p(θ | D).

The frequentist answer is different in kind: pick an estimator, such as maximum likelihood, and describe its uncertainty by imagining the experiment repeated (the bootstrap does this by resampling the data). Neither view is "wrong", but they can disagree loudly on small data. Toss a coin three times and get three heads: maximum likelihood says θ = 1, a coin that can never land tails. Any sensible prior refuses to believe that. Pick a prior and move the counts to see the three curves interact.

\[ \begin{aligned} \underbrace{p(\theta \mid D)}_{\text{posterior}} &= \frac{\overbrace{p(D \mid \theta)}^{\text{likelihood}}\ \overbrace{p(\theta)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{p(D)}_{\text{evidence}}} \\ p(D) &= \int p(D \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)\, d\theta \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: belief after the data equals how well each θ explains the data, times how plausible θ was beforehand, divided by a constant that makes the result integrate to 1. The constant p(D), the evidence, is the probability of the data averaged over the prior; we will use it in the project to judge priors.
Say it“The posterior of theta given D is the likelihood of D given theta, times the prior of theta, over the evidence.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(D \mid \theta) &= \theta^{\,h}\,(1-\theta)^{\,t} \\ \hat\theta_{\text{ML}} &= \frac{h}{h+t} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for h heads and t tails in a fixed order, each head contributes a factor θ and each tail (1 − θ). Seen as a function of θ this is not a probability distribution (its area is not 1). Its peak, the maximum-likelihood estimate, is the fraction of heads.
Say it“The likelihood is theta to the h times one minus theta to the t; its maximum is h over h plus t.”

Conjugacy

Beta–Bernoulli: updating a coin one toss at a time

The likelihood is a power of θ times a power of (1 − θ). If the prior has the same shape, the product does too, and the posterior stays in the same family. That family is the Beta distribution. A prior with this property is called conjugate to the likelihood, and it turns inference into arithmetic: a Beta(a, b) prior plus h heads and t tails gives a Beta(a + h, b + t) posterior. So a and b behave like imaginary tosses you saw before the experiment, a of them heads and b tails.

Because each posterior is the prior for the next toss, you can update one toss at a time or all at once; the order never matters. Set a prior, then toss: by hand, or ten at a time from a coin whose bias you choose. The faint curves are the earlier posteriors.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{Beta}(\theta \mid a, b) &= \frac{\Gamma(a+b)}{\Gamma(a)\,\Gamma(b)} \\ &\quad \times \theta^{\,a-1} (1-\theta)^{\,b-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a density on 0 ≤ θ ≤ 1 with two shape knobs, a and b. The fraction of Gamma functions (Γ(n) = (n − 1)! for whole numbers) only makes the area 1. a = b = 1 is flat; large equal a and b is a narrow bump at ½; a, b < 1 piles the mass at the two ends.
Say it“Beta of theta given a and b is gamma of a plus b over gamma a gamma b, times theta to the a minus one, times one minus theta to the b minus one.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\theta] &= \frac{a}{a+b} \\ \operatorname{mode}[\theta] &= \frac{a-1}{a+b-2} \\ \operatorname{var}[\theta] &= \frac{ab}{(a+b)^2\,(a+b+1)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the mean is the share of imaginary heads. The mode (the peak, defined when a, b > 1) is the share after removing one head and one tail. The variance shrinks roughly like 1/(a + b): the more tosses, real or imaginary, the narrower the belief.
Say it“The mean is a over a plus b; the mode is a minus one over a plus b minus two; the variance is a b over a plus b squared times a plus b plus one.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(\theta \mid D) &\propto \theta^{\,h+a-1}(1-\theta)^{\,t+b-1} \\ \theta \mid D &\sim \mathrm{Beta}(a+h,\ b+t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: multiply the likelihood θh(1 − θ)t by the Beta prior and the exponents add. The result is a Beta again, with the real counts added to the imaginary ones. Nothing else about the data matters: h and t are sufficient statistics.
Say it“Given the data, theta is Beta with a plus h and b plus t.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid D] &= \underbrace{\frac{a+b}{a+b+N}}_{\text{weight on prior}} \cdot \frac{a}{a+b} \\ &\quad + \underbrace{\frac{N}{a+b+N}}_{\text{weight on data}} \cdot \frac{h}{N} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and the ML estimate, with weights in proportion to imaginary tosses (a + b) and real tosses (N = h + t). This pull towards the prior mean is called shrinkage. As N grows the data's weight goes to 1 and Bayes agrees with maximum likelihood.
Say it“The posterior mean is the prior mean and the data's mean, weighted by a plus b imaginary tosses and N real ones.”

One more property, true for any prior and any model: if you average the posterior mean over all the data sets the prior thinks possible, you get back the prior mean. And on average the posterior is narrower than the prior: the prior variance splits into the average posterior variance plus the spread of the posterior means. Learning cannot be expected to make you more uncertain, though one surprising toss sometimes does.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\theta] &= \mathbb{E}_D\big[\,\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid D]\,\big] \\ \operatorname{var}[\theta] &= \mathbb{E}_D\big[\operatorname{var}[\theta \mid D]\big] \\ &\quad + \operatorname{var}_D\big[\,\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid D]\,\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the outer expectations run over data sets D drawn the way the prior predicts (first θ from the prior, then tosses from θ). The second identity says the prior's variance is shared between what the data will leave uncertain and how much the data will move the mean. Both terms are non-negative, so the expected posterior variance is at most the prior variance.
Say it“The prior mean is the average posterior mean; the prior variance is the average posterior variance plus the variance of the posterior mean.”
Prediction

The posterior predictive: what happens next?

Usually we don't want θ for its own sake; we want to predict the next toss. The Bayesian answer averages the prediction of every possible θ, each weighted by its posterior belief. For the coin this averaging has a neat closed form: the probability of heads next is just the posterior mean. With a flat prior it is (h + 1)/(N + 2), the rule of succession that Laplace used to ask whether the sun will rise tomorrow.

Predicting several tosses at once shows the real difference. Plugging in one estimate θ̂ pretends we know θ exactly; the posterior predictive keeps our doubt about θ, so it spreads its bets wider. Change the evidence and the number of future tosses, and compare the two bar charts.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(x = 1 \mid D) &= \int_0^1 \theta\, p(\theta \mid D)\, d\theta \\ &= \frac{h + a}{N + a + b} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the chance that the next toss is heads is the average of θ over the posterior, which is the posterior mean. It is never exactly 0 or 1 while a and b are positive, however lopsided the data.
Say it“The probability the next toss is heads is h plus a over N plus a plus b.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &p(k \text{ heads in } n \mid D) \\ &\quad = \binom{n}{k} \frac{B(k + a',\ n - k + b')}{B(a',\ b')} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: here a′ = a + h and b′ = b + t are the posterior's parameters and B(x, y) = Γ(x)Γ(y)/Γ(x + y) is the Beta function. This is the beta-binomial distribution: a binomial whose θ is itself uncertain. It is always wider than the binomial with θ fixed at its mean.
Say it“The chance of k heads in n more tosses is n choose k times B of k plus a-prime and n minus k plus b-prime, over B of a-prime and b-prime.”

More than two outcomes

Dirichlet–multinomial, and where add-k smoothing comes from

A die, a word, a product category: many variables have K outcomes, not two. The parameter is now a probability vector μ = (μ1, …, μK) that sums to 1, a point in a triangle when K = 3 (a simplex). The conjugate prior is the Dirichlet, the Beta's big sibling, with one pseudo-count αk per outcome. The posterior again just adds the observed counts mk.

Its predictive probability is the famous smoothing rule of language models and spam filters: add k to every count. Laplace's add-one is a flat Dirichlet prior; add-½ is the Jeffreys prior; add-nothing is maximum likelihood, which gives every unseen outcome probability zero. Add observations, roll the die, and turn α. The triangle shows the posterior over μ: each corner is a die that always shows that face.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol\mu \mid \boldsymbol\alpha) &= \frac{\Gamma(\alpha_0)}{\prod_k \Gamma(\alpha_k)} \prod_{k=1}^{K} \mu_k^{\alpha_k - 1} \\ \alpha_0 &= \textstyle\sum_k \alpha_k \\ p(\boldsymbol\mu \mid D) &= \mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol\mu \mid \boldsymbol\alpha + \mathbf{m}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the multinomial likelihood is ∏μkmk, a product of powers, and the Dirichlet is a product of powers too, so the posterior adds counts to pseudo-counts. With K = 2 this is exactly the Beta.
Say it“Dirichlet of mu given alpha is gamma of alpha-nought over the product of gamma alpha-k, times the product of mu-k to the alpha-k minus one; the posterior is Dirichlet with alpha plus m.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\mu_k] &= \frac{\alpha_k}{\alpha_0} \\ \operatorname{mode}[\mu_k] &= \frac{\alpha_k - 1}{\alpha_0 - K} \\ \operatorname{var}[\mu_k] &= \frac{\alpha_k(\alpha_0 - \alpha_k)}{\alpha_0^2(\alpha_0+1)} \\ \operatorname{cov}[\mu_j, \mu_k] &= -\frac{\alpha_j \alpha_k}{\alpha_0^2(\alpha_0+1)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same pattern as the Beta. The covariance is negative because the components share a fixed total of 1: if one face is more likely, the others must be less. The mode needs every αk > 1.
Say it“The mean of mu-k is alpha-k over alpha-nought; any two components have negative covariance.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(x = k \mid D) &= \frac{m_k + \alpha}{N + K\alpha} \\ \alpha = 1 &: \ \text{add-one (Laplace)} \\ \alpha = \tfrac12 &: \ \text{add-half (Jeffreys)} \\ \alpha \to 0 &: \ \text{ML, } m_k/N \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with a symmetric prior (αk = α for every k), the probability that the next observation is outcome k is its count plus α, over the total plus Kα. Smoothing is not a hack: it is the posterior predictive of a Dirichlet prior, and α is how many imaginary observations of every outcome you believe in.
Say it“The chance the next one is k is m-k plus alpha over N plus K alpha.”

Continuous data

A Gaussian's unknown mean

Now the data are real numbers. A thermometer reads a room's true temperature μ plus Gaussian noise with a known spread σ = 2 °C. Viewed as a function of μ, the likelihood of N readings is a Gaussian bump centred on their average, with width σ/√N. So a Gaussian prior on μ is conjugate, and the posterior is Gaussian too.

The update has a beautiful shape: precisions (one over variances) add, and the posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of the prior guess and the sample mean. Each reading can also be absorbed one at a time, nudging the mean towards it by a step that shrinks as evidence accumulates. Take readings and drag the prior's width.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mu \mid D) &= \mathcal{N}(\mu \mid \mu_N, \sigma_N^2) \\ \frac{1}{\sigma_N^2} &= \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{N}{\sigma^2} \\ \mu_N &= \frac{\sigma^2}{N\sigma_0^2 + \sigma^2}\,\mu_0 \\ &\quad + \frac{N\sigma_0^2}{N\sigma_0^2 + \sigma^2}\,\bar x \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: prior μ ~ N(μ0, σ02); readings xn ~ N(μ, σ2) with σ known; x̄ is their average, the ML estimate. The posterior precision is the prior's precision plus N times one reading's precision. The mean slides from μ0 (no data) to x̄ (lots of data). A very wide prior, σ0 → ∞, gives back maximum likelihood. One at a time: μN = μN−1 + (σN2/σ2)(xN − μN−1).
Say it“One over sigma-N squared is one over sigma-nought squared plus N over sigma squared; mu-N blends mu-nought and x-bar in proportion to their precisions.”

Uncertain spread

Unknown precision: the Gamma prior, then Normal-Gamma

Flip the problem: the mean is known but the noise level is not. It is easiest to work with the precision λ = 1/σ2. The likelihood in λ is a power of λ times e−λ·(something), so its conjugate prior is the Gamma distribution. Again the update is bookkeeping: each reading adds ½ to the shape and half its squared deviation to the rate. Take readings from the noisy thermometer and watch the belief about its precision settle.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{Gam}(\lambda \mid a, b) &= \frac{b^{a}}{\Gamma(a)}\, \lambda^{a-1} e^{-b\lambda} \\ \mathbb{E}[\lambda] &= \frac{a}{b}, \quad \operatorname{var}[\lambda] = \frac{a}{b^2} \\ \operatorname{mode}[\lambda] &= \frac{a-1}{b} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a density on λ > 0 with shape a and rate b. Large a with a/b fixed means a confident belief. The mode exists for a ≥ 1. (Some books use the scale 1/b instead of the rate b; the formulas change accordingly.)
Say it“Gamma of lambda given a and b is b to the a over gamma of a, times lambda to the a minus one, times e to the minus b lambda.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(\lambda \mid D) &= \mathrm{Gam}(\lambda \mid a_N, b_N) \\ a_N &= a_0 + \frac{N}{2} \\ b_N &= b_0 + \frac12 \sum_{n=1}^{N} (x_n - \mu)^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: N readings raise the shape by N/2 and the rate by half their total squared deviation from the known mean μ. So the prior is worth 2a0 imaginary readings whose average squared deviation is b0/a0.
Say it“a-N is a-nought plus N over two; b-N is b-nought plus half the sum of squared deviations.”

Both unknown: the Normal-Gamma prior

With both μ and λ unknown, the likelihood ties them together: the mean's uncertainty scales with the noise. The conjugate prior builds that link in. Draw λ from a Gamma, then μ from a Gaussian whose precision is κ0λ, so a noisier world also means a vaguer belief about the mean (Bishop writes β for our κ0). The result is the Normal-Gamma (or Gaussian-Gamma) distribution over the pair. Click the strip to add readings where you like: two readings far apart make the noise look large, a tight cluster makes it look small.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mu, \lambda) &= \mathcal{N}\big(\mu \mid \mu_0,\ (\kappa_0 \lambda)^{-1}\big) \\ &\quad \times \mathrm{Gam}(\lambda \mid a_0, b_0) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: not a Gaussian times an independent Gamma: the Gaussian's width depends on λ, which makes the joint density tilted, with high-precision values pinning μ more tightly. κ0 is how many imaginary readings back the prior guess μ0.
Say it“p of mu and lambda is normal in mu with mean mu-nought and precision kappa-nought lambda, times Gamma of lambda with a-nought and b-nought.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \kappa_N &= \kappa_0 + N, \qquad a_N = a_0 + \frac{N}{2} \\ \mu_N &= \frac{\kappa_0 \mu_0 + N \bar x}{\kappa_N} \\ b_N &= b_0 + \frac12 \sum_n (x_n - \bar x)^2 \\ &\quad + \frac{\kappa_0 N (\bar x - \mu_0)^2}{2 \kappa_N} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the posterior is Normal-Gamma again. The mean is a count-weighted average. The rate b collects the spread of the readings around their mean plus a penalty for disagreement between the sample mean and the prior guess: a surprise about the mean is evidence that the world is noisier.
Say it“kappa-N is kappa-nought plus N; mu-N is the weighted average; b-N adds half the scatter and a disagreement term.”

Integrate λ out of a Normal-Gamma and the belief about μ alone is no longer Gaussian but a heavier-tailed Student's t; for a mean vector with an unknown precision matrix, the Gamma becomes a Wishart. Both are the subject of the next chapter.

The general pattern

Conjugate priors in general, and priors that say nothing

Every pair so far is one instance of a single recipe. Bernoulli, categorical, Gaussian, Poisson and Gamma likelihoods all belong to the exponential family: the parameter meets the data only through a dot product η·u(x), where η is the natural parameter and u(x) a sufficient statistic. For any such family there is a conjugate prior of the same form, and its two knobs read as “ν imaginary observations whose average sufficient statistic is χ”.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(x \mid \boldsymbol\eta) &= h(x)\, g(\boldsymbol\eta)\, e^{\boldsymbol\eta^{\top} \mathbf{u}(x)} \\ p(\boldsymbol\eta \mid \boldsymbol\chi, \nu) &\propto g(\boldsymbol\eta)^{\nu}\, e^{\nu\, \boldsymbol\eta^{\top} \boldsymbol\chi} \\ p(\boldsymbol\eta \mid D) &\propto g(\boldsymbol\eta)^{\nu + N}\, e^{\boldsymbol\eta^{\top} \mathbf{s}_N} \\ \mathbf{s}_N &= \nu \boldsymbol\chi + \textstyle\sum_n \mathbf{u}(x_n) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: g(η) only normalises. Multiplying N likelihood terms by the prior raises g's power from ν to ν + N and adds the data's sufficient statistics to νχ, giving sN. The posterior is in the same family with ν → ν + N and χ → a weighted average of χ and the data's mean statistic. That is the pseudo-count story, in general.
Say it“The conjugate prior is g of eta to the nu, times e to the nu eta-transpose chi; the posterior has nu plus N and adds up the sufficient statistics.”

What if you know nothing? A noninformative prior tries to let the data speak alone. For a location parameter such as a mean, nothing should change if you shift the whole axis, which forces a flat prior. Flat over the entire real line has infinite area, so it is improper: not a real distribution, though it often still gives a proper posterior (here, the limit σ0 → ∞ of the Gaussian prior). For a scale parameter such as σ, nothing should change if you switch units, which forces p(σ) ∝ 1/σ, flat in log σ.

The catch is that “flat” depends on how you describe the unknown. A uniform prior on a coin's θ is far from flat on its log-odds; a prior flat on log-odds piles up near 0 and 1 in θ. Slide the Beta's a = b and see the same belief in both descriptions.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\sigma) &\propto \frac{1}{\sigma} \\ \int_{A}^{B} \frac{d\sigma}{\sigma} &= \int_{cA}^{cB} \frac{d\sigma}{\sigma} \\ &= \ln\frac{B}{A} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: under the 1/σ prior, the belief that σ lies between A and B equals the belief that it lies between cA and cB for any c > 0, so the prior is the same in metres or millimetres. Equivalently p(ln σ) is flat, and for the precision p(λ) ∝ 1/λ, which is the Gamma with a0 = b0 = 0.
Say it“p of sigma proportional to one over sigma; equal ratios get equal belief.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \phi &= \ln\frac{\theta}{1-\theta} \\ p(\phi) &= p(\theta)\left|\frac{d\theta}{d\phi}\right| \\ &= \frac{\theta^{a}(1-\theta)^{b}}{B(a,b)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: changing variables multiplies a density by the stretch factor dθ/dφ = θ(1 − θ). So a Beta(a, b) on θ is a different-looking density on the log-odds φ. Only as a = b → 0 (the improper Haldane prior) does it become flat in φ.
Say it“Phi is the log-odds; its density is theta to the a, one minus theta to the b, over B of a and b.”

Statistical learning

Bayesian, MAP and maximum-likelihood learning

Russell and Norvig frame learning itself as Bayesian inference over hypotheses. A sweet shop sells five kinds of bag that look identical: h1 is all cherry, h2 is 75% cherry and 25% lime, h3 half and half, h4 25% cherry, and h5 all lime. The shop stocks them in proportions 10%, 20%, 40%, 20%, 10%: that is the prior. You buy a bag and unwrap sweets one by one. There are three ways to predict the next flavour:

Bayesian learning keeps every hypothesis and averages their predictions, weighted by posterior probability. It is optimal on average, but the sum can be expensive when the hypothesis space is huge. MAP (maximum a posteriori) bets everything on the single most probable hypothesis. Maximum likelihood is MAP with a flat prior: it ignores the shop's stock list. With lots of data all three agree. With little data they don't. Choose the bag you were sold, then unwrap.

\[ \begin{aligned} P(h_i \mid \mathbf{d}) &\propto P(\mathbf{d} \mid h_i)\, P(h_i) \\ P(X \mid \mathbf{d}) &= \sum_i P(X \mid h_i)\, P(h_i \mid \mathbf{d}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: d is the sequence of flavours so far; each sweet is independent given the bag, so P(d | hi) is a product of per-sweet probabilities. The prediction for the next sweet X is the posterior-weighted vote of all five bags, the discrete version of the posterior predictive.
Say it“The posterior of h-i is proportional to the likelihood times the prior; the prediction is the sum of each hypothesis's prediction times its posterior.”
\[ \begin{aligned} h_{\text{MAP}} &= \arg\max_{h}\ P(\mathbf{d} \mid h)\, P(h) \\ h_{\text{ML}} &= \arg\max_{h}\ P(\mathbf{d} \mid h) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: MAP takes the peak of the posterior; ML takes the peak of the likelihood. Taking logs, MAP maximises log P(d | h) + log P(h): a fit term plus a prior term that charges unlikely (often complex) hypotheses. ML has no such charge, so it jumps to whatever explains the data so far best.
Say it“h-MAP maximises the likelihood times the prior; h-ML maximises the likelihood alone.”

From five hypotheses to a continuum. Russell and Norvig's next step (“learning with complete data”) lets the lime fraction be any θ between 0 and 1. Maximum-likelihood parameter learning then just counts: θ̂ = limes / sweets. Bayesian parameter learning puts a Beta(a, b) prior on θ, and the posterior is the Beta–Bernoulli update of section 2: the hyperparameters are virtual counts. In a Bayesian network, each conditional probability (say, the chance that a lime sweet comes in a green wrapper, and separately that a cherry one does) gets its own Beta prior. If the priors are independent and the data are complete, each posterior is updated from its own counts alone, so a network with thousands of parameters is learned by thousands of tiny Beta updates.

\[ \begin{aligned} &p(\theta_1, \theta_2 \mid D) \\ &\ = \mathrm{Beta}(\theta_1 \mid a_1 + g_1,\ b_1 + r_1) \\ &\ \quad \times \mathrm{Beta}(\theta_2 \mid a_2 + g_2,\ b_2 + r_2) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: θ1 = P(green wrapper | cherry), θ2 = P(green wrapper | lime). g1, r1 count green and red wrappers among cherry sweets; g2, r2 among lime ones. The likelihood factorises over the two parameters, so with independent priors so does the posterior: parameter independence is preserved by complete data.
Say it“The posterior over theta-one and theta-two is a product of two Betas, each updated by its own counts.”
Bayesian modelling

A posterior over whole curves

The same machinery works for a model with many parameters. Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman's example is smoothing: write the curve as a sum of smooth basis functions, μ(x) = Σj βjhj(x), and put a Gaussian prior on the coefficients, β ~ N(0, τI). With Gaussian noise the posterior over β is Gaussian again, so every draw from it is a whole curve. The prior variance τ is the dial: small τ insists on small coefficients and flat curves; as τ → ∞ the prior fades, the posterior mean becomes the least-squares fit, and the spread of posterior curves resembles what the bootstrap would give.

Here 30 noisy points (σ = 0.3) are fitted with 12 Gaussian bumps. Turn τ, drag points, and draw new curves from the posterior.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol\beta \mid \mathbf{y}] &= \Big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{H} + \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau}\mathbf{I}\Big)^{-1} \mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{y} \\ \operatorname{cov}[\boldsymbol\beta \mid \mathbf{y}] &= \sigma^2 \Big(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{H} + \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau}\mathbf{I}\Big)^{-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: H holds the basis functions evaluated at the data points (one row per point). The posterior mean is ridge regression with penalty σ2/τ, so ridge is MAP under a Gaussian prior. The covariance says how much the coefficients can still wobble. The curve at x has posterior mean h(x)·E[β] and variance h(x)⊤cov[β]h(x).
Say it“The posterior mean of beta is H-transpose-H plus sigma squared over tau times I, inverted, times H-transpose y; the covariance is sigma squared times that inverse.”

Real-world project

Project: click-through rates that don't overreact

An online shop shows fashion items in three slots and logs whether each impression is clicked. Which items will be clicked next week? The Open Bandit Dataset (Saito et al., 2021; CC BY 4.0) comes from ZOZOTOWN, Japan's largest fashion e-commerce site. Its public sample holds 20,000 impressions of 80 items, half chosen uniformly at random and half by a Bernoulli Thompson sampling bandit, which itself keeps a Beta posterior per item and shows items whose sampled click rate is highest. That is why some items were shown over a thousand times and others a handful.

Clicks are rare: about one in 250 impressions. We learn from 24–27 November 2019 () and test on 28–30 November (). import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from scipy import stats, special

# Open Bandit Dataset, small sample: uniform-random log + Bernoulli Thompson sampling log
d = pd.concat([pd.read_csv("obd/random/all/all.csv"), pd.read_csv("obd/bts/all/all.csv")])
day = pd.to_datetime(d.timestamp).dt.day
tr, te = d[day <= 27], d[day >= 28]            # learn on 24-27 Nov 2019, test on 28-30 Nov
c  = tr.groupby("item_id").click.sum().reindex(range(80)).values      # training clicks per item
n  = tr.groupby("item_id").click.size().reindex(range(80)).values     # training impressions
tc = te.groupby("item_id").click.sum().reindex(range(80)).values
tn = te.groupby("item_id").click.size().reindex(range(80)).values
m = c.sum() / n.sum()
print(f"train: {n.sum()} impressions, {c.sum()} clicks, pooled CTR {m:.5f}; test: {tn.sum()}, {tc.sum()}")

def log_evidence(a, b):                         # log p(all training clicks | Beta(a, b) prior)
    return np.sum(special.betaln(c + a, n - c + b) - special.betaln(a, b))
def heldout(a, b):                              # test log-likelihood with the posterior mean
    p = (c + a) / (n + a + b)
    return np.sum(tc * np.log(p) + (tn - tc) * np.log1p(-p))

print("ML items with 0 training clicks:", (c == 0).sum(), "| of those clicked in test:", ((c == 0) & (tc > 0)).sum())
for s in [1, 10, 100, 1000, 10000]:
    a, b = m * s, (1 - m) * s
    print(f"s = a+b = {s:>5}: log evidence {log_evidence(a, b):9.3f}   held-out log-lik {heldout(a, b):9.3f}")

for s in [1e5, 1e6, 1e8]:                       # no peak: the evidence creeps up to a ceiling
    print(f"s = {s:.0e}: log evidence {log_evidence(m * s, (1 - m) * s):.3f}")

a, b = m * 100, (1 - m) * 100                   # a moderate prior: 100 pseudo-impressions
post = (c + a) / (n + a + b)
lo, hi = stats.beta.ppf([[0.025], [0.975]], c + a, n - c + b)
for i in [6, 51, 59, 74]:
    print(f"item {i:2d}: {c[i]}/{n[i]:<4} ML {c[i]/n[i]:.4f}  posterior mean {post[i]:.4f}"
          f"  95% interval [{lo[i]:.4f}, {hi[i]:.4f}]  test {tc[i]}/{tn[i]}")
groups = {"0 training clicks": c == 0, "training CTR above 1%": c / n > 0.01}
for name, g in groups.items():
    print(f"{name:22s} ({g.sum():2d} items): test clicks predicted by ML {np.sum(c[g] / n[g] * tn[g]):5.2f},"
          f" by the posterior {np.sum(post[g] * tn[g]):5.2f}; actual {tc[g].sum()}")
print("chi-square test that all 80 items share one CTR: p =", round(stats.chi2_contingency(np.c_[c+tc, n+tn-c-tc])[1], 3))

An honest reading. With only 80 clicks spread over 80 items, the data cannot tell the items apart: a chi-square test finds no evidence that their click rates differ (p = 0.54), and the evidence keeps rising as s grows, so empirical Bayes would set every item to the pooled rate. That does not mean the items are identical; it means differences smaller than the noise are invisible in a week of 20,000 impressions. The full Open Bandit Dataset has about 26 million impressions, where the same Beta model would separate items clearly. The split by calendar day uses the logs' UTC timestamps.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Bayes' rule turns a prior belief about a parameter into a posterior: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. The posterior predictive averages predictions over that belief instead of trusting one estimate.
  • A conjugate prior keeps the posterior in the same family, and its parameters act as imaginary data: Beta(a, b) becomes Beta(a + h, b + t); Dirichlet(α) becomes Dirichlet(α + m).
  • The posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and the ML estimate, with weights in proportion to imaginary and real counts. That shrinkage is add-k smoothing, and it protects small counts from extreme estimates.
  • For Gaussian data, a Gaussian prior on the mean, a Gamma on the precision, or a Normal-Gamma on both are conjugate; precisions add and counts accumulate. Every exponential-family likelihood has a conjugate prior.
  • Bayesian learning averages all hypotheses; MAP picks the most probable one; ML is MAP with a flat prior. "Flat" depends on the parametrisation, and with little data the choice of prior matters most.

Check your understanding

You start with Beta(2, 2) and see 7 heads and 3 tails. What is the posterior, and what is P(next toss is heads)?

Beta(2 + 7, 2 + 3) = Beta(9, 5). The predictive probability of heads is its mean, 9/14 ≈ 0.643, between the prior mean 0.5 and the ML estimate 0.7.

A word never appeared in 10,000 words of training text, in a vocabulary of 20,000 words. What probability does add-one smoothing give it?

(0 + 1)/(10,000 + 20,000 × 1) = 1/30,000. Add-one is the posterior predictive of a flat Dirichlet prior; with a vocabulary this large, it hands a lot of mass to unseen words, which is why smaller α often works better for text.

Prior μ ~ N(20, 1²), noise σ = 2, and four readings average 23. What is the posterior mean?

Prior precision 1, data precision 4/4 = 1, so the two weigh equally: μN = (20 + 23)/2 = 21.5, with posterior variance 1/(1 + 1) = 0.5.

Why can MAP and the full Bayesian prediction disagree, even when MAP picks the right hypothesis?

MAP predicts with one hypothesis as if it were certain; Bayes averages over all of them. Early on, when several hypotheses are still plausible, the Bayesian prediction is a blend and is less extreme. They agree once one hypothesis holds nearly all the posterior mass.

Is a uniform prior on a coin's bias "noninformative"?

Only relative to that parametrisation. On the log-odds scale the same prior is a bump centred at 0, which does express a preference. Scale parameters call for p(σ) ∝ 1/σ, which is invariant to the choice of units.
