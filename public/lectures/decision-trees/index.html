Decision Trees

A flowchart of yes/no questions that a computer writes for you

Foundations

Twenty questions, learned from data

A decision tree splits the data with one simple question at a time — “is flipper length ≤ 206.5 mm?” — sending each example left or right. Each branch is split again, and again, until the groups at the bottom (the leaves) are nearly pure. To predict, a new example just walks from the top (the root) down to a leaf and takes that leaf's majority answer.

Geometrically, every question is a cut parallel to one axis, so a tree carves the feature space into rectangles. Algebraically, it learns no weights at all — only which feature to ask about, and where to cut. The algorithm used almost everywhere is CART (Breiman et al., 1984).

Scoring a group

Gini vs entropy: measuring a mess

We need a number for “how mixed is this group?”: 0 when everyone is the same class, largest when the classes are evenly mixed. Two classic choices: Gini impurity (the chance two random members disagree) and entropy (how many yes/no questions, on average, you'd still need to guess the class). Slide the mix:

\[ \text{Gini}(S) = 1 - \sum_{c} p_c^2 \]
Read it as: pc is the share of class c in the group. Pick two members at random (with replacement): Gini is the probability they belong to different classes.
Say it“Gini is one minus the sum of the squared class proportions.”
\[ H(S) = -\sum_{c} p_c \log_2 p_c \]
Read it as: entropy, in bits. A 50/50 group has 1 bit of uncertainty; a pure group has 0. It comes from information theory (Shannon, 1948).
Say it“Entropy is minus the sum of p times log base two of p.”

Why not just count mistakes?

The misclassification rate seems most natural, but it is too blunt for growing trees. Two candidate splits of 80 examples (40 per class):

Worked example

Information gain, by hand

The classic textbook example (Quinlan, 1986): 14 days, four weather features, and whether tennis was played. Which question should the tree ask first? For each feature, compute the entropy left after splitting; the drop from the starting entropy is the information gain. Click any “Play” cell to flip it and watch every gain recompute.

\[ \text{Gain}(S, A) = H(S) - \sum_{v \,\in\, \text{values}(A)} \frac{|S_v|}{|S|}\, H(S_v) \]
Read it as: starting uncertainty, minus the weighted average uncertainty of the groups after splitting on feature A. Big gain = the question removed a lot of uncertainty.
Say it“Gain is the entropy before, minus the weighted entropy of the children.”

The algorithm

Growing a tree, split by split

CART is greedy: at every leaf it tries every feature and every threshold, takes the best split, and repeats on the children. It never goes back to reconsider. Taming it

Pruning before it memorises

Left alone, a tree keeps splitting until every leaf is pure — even if that means a leaf for one noisy point. Training accuracy reaches 100%; test accuracy falls. Three ways to stop it: limit the depth, demand a minimum number of examples per leaf, or grow fully then prune back branches that don't earn their keep (cost-complexity pruning, with penalty α per leaf).

\[ R_\alpha(T) = R(T) + \alpha\,|T| \]
Read it as: R(T) is the tree's total (weighted) impurity on the training data; |T| is its number of leaves. Each leaf costs α. As α grows, branches whose impurity reduction is worth less than their extra leaves get cut.
Say it“The cost of a tree is its impurity plus alpha times its number of leaves.”

Numbers too

Trees for numbers

For a numeric target, each leaf predicts the average of its training examples, and the split criterion becomes the squared error (variance) of the two sides. The result is a staircase: depth 1 is a single step, depth 6 is 64 tiny steps that start chasing noise.

\[ \text{split score} = \frac{n_L}{n}\,\text{Var}(y_L) + \frac{n_R}{n}\,\text{Var}(y_R), \qquad \hat{y}_{\text{leaf}} = \bar{y}_{\text{leaf}} \]
Read it as: choose the cut that leaves the least spread of y on each side; each leaf predicts its mean. Same greedy algorithm, different impurity.
Say it“Minimise the weighted variance of the two sides; predict the mean in each leaf.”

print(export_text(tree, feature_names=["bill_length", "bill_depth", "flipper", "mass"]))

    Wrap-up

    Five lines to remember

    • A decision tree is a learned flowchart: each node asks “is feature ≤ threshold?”, each leaf gives an answer.
    • It is grown greedily: at each node, choose the split that most reduces impurity (Gini or entropy for classes, variance for numbers).
    • It carves space into axis-aligned rectangles, needs no feature scaling, and handles mixed data.
    • Grown fully it memorises the training set; limit depth, require bigger leaves, or prune with cost-complexity α.
    • Single trees are readable but unstable; averaging many of them (forests, boosting) is the next chapter.

    Check your understanding

    A group has 30 of class A and 10 of class B. What are its Gini impurity and entropy?

    p = (0.75, 0.25). Gini = 1 − (0.75² + 0.25²) = 0.375. Entropy = −(0.75 log₂ 0.75 + 0.25 log₂ 0.25) ≈ 0.811 bits. Check it with the impurity slider.

    Why does a decision tree not need features to be standardised, unlike k-NN?

    A split only asks whether a value is above or below a threshold. Rescaling a feature moves the threshold with it but never changes which examples go left or right.

    Why is the tree-growing algorithm called greedy, and what can go wrong?

    It picks the best split at each node without looking ahead. On XOR-like data no single first split helps, so a greedy tree may choose a poor first question even though a perfect two-level tree exists.

    Your fully grown tree has 100% training accuracy and 71% test accuracy. Name three fixes.

    Limit max_depth, raise min_samples_leaf, or prune with ccp_alpha (chosen by cross-validation). Or switch to a random forest, which averages many deep trees.

    In the penguin tree, why is flipper length the first question?

    It gives the biggest impurity reduction: Gentoos have much longer flippers (over ~207 mm), so one question separates almost all of them from the other two species.
