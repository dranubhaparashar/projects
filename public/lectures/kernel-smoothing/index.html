Kernel Smoothing & Local Regression

A small model fitted around every point

k-nearest neighbours predicts by averaging the k closest points, and its curve jumps every time a point enters or leaves the neighbourhood. Kernel smoothing keeps the idea and removes the jumps: every training point votes, with a weight that fades smoothly with distance, and around each query we can fit a small weighted line instead of a flat average. That recipe, LOESS, draws the trend line on countless scatter plots. In the figure is a year of real ozone readings from San Bernardino, California, plotted against the day's top temperature. Move your pointer or finger across the figure: the highlighted days are the ones that get a say at that temperature, and the short line is the local fit that sets the curve there.

The idea

Averaging the neighbours

Here are 100 noisy points scattered around a smooth curve (the faint line, which in real life you never see). To predict y at a new x0, the simplest honest answer is to look at nearby points. You can connect the dots, which reproduces every wiggle of the noise. You can average the k nearest neighbours (chapter 1.6), which is calmer but jumps whenever a point enters or leaves the group of k. You can fit a straight line to those k points. Or you can give every point a weight that fades smoothly with its distance, so nobody enters or leaves with a bang. Switch between the four methods and drag across the plot to move x0; watch the largest jump.

\[ \hat f(x_0) = \operatorname{Ave}\big(\,y_i \;\big|\; x_i \in N_k(x_0)\big) \]
Read it as: Nk(x0) is the set of the k training points whose x is closest to x0. Average their y values. Each of the k counts the same, every other point counts nothing, so sliding x0 past the midpoint between two points swaps one in and one out at once: a jump.
Say it“f-hat at x-nought is the average of the y's whose x's are among the k nearest neighbours of x-nought.”

The estimator

Nadaraya–Watson: weights that fade

In 1964 Èlizbar Nadaraya and Geoffrey Watson, independently, proposed the weighted version: centre a bump, the kernel, on the query point, give each training point the bump's height at its x as its weight, and take the weighted average of the y values. The bump's width λ is the one dial that matters. A fixed (metric) width keeps λ the same everywhere; an adaptive width stretches to reach the k-th nearest point, so sparse regions still get enough data. Try each kernel, shrink λ until the curve breaks, then switch to an adaptive width.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat f(x_0) &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N K_\lambda(x_0, x_i)\, y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^N K_\lambda(x_0, x_i)} \\ K_\lambda(x_0, x) &= D\!\left(\frac{|x - x_0|}{h_\lambda(x_0)}\right) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a weighted average of the y values, the weights being the kernel's heights. Dividing by the total weight makes the weights sum to one. The window half-width hλ(x0) is either the constant λ or, adaptively, the distance from x0 to its k-th nearest point.
Say it“f-hat at x-nought is the sum of K y over the sum of K, where K is D of the distance over the window width.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Epanechnikov:}\quad D(t) &= \tfrac34\,(1 - t^2) \\ \text{tri-cube:}\quad D(t) &= (1 - |t|^3)^3 \\ \text{(both zero for } |t| &> 1) \\ \text{Gaussian:}\quad D(t) &= \phi(t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: three common bump shapes. The first two have compact support: points beyond λ get exactly zero weight, so with a small fixed λ there may be nobody to average. Tri-cube is flatter on top and meets zero smoothly. The Gaussian (φ is the standard normal density) never reaches zero; for it λ is a standard deviation. The shape matters far less than the width.
Say it“Epanechnikov is three quarters of one minus t squared; tri-cube is one minus t cubed, cubed; Gaussian is phi of t.”

Bishop reaches the same formula from density estimation. Put a small bump f at every training pair (xn, tn) to estimate the joint density p(x, t), the Parzen method of chapter 1.6b. Then ask for the conditional mean of t given x. The answer is Nadaraya–Watson, and the same construction gives the whole conditional distribution, not just its mean. Press Show p(t | x₀) above to see it drawn sideways at the query.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(x, t) &= \frac1N \sum_{n=1}^N f(x - x_n,\ t - t_n) \\ \mathbb E[t \mid x] &= \sum_{n=1}^N k(x, x_n)\, t_n \\ k(x, x_n) &= \frac{g(x - x_n)}{\sum_m g(x - x_m)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each bump f has mean zero in t, and g(x) = ∫ f(x, t) dt is its shape along x. The conditional mean is a weighted sum of the targets with weights k(x, xn) that are positive and sum to 1 at every x: the equivalent kernel of this model.
Say it“The expected t given x is the sum over n of k of x and x-n times t-n, with k equal to g of x minus x-n over the sum of all the g's.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(t \mid x) &= \sum_{n=1}^N k(x, x_n)\, \mathcal N\!\big(t \mid t_n, \sigma^2\big) \\ \operatorname{var}[t \mid x] &= \sigma^2 + \sum_n k(x, x_n)\, t_n^2 - \mathbb E[t \mid x]^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with Gaussian bumps of spread σ in t, the prediction at x is a mixture of Gaussians, one per training point, each weighted by its kernel weight. Its variance is the bump's own σ² plus the spread of the nearby targets. The lab uses σ = 0.15.
Say it“p of t given x is a mixture of normals centred on the t-n, weighted by k of x and x-n.”
Bias at the edges

Local linear regression: fixing the edges

Near the left edge the true curve is climbing steeply, and every neighbour of x0 = 0 lies to its right, higher up. A weighted average of them must land too high: Nadaraya–Watson is biased at the boundary, and the bias is as large as the slope. The cure is to fit a weighted line instead of a weighted constant, then read the line at x0. A line can extrapolate the trend to the edge. Drag x₀ to the edges and back, and watch the equivalent-kernel weights below tilt, some of them turning negative.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{\alpha(x_0),\ \beta(x_0)}\ \sum_{i=1}^N K_\lambda(x_0, x_i) \\ &\qquad \times \big[\,y_i - \alpha(x_0) - \beta(x_0)\, x_i\,\big]^2 \\ &\hat f(x_0) = \hat\alpha(x_0) + \hat\beta(x_0)\, x_0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: at every query, solve a small weighted least-squares problem using only the points the kernel lets in. Keep the line's height at x0 and throw the line away; at the next query, fit a new one. Setting β = 0 gives back Nadaraya–Watson.
Say it“Choose alpha and beta at x-nought to minimise the kernel-weighted squared errors; the estimate is alpha-hat plus beta-hat times x-nought.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat f(x_0) &= b(x_0)^{\mathsf T}\big(\mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\mathbf W(x_0)\,\mathbf B\big)^{-1} \\ &\qquad \times \mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\mathbf W(x_0)\,\mathbf y \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^N l_i(x_0)\, y_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: B has rows (1, xi), W(x0) is diagonal with the kernel weights, and b(x0) = (1, x0). The answer is still a weighted sum of the y values, with weights li(x0) that blend the kernel with the line fit. These are the equivalent kernel. At the edges they reshape themselves, a trick called automatic kernel carpentry.
Say it“f-hat is b transpose times B-transpose W B inverse, times B-transpose W y: a weighted sum with weights l-i of x-nought.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb E\,\hat f(x_0) - f(x_0) \\ &\quad = \tfrac{f''(x_0)}{2}\sum_{i} (x_i - x_0)^2\, l_i(x_0) + R \\ &\text{because } \textstyle\sum_i l_i(x_0) = 1 \\ &\text{and } \textstyle\sum_i (x_i - x_0)\, l_i(x_0) = 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: expand f around x0 in a Taylor series. Local linear weights sum to one and have zero first moment, so the constant and the slope terms cancel exactly and only curvature (f″) and higher terms R are left. Nadaraya–Watson's weights also sum to one, but at an edge their first moment is not zero, so it pays an extra f′(x0) × ∑(xi − x0)li.
Say it“The bias is f-double-prime over two times the second moment of the weights, plus a remainder, because the weights sum to one and their first moment is zero.”

Curvature

Local polynomials: trimming the hills

Local linear regression removes the slope from the bias but not the curvature. At the top of a hill every neighbour is lower, so the weighted line sags below the peak: it trims the hills and fills the valleys. A local quadratic can bend with the hill, and the bias there almost vanishes. Nothing is free, though. A more flexible local model needs more extreme weights, and extreme weights pass more noise through. Step the degree from 0 to 3 and compare bias and spread at the peak and at the edge.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{\alpha,\ \beta_1, \ldots, \beta_d}\ \sum_{i=1}^N K_\lambda(x_0, x_i) \\ &\qquad \times \Big[\,y_i - \alpha - \sum_{j=1}^{d} \beta_j\,(x_i - x_0)^j\Big]^2 \\ &\hat f(x_0) = \hat\alpha \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a weighted polynomial of degree d around each query. Writing the powers in (xi − x0) changes nothing in the fit but makes the intercept the estimate itself. The bias now starts at the (d + 1)-th derivative of f.
Say it“Fit a degree-d polynomial in x-i minus x-nought by kernel-weighted least squares; f-hat is its intercept.”
\[ \operatorname{Var}\hat f(x_0) = \sigma^2\,\lVert l(x_0)\rVert^2 = \sigma^2 \sum_{i=1}^N l_i(x_0)^2 \]
Read it as: if each yi carries independent noise of variance σ², the estimate, being ∑liyi, has variance σ² times the sum of squared weights. Spreading weight evenly keeps ‖l‖² small; the larger positive and negative weights of high degrees make it grow.
Say it“The variance of f-hat is sigma squared times the squared length of the weight vector.”

Bias against variance

Choosing the kernel width

A narrow window averages few points: little bias, lots of variance, a wiggly curve. A wide window averages many: smooth and stable, but it flattens real features. The right λ depends on the data, and we can't compare with the true curve, so we estimate the error by leave-one-out cross-validation: predict each point from all the others. For kernel smoothers with a fixed width this needs only one fit, thanks to a shortcut through the smoother matrix. Slide λ and find the bottom of the cross-validation curve, then draw a new sample and see how much the best λ moves.

\[ \hat{\mathbf f} = \mathbf S_\lambda\,\mathbf y, \qquad \mathrm{df}_\lambda = \operatorname{trace}(\mathbf S_\lambda) = \sum_{i=1}^N S_{ii} \]
Read it as: the fitted values at all N training points are a linear map of the targets. Row i of the N × N smoother matrix Sλ is the equivalent kernel l(xi). Its trace counts effective degrees of freedom: N when the smoother copies the data, 1 for a flat average, 2 for a global line.
Say it“f-hat is S-lambda times y; the degrees of freedom are the trace of S-lambda.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{CV}(\lambda) &= \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\big(y_i - \hat f^{(-i)}_\lambda(x_i)\big)^2 \\ &= \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\left(\frac{y_i - \hat f_\lambda(x_i)}{1 - S_{ii}}\right)^{\!2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: f̂(−i) is the fit made without point i. Dropping a point from a weighted least-squares fit has a closed form: its left-out residual is its ordinary residual divided by 1 − Sii. A point that pulls the fit towards itself (large Sii) is penalised the most.
Say it“CV is the mean squared leave-one-out error, which equals the mean of the residual over one minus S-i-i, squared.”
\[ \mathrm{GCV}(\lambda) = \frac{\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\big(y_i - \hat f_\lambda(x_i)\big)^2}{\big(1 - \mathrm{df}_\lambda/N\big)^2} \]
Read it as: generalised cross-validation replaces each Sii by their average, dfλ/N. It needs only the trace, and it usually picks nearly the same λ.
Say it“GCV is the training error divided by one minus df over N, squared.”

Higher dimensions

Smoothing in many dimensions

Nothing in the recipe needs x to be a single number. With p inputs, measure closeness by the Euclidean distance (after standardising every input, or the one with the biggest units decides everything), and fit a local plane instead of a local line. Below, 250 points are scattered over a square and their y values (colour) follow a hill on a slope. Drag the ring to move the query and compare a local average with a local plane in the corners. Then turn up the dimension to see why this stops working beyond a handful of inputs.

\[ \begin{aligned} K_\lambda(x_0, x) &= D\!\left(\frac{\lVert x - x_0\rVert}{\lambda}\right) \\ b(X) &= (1,\ X_1,\ X_2,\ \ldots,\ X_p) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a radial kernel: the weight depends only on the distance to x0, so the window is a ball. A local linear fit now has p + 1 parameters at every query: a height and a slope along each input.
Say it“K is D of the length of x minus x-nought over lambda; the local basis is one and the p inputs.”
\[ \Pr\big(\text{within } \lambda \text{ of an edge}\big) = 1 - (1 - 2\lambda)^p \]
Read it as: for points spread uniformly in the unit cube [0, 1]p, the fraction lying within λ of some face. It grows fast with p, and near a face every window is lopsided, which is exactly where local averages are biased. Local linear fits help, but in high dimensions almost every point is a boundary point and the windows are nearly empty.
Say it“The chance of being within lambda of the boundary is one minus one-minus-two-lambda to the power p.”

Taming dimensions

Structured kernels and structured models

When the data are too thin for a free-form surface, add structure. One way is to shape the kernel. A matrix A turns the round window into an ellipse, and a small weight on an input says “closeness along this one matters less”. Below, y depends strongly on x₁ and only gently on x₂. Turn the weight on x₂ down from 1 towards 0 and watch the cross-validation error: a little attention to x₂ beats both full attention and none.

\[ \begin{aligned} K_{\lambda, A}(x_0, x) &= D\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{(x - x_0)^{\mathsf T} A\,(x - x_0)}}{\lambda}\right) \\ A &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & a \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the distance is measured through A. With a diagonal A, ajj weighs coordinate j; a = 1 is the round window, and a = 0 ignores x₂ completely, so the window becomes a vertical slab. Even then the local plane still has a slope along x₂, so the model becomes “a line in x₂ whose height and slope vary with x₁”.
Say it“K is D of the square root of x minus x-nought, transposed, times A, times x minus x-nought, over lambda.”

The other way is to restrict the function. An ANOVA decomposition writes f as a constant, plus one-input terms, plus two-input interactions, and so on; dropping the higher terms leaves models that low-dimensional smoothers can fit. Keeping only the one-input terms gives an additive model, fitted by smoothing each input in turn (backfitting, in the chapter on generalised additive models). A varying-coefficient model keeps a linear model in some inputs but lets its coefficients drift smoothly with another input Z, such as time or season. It is fitted by local regression in Z alone, and the project below uses one to ask how much an extra degree of heat raises ozone in January compared with July.

\[ \begin{aligned} f(X) = \alpha &+ \sum_{j} g_j(X_j) \\ &+ \sum_{k < l} g_{kl}(X_k, X_l) + \cdots \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: main effects, then pairwise interactions, then higher ones. Each gj is a curve in one input and each gkl a surface in two, so each can be smoothed with plenty of data even when p is large.
Say it“f is alpha plus a sum of one-input functions plus a sum of two-input functions, and so on.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &f(X) = \alpha(Z) + \beta_1(Z)\,X_1 + \cdots + \beta_q(Z)\,X_q \\ &\min_{\alpha(z_0),\ \beta(z_0)}\ \sum_{i} K_\lambda(z_0, z_i) \\ &\qquad \times \big[y_i - \alpha(z_0) - x_i^{\mathsf T}\beta(z_0)\big]^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for any fixed Z the model is an ordinary linear regression in X. The kernel works on Z only: at each z0, a weighted least-squares fit using the cases whose Z is close to z0.
Say it“f is alpha of Z plus beta-one of Z times X-one, and so on; fit alpha and beta at z-nought by kernel-weighted least squares in Z.”
Beyond least squares

Local likelihood: local logistic regression

Least squares is not special. Any model fitted by maximum likelihood can be made local: weight every point's log-likelihood by the kernel, maximise, and keep the fitted value at x0. For yes/no outcomes this gives local logistic regression. Below, the chance of a “yes” rises and then falls again with x. One global logistic curve can only rise or fall, so it fails; a local average of the 0s and 1s works but is biased at the edges; local logistic bends where the data bend. Compare the three methods and drag the query to see the small logistic curve fitted there.

\[ \ell\big(\beta(x_0)\big) = \sum_{i=1}^N K_\lambda(x_0, x_i)\;\ell\big(y_i,\ x_i^{\mathsf T}\beta(x_0)\big) \]
Read it as: ℓ(y, η) is one point's log-likelihood under a model with linear predictor η. The kernel decides how much each point's evidence counts at x0. With a Gaussian log-likelihood this is local least squares again; with a Bernoulli one it is local logistic regression; with xi replaced by lagged values of a time series it gives an autoregression whose coefficients drift over time.
Say it“The local log-likelihood is the kernel-weighted sum of each point's log-likelihood at the local parameters beta of x-nought.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \eta_i &= \alpha(x_0) + \beta(x_0)\,(x_i - x_0) \\ \max_{\alpha,\ \beta}\ &\sum_{i} K_\lambda(x_0, x_i)\big[\,y_i\,\eta_i - \log\!\big(1 + e^{\eta_i}\big)\big] \\ \hat p(x_0) &= \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\hat\alpha(x_0)}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a logistic regression weighted by the kernel, with x centred at x0 so the intercept α is the log-odds at x0 itself. There is no closed form, so each query runs a few steps of Newton's method. With K classes the same idea gives local multinomial logistic regression.
Say it“Maximise the kernel-weighted logistic log-likelihood; p-hat at x-nought is the sigmoid of alpha-hat.”

Computation

The cost of lazy learning

Kernel smoothers are memory-based: there is no training step, the whole dataset is the model, and all the work happens when you ask a question. Evaluating at M points costs N kernel weights each, and leave-one-out cross-validation over N points costs about N². Two tricks make it fast. With a compact kernel and sorted x, a binary search finds the few points inside each window. And binning rounds the data onto a grid of G cells first, so every query sums over G cells instead of N points, at a small cost in accuracy. Statistical software also fits at a few well-chosen vertices and interpolates between them. Set N and press Run: these are real timings in your browser.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{direct:}\quad & O(N M) \\ \text{sorted windows:}\quad & O\big((N + M)\log N + M k\big) \\ \text{binned, } G \text{ cells:}\quad & O(N + G M) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: N training points, M query points, k the typical number of points inside one window. The first line touches every pair. The second sorts once, then visits only each window's k points. The third spends one pass putting the data into G cells (each point split between its two nearest cell centres) and then treats every cell as a single weighted point.
Say it“Direct costs N times M; with sorting, N log N plus M times k; with binning, N plus G times M.”

Real-world project

Project: ozone on hot days

Ground-level ozone is not emitted by anything. It is cooked in sunlight from car and industrial exhaust, so it piles up on hot, bright, still days, and it harms lungs. The US Environmental Protection Agency publishes every monitor's readings in its Air Quality System. We take the station in San Bernardino, California (site 06-071-9004), east of Los Angeles and one of the most ozone-burdened places in the country. For each day of 2023 we have the MDA8, the highest 8-hour average ozone of the day in parts per billion (the number the US standard of 70 ppb is set on), and the day's maximum temperature from the same station. Days with less than 75% of hours recorded are dropped, leaving 358. Every model learns from 2023; 2022 (354 days) is kept back as a test of next year's data. Pick a degree and slide the span.

Does heat matter more in summer?

Ozone needs sunlight as well as heat, and a 25 °C day in January has far less sun than one in July. The same project in Python

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

tr = pd.read_csv("sb2023.csv"); te = pd.read_csv("sb2022.csv")    # EPA AQS site 06-071-9004
x, y, xt, yt = tr.tc.values, tr.o3.values, te.tc.values, te.o3.values # daily max temp (C), MDA8 ozone (ppb)

def loess(x, y, x0, frac, deg=1):
    """Local polynomial fit at x0: tricube weights over the nearest frac*n points."""
    d = np.abs(x - x0); q = max(2, int(frac * len(x) + 1e-10))
    h = np.sort(d)[q - 1]
    w = np.where(d < h, (1 - (d / h) ** 3) ** 3, 0.0)
    B = np.vander(x - x0, deg + 1, increasing=True)              # columns 1, (x-x0), (x-x0)^2
    l = w * (B @ np.linalg.solve(B.T @ (w[:, None] * B), np.eye(deg + 1)[0]))
    return l @ y, l                                              # fit and equivalent kernel

rmse = lambda e: np.sqrt(np.mean(np.square(e)))
n = len(x); b = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)
line_loo = [y[i] - np.polyval(np.polyfit(np.delete(x, i), np.delete(y, i), 1), x[i]) for i in range(n)]
print(f"{n} days in 2023, {len(xt)} in 2022; straight line: LOOCV {rmse(line_loo):.3f}, 2022 test {rmse(yt - np.polyval(b, xt)):.3f} ppb")
print(" span  deg     df   LOOCV   GCV    2022 test")
for deg in [0, 1, 2]:
    for frac in [0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0]:
        fit = np.array([loess(x, y, xi, frac, deg)[0] for xi in x])
        df = sum(loess(x, y, xi, frac, deg)[1][i] for i, xi in enumerate(x))        # trace of S
        keep = lambda i: np.arange(n) != i
        loo = [y[i] - loess(x[keep(i)], y[keep(i)], x[i], frac, deg)[0] for i in range(n)]
        gcv = np.mean((y - fit) ** 2) / (1 - df / n) ** 2
        test = [loess(x, y, xi, frac, deg)[0] for xi in xt]
        print(f" {frac:4.2f}   {deg}   {df:5.2f}  {rmse(loo):6.3f}  {np.sqrt(gcv):6.3f}  {rmse(yt - test):6.3f}")

# local likelihood: P(MDA8 > 70 ppb | temperature), Gaussian kernel of sd 2 C, against one global logistic curve
z, zt = (y > 70).astype(int), (yt > 70).astype(int)
def local_logit(x0, lam=2.0):
    m = LogisticRegression(C=1e12, tol=1e-12, max_iter=10000)
    m.fit((x - x0)[:, None], z, sample_weight=np.exp(-0.5 * ((x - x0) / lam) ** 2))
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-m.intercept_[0]))
g = LogisticRegression(C=1e12, tol=1e-12, max_iter=10000).fit(x[:, None], z)
print("  temp  local  global")
for x0 in [20, 25, 30, 35, 40]:
    print(f"  {x0:2d} C  {local_logit(x0):.4f}  {g.predict_proba([[x0]])[0, 1]:.4f}")
logloss = lambda p: -np.mean(zt * np.log(p) + (1 - zt) * np.log(1 - p))
pl, pg = np.array([local_logit(t) for t in xt]), g.predict_proba(xt[:, None])[:, 1]
print(f"2022 log-loss: local {logloss(pl):.4f}, global {logloss(pg):.4f}")

d, dt = tr.doy.values, te.doy.values
def vc(z0, x, y, d, lam):
    r = np.abs(d - z0); w = np.exp(-0.5 * (np.minimum(r, 365 - r) / lam) ** 2)
    B = np.c_[np.ones_like(x), x]
    return np.linalg.solve(B.T @ (w[:, None] * B), B.T @ (w * y))      # alpha, beta

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A kernel smoother predicts at x0 with a weighted average of the training targets, the weights fading smoothly with distance, so the curve has none of k-NN's jumps. That is Nadaraya–Watson, and Bishop derives it as the conditional mean of a Parzen density.
  • Fitting a weighted line, not a constant, at each query removes the slope from the bias, which cures the boundary bias; local quadratics also trim less off peaks but pay with variance σ²‖l(x0)‖².
  • The width λ matters far more than the kernel's shape. Every such smoother is linear, f̂ = Sλy, so df = trace Sλ, and LOOCV or GCV picks λ from one fit.
  • In many dimensions windows empty and most points sit near an edge. Structure rescues you: shaped kernels that down-weight inputs, additive models, and varying-coefficient models smoothed in one variable.
  • Any likelihood can be localised: local logistic regression gives a risk curve. On San Bernardino ozone the LOESS curve with span 0.3 is flat below about 20 °C and climbs steeply after, and letting the slope vary with the season cuts next year's error from 11.8 to 9.5 ppb.

Check your understanding

Why does the k-NN average jump, while a kernel average with the same k does not?

In the k-NN average every neighbour has weight 1/k and every other point weight 0, so when x0 passes the midpoint between the k-th and the (k+1)-th neighbour, one whole 1/k share swaps from one y to another. A kernel like Epanechnikov gives the point at the window's edge weight zero, and weights change continuously as x0 moves, so the estimate does too.

Nadaraya–Watson at x0 = 0 on a rising curve comes out too high. Why does a local line fix it?

All of x0's neighbours lie to its right, where the curve is higher, so any positive-weighted average of them overshoots by roughly f′(x0) times the weights' mean distance. A local line estimates that slope and extrapolates back to x0; its weights have first moment exactly zero, so the slope term cancels.

A smoother has dfλ = 2 on 100 points. What does it look like, and what if dfλ = 100?

df = 2 is about as rigid as a straight line (local linear with a huge window becomes the least-squares line). df = 100 means trace S = N: each fitted value is essentially its own y, so the curve interpolates the data and LOOCV will expose it.

Why is the shortcut CV = mean((yi − f̂(xi)) / (1 − Sii))² exact for a fixed λ but not for a LOESS span?

With a fixed λ the kernel weights at xi do not depend on which points are present, and deleting one case from a weighted least-squares fit has that closed form. A span picks the q nearest points, so removing xi pulls a new neighbour into the window and changes the weights themselves.

In the varying-coefficient model, LOOCV prefers a 7-day window but 2022 prefers about 30 days. Which should you trust?

The 2022 test. Neighbouring days share weather systems, so their errors are correlated; leaving out one day still leaves its almost-identical neighbours in the fit, and LOOCV rewards windows that copy them. When errors are correlated, leave out whole blocks (weeks or months) or test on a separate period.
