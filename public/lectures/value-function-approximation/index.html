Value‑Function Approximation

Objectives, semi-gradients and least-squares TD

Every method so far kept one number per state. A car on a hill has a position and a velocity, both real numbers, so it never visits the same state twice and a table never fills in. The fix is to learn a function v̂(s, w): a few hundred weights that cover a continuous space, where one update teaches many nearby states at once. This chapter asks what “a good approximation” means, how to chase it with gradients when the target itself is a guess, and how to solve for the answer in one shot. On the right, the colours are a prediction of how many steps a careless driver needs to reach the flag, learned in this page from 1,000 drives. Touch the phase plane to start a car anywhere and see whether the prediction holds.

The problem

From tables to functions

Russell and Norvig frame reinforcement learning as an agent that must learn what to do from nothing but rewards. Its easiest form is passive learning, which is also our topic: the policy π is fixed, and the agent only has to learn how good each state is under it, vπ(s). Chapters 3.1b–3.1e did this with tables. A table has two flaws. It needs every state to be visited, often, and it learns nothing about a state from its neighbours. With continuous states both flaws are fatal.

So we replace the table with a function of a weight vector w that has far fewer entries than there are states. The price is generalisation: changing one weight changes the value of every state that uses it. That is a feature when the states really are alike, and a compromise when they are not, because now improving one state can make another worse. Below is a long corridor of 500 states (a scaled-down version of Sutton and Barto’s random walk). From each state the walker jumps to one of the 50 states on either side at random; falling off the left end pays −1, off the right end +1. The simplest approximation is state aggregation: states are cut into groups, and each group shares one weight. Click any state to make one update there, then change the group size and click again.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\hat v(s,\mathbf{w}) \approx v_\pi(s) \\ &\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^d,\ \ d \ll |\mathcal{S}| \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: v̂ (“v-hat”) is our estimate of the state’s true value under π; it depends on the state and on d adjustable weights. With aggregation, w has one weight per group and v̂(s, w) is simply the weight of s’s group. A table is the special case d = |𝒮|: one weight per state, no sharing.
Say it“v-hat of s and w approximates v-pi of s, with d weights, far fewer than the states.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &S_t \mapsto U_t \\ &\text{MC: } U_t = G_t \\ &\text{TD: } U_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma\,\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf{w}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: every prediction method is a stream of training examples: an input state St and a target Ut that v̂(St) should move towards. Monte Carlo uses the actual return Gt; TD uses one reward plus its own estimate of the next state. What is new is that moving towards Ut at one state also moves every state that shares weights with it.
Say it“S-t maps to U-t: the state is the input, the target is the label.”

The objective

What “good” means: the value error VE

With fewer weights than states, no w can be right everywhere, so we must say which errors matter most. The standard answer weights each state’s squared error by how much time the policy spends there: the on-policy distribution μ. In the corridor every episode starts in the middle, so the middle is visited far more than the ends (the shaded histogram). Each group’s best weight is then the μ-weighted average of its true values. Drag the ten group levels to make VE as small as you can, then switch the weighting and see the best fit change at the rarely visited ends.

\[ \begin{aligned} \overline{\text{VE}}(\mathbf{w}) &= \sum_{s} \mu(s)\,e(s)^2 \\ e(s) &= v_\pi(s) - \hat v(s,\mathbf{w}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for each state, the squared gap e(s)² between the truth and the estimate, weighted by μ(s), the share of time the policy spends in s; μ sums to 1. Its square root, the RMSVE, is in the same units as the values. The bar over VE in Sutton and Barto just marks it as an average over states.
Say it“VE of w is the sum over states of mu of s times the squared error between v-pi and v-hat.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \eta(s) &= h(s) \\ &\quad + \sum_{\bar s}\eta(\bar s)\,p_\pi(s\,|\,\bar s) \\ p_\pi(s\,|\,\bar s) &= \sum_a \pi(a\,|\,\bar s)\,p(s\,|\,\bar s,a) \\ \mu(s) &= \frac{\eta(s)}{\sum_{s'}\eta(s')} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: in an episodic task, η(s) is the expected number of visits to s per episode. You arrive at s either by starting there (probability h(s)) or by stepping in from a state s̄ you visited, with pπ(s | s̄) the chance that one step under π takes you from s̄ to s. Normalising the visit counts gives μ. With discounting you would multiply the second term by γ.
Say it“Eta of s is the start probability plus the expected visits flowing in from every other state; mu is eta normalised.”

Two cautions from Sutton and Barto. First, VE is a yardstick, not the final goal: we approximate values in order to find better policies, and the w with the smallest VE is not guaranteed to give the best policy. Second, with nonlinear approximators VE can have many local minima; for linear ones such as aggregation it is a bowl with one bottom, and the methods below can find it.

Stochastic gradient

Gradient Monte Carlo: SGD on the value error

To shrink VE we could follow its gradient. We can’t compute the sum over all states, but the policy hands us states in exactly the proportions μ, so a step on one sampled state is a noisy step on VE: stochastic gradient descent. We also don’t know vπ(St), but the return Gt is an unbiased sample of it. Substituting it gives gradient Monte Carlo, which, with a step size that shrinks in the usual way, converges to a local minimum of VE. For aggregation the gradient ∇v̂ is 1 for the state’s own group and 0 elsewhere, so each update nudges one group weight towards the return. Run episodes and watch the estimate settle onto the dashed best fit from the last section.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{w}_{t+1} &= \mathbf{w}_t - \tfrac12\alpha\,\nabla e_t^2 \\ &= \mathbf{w}_t + \alpha\,e_t\,\nabla\hat v(S_t,\mathbf{w}_t) \\ e_t &= v_\pi(S_t)-\hat v(S_t,\mathbf{w}_t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: et is the error at the one state you just saw; take a small step α downhill on its square. The chain rule turns the gradient of the square into (error) × (gradient of the estimate); the ½ cancels the 2. ∇v̂ is the vector of partial derivatives of v̂ with respect to each weight.
Say it“The new w is the old w plus alpha times the error at S-t, times the gradient of v-hat at S-t.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} &+ \alpha\big[G_t-\hat v(S_t,\mathbf{w})\big] \\ &\times\nabla\hat v(S_t,\mathbf{w}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same step with the unknown truth replaced by the return that actually followed St. Because E[Gt | St = s] = vπ(s), each step points the right way on average. The cost is waiting until the episode ends to know Gt.
Say it“Nudge w by alpha times the return minus the estimate, times the gradient.”

Bootstrapping

Semi-gradient TD: chasing a target that moves

Put the TD target R + γv̂(S′, w) in place of Gt and the update looks the same, but something has changed: the target now depends on w. A true gradient of the squared TD error would include a term for how changing w moves the target. TD simply ignores it and treats the target as fixed, which is why the update is called a semi-gradient. It is not a step down any objective, so the SGD guarantee is gone. For a linear v̂ (aggregation is linear) it still converges, but to a different point, the TD fixed point, and in exchange it learns online, from every step, without waiting for episodes to end. Run both learners on the same episodes and compare where they head.

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta_t &= R_{t+1}+\gamma\,\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf{w}) - \hat v(S_t,\mathbf{w}) \\ \mathbf{w} &\leftarrow \mathbf{w} + \alpha\,\delta_t\,\nabla\hat v(S_t,\mathbf{w}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: δt is the TD error. The gradient is taken only of the estimate at St, never of the target’s v̂(St+1). The “full” derivative of ½δ² would multiply δ by (γ∇v̂(St+1) − ∇v̂(St)); dropping the first part is the “semi”. At a terminal St+1, v̂ is 0.
Say it“Nudge w by alpha times the TD error, reward plus gamma v-hat of the next state minus v-hat of this one, times the gradient here.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{w}_{\text{TD}} = \mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b} \\ &\mathbf{A} = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{x}_t(\mathbf{x}_t-\gamma\mathbf{x}_{t+1})^{\!\top}\big] \\ &\mathbf{b} = \mathbb{E}[R_{t+1}\mathbf{x}_t] \\ &\overline{\text{VE}}(\mathbf{w}_{\text{TD}}) \le \tfrac{1}{1-\gamma}\,\min_{\mathbf{w}}\overline{\text{VE}}(\mathbf{w}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with a linear v̂ = w⊤x(s), the expected TD update is b − Aw, and it is zero at wTD. The expectations are over the on-policy distribution. The bound (for continuing tasks with γ < 1) says TD’s answer is at most 1/(1−γ) times worse than the best possible VE; with γ near 1 that is a loose promise. Our corridor has γ = 1, where the bound says nothing, so we just measure.
Say it“w-TD is A-inverse b. Its value error is at most one over one-minus-gamma times the smallest.”

Russell & Norvig

Approximating direct utility estimation

Russell and Norvig reach the same idea from the other side. Direct utility estimation watches trials under a fixed policy and averages the observed reward-to-go at each state: Monte Carlo prediction. Replace the per-state averages with a function of hand-made features, fit it to the observed reward-to-go, and you have supervised learning. Their example uses the 4×3 grid world: a robot pays 0.04 for each step and ends at +1 or −1; it moves as intended 80% of the time and slips sideways otherwise. Utilities are approximated by a plane in the cell coordinates. The online update is the Widrow–Hoff (LMS) rule, which is exactly gradient Monte Carlo with features (x, y). Run trials, then switch to the 10×10 world with its goal in the middle and see a plane fail until you add a distance-to-goal feature.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat U_\theta(x,y) &= \theta_0 + \theta_1 x + \theta_2 y \\ &\quad\;[+\ \theta_3 f_3(x,y)] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a utility estimate for every cell from three (or four) numbers. f3 is an optional extra feature, the straight-line distance from the cell to the +1 goal. This is the same linear v̂(s, w) = w⊤x(s) as before, with features x(s) = (1, x, y[, f3]).
Say it“U-hat is theta-nought plus theta-one x plus theta-two y, plus optionally theta-three times the distance to the goal.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i &+ \alpha\,\big[u_j(s) - \hat U_\theta(s)\big] \\ &\times\frac{\partial \hat U_\theta(s)}{\partial\theta_i} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: uj(s) is the reward-to-go observed from state s in trial j. Each weight moves in proportion to the error and to its own feature: ∂Û/∂θ0 = 1, ∂Û/∂θ1 = x, ∂Û/∂θ2 = y.
Say it“Theta-i moves by alpha times the observed reward-to-go minus the estimate, times the partial derivative of U-hat with respect to theta-i.”

A practical rule

Choosing the step size

With a table, α = 1/10 moves an estimate one tenth of the way to its target, so it averages roughly the last ten targets. With features the same α does more, because the update adds αδ to every active weight and the estimate is their sum. Sutton and Barto’s rule of thumb: decide that you want to learn from about τ experiences of similar states, then set α by the formula below. It is exact when every feature vector has the same length, as here: the corridor is covered by n overlapping aggregations (“tilings”), each shifted by 50/n states, so every state has exactly n active features. Click a state with different n and τ: the clicked state always moves exactly 1/τ of the way, whatever n is. Then sweep τ to see how it trades speed against noise.

\[ \alpha = \big(\tau\;\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}]\big)^{-1} \]
Read it as: τ is the number of experiences you want the estimate to average over; x⊤x is the squared length of a feature vector, here the number of active features n. Dividing by it undoes the extra push that n active weights give. With one feature per state (x⊤x = 1) this is the tabular α = 1/τ.
Say it“Alpha is one over tau times the expected x-transpose-x.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\hat v(s,\mathbf{w}_{\text{new}}) - \hat v(s,\mathbf{w}) \\ &\quad = \alpha\,\mathbf{x}^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}\,\big[U - \hat v(s,\mathbf{w})\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for a linear v̂, one update at s changes the weights by α[U − v̂]x, so the estimate at s changes by that times x⊤x. With α from the rule, αx⊤x = 1/τ: one τ-th of the error is closed. Neighbours that share some of s’s features move by less.
Say it“The change at s is alpha times x-transpose-x times the error.”

Solving, not stepping

Least-squares TD: jump straight to the fixed point

Semi-gradient TD walks towards wTD = A−1b one small step at a time. But A and b are just averages over transitions, and we can estimate them directly from the data we have, then solve. That is least-squares TD (Bradtke and Barto, 1996): no step size to tune, and it uses every transition as fully as possible. The price is memory and arithmetic: A is d×d, so each step costs O(d²) with the incremental inverse below, against O(d) for TD. A small εI keeps Â invertible before every feature has been seen. Change the amount of data and compare LSTD with TD run on exactly the same episodes.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{A}}_t &= \sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\mathbf{x}_k(\mathbf{x}_k-\gamma\mathbf{x}_{k+1})^{\!\top} + \varepsilon\mathbf{I} \\ \hat{\mathbf{b}}_t &= \sum_{k=0}^{t-1}R_{k+1}\mathbf{x}_k \\ \mathbf{w}_t &= \hat{\mathbf{A}}_t^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{b}}_t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: add up one outer product per transition, and the reward times the features. Sutton and Barto’s Â and b̂ are these sums; the averages would divide both by t, which cancels in Â−1b̂ (it only rescales ε). At a terminal transition xk+1 = 0.
Say it“A-hat is the sum of x-k times x-k minus gamma x-k-plus-one transposed, plus epsilon I; b-hat sums reward times x; w is A-hat inverse b-hat.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{B} = \hat{\mathbf{A}}_{t-1}^{-1},\quad \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{x}_{t-1}-\gamma\mathbf{x}_{t} \\ &\hat{\mathbf{A}}_t^{-1} = \mathbf{B} - \frac{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\,\mathbf{u}^{\!\top}\mathbf{B}}{1+\mathbf{u}^{\!\top}\mathbf{B}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the Sherman–Morrison formula updates the inverse after adding one outer product, with matrix–vector products only: O(d²) per step instead of O(d³) for a fresh inverse. Start from Â0−1 = I/ε. The page runs both routes and checks they agree.
Say it“The new inverse is the old inverse minus a rank-one correction, divided by one plus a scalar.”

Non-parametric

Memory-based and kernel-based approximation

So far v̂ has been a fixed formula with weights. A memory-based method keeps the training examples themselves, pairs (s, g) of a state and a target, and does no work until it is asked about a query state. Then it retrieves the stored states nearest the query and combines their targets: lazy learning. Its accuracy grows with the data instead of being capped by a feature set, and it puts its effort only where queries are actually made, which in RL means the states the agent really meets. Its costs are memory and search time per query. Here the memory is Mountain Car states from the project’s drives, each stored with the number of steps the car still needed. Drag the query point and compare the ways of combining neighbours.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat v(s,\mathcal{D}) &= \frac{\sum_{s'\in\mathcal{D}} k(s,s')\,g(s')}{\sum_{s'\in\mathcal{D}} k(s,s')} \\ k(s,s') &= e^{-\lVert s-s'\rVert^2/2\sigma^2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a weighted average of the stored targets g(s′), with weights given by a kernel k that measures how much s′ should count for s. Nearest-neighbour uses weight 1 for the closest state only, k-NN weight 1 for the k closest; the Gaussian kernel fades smoothly with distance, σ setting how fast. Sutton and Barto also write the unnormalised sum Σk(s,s′)g(s′); dividing by Σk keeps the answer on the targets’ scale.
Say it“v-hat at s is the kernel-weighted average of the stored targets; the Gaussian kernel is e to the minus squared distance over two sigma squared.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &k(s,s') = \mathbf{x}(s)^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}(s') \\ &\text{if } \mathbf{w}=\textstyle\sum_i c_i\,\mathbf{x}(s_i): \\ &\quad \mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}(s) = \textstyle\sum_i c_i\,k(s,s_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the kernel trick in reverse: any feature vector defines a kernel, the dot product of two states’ features. For tile coding, k(s, s′) is the number of tiles the two states share. A linear method whose weights are a combination of training feature vectors (as with least squares, ridge, or SGD started from zero) makes exactly the predictions of a kernel method over its training states.
Say it“A kernel from features is x of s dot x of s-prime; a linear prediction is then a kernel-weighted sum over the training states.”

Looking deeper

Interest and emphasis

VE weights states by μ, but sometimes we care about some states more than others, say the start state, whose value is what a planner will ask about. Sutton and Barto add two signals. The interest It ≥ 0 says how much we care about getting St right; the objective weights each state by μ(s) times its interest. The emphasis Mt multiplies each update. For Monte Carlo, Mt = It is enough. For bootstrapping methods it is not: the target at a state we care about leans on the estimate n steps later, so that later state must be learned well too, and the emphasis passes interest forward to it. The chain below has six states, a reward of 1 per step, and two weights: one shared by states 1–3, one by states 4–6. Only state 1 is interesting. Switch between the three weightings and watch the estimate at state 1.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} &+ \alpha\,M_t\big[G_{t:t+n} - \hat v(S_t,\mathbf{w})\big] \\ &\times\nabla\hat v(S_t,\mathbf{w}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the usual n-step semi-gradient TD update, scaled by the emphasis Mt. Gt:t+n is n rewards plus γn times the estimate n steps later (or just the rewards if the episode ends first). Mt = 0 means “don’t update here at all”.
Say it“Nudge w by alpha times the emphasis times the n-step error, times the gradient.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &M_t = I_t + \gamma^n M_{t-n} \\ &M_t = 0 \ \text{ for } t < 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a state is emphasised for its own interest plus, discounted, the emphasis of the state n steps earlier, the one whose n-step target bootstraps from it. Interest is handed forward along the chain of targets, n steps at a time.
Say it“M-t is I-t plus gamma to the n times M of t minus n.”

Real-world project

Project: how long until the flag?

Mountain Car comes from Andrew Moore’s 1990 thesis and is a standard benchmark in Gymnasium, the maintained successor of OpenAI Gym (MIT licence), as MountainCar-v0. An underpowered car sits in a valley; its engine cannot climb the right-hand hill directly, so it must rock back and forth to build up speed. The state is (position, velocity), two real numbers. Our driver follows a fixed, careless policy: push the way you are already moving, except that one time in five it presses a random pedal (left, none or right). Each step costs −1 and the episode ends at the flag, so vπ(s) is minus the expected number of steps still to go. We learn it from drives that start where Gymnasium starts them (position between −0.6 and −0.4, at rest), using 8 overlapping 9×9 grids (tile coding, the subject of the next chapter) for 648 features, exactly 8 of them active in any state.

To grade the predictions we need the truth. We took 1,467 states from 100 held-out drives (every tenth state, so they follow the on-policy distribution μ) and, in Python, ran 1,000 further drives from each and averaged their lengths; the average’s standard error is about 0.4 steps. The RMSVE below is measured on those states, in steps.

1 · The physics, checked against Gymnasium

\[ \begin{aligned} \dot x_{t+1} &= \operatorname{clip}\big(\dot x_t + 0.001\,(A_t - 1) \\ &\qquad - 0.0025\cos 3x_t,\ \pm 0.07\big) \\ x_{t+1} &= \operatorname{clip}\big(x_t + \dot x_{t+1},\ -1.2,\ 0.6\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: At is 0 (push left), 1 (coast) or 2 (push right), so the engine adds ±0.001 to the velocity. Gravity pulls along the valley floor, whose height is sin 3x, with strength 0.0025·cos 3x. Speed is capped at 0.07; hitting the left wall stops the car dead. The flag is at x = 0.5. The page computes these lines in the same order as Gymnasium’s mountain_car.py.
Say it“New velocity is old velocity plus a thousandth times the push, minus 0.0025 cos three x, clipped; new position is old position plus new velocity, clipped.”

2 · Four learners, one data stream

All four learners see the same drives in the same order. The same project in Python

import math, numpy as np, gymnasium as gym, scipy.sparse as sp
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge
from mulberry import Mulberry                     # the page's random-number generator, ported to Python

env = gym.make("MountainCar-v0", max_episode_steps=100000)   # no 200-step cut-off: every episode reaches the flag
EPS, NT, G = 0.2, 8, 8                            # careless pumping driver; 8 tilings of 9x9 tiles
d = NT * (G + 1) ** 2                             # 648 features
def tiles(o):                                     # indices of the 8 active tiles for observation o
    u = min(max((float(o[0]) + 1.2) / 1.8 * G, 0.0), G)  # clamp: float32(-0.07) is a hair below -0.07
    w = min(max((float(o[1]) + 0.07) / 0.14 * G, 0.0), G)
    return [k * (G + 1) ** 2 + math.floor(u + k % NT / NT) * (G + 1) + math.floor(w + 3 * k % NT / NT) for k in range(NT)]
def policy(o, R):                                 # push the way you are moving; 1 time in 5, a random pedal
    return int(R() * 3) if R() < EPS else (2 if o[1] >= 0 else 0)

R, episodes = Mulberry(1), []
for i in range(1000):                             # 1,000 training episodes from Gymnasium's own start states
    o, _ = env.reset(seed=i); ep = []
    while True:
        o2, r, term, trunc, _ = env.step(policy(o, R)); ep.append((tiles(o), r, None if term else tiles(o2))); o = o2
        if term: break
    episodes.append(ep)

T = np.load("truth.npz")                          # 1,467 held-out on-policy states, truth = mean of 1,000 rollouts
XT = [tiles(np.array(s, dtype=np.float32)) for s in T["TS"]]
def rmsve(w): return math.sqrt(np.mean([(w[x].sum() + T["V"][i]) ** 2 for i, x in enumerate(XT)]))   # v = -steps

def run(alpha, ckpts):
    wm, wt, A, b, out = np.zeros(d), np.zeros(d), np.zeros((d, d)), np.zeros(d), []
    rows, cols, Gs = [], [], []
    for n, ep in enumerate(episodes, 1):
        Gt = -len(ep)                             # return from the first state = minus the steps to the flag
        for x, r, x2 in ep:
            wm[x] += alpha * (Gt - wm[x].sum())   # gradient Monte Carlo
            target = r + (0.0 if x2 is None else wt[x2].sum())
            wt[x] += alpha * (target - wt[x].sum())   # semi-gradient TD(0)
            for i in x:                           # LSTD statistics: A += x (x - x')^T, b += r x
                A[i, x] += 1; b[i] += r
                if x2 is not None: A[i, x2] -= 1
            rows += [len(Gs)] * NT; cols += x; Gs.append(Gt); Gt += 1
        if n in ckpts:
            wl = np.linalg.solve(A + 0.01 * np.eye(d), b)                     # LSTD, epsilon = 0.01
            X = sp.csr_matrix((np.ones(len(rows)), (rows, cols)), shape=(len(Gs), d))
            wr = Ridge(alpha=0.01, fit_intercept=False, solver="cholesky").fit(X, np.array(Gs, float)).coef_
            out.append((n, len(Gs), rmsve(wm), rmsve(wt), rmsve(wl), rmsve(wr)))
    return out

print(f"training steps per episode: mean {np.mean([len(e) for e in episodes]):.2f}; test states {len(XT)}, "
      f"mean true time-to-goal {T['V'].mean():.2f} steps")
for tau in (2, 10):                               # step size alpha = 1 / (tau * x.x), and x.x = 8 active tiles
    print(f"\nRMS value error in steps, alpha = 1/({tau} x 8) for the two online learners")
    print(" episodes   steps   gradient MC   semi-grad TD     LSTD   least squares on returns")
    for n, s, a, b_, c, e in run(1 / (tau * NT), {10, 30, 100, 300, 1000}):

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Approximation replaces a table with v̂(s, w), d « |𝒮| weights; every update generalises to the states that share weights, for better and for worse.
  • The yardstick is VE: squared value error weighted by the on-policy distribution μ, so errors in states the policy rarely visits count for little.
  • Gradient Monte Carlo is true SGD on VE with the return as an unbiased target; semi-gradient TD bootstraps, ignores how its target depends on w, learns online, and converges (for linear v̂) to the TD fixed point, which can be worse than the best fit.
  • Pick α = 1/(τ·x⊤x) to learn from about τ experiences; LSTD skips step sizes entirely by solving Âw = b̂, at O(d²) per step.
  • Memory and kernel methods store examples and average neighbours at query time; interest says which states matter, and emphasis carries that interest forward to the states bootstrapping relies on.

Check your understanding

Why does VE weight states by μ rather than equally?

With fewer weights than states some errors are unavoidable, and μ puts them where the policy spends little time. Those are the states whose values the agent least needs when it acts; it is also the weighting that on-policy sampling delivers for free, so SGD on sampled states descends exactly this objective.

Gradient MC and semi-gradient TD use the same form of update. Why is only one of them true SGD?

Gradient MC’s target Gt does not depend on w, so the update is an unbiased sample of the negative gradient of VE. TD’s target contains v̂(St+1, w); differentiating the squared TD error would add a term for it, and TD drops that term. The result is not the gradient of any fixed objective.

You use 8 tilings and want each update to close about a tenth of the error. What α?

x⊤x = 8, so α = 1/(10 × 8) = 0.0125. Then αx⊤x = 1/10 and the estimate at the updated state moves one tenth of the way to its target.

In the project, LSTD was within about a step of its final error after only 30 drives, while TD with τ = 10 needed about 1,000 drives to get there. Why, and what does LSTD pay for it?

LSTD estimates A and b from all transitions and solves directly, so it doesn’t wait for values to propagate back, one small step at a time, from the flag. It pays with a 648×648 matrix and O(d²) work per step (or O(d³) per batch solve), against O(d) for TD.

With interest only on the start state, why does n-step TD need emphasis while Monte Carlo doesn’t?

MC’s target at the start state is the real return, so updating that state alone is enough. TD’s target leans on the estimate n steps later; if that state is never updated, its estimate stays wrong (or, if it shares the start state’s weight, the update can chase itself upwards). Emphasis Mt = It + γnMt−n updates exactly the states the interesting targets depend on.
