Likelihood Inference & the Bootstrap

How sure is the fit? Ask the data again, or ask the likelihood

Every curve we fit comes from one sample. Draw another sample from the same world and the curve would move. How far? There are two classic answers. The first is a formula: maximum likelihood says the spread of an estimate is the inverse of the Fisher information, the curvature of the log-likelihood at its peak. The second needs no formula at all: pretend your sample is the world, draw new samples from it with replacement, refit each one and watch the curve wobble. That is Efron’s bootstrap. On the right, click the plot to draw a resample. Big dots were picked several times, hollow dots not at all, and each resample adds one faint curve.

The idea

Resampling the data you have

Most of the models in this book were fitted by minimising a sum of squares or a cross-entropy. Both are special cases of one idea, maximum likelihood, and this chapter is about the question that comes after every fit: how much would the answer change with different data? A standard error, a confidence band, a p-value: all of them describe the spread of an estimate over samples we never drew.

Bradley Efron’s idea (1979) is to make those samples. Call the training data Z = (z1, …, zN) and the quantity you compute from it S(Z): a mean, a median, a fitted curve. In the real world Z was drawn from an unknown distribution F. We cannot draw from F again, but we can draw from the next best thing, the data itself: pick N points from Z at random with replacement. Some points appear twice or three times, some not at all. That is a bootstrap sample Z*. Make B of them, compute S(Z*1), …, S(Z*B), and their spread stands in for the spread you would see over real repeated samples.

Below, the data are N draws from a skewed distribution that we know (so we can cheat and also draw fresh samples from the real world). Pick a statistic and compare the two histograms: the bootstrap world, built from one sample, against the real world, built from 4,000 fresh ones.

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}[S] &= \frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\big(S^{*b} - \bar S^{*}\big)^2 \\ \bar S^{*} &= \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} S^{*b}, \quad S^{*b} = S(\mathbf{Z}^{*b}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: compute the statistic on each of the B bootstrap samples, then take the ordinary sample variance of those B numbers. Its square root is the bootstrap standard error. Nothing about S has to be known: no formula, no derivative, just the ability to compute it.
Say it“The bootstrap variance of S is one over B minus one times the sum of the squared deviations of S of Z-star-b from their mean.”
\[ \Big[\,S^{*}_{(2.5\%)},\ S^{*}_{(97.5\%)}\,\Big] \]
Read it as: the percentile interval. Sort the B bootstrap values and keep the middle 95%. If the bootstrap histogram is skewed, so is the interval, which a “± 1.96 standard errors” interval can never be.
Say it“From the 2.5th percentile of the bootstrap values to the 97.5th.”

Prediction error

Every resample leaves out a third

Can the bootstrap also estimate a model’s prediction error? The obvious recipe fits the model to each bootstrap sample and scores it on the original training set, treating the original as the test set. That is the plain bootstrap error estimate.

\[ \widehat{\text{Err}}_{\text{boot}} = \frac{1}{BN}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{i=1}^{N} L\big(y_i, \hat f^{*b}(x_i)\big) \]
Read it as: f̂*b is the model fitted to bootstrap sample b, and L is the loss, here the squared error. Score every resampled model on every original point, then average. The flaw: most of the points it is scored on were also in its training sample.
Say it“Err-boot is the average over b and i of the loss of f-star-b at x-i.”

How many? A given point is missed by one draw with probability 1 − 1/N, and by all N draws with probability (1 − 1/N)N. So a bootstrap sample contains about 63.2% of the distinct original points, and leaves out the other 36.8%: the out-of-bag points.

\[ \begin{aligned} \Pr\{\,i \in \mathbf{Z}^{*b}\} &= 1 - \Big(1 - \frac{1}{N}\Big)^{N} \\ &\approx 1 - e^{-1} = 0.632 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the chance that observation i is drawn at least once. The limit e−1 = 0.368 arrives fast: at N = 10 the chance of being left out is already 0.349.
Say it“The probability that point i is in bootstrap sample b is one minus one-minus-one-over-N to the N, about one minus e to the minus one, or 0.632.”

The cure mirrors cross-validation: score each point only with models that never saw it. Let C−i be the set of bootstrap samples that do not contain point i. The leave-one-out bootstrap estimate averages each point’s loss over those samples only. Draw resamples: in-bag points (filled, sized by how often they were drawn) hug the fit; out-of-bag points (hollow, with their misses drawn in red) are the honest test.

\[ \widehat{\text{Err}}^{(1)} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{|C^{-i}|}\sum_{b\in C^{-i}} L_i^{*b} \]
Read it as: Li*b = L(yi, f̂*b(xi)) is point i’s loss under the model fitted to resample b. For each point i, average its loss over the |C−i| models trained without it, then average over points. No point is ever scored by a model that trained on it, so the overfitting bias is gone. A new bias appears instead: each model learned from only about 0.632N distinct points, so it is a little worse than a model trained on all N.
Say it“Err-one is the average over i of the mean loss of point i over the bootstrap samples that leave it out.”

Correcting the bias

The .632 and .632+ estimators

The training error is too low and the leave-one-out bootstrap is usually too high. Efron (1983) proposed a weighted average that pulls Err(1) back towards the training error by the amount you would expect when a model sees 63.2% of the data:

\[ \widehat{\text{Err}}^{(.632)} = 0.368\cdot\overline{\text{err}} + 0.632\cdot\widehat{\text{Err}}^{(1)} \]
Read it as: err-bar is the training error of the model fitted to all N points. The weights come from the 63.2% calculation above: roughly, Err(1) behaves like a model trained on 0.632N points, so its excess over the training error is scaled back by that factor.
Say it“Err point-six-three-two is 0.368 times the training error plus 0.632 times Err-one.”

It works well when the model overfits a little, and breaks when it overfits a lot. The classic failure: two classes with no relation to x, and a 1-nearest-neighbour classifier. Every training point is its own nearest neighbour, so err-bar = 0; every prediction is a coin toss, so the true error and Err(1) are both 0.5. The .632 rule reports 0.632 × 0.5 = 0.316, far too hopeful. Efron and Tibshirani (1997) repaired this with two more ingredients. The first is the error you would get if inputs and labels had nothing to do with each other: pair every response with every prediction.

\[ \hat\gamma = \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{i'=1}^{N} L\big(y_i,\ \hat f(x_{i'})\big) \]
Read it as: the no-information error rate. Score every response yi against every prediction f̂(xi′), matched or not. For two classes this is p̂1(1 − q̂1) + (1 − p̂1)q̂1, where p̂1 is the share of class-1 labels and q̂1 the share of class-1 predictions.
Say it“Gamma-hat is the average loss over all N-squared pairings of a response with a prediction.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat R &= \frac{\widehat{\text{Err}}^{(1)} - \overline{\text{err}}}{\hat\gamma - \overline{\text{err}}} \\ \hat w &= \frac{0.632}{1 - 0.368\,\hat R} \\ \widehat{\text{Err}}^{(.632+)} &= (1 - \hat w)\,\overline{\text{err}} \\ &\quad + \hat w\,\widehat{\text{Err}}^{(1)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: R̂ is the relative overfitting rate: 0 when Err(1) equals the training error (no overfitting), 1 when it reaches the no-information rate (all fit, no signal). The weight on Err(1) slides from 0.632 at R̂ = 0 up to 1 at R̂ = 1. In practice Err(1) is capped at γ̂ and R̂ is kept between 0 and 1.
Say it“R-hat is Err-one minus err-bar over gamma-hat minus err-bar; w-hat is 0.632 over one minus 0.368 R-hat; Err point-six-three-two-plus is the w-hat blend of err-bar and Err-one.”

The example, continued

Choosing k: cross-validation against the bootstrap

Chapter 1.10 used cross-validation to choose a model’s complexity. The bootstrap estimators can do the same job. Which is better? Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman ran the comparison on k-nearest-neighbour models, for regression and for classification: draw a training set, estimate the error of every candidate k with each method, let each method pick its k, and measure how much worse the picked model is than the best one available, using the true error, which a simulation knows.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{rel. error} &= \frac{\text{Err}(\hat\alpha) - E_{\min}}{E_{\max} - E_{\min}} \\ E_{\min} &= \min_\alpha \text{Err}(\alpha) \\ E_{\max} &= \max_\alpha \text{Err}(\alpha) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: α runs over the candidate models (here the values of k) and α̂ is the one the method chose. 0 means it chose the best model there was; 1 means it chose the worst. Only the ranking of the candidates matters, not whether the estimated error is right in level.
Say it“The relative error is the true error of the chosen model minus the smallest true error, over the range of true errors.”

A smoothing example

Three ways to put a band on a curve

Now the inference problem proper. We have N = 50 points and want a smooth curve through them with an honest band around it. Use a cubic spline with K basis functions h1(x), …, hK(x): with 7 functions, three interior knots sit at the quartiles of x (the B-splines are drawn under the plot). Stack the basis values into an N × K matrix H whose row i is h(xi)⊤, and fit by least squares.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu(x) &= \sum_{j=1}^{K}\beta_j h_j(x) \\ \hat\beta &= (\mathbf{H}^\top\mathbf{H})^{-1}\mathbf{H}^\top\mathbf{y} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the curve is a weighted sum of K bumps. Least squares picks the weights β̂, exactly as in linear regression, with the bumps playing the role of features.
Say it“Mu of x is the sum of beta-j h-j of x; beta-hat is H-transpose-H inverse, H-transpose y.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\beta) &= (\mathbf{H}^\top\mathbf{H})^{-1}\hat\sigma^2 \\ \hat\sigma^2 &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(y_i - \hat\mu(x_i)\big)^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the weights inherit the noise of y through the fixed matrix (H⊤H)−1H⊤. σ̂² is the noise variance estimated from the residuals; dividing by N rather than N − K makes it the maximum-likelihood estimate.
Say it“The variance of beta-hat is H-transpose-H inverse times sigma-hat squared.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\mathrm{se}}\big[\hat\mu(x)\big] &= \hat\sigma\,\sqrt{h(x)^\top(\mathbf{H}^\top\mathbf{H})^{-1}h(x)} \\ \text{band:}\ \ &\hat\mu(x) \pm 1.96\cdot\widehat{\mathrm{se}}\big[\hat\mu(x)\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the fitted value at x is h(x)⊤β̂, so its variance is the quadratic form of h(x) with the covariance of β̂. Where data are sparse (the ends), the form is large and the band widens. ±1.96 standard errors gives a pointwise 95% band if the errors are Gaussian.
Say it“The standard error of mu-hat at x is the square root of h-of-x-transpose H-transpose-H inverse h of x, times sigma-hat.”

The bootstrap gets a band with no algebra. The nonparametric bootstrap resamples the (xi, yi) pairs. The parametric bootstrap keeps the xi and the fitted curve, and makes new responses by adding Gaussian noise of the estimated size. Either way, refit B times and keep the middle 95% of the curves at every x.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{nonparametric: } (x_i^*, y_i^*) \text{ drawn} \\ &\qquad\text{with replacement from } \mathbf{Z} \\ &\text{parametric: } y_i^* = \hat\mu(x_i) + \varepsilon_i^* \\ &\qquad \varepsilon_i^* \sim N(0, \hat\sigma^2) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the nonparametric version trusts nothing but the data; the parametric one trusts the model: the curve’s shape and Gaussian noise of variance σ̂². Each new data set gives a new μ̂*(x); the 2.5% and 97.5% points across them, at each x, make the band.
Say it“Either draw the pairs again with replacement, or keep x and set y-star to mu-hat of x plus fresh Gaussian noise with variance sigma-hat squared.”

Maximum likelihood inference

The log-likelihood’s curvature: Fisher information

Where did the least-squares formula come from? From a general recipe. Write down a probability model for one observation, a density gθ(z) with unknown parameter θ. The likelihood is the probability of the whole data set, viewed as a function of θ; its logarithm turns the product into a sum.

\[ \begin{aligned} L(\theta;\mathbf{Z}) &= \prod_{i=1}^{N} g_\theta(z_i) \\ \ell(\theta;\mathbf{Z}) &= \sum_{i=1}^{N}\log g_\theta(z_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: plug the data into the model and ask how probable they are for each candidate θ. The maximum-likelihood estimate θ̂ is the θ that makes the data most probable. Its log, ℓ, has the same peak and is easier to work with.
Say it“The likelihood of theta is the product of g-theta of z-i; the log-likelihood is the sum of their logs.”

The slope of ℓ is the score; it is zero at the peak. How sharply the peak curves is measured by the information: minus the second derivative. A sharp peak means the data rule out nearby values of θ firmly, so the estimate is precise.

\[ \begin{aligned} \dot\ell(\theta;\mathbf{Z}) &= \sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\,\ell(\theta;z_i)}{\partial\theta} \\ \dot\ell(\hat\theta;\mathbf{Z}) &= 0 \\ \mathbf{I}(\theta) &= -\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial^2\ell(\theta;z_i)}{\partial\theta\,\partial\theta^\top} \\ \mathbf{i}(\theta) &= \mathrm{E}_\theta\big[\mathbf{I}(\theta)\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ℓ̇ (“ell-dot”) is the score. I(θ) is the information matrix; evaluated at θ̂ it is the observed information. i(θ), its average over data sets drawn from the model, is the Fisher information. With one parameter both are single numbers; with several, matrices.
Say it“The score is the sum of the first derivatives of the log-likelihood; the information is minus the sum of second derivatives; the Fisher information is its expectation.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\theta &\;\to\; N\big(\theta_0,\ \mathbf{i}(\theta_0)^{-1}\big) \\ \widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\hat\theta_j) &= \sqrt{\mathbf{i}(\hat\theta)^{-1}_{jj}} \text{ or } \sqrt{\mathbf{I}(\hat\theta)^{-1}_{jj}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: as N grows, over repeated samples from the true parameter θ0, the estimate is approximately normal, centred on the truth, with variance equal to the inverse Fisher information. We don’t know θ0, so we plug in θ̂. The Wald interval θ̂j ± 1.96·se follows.
Say it“Theta-hat tends to a normal with mean theta-zero and variance the inverse Fisher information; the standard error is the root of the jj-th entry of the inverse information.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \Big\{\theta:\ &2\big[\ell(\hat\theta) - \ell(\theta)\big] \le 3.84\Big\} \\ &3.84 = \chi^{2\,(0.95)}_{1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the likelihood-ratio interval: keep every θ whose log-likelihood is within 1.92 of the peak. The Wald interval replaces the curve by its best-fitting parabola at the peak; this one uses the curve itself, so it can be lopsided and stays inside the parameter’s allowed range.
Say it“All theta for which twice the drop in log-likelihood from the peak is at most the chi-squared one quantile, 3.84.”

Back to the spline. Its model says yi = μ(xi) + εi with Gaussian εi of variance σ², so θ = (β, σ²). Maximising the Gaussian log-likelihood over β is minimising the sum of squares, which is why least squares is maximum likelihood. Differentiate twice and the information for β drops out in one line:

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell(\beta,\sigma^2) &= -\frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) \\ &\quad - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(y_i - h(x_i)^\top\beta\big)^2 \\ \mathbf{I}(\beta) &= \frac{\mathbf{H}^\top\mathbf{H}}{\sigma^2} \\ \mathbf{I}(\hat\beta)^{-1} &= (\mathbf{H}^\top\mathbf{H})^{-1}\hat\sigma^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the second derivative of the sum of squares with respect to β is 2H⊤H, so minus the second derivative of ℓ is H⊤H/σ². Its inverse, with σ̂² plugged in, is exactly the covariance used for the band above. The ML estimate of σ² is RSS/N.
Say it“The information for beta is H-transpose-H over sigma squared, so its inverse is H-transpose-H inverse times sigma-hat squared.”
Bootstrap versus maximum likelihood

The bootstrap is likelihood by computer

In the spline example the parametric bootstrap and the formula are not merely close: they are the same thing. Each parametric resample is the fixed matrix (H⊤H)−1H⊤ applied to Gaussian data, so the resampled weights have an exactly known distribution.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\beta^{*} &= (\mathbf{H}^\top\mathbf{H})^{-1}\mathbf{H}^\top\mathbf{y}^{*} \\ &\sim N\big(\hat\beta,\ \hat\sigma^2(\mathbf{H}^\top\mathbf{H})^{-1}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with y* = Hβ̂ + ε*, the weights β̂* are a linear function of Gaussian noise, so they are Gaussian with mean β̂ and exactly the inverse-information covariance. As B grows, the bootstrap band converges to the least-squares band. In general (non-Gaussian models) the parametric bootstrap agrees with maximum likelihood, not with least squares.
Say it“Beta-hat-star is H-transpose-H inverse H-transpose y-star, distributed normally with mean beta-hat and covariance sigma-hat squared H-transpose-H inverse.”

So why bother with the bootstrap? Because the formula only exists for the fit you wrote down in advance. Real analyses make choices from the data: which variables, how many knots, how much smoothing. The band from the formula pretends those choices were fixed. The bootstrap can repeat the choice inside every resample, so the band carries that extra uncertainty too. Here the number of basis functions K is chosen by 5-fold cross-validation, once on the data (for the fit) and again in every bootstrap sample. Switch between fixed K and K chosen afresh.

Bootstrap and Bayes

The bootstrap as a poor man’s posterior

A Bayesian answers “how sure?” differently: put a prior distribution on θ, then update it with the data to a posterior, which is a distribution for θ itself. The bootstrap distribution, built by resampling, turns out to resemble a posterior for a prior that says almost nothing.

\[ \Pr(\theta \mid \mathbf{Z}) = \frac{\Pr(\mathbf{Z}\mid\theta)\,\Pr(\theta)}{\int \Pr(\mathbf{Z}\mid\theta)\,\Pr(\theta)\,d\theta} \]
Read it as: posterior = likelihood × prior, rescaled to integrate to 1. The likelihood is the same L(θ; Z) as in maximum likelihood; the prior Pr(θ) is what you believed before the data.
Say it“The posterior of theta given Z is the likelihood times the prior, over its integral.”

The simplest case shows the link. One observation z ~ N(θ, 1) and a prior θ ~ N(0, τ). The parametric bootstrap would draw z* ~ N(z, 1).

\[ \begin{aligned} \theta \mid z \;&\sim\; N\!\left(\frac{z}{1 + 1/\tau},\ \frac{1}{1 + 1/\tau}\right) \\ &\to\ N(z,\ 1) \ \text{ as } \tau\to\infty \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the posterior mean shrinks z towards the prior mean 0 by the factor 1/(1 + 1/τ), and the posterior variance is a little less than 1. As the prior variance τ grows, the prior stops mattering and the posterior becomes N(z, 1): exactly the parametric bootstrap distribution of z*.
Say it“Theta given z is normal with mean z over one plus one-over-tau and variance one over one plus one-over-tau, which tends to normal z, one as tau grows.”

The same is true of the plain, nonparametric bootstrap. Suppose the data can take only L distinct values, with unknown probabilities w = (w1, …, wL), and you observe proportions ŵ. Give w a Dirichlet prior, the standard prior for a probability vector.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{prior:}\ & w \sim \mathrm{Di}_L(a\mathbf{1}) \\ \text{posterior:}\ & w \mid \hat w \sim \mathrm{Di}_L(a\mathbf{1} + N\hat w) \\ a\to 0\text{:}\ & w \mid \hat w \sim \mathrm{Di}_L(N\hat w) \\ \text{bootstrap:}\ & N\hat w^{*} \sim \mathrm{Mult}(N,\ \hat w) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the posterior adds the observed counts Nŵ to the prior’s pseudo-counts a. As a → 0 the prior says nothing, and the posterior gives each observed value a random weight around its observed share. The bootstrap also gives each value a random weight, its share of a multinomial draw. The two are different distributions with almost the same shape.
Say it“Prior Dirichlet a-one; posterior Dirichlet a-one plus N w-hat, tending to Dirichlet N w-hat; the bootstrap counts are multinomial N, w-hat.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{E}_{\text{boot}}(w_j) &= \mathrm{E}_{\text{post}}(w_j) = \hat w_j \\ \mathrm{Var}_{\text{boot}}(w_j) &= \frac{\hat w_j(1-\hat w_j)}{N} \\ \mathrm{Var}_{\text{post}}(w_j) &= \frac{\hat w_j(1-\hat w_j)}{N+1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the bootstrap and the flat-prior posterior agree on the centre and differ in spread only by the factor N/(N + 1). So the bootstrap distribution of a statistic is, to a close approximation, its posterior under a noninformative prior, obtained without writing a prior or a likelihood. Drawing the Dirichlet weights directly instead of resampling is Rubin’s (1981) Bayesian bootstrap.
Say it“Both centre on w-hat-j; the bootstrap variance is w-hat-j times one minus w-hat-j over N, the posterior’s over N plus one.”

Real-world project

Project: how fast is London’s NO₂ falling?

Nitrogen dioxide comes mostly from road traffic, and London spent the 2010s fighting it: cleaner buses, the Ultra Low Emission Zone from April 2019. The Greater London Authority publishes the London-wide monthly mean NO₂ at roadside and at background monitoring sites from January 2008 (London Datastore, “London Average Air Quality Levels”, Open Government Licence v2; the readings come from the London Air Quality Network). We use all 139 months up to August 2019 (July 2019 is missing, and one row labelled “Dec-12” sits between November 2013 and January 2014, so we read it as December 2013).

The model is the smoothing example with one addition for the seasons: NO₂ = trend(t) + a·cos(2πm/12) + b·sin(2πm/12) + noise, where the trend is a cubic B-spline in time t with K basis functions (equally spaced knots) and m is the month of the year. That is K + 2 weights, fitted by least squares. Then we put four kinds of interval on it: the Fisher-information formula (with the delta method for curved functions of β: se² ≈ ∇g⊤ I(β̂)−1 ∇g), the nonparametric (pairs) bootstrap, the parametric bootstrap, and a moving-block bootstrap that resamples runs of 12 consecutive residuals, in case neighbouring months are related. Three questions, four kinds of interval

By how much did the de-seasonalised level change from January 2009 to January 2019? By what percentage did it fall? How big is the seasonal swing, A = √(a² + b²), the gap between the average winter peak and the yearly mean? The first is linear in β, so the formula is exact for it; the other two are curved, so the formula relies on the delta method. Every bootstrap uses B = 1,000 resamples with fixed seeds, the same ones the Python reference below uses.

import csv, sys, numpy as np
from scipy.interpolate import BSpline
from scipy.stats import norm

rows = list(csv.DictReader(open(sys.argv[1], encoding="utf-8-sig")))   # air-quality-london-monthly-averages.csv
y = np.array([float(r["London Mean Roadside:Nitrogen Dioxide (ug/m3)"]) for r in rows])   # ug/m3
MON = "JanFebMarAprMayJunJulAugSepOctNovDec"
m = np.array([(int(r["Month"][4:]) - 8) * 12 + MON.index(r["Month"][:3]) // 3 for r in rows])   # months since Jan 2008
bad = np.flatnonzero(np.diff(m) <= 0) + 1; m[bad] = m[bad - 1] + 1   # the row labelled Dec-12 between Nov-13 and Jan-14
t = 2008 + m / 12; N = len(y)

def mulberry32(a):                                # the page's random numbers, so both draw the same resamples
    a &= 0xFFFFFFFF
    def r():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        z = (a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        z = ((z + ((z ^ (z >> 7)) * (61 | z) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ z
        return ((z ^ (z >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 2**32
    return r
def gauss(r):
    u = 0.0
    while u == 0: u = r()
    return np.sqrt(-2 * np.log(u)) * np.cos(2 * np.pi * r())

def basis(tt, K, a=2008.0, b=2008 + 140 / 12):    # K cubic B-splines (equal knots) + a yearly cosine and sine
    kn = np.r_[[a] * 4, np.linspace(a, b, K - 2)[1:-1], [b] * 4]
    B = BSpline.design_matrix(np.clip(tt, a, b - 1e-9), kn, 3).toarray()

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • The bootstrap treats the sample as the population: resample N points with replacement, recompute the statistic B times, and use the spread as its standard error or the middle 95% as its interval.
  • A bootstrap sample contains about 63.2% of the distinct points. Scoring models on points they trained on is optimistic; the leave-one-out bootstrap Err(1) is pessimistic; .632 blends them, and .632+ raises the weight on Err(1) when the model overfits.
  • Maximum likelihood gives standard errors from curvature: se(θ̂) ≈ 1/√I(θ̂). For Gaussian least squares this is the familiar σ̂²(H⊤H)−1.
  • The parametric bootstrap reproduces the likelihood answer; the bootstrap’s real power is the cases with no formula, such as a fit whose complexity was itself chosen from the data.
  • The bootstrap distribution is approximately a Bayesian posterior under a noninformative prior: same centre, spread smaller by only a factor N/(N + 1) in variance.

Check your understanding

A data set has N = 5 points. What is the chance that a given point is left out of a bootstrap sample?

(1 − 1/5)5 = 0.85 = 0.328. So on average a resample contains 67.2% of the distinct points, a little more than the large-N limit of 63.2%.

Why does the plain bootstrap error estimate, which scores each resampled model on the original training set, come out too low?

About 63.2% of the points it is scored on were in that model’s training sample, so much of the “test” is really training error. For a 1-nearest-neighbour model those points are predicted perfectly.

The labels are pure noise and you use 1-NN. What do err-bar, Err(1), .632 and .632+ report?

err-bar = 0 and Err(1) ≈ 0.5. The .632 rule gives about 0.316. The no-information rate γ̂ ≈ 0.5, so R̂ = 1, ŵ = 1, and .632+ gives about 0.5, the true error.

The log-likelihood of a Poisson rate peaks at λ̂ = 4 from N = 25 counts. What is the Wald 95% interval?

The information at the peak is I(λ̂) = N/λ̂ = 6.25, so se = 1/√6.25 = 0.4 and the interval is 4 ± 1.96 × 0.4 = [3.22, 4.78].

You choose the number of spline knots by cross-validation and then report the least-squares band. What has the band left out, and how would you put it back?

The uncertainty of the choice itself: another sample could have led to a different number of knots. Bootstrap the whole procedure, re-running the cross-validation inside every resample, and take the band from those fits.
