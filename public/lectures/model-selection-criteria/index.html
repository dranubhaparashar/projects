Model Selection Criteria

Optimism, Cp, AIC, BIC and minimum description length

Cross-validation judges a model by refitting it again and again. There is a cheaper route: fit each candidate once, take its training error, and add a penalty for every parameter it used. Choose the penalty well and the training error stops flattering. This chapter derives four such penalties, Mallows’ Cp, Akaike’s AIC, Schwarz’s BIC and Rissanen’s minimum description length, and shows when to trust each one. On the right, drag any point and watch AIC and BIC choose their polynomials.

The flaw

Training error is optimistic

Chapter 1.10 measured a model on data it had never seen. This chapter takes the other road: keep the training error, but correct it. To do that we must say exactly which error we are after. Keep the inputs x1, …, xN fixed, fit the model to their responses yi, and then imagine nature drawing fresh responses Y0i at the very same inputs. The average loss on those fresh responses is the in-sample error. The loss on the responses the model was fitted to is the training error. The fit has already bent towards the noise in its own yi, and it gets no such help with the fresh ones, so the training error comes out too low. The gap is called the optimism.

In-sample error is not the same as the test error at new inputs from chapter 1.10, but for choosing between models it usually ranks them the same way, and it has one great advantage: its gap from the training error can be worked out exactly. Raise the number of parameters: the average optimism climbs in a straight line.

\[ \begin{aligned} \overline{\text{err}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} L\big(y_i,\hat f(x_i)\big) \\ \text{Err}_{\text{in}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{E}_{Y^0}\big[L_i^0 \mid \mathcal{T}\big] \\ L_i^0 &= L\big(Y_i^0,\hat f(x_i)\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: err-bar averages the loss L over the N training pairs, the very responses the fit was built from. Errin keeps the fitted model f̂ and the same inputs, but scores it on new responses Y0i drawn at those inputs, averaged over that fresh noise; the bar “| 𝒯” says the training set is held fixed. With squared error and a known truth f, Errin = σ² + (1/N)∑(f(xi) − f̂(xi))².
Say it“err-bar is the average training loss; Err-in is the average loss on fresh responses at the same inputs.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{op} &\equiv \text{Err}_{\text{in}} - \overline{\text{err}} \\ \omega &\equiv \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}(\text{op}) = 2\,\frac{d}{N}\,\sigma_\varepsilon^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: op is the optimism of one training set: how much its training error flatters. ω is its average over many training sets that share the inputs and differ only in their noise. For a least-squares fit with d parameters and noise variance σε², that average is exactly 2dσε²/N: every parameter adds the same amount, and more data shrinks it.
Say it“Optimism is in-sample error minus training error; on average it is two d sigma-squared over N.”

Where optimism comes from

Each point pulls its own fit

Why is the training error too low? Because each yi helps choose its own fitted value. If the noise pushes yi up, the fit leans up towards it, and the residual yi − ŷi comes out smaller than the real miss. The more a fitted value follows its own response, the larger the covariance between them, and the larger the optimism. That gives a general formula, valid for squared error, 0–1 loss and several other losses, which no longer mentions parameters at all.

For a linear smoother, any method whose fitted values are a fixed matrix S times the responses (least squares, ridge, k-nearest neighbours, splines), the covariance is simply σ² times the diagonal entry Sii, the point’s self-influence or leverage. Drag any point up or down: its own fitted value follows by exactly Sii times your move.

\[ \omega = \frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{Cov}(\hat y_i, y_i) \]
Read it as: the covariance is taken over repeated training sets at the same inputs: when the noise in yi goes up, how far does ŷi go up with it? Sum over points, double, divide by N. A model that ignores its own responses (a fixed constant) has zero optimism; one that interpolates them (ŷi = yi) has the most.
Say it“Omega is two over N times the sum of the covariances between each fitted value and its own response.”
\[ \begin{gathered} \hat{\mathbf y} = \mathbf S\mathbf y \;\Rightarrow\; \operatorname{Cov}(\hat y_i, y_i) = \sigma_\varepsilon^2\, S_{ii} \\ \omega = \frac{2\,\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{N}\operatorname{trace}(\mathbf S) \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: nudge yi by Δ and ŷi moves by SiiΔ. For least squares S is the hat matrix, whose trace is the number of parameters d, so the formula gives back 2dσ²/N.
Say it“If y-hat is S times y, each covariance is sigma-squared times S-i-i, and omega is two sigma-squared over N times the trace of S.”

Estimating in-sample error

Cp and AIC: add the optimism back

If the training error is too low by 2dσ²/N on average, the remedy writes itself: add that amount back. The only unknown is the noise variance σ². Mallows’ suggestion is to estimate it from a low-bias model, the largest one in the list, whose residuals are mostly noise. The result is the Cp statistic, an estimate of the in-sample error for each candidate. Compute it for every model and keep the smallest.

Akaike asked the same question for any model fitted by maximum likelihood. His information criterion estimates how well the fitted model would predict new data, measured by log-likelihood, and the correction is again two per parameter. For Gaussian noise with known variance AIC and Cp rank models identically; for logistic regression the log-likelihood is the binomial one. When a model has a tuning knob α instead of a parameter count, replace d by its effective count d(α), the subject of the next section. Below, the truth is known, so the page can also draw the real in-sample error that Cp is trying to estimate. Change how σ² is estimated and watch the choice move.

\[ C_p = \overline{\text{err}} + 2\,\frac{d}{N}\,\hat\sigma_\varepsilon^2 \]
Read it as: the training error plus the average optimism, with σ² replaced by an estimate. Because it uses an average optimism, Cp is right on average, not for every sample: for one data set it can still pick too big or too small a model.
Say it“C-p is the training error plus two d over N times the estimated noise variance.”
\[ \begin{gathered} \text{AIC} = -\frac{2}{N}\,\text{loglik} + 2\,\frac{d}{N} \\ \text{loglik} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\log \Pr\nolimits_{\hat\theta}(y_i) \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: loglik is the maximised log-likelihood; −2/N times it is the training loss on the log scale, and 2d/N is its optimism (exact for linear Gaussian models, and approximately true for large N in general). With Gaussian noise and known σ², AIC = Cp/σ² + a constant. With σ² unknown and set to RSS/N, N·AIC = N log(RSS/N) + 2d + a constant, the form used in the project.
Say it“AIC is minus two over N times the log-likelihood, plus two d over N.”

When d is not a count

The effective number of parameters

Cp and AIC need d. For least squares, d counts the columns. But ridge regression keeps all its coefficients and shrinks them; a nearest-neighbour smoother has no coefficients at all; and a procedure that chooses its predictors from many has looked at the data more than its final count admits. The covariance formula from the last section gives one definition that covers them all: the effective degrees of freedom is the total self-influence, measured in units of σ².

For a linear smoother it is trace(S): 12 coefficients under a ridge penalty may be worth only 4 parameters, and k-NN on N points is worth N/k. For an adaptive procedure there is no S, but the covariance can still be estimated by simulation. Picking the best one of ten useless predictors costs nearly four parameters, not one. Pick a method and slide its knob: the line is the formula, the dots are simulation.

\[ \operatorname{df}(\hat{\mathbf y}) = \frac{1}{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{Cov}(\hat y_i, y_i) \]
Read it as: the effective number of parameters is the total covariance between fitted values and their own responses, divided by the noise variance. Plug it in for d in Cp or AIC and the optimism formula stays correct.
Say it“The degrees of freedom of y-hat is the sum of the covariances of each fitted value with its response, over sigma-squared.”
\[ \begin{gathered} \operatorname{df}(\mathbf S) = \operatorname{trace}(\mathbf S) \\ \operatorname{df}_{\text{ridge}}(\lambda) = \sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda} \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: for a linear smoother, add up the diagonal of S. For ridge regression S = X(XTX + λI)−1XT, and with dj² the eigenvalues of XTX each direction contributes a fraction between 0 and 1: all of it at λ = 0, almost none when λ is huge.
Say it“The df of a smoother is the trace of S; for ridge it is the sum of d-j-squared over d-j-squared plus lambda.”

A different question

BIC: the Bayesian shortcut

Cp and AIC ask which model will predict best. A Bayesian asks which model is most probable given the data. Give each candidate a prior probability; then its posterior probability is proportional to the prior times its evidence Pr(Z | ℳ), the probability the model gave to the data, averaged over its own parameter prior (chapter 1.2e computed it exactly). That integral is usually hard. Schwarz’s shortcut is a Laplace approximation around the best fit: the evidence is roughly the best-fit likelihood times a penalty of N−1/2 per parameter, because the region of good parameter values shrinks like 1/√N in every direction.

Multiply the log by −2 and you have the Bayesian information criterion. Its penalty, log N per parameter, beats AIC’s 2 as soon as N > e² ≈ 7.4. Below is a polynomial regression with a Gaussian prior, where the exact evidence is available, so you can see how good the shortcut is. Slide N and compare the two curves and the posterior probabilities they imply.

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\Pr(\mathcal{M}_m\mid \mathbf{Z})}{\Pr(\mathcal{M}_\ell\mid \mathbf{Z})} &= \frac{\Pr(\mathcal{M}_m)}{\Pr(\mathcal{M}_\ell)} \\ &\quad\times\frac{\Pr(\mathbf{Z}\mid \mathcal{M}_m)}{\Pr(\mathbf{Z}\mid \mathcal{M}_\ell)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the posterior odds of model m against model ℓ are the prior odds times the Bayes factor, the ratio of the two evidences. With equal prior probabilities only the evidence matters. Z is the whole training set.
Say it“Posterior odds equal prior odds times the Bayes factor.”
\[ \begin{gathered} \log \Pr(\mathbf{Z}\mid \mathcal{M}_m) = \text{loglik}_m \\ \qquad - \frac{d_m}{2}\log N + O(1) \\[6pt] \text{BIC} = -2\,\text{loglik} + (\log N)\,d \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: the log evidence is the maximised log-likelihood loglikm = log Pr(Z | θ̂m, ℳm), minus half log N for each of the dm parameters, plus a remainder that stays bounded as N grows (it depends on the prior). BIC is −2 times the first two terms. For Gaussian noise with known σ², BIC = (N/σ²)[err-bar + (log N)(d/N)σ²]: AIC with 2 replaced by log N.
Say it“Log evidence is about the best log-likelihood minus d over two log N; BIC is minus two log-likelihood plus log N times d.”
\[ \Pr(\mathcal{M}_m\mid \mathbf{Z}) \approx \frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\text{BIC}_m}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{M} e^{-\frac{1}{2}\text{BIC}_\ell}} \]
Read it as: with equal priors, turn BIC values into approximate posterior probabilities of the M candidates. Only differences in BIC matter: a lead of 2 means odds of e ≈ 2.7 to 1, a lead of 10 means about 150 to 1. Treat these as rough, because the O(1) term has been thrown away.
Say it“The probability of model m is about e to the minus half its BIC, over the same sum for every model.”

Consistent or efficient

AIC or BIC? It depends on the truth

BIC’s heavier penalty makes it choose smaller models. Which habit is better depends on what the world is like. If one of the candidates is exactly true, BIC is consistent: as N grows, the chance that it picks the true model goes to 1. AIC never gets there; it keeps a fixed chance of adding a parameter it does not need. If none of the candidates is true, because the truth is more complicated than any model in the list (the usual situation with real data), the right model size keeps growing with N. Then AIC is efficient: its choice predicts about as well as the best possible choice, while BIC tends to stop too early. And with small samples, BIC’s penalty often makes it choose models that are too simple.

Below, 300 data sets are drawn for every N and both criteria choose a polynomial from d = 1 to 12. Switch the truth, then slide N.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{AIC} &= N\log\hat\sigma^2_d + 2d \\ \text{BIC} &= N\log\hat\sigma^2_d + (\log N)\,d \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with σ² unknown, −2 loglik = N log σ̂d² + a constant, where σ̂d² = RSSd/N. An extra parameter is worth adding only if it lowers N log σ̂² by more than 2 (AIC) or more than log N (BIC). A useless parameter lowers it by roughly a χ²1 amount, which tops 2 about 16% of the time, whatever N is; topping log N becomes ever rarer as N grows.
Say it“AIC is N log sigma-hat-squared plus two d; BIC is the same with log N in place of two.”

Models as codes

Minimum description length

Rissanen’s view starts somewhere else entirely: a model is a way to compress data. Suppose you must send your data to a friend down a wire, as cheaply as possible. Shannon showed that a message with probability Pr(z) needs about −log₂ Pr(z) bits in the best code, so likely messages get short codewords and rare ones long. No code can beat the entropy on average. Here is a code for a five-symbol weather report:

To send data with a model you first send the model’s parameters, then the data coded with the model’s probabilities. A rich model makes the data cheap but costs bits to describe. Pick the model with the shortest total message. Sending a parameter to the precision the data justify (about 1/√N) costs about ½ log₂ N bits, so the total length is BIC/2 measured in bits: MDL and BIC are the same criterion reached from coding instead of probability. Below, a stream of bits; the candidate models predict each bit from the previous k bits, with 2k probabilities to send. Pick a source and see which memory length compresses best.

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathbb{E}(\text{length}) \ge -\sum_{i} p_i\log_2 p_i \\ p_i = \Pr(z_i) \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: whatever prefix code you use for messages zi sent with probabilities Pr(zi), the average codeword length is at least the entropy. Codeword lengths of exactly −log₂ Pr(zi) reach it; whole bits get within one bit of it.
Say it“The expected code length is at least minus the sum of p log-two p.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{length} &= -\log \Pr(\mathbf{y}\mid \theta, \mathcal{M}, \mathbf{X}) \\ &\quad - \log \Pr(\theta\mid \mathcal{M}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the cost of sending the responses y with model ℳ is the bits for the data given the parameters θ (the receiver already has the inputs X) plus the bits for θ itself. Minimising it is the same as maximising the posterior of θ, and choosing the model with the minimum length is, to first order, choosing by BIC.
Say it“Length is minus log the probability of the data given the model, minus log the probability of the parameters.”
\[ \begin{aligned} L(k) &= \underbrace{-\sum_{t}\log_2 \hat p_k(x_t \mid \mathbf{h}_t)}_{\text{data, given the model}} \\ &\quad + \underbrace{\frac{2^k}{2}\log_2 N}_{\text{parameters}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a model with memory k keeps one probability of a 1 for each of the 2k possible histories ht = (xt−k, …, xt−1), estimated by counting. The first term is the length of the bit stream coded with those probabilities; the second sends the 2k probabilities at ½ log₂ N bits each. Sending the raw bits costs exactly N.
Say it“L of k is the code length of the data under the order-k model plus two-to-the-k over two times log-two N.”

What the estimates estimate

Conditional or expected test error?

Chapter 1.10 had two targets. Err𝒯 is the test error of the model you fitted, on your training set 𝒯. Err is its average over all the training sets you might have drawn. Err𝒯 is what you would like to know. The awkward truth is that cross-validation, and criteria like Cp, mostly estimate Err: they tell you how well this kind of model does on this kind of data, and very little about this particular fit.

To see it, take ordinary least squares with p = 10 Gaussian inputs and draw many training sets. With identity-covariance inputs the true conditional error is known exactly for each one, so we can set the 10-fold CV estimate beside it. If CV knew about the particular training set, the dots would hug the diagonal. Run more training sets and look at the cloud.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Err}_{\mathcal{T}} &= \mathbb{E}_{X^0,Y^0}\big[L\big(Y^0,\hat f(X^0)\big)\,\big|\,\mathcal{T}\big] \\ \text{Err} &= \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}\big[\text{Err}_{\mathcal{T}}\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Err𝒯 fixes the training set and averages the loss over new inputs and responses; Err also averages over the training set. Unlike Errin, both use new inputs.
Say it“Err-T is the expected loss at a new point given this training set; Err is its average over training sets.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Err}_{\mathcal{T}} &= \sigma^2 + \lVert\hat\beta - \beta\rVert^2 \\ \text{Err} &= \sigma^2\Big(1 + \frac{p}{N-p-1}\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for least squares with inputs X ~ N(0, I) and no intercept, a new point’s squared error is the noise plus the squared distance of the fitted coefficients from the true ones. Averaging over training sets gives the second line (the mean of an inverse Wishart matrix).
Say it“Err-T is sigma-squared plus the squared coefficient error; Err is sigma-squared times one plus p over N minus p minus one.”

Real-world project

Project: how far back should a demand forecast look?

Great Britain’s electricity system operator, NESO, publishes the national demand on the transmission network every half hour (Historic Demand Data, NESO Open Data Licence). Averaging the 48 half-hours gives one number per day, in gigawatts. Demand has a strong weekly rhythm (weekends are quieter), a yearly one (winters are dearer) and a slow downward drift as rooftop solar grows. A classic forecaster is the autoregression AR(p): tomorrow’s demand is a weighted sum of the last p days plus a constant. The question for this chapter is p. One day back? A week? Four?

We fit on 2022–2024 and keep 2025 sealed for one-day-ahead forecasts. Every candidate uses the same 1,068 training days (the first 28 are reserved as history), so the criteria compare like with like; with a pure autoregression, ordinary 10-fold cross-validation over those rows is valid (Bergmeir, Hyndman & Koo, 2018). The same project in Python

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold, cross_val_predict

def daily(years):                                   # NESO Historic Demand Data, one CSV per year
    d = pd.concat(pd.read_csv(f"demanddata_{y}.csv", usecols=["SETTLEMENT_DATE", "ND"]) for y in years)
    s = d.SETTLEMENT_DATE.str.strip('"')
    iso = s.str[4] == "-"                           # 2025 switched to ISO dates
    d["date"] = pd.to_datetime(s.where(iso), format="%Y-%m-%d").fillna(pd.to_datetime(s.where(~iso), format="mixed", dayfirst=True))
    return (d.groupby("date").ND.mean() / 1000).round(3)          # daily mean demand, GW (to 1 MW)

P = 28                                              # look back at most four weeks
for train, test in (((2022, 2023, 2024), 2025), ((2016, 2017, 2018), 2019)):
    y = daily(list(train) + [test]).values
    n_tr = len(y) - (366 if test % 4 == 0 else 365)
    lags = np.column_stack([y[P - j:n_tr - j] for j in range(1, P + 1)])   # column j-1 = demand j days earlier
    target = y[P:n_tr]; N = len(target)
    rows = []
    for p in range(1, P + 1):
        X = lags[:, :p]
        m = LinearRegression().fit(X, target)
        rss = np.sum((target - m.predict(X)) ** 2); d = p + 1          # p lags + intercept
        aic = N * np.log(rss / N) + 2 * d
        bic = N * np.log(rss / N) + np.log(N) * d
        cv = np.mean((target - cross_val_predict(LinearRegression(), X, target, cv=KFold(10))) ** 2)
        Xt = np.column_stack([y[n_tr - j:len(y) - j] for j in range(1, p + 1)])
        test_rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((y[n_tr:] - m.predict(Xt)) ** 2))
        rows.append((p, np.sqrt(rss / N), aic, bic, np.sqrt(cv), test_rmse))
    r = np.array(rows)
    pick = {k: int(r[np.argmin(r[:, c]), 0]) for k, c in (("AIC", 2), ("BIC", 3), ("10-fold CV", 4))}
    naive = np.sqrt(np.mean((y[n_tr:] - y[n_tr - 7:len(y) - 7]) ** 2))
    print(f"train {train[0]}-{train[-1]} (N = {N} days), test {test}: picks {pick}")
    print("   p  train RMSE      AIC        BIC     CV RMSE  test RMSE")
    for p in sorted({1, 7, 14, 21, *pick.values(), 28}):
        print("  %2d   %.5f  %9.4f  %9.4f   %.5f   %.5f" % tuple(r[p - 1]))
    print(f"  same day last week: test RMSE {naive:.5f}\n")

The browser fits every order with one QR factorisation of the lagged design (one per fold for CV) instead of separate least-squares solves; every AIC, BIC and RMSE in the printout is matched by the page to the digits shown, and the chosen orders are identical. Two honest caveats. First, demand has no true finite order: AIC and CV keep finding small gains at each weekly echo (lags 22, 27, 29, 43, 50…), so if you let them look further back than four weeks they choose longer models still, while BIC holds at 22. Second, the differences between the leading orders are small, about 0.02 GW in test RMSE, against a typical one-day miss of 1.4 GW; a criterion is a principled tie-breaker, not an oracle. Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Training error flatters by the optimism, on average (2/N)∑Cov(ŷi, yi): exactly 2dσ²/N for least squares with d parameters.
  • Cp and AIC add that optimism back: training loss + 2·d/N (times σ̂² for squared error), with σ̂² from a low-bias model.
  • When parameters are shrunk, smoothed or selected, use the effective degrees of freedom: trace(S) for linear smoothers, simulation for adaptive ones.
  • BIC = −2 loglik + (log N)d approximates −2 log evidence; it is consistent when the true model is in the list, while AIC predicts better when it is not. MDL reaches BIC by counting bits.
  • CV and these criteria estimate the expected error Err, not the error of your particular fit, Err𝒯.

Check your understanding

A least-squares model with 20 parameters is fitted to 100 points with noise variance 4. By how much does its training error flatter, on average?

ω = 2dσ²/N = 2 × 20 × 4 / 100 = 1.6 in mean squared error. Its in-sample error is on average 1.6 higher than its training error.

Why does Cp estimate σ² from the largest model rather than from each candidate?

A small candidate’s residuals contain its bias as well as noise, so its own variance estimate is too big, and an inflated σ̂² makes every extra parameter look too expensive. The largest model has little bias, so its residual variance (with N − d in the denominator) is close to the noise alone.

A 5-nearest-neighbour smoother is fitted to 200 points. What is its effective number of parameters?

Each fitted value is the average of 5 responses including its own, so Sii = 1/5, and df = trace(S) = 200/5 = 40.

With N = 1,000, how much must an extra parameter lower −2 loglik to be accepted by AIC? By BIC?

AIC needs a drop of more than 2; BIC needs more than log 1000 ≈ 6.9. A useless parameter passes AIC’s bar about 16% of the time, BIC’s under 1%.

Your 10-fold CV error is unusually low for this kind of model. Does that mean your particular fitted model is unusually good?

Not necessarily. CV’s estimate is only weakly related to the conditional error of the specific fit; it mostly estimates the average error over training sets. A low CV number can come from an easy split of the data as much as from a good fit.
