Bayesian Neural Networks

Keep every network the data allow, and let them argue where the data run out

Training a network the usual way picks one set of weights and trusts it completely, even far from anything it has seen. The Bayesian view keeps a whole distribution over weights: every network that explains the data reasonably well, weighted by how well. Where the data are dense these networks agree; where the data stop they fan out, and that spread is an honest error bar. On the right, each thin line is one network drawn from the posterior by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. Tap the plot to add a measurement, or drag one, and watch the fan close where you put data.

The idea

A posterior over weights

Chapter 1.2d put a Gaussian prior on the weights of a linear model and got a Gaussian posterior back, in closed form. A neural network uses the same two ingredients: a prior that says small weights are more plausible, and a likelihood that says the outputs should land near the targets, give or take noise with precision β. Bayes’ rule combines them into a posterior over weights. The difference is that a network’s output depends on its weights through tanh units, so the posterior is no longer Gaussian, and no formula gives it to us.

We can still look at it. Below, an eight-hidden-unit network (25 weights) is fitted to the measurements on the left and right; there are none in the middle. The lines are networks drawn from the prior or from the posterior. Slide the number of points seen from 0 to 13 and watch the prior’s wild guesses collapse onto the data, while the gap and the edges stay uncertain.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf w \mid \mathcal D) &= \frac{p(\mathcal D \mid \mathbf w)\, p(\mathbf w)}{p(\mathcal D)} \\ p(\mathbf w \mid \alpha) &= \mathcal N(\mathbf w \mid \mathbf 0, \alpha^{-1} I) \\ p(\mathcal D \mid \mathbf w) &= \prod_{n} \mathcal N\big(t_n \mid y_n, \beta^{-1}\big) \\ y_n &= y(\mathbf x_n, \mathbf w) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the posterior is prior times likelihood, rescaled to sum to one. The prior is a round Gaussian of precision α (large α means small weights). The likelihood treats each target tn as the network’s output plus Gaussian noise of precision β. Because y(x, w) bends through tanh units, the product is not Gaussian in w.
Say it“The posterior of w given the data is the likelihood times the prior over the evidence; the prior is normal with precision alpha, the likelihood a product of normals centred on the network’s outputs.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(t \mid \mathbf x, \mathcal D) &= \int p(t \mid \mathbf x, \mathbf w)\, p(\mathbf w \mid \mathcal D)\, d\mathbf w \\ &\approx \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} \mathcal N\big(t \mid y(\mathbf x, \mathbf w^{(s)}), \beta^{-1}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: to predict, don’t pick one network: ask every network and weight its answer by its posterior probability. With S weight vectors w(s) sampled from the posterior, the integral becomes an average. The mean of the S outputs is the prediction; their spread, plus the noise 1/β, is its uncertainty.
Say it“The predictive distribution is the integral of the likelihood times the posterior, approximately the average over S posterior samples.”

How are the posterior draws made? By Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC), Radford Neal’s tool of choice for networks. Picture the weights as a ball rolling on a landscape whose height is the negative log posterior U(w). Give the ball a random kick, let it roll for a while by simulating Newton’s laws, and record where it stops. Valleys are visited often, ridges rarely, in exact proportion to the posterior. A final accept-or-reject test corrects the small errors of the simulation.

\[ \begin{aligned} H(\mathbf w, \mathbf p) &= U(\mathbf w) + \tfrac12 \mathbf p^{\top}\mathbf p \\ U(\mathbf w) &= \tfrac{\beta}{2}\textstyle\sum_n \big(y(\mathbf x_n,\mathbf w)-t_n\big)^2 \\ &\quad + \tfrac{\alpha}{2}\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf w \\ &= -\ln p(\mathbf w \mid \mathcal D) + \text{const} \\ P(\text{accept}) &= \min\!\big(1,\ e^{-\Delta H}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: p is a made-up momentum, one per weight, drawn fresh from a standard Gaussian at every step. The total energy H is potential (badness of fit plus weight penalty) plus kinetic. The leapfrog simulation almost conserves H; if it drifted by ΔH, accept the new weights with probability e−ΔH. Gradients come from backpropagation, so each step costs a few forward and backward passes.
Say it“H of w and p is U of w plus half p transpose p, where U is beta over two times the squared errors plus alpha over two times w squared; accept with probability the minimum of one and e to the minus delta H.”
Looking at the posterior

The shape of a weight posterior

A 25-weight posterior can’t be drawn, so shrink the network until it can. Take one hidden unit and no biases: y = v·tanh(u·x), just two weights. Now the whole posterior fits on a plane and we can compute it exactly on a grid. Two things jump out. First, it is not Gaussian: with few points it is a bent ridge, because once u is large, tanh saturates and making u larger changes little. Second, there are two identical peaks: flipping the sign of both u and v gives exactly the same function, since tanh is odd. A network with M hidden units has 2M·M! such copies of every mode (flip any unit’s signs, shuffle the units).

The Laplace approximation fits a Gaussian at one peak: same centre, same curvature. Slide the number of points and watch the exact posterior become more Gaussian, and the Laplace ellipses hug it more tightly. Hover the weight plane to see the function each (u, v) stands for.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln p(\mathbf w \mid \mathcal D) ={}& -\frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf w \\ &- \frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big(y(\mathbf x_n, \mathbf w) - t_n\big)^2 + \text{const} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the log posterior is minus the regularised error. Its peak wMAP is exactly what ordinary training with weight decay finds; the Bayesian story adds what happens around the peak.
Say it“The log posterior is minus alpha over two w squared minus beta over two times the sum of squared errors, plus a constant.”
\[ \begin{aligned} q(\mathbf w \mid \mathcal D) &= \mathcal N\big(\mathbf w \mid \mathbf w_{\text{MAP}},\, A^{-1}\big) \\ A &= -\nabla\nabla \ln p(\mathbf w \mid \mathcal D)\big|_{\mathbf w_{\text{MAP}}} = \alpha I + \beta \mathbf H \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: A is the curvature of the negative log posterior at the peak: the prior contributes αI, the data contribute β times H, the Hessian of the sum-of-squares error. A big curvature in some direction means a narrow, well-pinned posterior there. H can be the exact Hessian, or the outer-product approximation Σ gngn⊤ (gn = ∇wy(xn)), which is always positive and drops the terms weighted by the residuals.
Say it“q of w is a Gaussian centred at w-MAP with covariance A inverse, where A is alpha times the identity plus beta times the Hessian.”
exact ln p(D), from the grid
Laplace, both peaks

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln p(\mathcal D) \approx{}& \ln p(\mathcal D \mid \mathbf w_{\text{MAP}}) + \ln p(\mathbf w_{\text{MAP}}) \\ &+ \frac{W}{2}\ln 2\pi - \frac12 \ln |A| \;+\; \ln\!\big(2^{M} M!\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the Laplace estimate of the evidence: the height of the peak, times the volume of its Gaussian, (2π)W/2|A|−1/2. Since there are 2MM! equal peaks, the total mass is that many times one peak’s. Here M = 1 and W = 2, so the correction is ln 2. It only matters when you compare networks of different sizes; within one network it is a constant.
Say it“Log evidence is about the log likelihood plus the log prior at the peak, plus W over two log two pi, minus half log det A, plus log of two to the M times M factorial.”
The Laplace predictive

Error bars from one trained network

Sampling is accurate but slow. Bishop’s shortcut needs just one ordinary training run. Find wMAP, build A, and then make one more approximation: near wMAP, treat the network’s output as a linear function of the weights. A Gaussian pushed through a linear function stays Gaussian, so the predictive distribution comes out in closed form, exactly as for Bayesian linear regression, with the gradient g(x) playing the part of the basis functions.

Drag the points and compare the three ways of turning the posterior into a band: the linearised formula, networks drawn from q(w) and run through the real tanh units, and the HMC sampler from the first section.

\[ \begin{aligned} y(\mathbf x, \mathbf w) &\approx y(\mathbf x, \mathbf w_{\text{MAP}}) + \mathbf g^{\top}(\mathbf w - \mathbf w_{\text{MAP}}) \\ \mathbf g &= \nabla_{\mathbf w}\, y(\mathbf x, \mathbf w)\big|_{\mathbf w_{\text{MAP}}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a first-order Taylor expansion in the weights (not in x). g tells how much the output at this x moves when each weight is nudged; backpropagation computes it, one entry per weight.
Say it“y of x and w is about y at w-MAP plus g transpose times w minus w-MAP, where g is the gradient of y with respect to the weights.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(t \mid \mathbf x, \mathcal D) &\approx \mathcal N\big(t \mid y(\mathbf x, \mathbf w_{\text{MAP}}),\ \sigma^2(\mathbf x)\big) \\ \sigma^2(\mathbf x) &= \underbrace{\beta^{-1}}_{\text{noise}} + \underbrace{\mathbf g^{\top} A^{-1} \mathbf g}_{\text{unsure weights}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the prediction is the trained network’s output. The variance has two parts: noise that no amount of data removes, and weight uncertainty, which is large where the output is sensitive to directions in weight space that the data left loose. Away from the data, g points into exactly those loose directions.
Say it“The predictive is normal with mean y at w-MAP and variance one over beta plus g transpose A inverse g.”

Hyperparameters

Tuning α and β by the evidence

So far α and β were dials. David MacKay’s evidence framework sets them from the training data alone, with no validation set: choose the pair that makes the observed data most probable, after integrating out the weights. With the Laplace approximation that integral has a formula, and maximising it gives two re-estimation rules that look just like those for Bayesian linear regression (chapter 1.2e). The catch: A, and so the rules, depend on wMAP, which depends on α and β. So we alternate: train, re-estimate, train again.

Press “One evidence step” a few times and watch α and β settle, the band adjust and the count of well-determined parameters, γ, emerge.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln p(\mathcal D \mid \alpha, \beta) \approx{}& -E(\mathbf w_{\text{MAP}}) - \tfrac12 \ln |A| + \tfrac{W}{2}\ln\alpha \\ &+ \tfrac{N}{2}\ln\beta - \tfrac{N}{2}\ln 2\pi \\ E(\mathbf w) ={}& \tfrac{\beta}{2}\textstyle\sum_n (y_n - t_n)^2 + \tfrac{\alpha}{2}\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf w \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: fit, minus an Occam penalty. −E rewards a good fit with small weights; −½ln|A| charges for every direction in which the data pinned the weights much more tightly than the prior did; the α and β terms are the normalising constants of the prior and the noise.
Say it“Log evidence is about minus E at w-MAP minus half log det A plus W over two log alpha plus N over two log beta minus N over two log two pi.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \gamma &= \sum_{i=1}^{W} \frac{\lambda_i}{\alpha + \lambda_i} \\ \alpha &\leftarrow \frac{\gamma}{\mathbf w_{\text{MAP}}^{\top}\mathbf w_{\text{MAP}}} \\ \frac{1}{\beta} &\leftarrow \frac{1}{N-\gamma}\sum_{n}\big(y_n - t_n\big)^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: λi are the eigenvalues of βH, the data’s curvature. A direction with λi >> α is set by the data and counts as one parameter; one with λi << α is set by the prior and counts as zero. γ is the effective number of parameters. The new α is γ divided by the weights’ squared size; the noise variance divides the squared errors by N − γ, not N, because γ parameters were spent fitting. At the fixed point αw⊤w = γ and βΣ(y − t)2 = N − γ, so E(wMAP) = N/2 exactly.
Say it“Gamma is the sum of lambda-i over alpha plus lambda-i; alpha becomes gamma over w squared, and one over beta becomes the squared error over N minus gamma.”

Two classes

Bayesian networks for classification

For two classes the network ends in a logistic sigmoid, y = σ(a), and the noise model is a coin flip instead of a Gaussian. Two things change. There is no β to tune, only α. And the predictive integral now runs through a sigmoid, so even with a Gaussian over weights there’s no exact formula. Bishop linearises the activation a (not the probability), which makes a Gaussian, then uses chapter 1.3b’s probit trick to average the sigmoid over it. The result: probabilities are pulled towards ½ wherever the network is unsure of a, which is to say away from the data.

Drag the points, then set α by the evidence. Hover anywhere to read the activation, its variance and the two probabilities.

\[ \begin{aligned} E(\mathbf w) ={}& -\sum_{n} t_n \ln y_n \\ &- \sum_n (1-t_n)\ln(1-y_n) \\ &+ \tfrac{\alpha}{2}\mathbf w^{\top}\mathbf w \\ \ln p(\mathcal D \mid \alpha) \approx{}& -E(\mathbf w_{\text{MAP}}) - \tfrac12\ln|A| + \tfrac{W}{2}\ln\alpha \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: train by minimising cross-entropy plus weight decay. A = H + αI as before, with H now the Hessian of the cross-entropy (here its outer-product form Σ yn(1−yn) bnbn⊤). The evidence has no β terms, and α is re-estimated by the same γ/w⊤w rule.
Say it“E is the cross-entropy plus alpha over two w squared; log evidence is about minus E at the peak minus half log det A plus W over two log alpha.”
\[ \begin{aligned} a(\mathbf x, \mathbf w) &\approx a_{\text{MAP}}(\mathbf x) \\ &\quad + \mathbf b^{\top}(\mathbf w - \mathbf w_{\text{MAP}}) \\ \sigma_a^2 &= \mathbf b^{\top}A^{-1}\mathbf b \\ p(t{=}1 \mid \mathbf x, \mathcal D) &\approx \sigma\big(\kappa(\sigma_a^2)\, a_{\text{MAP}}\big) \\ \kappa(\sigma^2) &= \big(1 + \pi\sigma^2/8\big)^{-1/2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: b is the gradient of the activation with respect to the weights. The activation is approximately Gaussian with variance σa2; averaging a sigmoid over that Gaussian is well approximated by shrinking the activation by κ ≤ 1. Since κ never flips the sign of a, the 0.5 boundary does not move; only the confidence around it does.
Say it“The probability of class one is about sigma of kappa times a-MAP, where kappa is one over the square root of one plus pi sigma-a squared over eight.”

Infinite width

Infinitely wide: a network becomes a Gaussian process

Radford Neal noticed something remarkable in the mid-1990s. Take a one-hidden-layer network, put independent priors on its weights, and scale the output weights’ prior by 1/√H. The output at any input is then a sum of H independent, identically distributed terms, one per hidden unit. As H grows, the central limit theorem makes that sum Gaussian, and jointly Gaussian across inputs: the prior over functions becomes a Gaussian process. Its kernel is the average product of a hidden unit’s outputs at two inputs. Christopher Williams worked out that average in closed form for erf units.

Slide the width H from one unit to over a thousand. Watch the sampled functions stop looking like a few steps, the histogram of outputs at x = 1 turn Gaussian, and the sample covariance settle onto Williams’ kernel.

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x) &= \sum_{j=1}^{H} v_j\, h(x; \mathbf u_j) \\ v_j &\sim \mathcal N\big(0, \sigma_v^2/H\big) \\ K(x, x') &= \operatorname{cov}\big(f(x), f(x')\big) \\ &= \sigma_v^2\, \mathbb E_{\mathbf u}\big[h(x;\mathbf u)\,h(x';\mathbf u)\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each hidden unit h has random input weights uj; its output weight vj has variance shrinking like 1/H, so the total variance stays fixed however wide the layer. The covariance of f does not depend on H at all; only the shape of the distribution does, and it tends to Gaussian.
Say it“f of x is the sum of v-j times h of x, with v-j normal with variance sigma-v squared over H; the covariance of f at x and x-prime is sigma-v squared times the expected product of the hidden outputs.”
\[ \begin{aligned} K(x,x') &= \sigma_v^2\,\frac{2}{\pi}\,\arcsin c \\ c &= \frac{2\,\tilde{\mathbf x}^{\top}\Sigma\,\tilde{\mathbf x}'}{\sqrt{(1+2\tilde{\mathbf x}^{\top}\Sigma\tilde{\mathbf x})(1+2\tilde{\mathbf x}'^{\top}\Sigma\tilde{\mathbf x}')}} \\ \tilde{\mathbf x} &= (1, x), \quad \Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_0^2, \sigma_u^2) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Williams’ kernel for h = erf(u0 + u1x), with bias and input weights drawn with variances σ02 and σu2. Unlike the squared-exponential kernel it is not stationary: it depends on where x and x′ are, not just how far apart. Larger σu means steeper units and rougher functions.
Say it“K is sigma-v squared times two over pi times the arcsine of two x-tilde Sigma x-tilde-prime over the square root of the product of one plus two x-tilde Sigma x-tilde terms.”

Two things are lost in the limit. Hidden units become a crowd in which no single unit matters, so the network can no longer learn features that are shared between tasks; and with several outputs, each becomes an independent GP, so the outputs stop informing one another. Infinite width is a beautiful special case, not the reason networks work. It returned in 2018 for deep networks as the “NNGP” and the neural tangent kernel.

Averaging

Bayes, bagging and boosting

The Elements of Statistical Learning puts Bayesian networks next to two other ways of combining many models. All three predict by averaging, but they produce their members differently. Bayes draws weights from the posterior: the data stay fixed and the weights are random. Bagging refits the same model to bootstrap resamples of the data and weights every fit equally: the data are perturbed, and each fit is a point estimate. Boosting builds members one after another, each fitted to what the earlier ones got wrong, and adds them up with a small step size: it is not an average of rival explanations at all, but a growing sum.

The link between the first two is closer than it looks: ESL §8.4 shows that the bootstrap distribution of a fit approximates its posterior under a non-informative prior, so bagging is a kind of poor man’s Bayes. Pick a method and slide the number of members; the dashed line is the true curve the data came from.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat f_{\text{Bayes}}(x) &= \int f(x,\mathbf w)\, p(\mathbf w\mid\mathcal D)\, d\mathbf w \\ &\approx \frac1S\sum_{s=1}^{S} f\big(x, \mathbf w^{(s)}\big) \\ \hat f_{\text{bag}}(x) &= \frac1B \sum_{b=1}^{B} f\big(x, \hat{\mathbf w}^{*b}\big) \\ \hat f_{\text{boost}}(x) &= \sum_{m=1}^{M} \nu\, f_m(x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: w(s) are posterior draws; ŵ*b is the best fit to the b-th bootstrap sample; fm is the m-th small network, fitted to the residuals t − Σk<m νfk(x), and ν ≤ 1 is the shrinkage. The first two are averages, so adding members reduces noise; the third is a sum, so adding members adds complexity.
Say it“Bayes averages f over posterior samples; bagging averages the fits to B bootstrap samples; boosting sums nu times the M stagewise fits.”

A famous win

The NIPS 2003 challenge, and why the Bayes nets won

In 2003 Isabelle Guyon and colleagues ran a feature-selection challenge at the NIPS conference. Five two-class problems had many more features than examples, and to test whether entrants could tell signal from noise, the organisers mixed in meaningless probe features. Entries were scored by balanced error rate on held-out test sets.

The winning entry, by Radford Neal and Jianguo Zhang, used Bayesian neural networks (and Dirichlet diffusion trees) fitted by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. Two ingredients did the feature selection: first a cheap univariate screen, then automatic relevance determination (ARD), which gives every input its own prior precision αc and lets the evidence, or the posterior, decide how large that input’s weights may be. Irrelevant inputs end up with huge αc, and their weights are squeezed to zero.

ESL §11.9.2 then reruns the contest’s idea under controlled conditions: Bayesian networks against boosted and bagged networks, boosted trees and random forests, all given the same reduced feature sets, to pull apart the three ingredients of the winning entry: a flexible model, averaging over many fits, and feature selection. The project below asks the same question of averaging on new data. Here, first, is ARD on a small copy of the challenge’s recipe: two real inputs plus probe inputs of pure noise. Step the evidence and watch the probes get switched off.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf w \mid \boldsymbol\alpha) &= \prod_{c} \mathcal N\big(\mathbf w_c \mid \mathbf 0, \alpha_c^{-1} I\big) \\ \gamma_c &= \sum_{i \in c}\big(1 - \alpha_c\,(A^{-1})_{ii}\big) \\ \alpha_c &\leftarrow \frac{\gamma_c}{\Vert \mathbf w_c \Vert^2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: group c holds all the weights leaving input c (one group per input, plus one for the biases and one for the output layer). γc counts how many of that group’s weights the data actually determined: (A−1)ii is a weight’s posterior variance and 1/αc its prior variance, so each term is the fraction of prior uncertainty the data removed. Same rule as before, one group at a time. 1/√αc, the typical size of input c’s weights, is its relevance.
Say it“The prior is a product of normals, one precision alpha-c per group; gamma-c is the sum over the group of one minus alpha-c times the diagonal of A inverse, and alpha-c becomes gamma-c over the group’s squared weights.”

Real-world project

Project: concrete strength beyond 56 days

Concrete gains strength for months after it is poured, but labs mostly test it at 28 days. I-Cheng Yeh’s data set records the compressive strength of 1,030 lab specimens: seven ingredients (kg per m³: cement, blast-furnace slag, fly ash, water, superplasticiser, coarse and fine aggregate), the age when it was crushed, and the strength in megapascals. We give the networks only specimens aged 56 days or less: 640 to learn from and 200 for an in-range test. The other 190, aged 90 to 365 days, are a test of extrapolation: older than anything the network has seen. Would its error bars admit that?

Each model is an 8-6-1 tanh network (61 weights) on standardised inputs, with log age as an input and log strength as the target, because the scatter grows with strength. We train five networks from different random starts, each tuned by MacKay’s evidence loop (12 rounds), and a bagged ensemble of ten bootstrap fits at the best network’s α and β. Everything below is trained in your browser. Press Train if it hasn’t started.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln s &= y(\mathbf x, \mathbf w) + \varepsilon \\ \text{90\% interval} &= \exp\!\big(m \pm 1.645\,\sigma\big) \\ \bar m &= \frac1K\sum_k m_k \\ \bar\sigma^2 &= \frac1K\sum_k \big(\sigma_k^2 + m_k^2\big) - \bar m^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the network predicts log strength s; a Gaussian interval in log space becomes a lopsided interval in MPa after exponentiating. To combine K networks, each with its own Laplace mean mk and variance σk2, match the mixture’s mean and variance with one Gaussian: the variance is the average variance plus the disagreement between the means. For bagging, σk2 = 1/β for every member.
Say it“Log strength is the network plus noise; the interval is e to the m plus or minus 1.645 sigma; mixing K nets, the mean is the average mean and the variance is the average of sigma-k squared plus m-k squared, minus the mean squared.”

Who covers the truth?

A 90% interval should contain the true strength for about 90% of mixes; much less means overconfident, much more means needlessly vague. Root-mean-square error (RMSE) measures the point predictions. The two tree ensembles are scikit-learn results for comparison (they give no intervals) and are not rerun in the page.

    s = [seed]
    def r():
        s[0] = (s[0] + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF; a = s[0]

X = R[:, :8].copy(); X[:, 7] = np.log(age); y = np.log(R[:, 8])        # inputs with log(age); target log(MPa)
p, r = list(np.where(age <= 56)[0]), rng(2055)                         # shuffle the 840 mixes aged 1 to 56 days

tr, te = np.array(p[:640]), np.array(p[640:])                          # 640 to learn from, 200 in-range test

mu, sd, my, sy = X[tr].mean(0), X[tr].std(0), y[tr].mean(), y[tr].std()
Z, T = (X - mu) / sd, (y - my) / sy
D, H = 8, 6; W = H * (D + 1) + H + 1                                   # 8-6-1 tanh network, 61 weights

def net(w, Xs):                                                        # outputs and Jacobian dy/dw (N x W)
    A = w[:H * (D + 1)].reshape(H, D + 1); v, c = w[H * (D + 1):-1], w[-1]
    h = np.tanh(Xs @ A[:, :D].T + A[:, D]); g = (1 - h * h) * v
    J = np.hstack([np.concatenate([g[:, [j]] * Xs, g[:, [j]]], 1) for j in range(H)] + [h, np.ones((len(Xs), 1))])
    return h @ v + c, J

def E(w, Xs, t, a, b): r = net(w, Xs)[0] - t; return 0.5 * b * r @ r + 0.5 * a * w @ w

def lm(w, Xs, t, a, b, mu=1e-2):                                       # Levenberg-Marquardt to the MAP weights
    e = E(w, Xs, t, a, b)
    for it in range(80):                                               # at most 80 steps per call
        f, J = net(w, Xs); grad = b * J.T @ (f - t) + a * w; G = b * J.T @ J + a * np.eye(W)
        while True:
            w2 = w + np.linalg.solve(G + mu * np.eye(W), -grad); e2 = E(w2, Xs, t, a, b)
            if e2 < e: mu = max(mu / 3, 1e-9); break
            mu *= 4
            if mu > 1e12: return w
        done = e - e2 < 1e-10 * max(1, e2); w, e = w2, e2
        if done: break
    return w

def evidence(w, a, b, Xs, t):                                          # PRML 5.7.2 with the Gauss-Newton Hessian
    f, J = net(w, Xs); N = len(t); A = b * J.T @ J + a * np.eye(W)
    gamma = W - a * np.trace(np.linalg.inv(A))
    logev = -E(w, Xs, t, a, b) - 0.5 * np.linalg.slogdet(A)[1] + 0.5 * W * np.log(a) + 0.5 * N * np.log(b) - 0.5 * N * np.log(2 * np.pi)
    return A, gamma, logev, ((f - t) ** 2).sum()

def init(seed):                                                        # Box-Muller start, identical to the page's
    r, w = rng(seed), np.zeros(W)
    def gs():
        u = 0
        while u == 0: u = r()
        return np.sqrt(-2 * np.log(u)) * np.cos(2 * np.pi * r())
    for i in range(H * (D + 1)): w[i] = gs() / np.sqrt(D + 1)
    for j in range(H): w[H * (D + 1) + j] = gs() / np.sqrt(H)
    return w

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A Bayesian network keeps a posterior over its weights and predicts by averaging over it; the spread of that average is an error bar that grows where data are missing.
  • The weight posterior is non-Gaussian and has 2MM! copies of every mode. The Laplace approximation fits a Gaussian at one: centre wMAP, precision A = αI + βH.
  • Linearise the output in the weights and the predictive is Gaussian: variance 1/β + g⊤A−1g. For classification, linearise the activation and shrink it by κ(σa2).
  • The evidence sets α and β from training data alone: α = γ/w⊤w and 1/β = Σ(y − t)2/(N − γ), where γ counts well-determined parameters; per-input αs give ARD.
  • As width goes to infinity, the prior over functions becomes a Gaussian process. In practice, averaging over several modes (HMC, mixed Laplace nets, bagging) is what keeps error bars honest out of range: on concrete, 90% intervals covered 96% of never-seen older mixes, against 80% for one Laplace net.

Check your understanding

Why isn’t a network’s weight posterior Gaussian, even with a Gaussian prior and Gaussian noise?

Because the outputs depend on the weights non-linearly (through tanh units). The log likelihood is then not a quadratic in w, so prior times likelihood is not Gaussian; it can be skewed, ridged and multimodal.

A network has 3 hidden units. How many equivalent copies does each posterior mode have, and when do they matter?

23 × 3! = 48. They give identical predictions, so they don’t affect a single network’s predictive; they only matter when comparing the evidence of networks with different numbers of hidden units.

Where does g⊤A−1g come from, and why does it grow away from the data?

It is the variance of the linearised output g⊤w when w ~ N(wMAP, A−1). Away from the data the output is sensitive to weight directions the data never constrained, which have small curvature in A and so large variance in A−1.

The evidence loop reports γ = 7.6 for a network with 25 weights. What does that mean?

About 7.6 directions in weight space are pinned down by the data (curvature λi much larger than α); the remaining directions are left to the prior. The noise estimate divides the squared errors by N − 7.6 to account for them.

Moderation by κ never changes which class is more likely. So what is it for?

For decisions that depend on the probability itself: costs, rejecting uncertain cases, combining with other evidence. Far from the data the MAP network can say 0.99 while the moderated output says 0.7; only the second is an honest basis for acting.
