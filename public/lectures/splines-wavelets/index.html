Basis Expansions: Splines & Wavelets

Flexible curves built from simple pieces

A straight line is easy to fit but rarely true. The trick in this chapter is to keep the easy part, a model that is linear in its coefficients, while feeding it new inputs: transformed copies h1(x), h2(x), … of the original. Choose them as little polynomial pieces joined at knots and you get splines; choose them as tiny waves at every scale and you get wavelets. On the right is one real heartbeat from the MIT-BIH database, with noise added, and a cubic spline built from the coloured bumps underneath it. Drag the knots (the handles along the top) towards the tall spike and watch the curve catch it.

The idea

One recipe, many bases

Linear regression fits f(x) = β0 + β1x. A linear basis expansion keeps the fitting machinery and changes the inputs: pick M functions h1, …, hM in advance, compute them for every training point, and fit a linear model in the new columns. The model is still linear in β, so least squares, standard errors and everything else from chapter 1.2 carry over unchanged. Only the shape of f changes: it is whatever weighted sum of the hm fits best.

The data below are 100 noisy points from a curve that wiggles fast on the left and slowly on the right (dashed). Switch between bases and slide M. Watch the strip underneath: it shows the basis functions themselves, before they are weighted. Global polynomials use functions that live everywhere; the others use local pieces.

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x) &= \sum_{m=1}^{M} \beta_m\, h_m(x) \\ \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} &= (\mathbf H^{\top}\mathbf H)^{-1}\mathbf H^{\top}\mathbf y \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the hm are fixed functions of x chosen before seeing y. H is the table with one row per training point and one column per basis function, Him = hm(xi). Fitting is ordinary least squares on that table. Once the basis is chosen, nothing about the fit is new; all the modelling lives in the choice of the hm.
Say it“f of x is the sum over m of beta-m times h-m of x; beta-hat is H-transpose-H inverse, H-transpose y.”

Every basis family raises the same question: how many functions, and which? There are three broad answers, and this chapter and the next use all of them. Restriction fixes the class in advance (additive models, a set number of knots). Selection starts from a large set and keeps only the functions that earn their place, as in the next section. Regularisation keeps every function but shrinks the coefficients, as ridge regression did and as smoothing splines will in chapter 1.4b.

Selection

Dictionaries and greedy selection

Instead of committing to a handful of functions, collect a large dictionary D of candidates, far more than you could ever fit at once, and let the data choose. Two classic dictionaries are radial basis functions, bumps Kλ(ξ, x) centred at many places ξ with many widths λ, and the hidden units of a neural network, where every unit is a squashed linear function of x. In both, the centres, widths or slopes can themselves be learned, which makes the basis adaptive, and the problem no longer linear.

A simple way to choose from a fixed dictionary is forward stepwise selection: start with the constant and, at each step, add the candidate that cuts the residual sum of squares the most. Here the dictionary has a hinge pointing each way at every one of the 100 data points (200 functions, the building blocks of MARS in chapter 1.7a), or Gaussian bumps of three widths at every point (300 functions). Press Add one repeatedly and watch the two error curves part company.

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x) &= \sum_{m=1}^{M} \beta_m\, K_{\lambda_m}(\xi_m, x) \\ f(x) &= \sum_{m=1}^{M} \beta_m\, \sigma(\alpha_m x + b_m) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: top, a radial basis function model: M bumps with centres ξm and widths λm. Bottom, a one-hidden-layer neural network: M sigmoids with slopes αm and offsets bm. Both are basis expansions whose basis functions have their own parameters. Fix those, and the model is linear in β; learn them, and you have an adaptive basis.
Say it“f is a sum of M bumps, each with its own centre and width; or a sum of M sigmoids, each with its own slope and offset.”
\[ h^{*} = \arg\max_{h \in \mathcal D}\ \frac{(\mathbf r^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf h})^{2}}{\lVert\tilde{\mathbf h}\rVert^{2}} \]
Read it as: r is the current residual vector. h̃ is the candidate column with everything already in the model projected out, so only its new direction counts. The fraction is exactly how much the residual sum of squares would fall if h joined the model. Greedy selection adds the winner and repeats.
Say it“Pick the h in the dictionary with the largest squared inner product between the residual and h-tilde, over the squared length of h-tilde.”

Local pieces

Piecewise polynomials and splines

Cut the x-axis at a few knots ξ1 < ξ2 < … and fit a separate polynomial in each piece. With constants you get a staircase; with lines, a zig-zag that may jump at each knot. Now add constraints: ask the pieces to meet (continuity), then to meet with the same slope, then with the same curvature. Each constraint removes one free parameter per knot, and the curve becomes smoother. A piecewise cubic whose value, slope and curvature are all continuous is a cubic spline; it is the smoothest you can go without collapsing the pieces into a single cubic.

Step through the seven options and drag the two knots. The lower plot shows a derivative of the fit, so you can see exactly where it jumps: the cubic spline’s third derivative is a step function, and the size of each step is fixed by one coefficient.

\[ \begin{aligned} h_j(x) &= x^{\,j-1} \\ h_{M+\ell}(x) &= (x-\xi_\ell)_+^{M-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the truncated power basis for a spline of order M (degree M − 1) with K knots, j = 1, …, M and ℓ = 1, …, K: a global polynomial plus, at every knot, a term that is zero to the left and rises like (x − ξ)M−1 to the right. (z)+ means max(z, 0). Order 4 gives cubic splines: 4 + K functions.
Say it“h-j is x to the j minus one for the first M; then one more per knot, x minus xi-ell, positive part, to the power M minus one.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p &= (d+1)(K+1) \\ &\quad - K\,(c+1) \\ \Delta f'''(\xi_\ell) &= 6\,\beta_{4+\ell} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: p is the number of free parameters. K knots make K + 1 pieces, each a polynomial of degree d with d + 1 coefficients. Asking the value and the first c derivatives to match at a knot costs c + 1 parameters per knot (c = −1 means no constraint). For a cubic spline (d = 3, c = 2) that leaves 4 + K. Second line: the only thing allowed to jump at a cubic spline’s knot is the third derivative; its jump Δf‴(ξℓ), right limit minus left, is 6 times that knot’s coefficient.
Say it“p equals d plus one times K plus one, minus K times c plus one. The jump in f-triple-prime at knot ell is six beta four-plus-ell.”

Behaving at the edges

Natural cubic splines

Polynomials are badly behaved near the ends of the data, where there are few points to hold them down, and beyond the data they shoot off. Cubic splines inherit some of this: their first and last pieces are free cubics. A natural cubic spline adds one more rule: beyond the two boundary knots the function must be linear. That removes two parameters at each end (the quadratic and cubic terms there), so K knots now cost only K parameters, and the freed budget can buy more interior knots.

The variance of a fitted value is easy to compute for any basis (formula on the right), so we can see the edge problem directly. Fifty x-values are scattered uniformly on [0, 1], and four models are compared, as in Figure 5.3 of The Elements of Statistical Learning. Switch to the extrapolation view to see what happens outside the data, and draw new x’s to check that the pattern is not luck.

\[ \begin{aligned} N_1(x) &= 1, \quad N_2(x) = x \\ N_{k+2}(x) &= d_k(x) - d_{K-1}(x) \\ d_k(x) &= \frac{(x-\xi_k)_+^3 - (x-\xi_K)_+^3}{\xi_K - \xi_k} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a basis of exactly K functions for natural cubic splines with knots ξ1, …, ξK. Each dk is a difference of two truncated cubics scaled so that, past the last knot, the cubic and quadratic parts cancel; subtracting dK−1 makes each N linear beyond ξK, and every N is plainly linear before ξ1.
Say it“N-one is one, N-two is x, N k-plus-two is d-k minus d K-minus-one, where d-k is the difference of truncated cubics over xi-K minus xi-k.”
\[ \operatorname{Var}\hat f(x) = \sigma^{2}\, \mathbf h(x)^{\top}(\mathbf H^{\top}\mathbf H)^{-1}\mathbf h(x) \]
Read it as: h(x) is the row of basis values at x. The fitted value is h(x)⊤β̂, and β̂ has covariance σ2(H⊤H)−1, so this is just the variance of a linear combination. It depends on where the x’s are and on the basis, not on y. We take σ2 = 1.
Say it“The variance of f-hat at x is sigma squared times h of x transpose, H-transpose-H inverse, h of x.”

Example: heart disease

Splines inside a logistic model

Basis expansions are not only for regression. Put natural-spline columns into a logistic regression and every risk factor gets its own curved effect on the log-odds, while the model stays additive: the effects simply add up. The data are 462 men from a high-risk region of the Western Cape, South Africa (Rousseauw et al., 1983), 160 of whom had coronary heart disease. Following ESL, each continuous risk factor gets a natural cubic spline with 4 degrees of freedom (knots at its minimum, quartiles and maximum); family history is a single 0/1 column.

Terms are removed one at a time by AIC, which charges each parameter 2 units of deviance. Press Backward step until AIC stops falling, then pick a term to plot: the curve is its fitted contribution to the log-odds, with a band of ±2 standard errors. You can also toggle terms in and out yourself.

\[ \log\frac{p(x)}{1-p(x)} = \theta_0 + \sum_{j} \mathbf h_j(x_j)^{\top}\boldsymbol\theta_j \]
Read it as: p(x) is the probability of heart disease for a man with risk factors x. Each risk factor xj is expanded into its own few spline columns hj(xj) with coefficients θj. Stack all the columns side by side and this is an ordinary logistic regression, fitted by Newton’s method (iteratively reweighted least squares) as in chapter 1.3.
Say it“The log-odds of heart disease is theta-nought plus the sum over risk factors of h-j of x-j transpose theta-j.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf z &= \mathbf h_j(x) - \bar{\mathbf h}_j \\ \hat f_j(x) &= \mathbf z^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_j \\ \operatorname{Var}\hat f_j(x) &= \mathbf z^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}_{jj}\,\mathbf z \\ \text{AIC} &= \text{deviance} + 2p \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the plotted curve for term j is its part of the log-odds, centred so it averages zero over the men: z is the basis row minus the average row h̄j. Σ̂jj is that term’s block of the inverse Hessian, the estimated covariance of θ̂j. Deviance is −2 × log-likelihood and p the number of parameters.
Say it“z is the centred basis row; f-hat-j is z transpose theta-hat-j, and its variance is z transpose Sigma-hat-j-j z. AIC is deviance plus two p.”

Example: speech

Filtering: smooth coefficients from a basis

Sometimes the inputs are curves. Each example here is a spoken vowel turned into a log-periodogram: its loudness at 256 frequencies. The task is to tell “aa” (the vowel of bott) from “ao” (the vowel of bought), using 1,278 sounds from TIMIT’s training speakers and testing on 439 sounds from its test speakers. A plain logistic regression gives each of the 256 frequencies its own coefficient. Neighbouring frequencies carry nearly the same information, so those coefficients come out wildly jagged, and they overfit.

The fix is a basis expansion of the coefficient curve: require β(f) to be a natural cubic spline in frequency with M degrees of freedom. Algebraically that is the same as filtering each input first, x* = H⊤x, and fitting an ordinary logistic regression on the M filtered features. Any basis can do the filtering: splines here, wavelets or Fourier components elsewhere. Slide M and compare the jagged and smooth coefficient curves and their test errors.

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta(f) &= \sum_{m=1}^{M} h_m(f)\,\theta_m, \quad \boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf H\boldsymbol\theta \\ \mathbf x^{\top}\boldsymbol\beta &= \mathbf x^{\top}\mathbf H\boldsymbol\theta = (\mathbf x^{*})^{\top}\boldsymbol\theta \\ \mathbf x^{*} &= \mathbf H^{\top}\mathbf x \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: H is the 256 × M table of spline basis functions evaluated at the 256 frequencies. Constraining the coefficients to be smooth, β = Hθ, leaves only M numbers θ to learn. Moving H across the inner product shows the same model as a regression on the filtered features x* = H⊤x: M weighted sums of the loudness curve.
Say it“Beta of f is a sum of M spline functions with weights theta, so x-transpose-beta equals x-star transpose theta, where x-star is H-transpose x.”

Computation

B-splines: the same space, built better

The truncated power basis is easy to write down but bad to compute with. Its functions (x − ξ)3+ for nearby knots are almost identical over most of the range, so the columns of H are nearly collinear and H⊤H is close to singular. Rounding errors then swamp the answer. B-splines span exactly the same space of splines but use functions that are each non-zero on only M neighbouring intervals. They come from a simple recursion (Cox and de Boor): start with box functions, and blend each pair of neighbours with linear weights to raise the order by one.

To make the recursion work at the ends, the boundary knots are repeated M times. Step the order from 1 to 4 and pick a basis function: the dashed curves are the two lower-order parents it is blended from. Then slide K to see how fast the two bases’ condition numbers part.

\[ \begin{aligned} B_{i,1}(x) &= \mathbb 1[\tau_i \le x < \tau_{i+1}] \\ B_{i,m} &= w_{i,m}B_{i,m-1} \\ &\quad + (1 - w_{i+1,m})B_{i+1,m-1} \\ w_{i,m}(x) &= \frac{x-\tau_i}{\tau_{i+m-1}-\tau_i} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: τ is the augmented knot sequence (boundary knots repeated M times, interior knots in between). Order 1: box functions, one per interval. Order m: each function is its left parent times a ramp w that rises from 0 to 1 across that parent’s support, plus its right parent times a ramp 1 − w that falls from 1 to 0. A term with a zero denominator is taken as zero.
Say it“B-i-one is the indicator of the i-th interval. B-i-m is w-i-m times B-i-m-minus-one, plus one minus w-i-plus-one-m times B-i-plus-one-m-minus-one, where w is x minus tau-i over the parent’s span.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\sum_i B_{i,M}(x) = 1 \\ &B_{i,M}(x) = 0 \ \text{ outside } [\tau_i, \tau_{i+M}] \\ &\kappa(\mathbf H^{\top}\mathbf H) = \lambda_{\max} / \lambda_{\min} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: B-splines are non-negative, sum to one everywhere (a partition of unity) and are local: each spans M intervals. So every row of H has at most M non-zeros, H⊤H is banded, and a fit with N points and K knots costs O(N) work. κ is the condition number: roughly how many times rounding errors can be magnified when solving with that matrix.
Say it“The B-splines sum to one and each is zero outside M knot intervals. Kappa is the largest eigenvalue over the smallest.”

A basis for every scale

Wavelet bases and the wavelet transform

Splines with fixed knots spread their flexibility evenly. Many signals do not: an ECG is flat for most of a beat and then spikes in 30 milliseconds. A wavelet basis contains functions at every location and every scale: broad ones for slow trends and narrow ones for sharp events. They are all shifted and squeezed copies of one father function φ (which averages) and one mother function ψ (which measures change), and they are orthonormal, so the coefficients of a signal are just inner products.

Haar wavelets are boxes and steps; Daubechies wavelets (D4, and the “least asymmetric” symmlet-8 that ESL uses) are smoother and have more vanishing moments: they ignore polynomials up to degree 1 and 7 respectively. Slide the resolution j to rebuild the signal from coarse to fine, and click any coefficient in the lower plot to see the basis function it multiplies.

\[ \begin{aligned} \phi_{j,k}(x) &= 2^{j/2}\,\phi(2^{j}x - k) \\ \psi_{j,k}(x) &= 2^{j/2}\,\psi(2^{j}x - k) \\ V_{j+1} &= V_j \oplus W_j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: j is the scale (larger j, narrower functions), k the position. Vj is everything you can build from father functions at scale j: for Haar, functions that are constant on 2j equal pieces. Going up one level adds the details Wj, the span of the mother functions ψj,k. So a signal is a coarse average plus details at every finer scale.
Say it“Phi-j-k is two to the j over two times phi of two to the j x minus k, likewise psi. V j-plus-one is V-j plus the detail space W-j.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf y^{*} &= \mathbf W^{\top}\mathbf y, \quad \mathbf W^{\top}\mathbf W = \mathbf I \\ a_k &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(y_{2k} + y_{2k+1}) \\ d_k &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(y_{2k} - y_{2k+1}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: W is the N × N matrix whose columns are the discrete wavelet basis functions. Because it is orthogonal, the transform is lossless and y = Wy*. It is computed in O(N) by a pyramid: filter into averages a and differences d, halve, and repeat on the averages. For Haar (the last two lines) the filters are just scaled sums and differences of neighbours.
Say it“y-star is W-transpose y. For Haar, a-k is the scaled sum of a pair, d-k the scaled difference.”

Adaptive filtering

Wavelet shrinkage: keep the big coefficients

The previous plot showed the key fact: for many real signals, a few wavelet coefficients hold almost all the energy. White noise does the opposite: an orthogonal transform turns it into white noise again, spread evenly over all N coefficients. So transform, shrink the small coefficients towards zero, and transform back. Big coefficients, which are probably signal, survive; small ones, probably noise, vanish. Because the decision is made coefficient by coefficient, the smoothing adapts by itself: heavy where the signal is flat, light where it jumps. That is adaptive wavelet filtering (Donoho and Johnstone, 1994).

The rule comes from a lasso in the wavelet domain, and because W is orthogonal it has a closed form: soft thresholding. Donoho and Johnstone’s test signals below have a signal-to-noise ratio of 7. Try each signal and rule, and compare with the best cubic regression spline with equally spaced knots, chosen with the truth in hand (an oracle no real analysis has).

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{\boldsymbol\theta}\ \lVert\mathbf y - \mathbf W\boldsymbol\theta\rVert^{2} + 2\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol\theta\rVert_{1} \\ &\Rightarrow\ \hat\theta_j = \operatorname{sign}(y^{*}_j)\,\big(|y^{*}_j| - \lambda\big)_+ \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: since W is orthogonal, ‖y − Wθ‖2 = ‖y* − θ‖2, so the problem splits into N one-dimensional lassos. Each answer moves y*j towards zero by λ and stops at zero. Hard thresholding instead keeps a coefficient untouched if |y*j| > λ and zeroes it otherwise. The coarsest averages are left alone.
Say it“Theta-hat-j is the sign of y-star-j times the positive part of its size minus lambda.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\sigma &= \frac{\operatorname{median}_k |d_{1,k}|}{0.6745} \\ \lambda_{\text{univ}} &= \hat\sigma\sqrt{2\log N} \\ \text{SURE}(t) &= n - 2\,\#\{k : |x_k| \le t\} \\ &\quad + \textstyle\sum_k \min(|x_k|, t)^{2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the universal threshold sits just above the largest value N pure-noise coefficients are likely to reach, so almost no noise survives. σ is estimated from the finest details d1,k, which are mostly noise; the median makes it robust to the few signal spikes there. SURE (Stein’s unbiased risk estimate) instead picks, level by level, the t that minimises an unbiased estimate of the squared error of soft thresholding, with xk = dk/σ̂.
Say it“Lambda-universal is sigma-hat root two log N, with sigma-hat the median absolute finest detail over 0.6745. SURE of t is n minus twice the count below t plus the sum of the squared clipped sizes.”

Real-world project

Project: cleaning a real heart trace

The MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database (Moody and Mark, 2001; PhysioNet, ODC-By licence) holds two-channel ambulatory ECGs sampled at 360 Hz, with every beat annotated by cardiologists. We take three 11.4-second strips (4,096 samples) of lead MLII from record 100, a clean recording, and add noise of two kinds: white Gaussian noise, the textbook assumption, and real muscle artefact from the MIT-BIH Noise Stress Test Database (Moody, Muldrow and Mark, 1984), with its slow drift removed. Knowing the clean signal lets us score every method exactly.

Two smoothers compete. A cubic regression spline with K equally spaced knots, K chosen by generalised cross-validation, which needs no clean copy. And wavelet shrinkage with six levels. Each result also goes through a simple R-peak detector, because what a clinician wants from a trace like this is the heart rate. Start with white noise at 0 dB, then switch to muscle noise.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{MSE} &= \tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_i(\hat s_i - s_i)^{2} \\ \text{SNR} &= 10\log_{10}\frac{\operatorname{var}(s)}{\text{MSE}}\ \text{dB} \\ \text{GCV}(K) &= \frac{N\cdot\text{RSS}(K)}{\big(N - (K+4)\big)^{2}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: s is the clean trace and ŝ any version of it (noisy or denoised); every 10 dB is a tenfold cut in squared error. GCV estimates leave-one-out error from the training fit alone: the residual sum of squares, inflated by how many of the N data points the K + 4 spline coefficients have used up. It assumes the noise is independent from sample to sample.
Say it“SNR is ten log-ten of the signal’s variance over the mean squared error. GCV of K is N times RSS over N minus K plus four, squared.”

The same project in Python

The reference below uses SciPy’s least-squares B-spline routine for the splines and builds each level of the wavelet transform as an explicit orthogonal N × N matrix, a completely different route from the page’s fast pyramid. (The PyWavelets package could not be loaded on the machine that built this page; the symmlet-8 filter was instead derived from scratch by spectral factorisation and agrees with the published coefficients to 10−12.)

import json, numpy as np
from scipy.interpolate import make_lsq_spline

D = json.load(open("x018-data.json"))          # MIT-BIH record 100 (MLII) + noise, 4096 samples
fs, N = 360, 4096
S8 = [-0.003382415951004992, -0.0005421323317999858, 0.031695087811525975, 0.00760748732497662,
      -0.14329423835127228, -0.061273359067810444, 0.4813596512590535, 0.7771857516996276,
      0.3644418948361786, -0.05194583810788162, -0.0272190299171035, 0.04913717967373004,
      0.0038087520138943495, -0.01495225833706221, -0.00030292051472412544, 0.00188995033276769]
HAAR = [2 ** -0.5, 2 ** -0.5]

def W_matrix(h, n):                            # one level of the periodic filter bank, n x n orthogonal
    g = [(-1) ** k * h[len(h) - 1 - k] for k in range(len(h))]
    M = np.zeros((n, n))
    for r in range(n // 2):
        for k in range(len(h)):
            M[r, (2 * r + k) % n] += h[k]; M[n // 2 + r, (2 * r + k) % n] += g[k]
    return M

def dwt(y, h, J):                              # [a_J | d_J | ... | d_1]
    c = y.copy(); n = len(y)
    for _ in range(J): c[:n] = W_matrix(h, n) @ c[:n]; n //= 2
    return c

def idwt(c, h, J):
    c = c.copy(); n = len(c) >> J
    for _ in range(J): n *= 2; c[:n] = W_matrix(h, n).T @ c[:n]
    return c

def sure_t(x):                                  # SURE threshold for unit-variance coefficients x
    a = np.sort(np.abs(x)); n = len(a); cap = np.sqrt(2 * np.log(n))
    best, bt = n, 0.0
    for i, t in enumerate(a):
        if t > cap: break
        risk = n - 2 * (i + 1) + np.sum(np.minimum(a, t) ** 2)
        if risk < best: best, bt = risk, t
    return bt

def shrink(y, h, J=6, rule="universal"):
    c = dwt(y, h, J); n0 = N >> J
    sig = np.median(np.abs(c[N // 2:])) / 0.6745      # noise level from the finest details
    lam = sig * np.sqrt(2 * np.log(N))
    for j in range(J):
        a, b = n0 << j, n0 << (j + 1)
        t = lam if rule == "universal" else sig * sure_t(c[a:b] / sig)
        c[a:b] = np.sign(c[a:b]) * np.maximum(np.abs(c[a:b]) - t, 0)
    return idwt(c, h, J)

def spline(y, K):                               # cubic regression spline, K equally spaced interior knots
    x = np.arange(N, dtype=float); kn = (N - 1) * np.arange(1, K + 1) / (K + 1)
    t = np.r_[[0.0] * 4, kn, [N - 1.0] * 4]
    return make_lsq_spline(x, y, t, k=3)(x)

def peaks(s):                                   # R peaks: local maxima (+-0.2 s) above a threshold
    med, hi = np.median(s), np.percentile(s, 98); thr = med + 0.45 * (hi - med); w = 72
    return [i for i in range(N) if s[i] >= thr and s[i] == s[max(0, i - w):i + w + 1].max()
            and s[i] > s[max(0, i - w):i].max(initial=-9)]

def score(s, c, beats):
    p = peaks(s); hit = sum(any(abs(q - b) <= 36 for q in p) for b in beats)
    return (np.sqrt(np.mean((s - c) ** 2)), 10 * np.log10(np.var(c) / np.mean((s - c) ** 2)),
            np.mean(s[beats] - c[beats]), hit, len(p) - hit, 60 * fs / np.diff(p).mean())

def methods(y):
    gcv = {K: N * np.sum((y - spline(y, K)) ** 2) / (N - K - 4) ** 2 for K in range(50, 2001, 50)}
    K = min(gcv, key=gcv.get)
    return [("noisy signal", y), (f"spline, GCV K={K}", spline(y, K)), ("Haar, universal", shrink(y, HAAR)),
            ("symmlet-8, universal", shrink(y, S8)), ("symmlet-8, SURE", shrink(y, S8, rule="sure"))]

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A basis expansion replaces x by M fixed functions hm(x) and fits a linear model in them; the fitting is ordinary least squares (or logistic regression), and all the modelling is in the choice of basis.
  • Splines are piecewise polynomials joined at knots with matching derivatives; a cubic spline with K knots has K + 4 parameters, a natural cubic spline (linear beyond the boundary knots) only K, and it tames the wild variance at the edges.
  • B-splines span the same spaces as the truncated power basis but are local, sum to one and give banded, well-conditioned systems: the way splines are actually computed.
  • Constraining coefficients to a smooth basis is filtering: x* = H⊤x. On the phoneme data, 12 spline features beat 256 raw ones on new speakers.
  • Wavelets give an orthonormal basis at every place and scale; soft-thresholding the coefficients is a lasso that smooths adaptively. It shines when the noise is white and fails when the noise shares the signal’s frequencies, as real muscle artefact does.

Check your understanding

A piecewise cubic with 3 knots is required to have continuous value and first derivative, but not second. How many parameters does it have?

(d + 1)(K + 1) − K(c + 1) with d = 3, K = 3, c = 1: 4 × 4 − 3 × 2 = 10. A full cubic spline (c = 2) would have 4 × 4 − 3 × 3 = 7 = K + 4.

Why does a natural cubic spline with K knots have exactly K degrees of freedom?

A cubic spline with K knots has K + 4. Being linear beyond each boundary knot sets the second and third derivatives to zero there: two constraints at each end, four in all, leaving K.

Your truncated-power spline fit gives nonsense coefficients when you add many close knots, but the B-spline fit with the same knots is fine. Is the model different?

No: both span exactly the same space of functions, so the exact least-squares fitted curve is identical. The truncated power columns are nearly collinear, so H⊤H is badly conditioned and rounding errors are magnified; B-splines are local and their H⊤H is banded and well conditioned.

Why estimate the noise level from the finest wavelet coefficients, and why with a median rather than a standard deviation?

For a smooth or piecewise-smooth signal, the finest details are almost all noise. The few that are large because of a sharp edge in the signal would inflate a standard deviation; the median absolute value (divided by 0.6745 to make it estimate σ for Gaussian noise) ignores them.

On muscle-artefact noise GCV picks the largest K it is offered and the spline barely smooths. Why?

GCV assumes independent errors. Muscle noise is strongly correlated from one sample to the next, so it looks like signal: a wigglier fit seems to keep improving the leave-one-out error. The same correlation hides the noise from the finest wavelet level, so σ̂ from that level is far too small.
