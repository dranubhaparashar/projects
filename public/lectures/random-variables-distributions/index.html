Random Variables, Expectations & Distributions

Give chance a number, then give the number a shape

The total of two dice, the number of heads in ten tosses, the hours until the next big earthquake: each is a random variable, a number whose value is left to chance. Draw it many times and the values pile up into a shape, its distribution. A handful of shapes turn up again and again in machine learning, and this chapter builds each one with you. On the right, tap the figure to pour samples: watch the pile grow into the curve that predicts it. The last button pours real waiting times between earthquakes.

The objects

Mass, density and the cumulative distribution

A random variable is a rule that attaches a number to every outcome of an uncertain process. Roll two dice and add them: the outcome is a pair of faces, the random variable is their total. Some random variables are discrete: they take values you can list, like 2, 3, …, 12. Others are continuous: a waiting time can be 1.7 hours, 1.71 hours, or anything in between.

For a discrete variable we give every value its own probability, the probability mass function (pmf). A continuous variable has so many values that each single one has probability zero: the chance that you wait exactly 1.700000… hours is nil. So we describe it by a probability density function (pdf), probability per unit length, and read probabilities off as areas. The cumulative distribution function (CDF) works for both. Pick a variable and move the interval; watch the area (or the bars) and the CDF agree.

\[ 0 \le P(x) \le 1, \qquad \sum_{x} P(x) = 1 \]
Read it as: a pmf hands each possible value a probability between 0 and 1, and the probabilities of all the values add up to exactly one. The probability of a set of values is the sum of their bars.
Say it“Each P of x lies between zero and one, and the sum over all x of P of x is one.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &p(x) \ge 0, \qquad \int p(x)\,dx = 1 \\ &P(a \le x \le b) = \int_a^b p(x)\,dx \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a density is never negative and its total area is one. The probability of landing in an interval is the area above it. A density has no upper limit of 1: only the area has to behave.
Say it“p of x is at least zero and integrates to one; the probability that x lies between a and b is the integral of p from a to b.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &P\big(z < x < z+\delta\big) \approx p(z)\,\delta \\ &\text{for small } \delta \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: this is what a density means. Over a narrow interval the curve is nearly flat, so the area is height times width. Height is therefore probability per unit of x: per hour, per metre, per dollar.
Say it“The chance that x falls in a small interval of width delta starting at z is about p of z times delta.”
\[ \begin{aligned} F(z) &= P(x \le z) = \int_{-\infty}^{z} p(x)\,dx \\ p(x) &= F'(x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the CDF collects all the probability up to z. It climbs from 0 to 1 and never goes down. For a density it is smooth and its slope is the density; for a pmf it is a staircase that jumps by P(x) at each value.
Say it“F of z is the probability that x is at most z: the integral of p up to z. The density is the derivative of F.”

The technical details

Change of variables: densities stretch and squeeze

Suppose you know the density of x and you compute y = g(x). What is the density of y? The tempting answer, “plug g−1(y) into the old density”, is wrong. Probability is conserved, not height. The thin slice of x-values between x and x + dx holds some probability mass, and all of it lands in the slice of y-values that g maps it to. Where g is steep the y-slice is wide, so the same mass is spread thinly and the density falls. Where g is flat the mass is squeezed and the density rises.

Two pieces of fine print come with densities. A set with no length (a single point, or any list of points) has measure zero: the probability of landing in it is zero, so you can change a density at such points without changing any probability. A statement that holds everywhere except on a set of measure zero holds almost everywhere. Pick a transformation and slide the slice: the two shaded strips always hold the same mass. Then switch on the naive answer and watch it go wrong.

\[ \begin{aligned} p_y(y) &= p_x(x)\left|\frac{dx}{dy}\right| \\ &= \frac{p_x\big(g^{-1}(y)\big)}{\big|\,g'\big(g^{-1}(y)\big)\big|} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: to get y’s density at y, find the x that maps there, take x’s density, and divide by how much g stretches lengths at that point. A stretch of 3 spreads the mass over three times the length, so the density is a third as tall.
Say it“p-y of y equals p-x at g-inverse of y, divided by the absolute slope of g there.”
\[ p_x(\mathbf{x}) = p_y\big(g(\mathbf{x})\big)\left|\det\frac{\partial g(\mathbf{x})}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\right| \]
Read it as: the same rule in many dimensions. The Jacobian matrix ∂g/∂x describes how g distorts a tiny box around x, and its absolute determinant is the factor by which the box’s volume grows. Example: y = 2x in two dimensions quadruples every area, so px(x) = 4 py(2x). Normalising flows, later in the book, are built on exactly this formula.
Say it“p-x of x equals p-y of g of x times the absolute determinant of the Jacobian of g.”

The summaries

Expectation, variance and covariance

A whole distribution is a lot to carry around. Usually we want a few numbers. The expectation of a function f(x) is its average value when x is drawn from the distribution: each value of f weighted by how likely it is. It is linear, E[αf + βg] = αE[f] + βE[g], which makes it easy to compute with. When you cannot do the sum or the integral, draw samples and average them: the Monte Carlo estimate gets closer as you draw more, with an error that shrinks like 1/√N. Pour draws in and watch the estimate settle inside its funnel.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[f] &= \sum_x P(x)\,f(x) \\ \mathbb{E}[f] &= \int p(x)\,f(x)\,dx \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the long-run average of f(x). For a discrete variable, weight each value of f by its probability and add; for a continuous one, weight by the density and integrate. With f(x) = x this is the mean of x.
Say it“The expectation of f is the sum over x of P of x times f of x, or the integral of p of x times f of x.”
\[ \mathbb{E}[f] \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} f(x_n) \]
Read it as: draw N independent samples x1, …, xN from the distribution and average f over them. The estimate is right on average, and its typical error is the standard deviation of f divided by √N: a hundred times more draws buy one more correct digit.
Say it“The expectation of f is approximately one over N times the sum of f of x-n.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}[f] &= \mathbb{E}\Big[\big(f(x) - \mathbb{E}[f(x)]\big)^2\Big] \\ &= \mathbb{E}\big[f(x)^2\big] - \mathbb{E}\big[f(x)\big]^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the variance measures spread: the average squared distance of f(x) from its own mean. Expanding the square gives the second form, which is handy for computing. Its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the units of f.
Say it“The variance of f is the expected squared deviation of f from its mean, which equals the mean of f squared minus the square of the mean.”

Two variables at once: covariance

For two random variables, the covariance says whether they move together. Take each point’s distance from the mean in x and in y and multiply: the product is positive when both are above or both below (the teal rectangles), negative when one is above and the other below (the orange ones). Covariance is the average product. Divide by both standard deviations and you get the correlation, between −1 and 1. Drag the points, then try the parabola: covariance zero, yet y is completely determined by x. Zero covariance means no linear relationship, not independence.

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Cov}[x,y] &= \mathbb{E}\big[(x-\mathbb{E}[x])\,(y-\mathbb{E}[y])\big] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[xy]-\mathbb{E}[x]\,\mathbb{E}[y] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the average product of the two deviations. Big and positive: when x is high, y tends to be high. Negative: they pull opposite ways. Independent variables always have zero covariance; the reverse is not true. The points here are treated as the whole distribution, each with probability 1/n.
Say it“The covariance of x and y is the expected product of their deviations from their means, which is E of x y minus E of x times E of y.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\operatorname{Cov}[\mathbf{x}]_{i,j} = \operatorname{Cov}[x_i, x_j] \\ &\operatorname{Cov}[x_i, x_i] = \operatorname{Var}[x_i] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for a vector of variables, collect every pairwise covariance into a square, symmetric covariance matrix. The diagonal holds the variances. This matrix is the star of the next chapter, on the multivariate Gaussian.
Say it“Entry i, j of the covariance matrix is the covariance of x-i and x-j; the diagonal entries are the variances.”

Two outcomes

Bernoulli and binomial: coins and counts

The simplest random variable has two values: heads or tails, spam or not, click or no click. Code them as x = 1 and x = 0. The Bernoulli distribution has one parameter, μ = P(x = 1). Toss N such coins independently and count the heads, m: that count follows the binomial distribution. Given data, the natural estimate of μ is the one that makes the data most probable, the maximum-likelihood estimate, and it turns out to be the fraction of heads.

There is a catch. Toss a coin three times and see three heads, and maximum likelihood says μ = 1: it predicts every future toss will be heads. With little data, it overfits; the Bayesian chapter fixes this with a prior. Set μ and N, then toss. The bars show where a thousand repeats of your experiment landed.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\operatorname{Bern}(x \mid \mu) = \mu^{x}(1-\mu)^{1-x} \\ &x \in \{0, 1\} \\ &\mathbb{E}[x] = \mu, \qquad \operatorname{var}[x] = \mu(1-\mu) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the powers are a switch: for x = 1 the formula gives μ, for x = 0 it gives 1 − μ. The mean is μ; the variance is largest, ¼, for a fair coin and zero for a coin that always lands the same way.
Say it“Bernoulli of x given mu is mu to the x times one minus mu to the one minus x.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \ln p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mu) &= \sum_{n=1}^{N} \big[x_n \ln\mu \\ &\qquad + (1-x_n)\ln(1-\mu)\big] \\ \mu_{\text{ML}} &= \frac{m}{N} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the tosses are independent, so the probability of the whole data set 𝒟 is a product, and its log a sum. Only the number of heads m matters (a sufficient statistic). Set the derivative to zero and the best μ is the fraction of heads.
Say it“The log-likelihood is the sum of x-n log mu plus one minus x-n log one minus mu; it peaks at mu equals m over N.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\operatorname{Bin}(m \mid N, \mu) \\ &\quad = \binom{N}{m}\,\mu^{m}(1-\mu)^{N-m} \\ &\mathbb{E}[m] = N\mu \\ &\operatorname{var}[m] = N\mu(1-\mu) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: any particular sequence with m heads has probability μm(1 − μ)N−m, and there are “N choose m” = N!/(m!(N − m)!) such sequences. A count is a sum of N independent Bernoullis, so its mean and variance are N times theirs.
Say it“Binomial of m given N and mu is N choose m, times mu to the m, times one minus mu to the N minus m.”

K outcomes

Multinoulli and multinomial: dice and words

A die has six outcomes, a language model chooses among fifty thousand words. Code the outcome as a one-hot vector: K slots, all zero except a single 1 in the slot that happened. The multinoulli (or categorical) distribution gives slot k probability μk, with the μs adding to one. Roll N times and count how often each face came up: the counts follow the multinomial. If every μk = 1/K, you have the discrete uniform distribution: a fair die.

Drag the tops of the bars to load the die (the others rescale so the total stays one), then roll. The dots show how often each face has come up so far.

\[ \begin{aligned} &p(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}) = \prod_{k=1}^{K} \mu_k^{\,x_k} \\ &x_k \in \{0,1\},\ \ \textstyle\sum_k x_k = 1 \\ &\textstyle\sum_k \mu_k = 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same switch trick as Bernoulli. Every factor with xk = 0 equals 1, so the product picks out the one μk whose slot is lit. The mean of the one-hot vector is μ itself.
Say it“p of x given mu is the product over k of mu-k to the power x-k.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\sum_{k} m_k \ln\mu_k + \lambda\Big(\sum_k \mu_k - 1\Big) \\ &\Rightarrow\ \mu_k^{\text{ML}} = \frac{m_k}{N} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the log-likelihood of N rolls only needs the counts mk. Maximise it while keeping the μs summing to one (a Lagrange multiplier λ enforces the constraint; it comes out as −N). The answer: each face’s estimated probability is the share of rolls it won.
Say it“Maximise the sum of m-k log mu-k plus lambda times the constraint; the best mu-k is m-k over N.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\operatorname{Mult}(m_1, \dots, m_K \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}, N) \\ &\quad = \frac{N!}{m_1!\,m_2!\cdots m_K!}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\mu_k^{\,m_k} \\ &\mathbb{E}[m_k] = N\mu_k \\ &\operatorname{var}[m_k] = N\mu_k(1-\mu_k) \\ &\operatorname{cov}[m_j, m_k] = -N\mu_j\mu_k \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the binomial with K piles instead of two. The fraction counts the orderings that give these counts. The covariance is negative because the counts compete: N is fixed, so one more six means one fewer of something else.
Say it“The multinomial is N factorial over the product of the m-k factorials, times the product of mu-k to the m-k.”

Continuous shapes

Uniform, exponential and Laplace

Three continuous shapes cover a surprising amount of ground. The uniform spreads probability evenly over an interval: it is how a computer’s random number generator starts. The exponential describes waiting times when events arrive at a steady rate and independently, and it has no memory: having waited an hour already tells you nothing about how much longer you’ll wait. The Laplace is two exponentials back to back, a sharp peak with heavier tails than a Gaussian; it is the distribution behind the L1 penalty and the absolute-error loss.

Pick a shape and turn its dials. For the exponential, the second dial is how long you have already waited: the orange curve is the density of the total wait given that, and it is the same shape slid along.

\[ \begin{aligned} &U(x \mid a, b) = \frac{1}{b-a}, \quad a \le x \le b \\ &\mathbb{E}[x] = \frac{a+b}{2}, \quad \operatorname{var}[x] = \frac{(b-a)^2}{12} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a flat density over [a, b], zero outside. Its height is whatever makes the rectangle’s area one. The mean sits in the middle.
Say it“U of x given a and b is one over b minus a; the mean is the midpoint and the variance is the width squared over twelve.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &p(x; \lambda) = \lambda\,\mathbf{1}_{x \ge 0}\,e^{-\lambda x} \\ &\mathbb{E}[x] = \frac{1}{\lambda}, \quad \operatorname{var}[x] = \frac{1}{\lambda^2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: λ is the rate: events per unit time. The indicator 1x≥0 is 1 for x ≥ 0 and 0 otherwise, so negative waits are impossible. The density starts at height λ and halves every ln 2/λ. The mean wait is 1/λ.
Say it“p of x is lambda, times the indicator that x is non-negative, times e to the minus lambda x.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &P(x > s+t \mid x > s) \\ &\quad = \frac{e^{-\lambda (s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} = e^{-\lambda t} = P(x > t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the exponential is memoryless. Given that you have already waited s, the chance of waiting at least t more is the same as it was at the start. It is the only continuous distribution with this property, which is why it describes events that strike without warning.
Say it“The chance of waiting past s plus t, given you waited past s, equals the chance of waiting past t.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\operatorname{Laplace}(x; \mu, \gamma) \\ &\quad = \frac{1}{2\gamma}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{|x-\mu|}{\gamma}\Big) \\ &\mathbb{E}[x] = \mu, \quad \operatorname{var}[x] = 2\gamma^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: an exponential on each side of μ, each with half the area. The peak is a sharp corner, not a smooth hill. Maximising a Laplace likelihood means minimising the sum of absolute errors |x − μ|, whose answer is the median.
Say it“Laplace of x is one over two gamma times e to the minus the absolute distance from mu over gamma.”

Data as a distribution

The Dirac delta and the empirical distribution

Sometimes we want a distribution that puts all its probability on one point. No ordinary density can do that, but we can imagine a bump of area one that gets narrower and taller without end. The limit is the Dirac delta δ(x): zero everywhere except at 0, infinitely tall there, with area one. It is not a true function but a generalised one, defined by what it does inside an integral.

Put a delta on every data point, each with weight 1/m, and you get the empirical distribution: the data set itself, viewed as a distribution. Below are the first m real waiting times (in hours) between magnitude 5.5+ earthquakes from the project. Shrink the bump width to zero and the smooth curve becomes a row of spikes; the CDF below doesn’t care either way.

\[ \begin{aligned} &p(x) = \delta(x-\mu) \\ &\int \delta(x-\mu)\,dx = 1 \\ &\int f(x)\,\delta(x-\mu)\,dx = f(\mu) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a spike at μ holding all the probability. Integrating any function against it simply picks out the function’s value at μ (the sifting property). Think of it as the limit of a bump of width h and height about 1/h, as h → 0.
Say it“p of x is the Dirac delta at mu; it integrates to one, and integrating f against it gives f of mu.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\hat p(x) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta\big(x - x^{(i)}\big) \\ &\hat F(z) = \frac{1}{m}\,\#\{\,i : x^{(i)} \le z\,\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: mass 1/m on each of the m data points. Its CDF is the share of points at or below z. An expectation under p̂ is just the average over the data, so Monte Carlo is expectation under the empirical distribution. Among all distributions, p̂ gives the training data the highest likelihood; for discrete data it is simply the table of observed frequencies.
Say it“p-hat of x is one over m times the sum of deltas at the data points; F-hat of z is the fraction of points at or below z.”

Building bigger shapes

Mixtures: one distribution from several

Real data is often a blend. Earthquake waiting times are a blend of quick aftershocks and the long, steady background; heights in a room are a blend of children and adults. A mixture models this with a two-step recipe: first pick a component c with probability P(c = i), then draw x from that component. The identity c is a latent variable: it shapes the data but is never written down. Given an x, Bayes’ rule tells you how responsible each component is for it.

Gaussian mixtures are remarkably flexible: with enough components they can approximate any smooth density as closely as you like. Drag the peaks sideways to move a component and up or down to sharpen or widen it, set the weights, and slide the probe to read the responsibilities.

\[ \begin{aligned} P(x) &= \sum_{i} P(c = i)\,P(x \mid c = i) \\ p(x) &= \sum_i \pi_i\,\mathcal{N}\big(x;\, \mu_i, \sigma_i^2\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the sum rule, with the component as the hidden variable. The weights πi = P(c = i) add to one, so the mixture is a proper distribution. The second line is the Gaussian mixture, where each component is a bell curve with its own centre and width.
Say it“P of x is the sum over components of the chance of picking component i times the chance of x under it.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &P(c = i \mid x) \\ &\quad = \frac{P(c = i)\,p(x \mid c = i)}{\sum_j P(c = j)\,p(x \mid c = j)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the responsibility of component i for the point x: its share of the total height of the mixture at x. It is Bayes’ rule from the last chapter. Fitting a mixture to data alternates between computing these and refitting each component to the points it is responsible for: the EM algorithm, which the project uses.
Say it“The chance it was component i, given x, is its weighted height at x divided by the total.”

Useful functions

Sigmoid and softplus: squashing into range

Distributions have parameters with rules: a Bernoulli’s μ must lie between 0 and 1, an exponential’s rate or a Gaussian’s standard deviation must be positive. A model, though, produces any real number. Two smooth functions bridge the gap. The logistic sigmoid σ squashes the whole real line into (0, 1); the softplus ζ maps it onto (0, ∞), a smoothed version of max(0, x). Both flatten out (saturate) far from zero, where small changes in x barely move them; that is where learning by gradient slows down.

The two are tied together by a family of identities worth knowing by heart. Slide x and check every one of them numerically in the table.

\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma(x) &= \frac{1}{1+e^{-x}} \\ \zeta(x) &= \log\big(1+e^{x}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: σ(0) = ½, σ tends to 1 for large x and to 0 for very negative x. ζ(x) is close to 0 for very negative x and close to x for large x, with a smooth bend at zero instead of ReLU’s corner. The name “softplus” is short for “soft version of x+ = max(0, x)”.
Say it“Sigma of x is one over one plus e to the minus x; zeta of x is the log of one plus e to the x.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma(x) &= \frac{e^{x}}{e^{x} + e^{0}} \\ \frac{d}{dx}\sigma(x) &= \sigma(x)\big(1-\sigma(x)\big) \\ 1-\sigma(x) &= \sigma(-x) \\ \log\sigma(x) &= -\zeta(-x) \\ \frac{d}{dx}\zeta(x) &= \sigma(x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: σ is a two-way softmax; its slope peaks at ¼ at x = 0 and is a product of the two probabilities; it is symmetric about (0, ½); its log is a negated softplus, which is how losses on probabilities are computed stably; and softplus is the running integral of σ.
Say it“The slope of sigma is sigma times one minus sigma; one minus sigma of x is sigma of minus x; log sigma of x is minus zeta of minus x; the slope of zeta is sigma.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma^{-1}(x) &= \log\frac{x}{1-x} \\ \zeta^{-1}(x) &= \log\big(e^{x}-1\big) \\ \zeta(x) &= \int_{-\infty}^{x}\sigma(y)\,dy \\ \zeta(x) - \zeta(-x) &= x \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the inverse of σ is the logit, the log-odds (defined for x between 0 and 1); the softplus has an inverse too (for x > 0); softplus is the area under σ so far; and the last identity mirrors x = x+ − x−, splitting a number into its positive and negative parts. In the change-of-variables lab, y = logit(x) turned a uniform x into the logistic distribution, whose density is exactly σ′(y).
Say it“Sigma inverse is the log-odds; zeta inverse is log of e to the x minus one; zeta is the integral of sigma; zeta of x minus zeta of minus x is x.”

Real-world project

Project: waiting for the next earthquake

The U.S. Geological Survey’s ComCat catalogue lists every recorded earthquake, and it is in the public domain. We downloaded two slices of it: every magnitude 5.5+ quake in the world from 2014 to 2023 (4,652 quakes), and every magnitude 3+ quake in southern California (32–36.5°N, 114–121°W) over the same ten years (2,529 quakes, including the 2019 Ridgecrest sequence).

If quakes struck independently at a steady rate (a Poisson process), three things would follow, one for each kind of distribution in this chapter. The waiting times between quakes would be exponential. The number of quakes per day would be Poisson, the binomial’s limit. And the hour of the day would be uniform, a multinomial with 24 equal cells. Let’s test all three, then try the obvious fix: a mixture. The window removes quakes that come within w hours of the previous one, a crude way to strip out aftershocks. Switch catalogue and window; every lab below updates.

Waiting times: one exponential, or a mixture of two?

Fitting an exponential by maximum likelihood takes one line: the best rate is one over the average wait. If the window w is on, each kept quake waited at least w, so we fit the wait beyond w. Memorylessness says that if the waits were exponential to begin with, the extra wait is exponential with the same rate. The mixture has a fast component (aftershocks) and a slow one (the background); EM fits it. Switch the view: the log-hours view is a change of variables, and it shows the two humps plainly.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\ln p(g_1, \dots, g_n \mid \lambda) \\ &\quad = n\ln\lambda - \lambda\textstyle\sum_{i} g_i \\ &\Rightarrow\ \hat\lambda = \frac{n}{\sum_i g_i} = \frac{1}{\bar g} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the waits gi are independent exponentials, so the log-likelihood is a sum of ln λ − λgi. Its derivative n/λ − ∑gi is zero at λ = 1/mean wait.
Say it“The log-likelihood is n log lambda minus lambda times the total wait; the best lambda is one over the mean wait.”
\[ \begin{aligned} r_i &= \frac{\pi\lambda_1 e^{-\lambda_1 g_i}}{\pi\lambda_1 e^{-\lambda_1 g_i} + (1-\pi)\lambda_2 e^{-\lambda_2 g_i}} \\ \pi &\leftarrow \frac{1}{n}\sum_i r_i, \qquad \lambda_1 \leftarrow \frac{\sum_i r_i}{\sum_i r_i\, g_i} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: EM for a mixture of two exponentials. The E step computes each wait’s responsibility ri for the fast component; the M step refits the weight as the average responsibility and each rate as one over a responsibility-weighted mean wait (λ2 uses 1 − ri). Each round can only raise the likelihood.
Say it“r-i is the fast component's share of the mixture density at g-i; pi becomes the mean of the r's, and lambda-one the sum of r over the sum of r times g.”
\[ D = \max_z\,\big|\hat F(z) - F(z)\big| \]
Read it as: the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance: the largest vertical gap between the data’s empirical CDF and the model’s CDF. It reads like a probability: D = 0.09 means some threshold z where the model is off by nine percentage points in the share of waits below z.

Counts per day: the Poisson test

Cut each day into n tiny slots, each with a tiny chance λ/n of a quake. The day’s count is then binomial, and as the slots shrink it becomes the Poisson distribution. Its mean and variance are both λ, so the ratio variance/mean, the dispersion index, should be about 1. Clustering pushes it above 1.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\lim_{n \to \infty} \operatorname{Bin}\Big(k \;\Big|\; n, \frac{\lambda}{n}\Big) \\ &\quad = \frac{\lambda^{k} e^{-\lambda}}{k!} \\ &\mathbb{E}[k] = \operatorname{var}[k] = \lambda \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: many chances, each tiny, with a fixed expected total λ: the binomial pmf settles to λke−λ/k!. In particular, the chance of a quiet day is e−λ, and “at least one quake today” is a Bernoulli variable with μ = 1 − e−λ.
Say it“As n grows, binomial of k with n tries at lambda over n becomes lambda to the k, e to the minus lambda, over k factorial.”

Hour of the day: the uniform test

The Earth has no clock, so each quake’s hour (in UTC) should be one roll of a fair 24-sided die: the counts per hour are multinomial with every μk = 1/24. Pearson’s chi-squared statistic adds up the squared surprises; if the die is fair it follows a χ² distribution with 23 degrees of freedom, whose mean is 23.

\[ \chi^2 = \sum_{k=1}^{24} \frac{\big(m_k - N/24\big)^2}{N/24} \]
Read it as: for each hour, how far the count is from the N/24 a fair die would give, squared and scaled by the expected count. The p-value is the chance a truly fair die would produce a statistic at least this large. A tiny p-value says the hours are not uniform.
Say it“Chi-squared is the sum over the 24 hours of the squared gap between the count and N over 24, divided by N over 24.”

import json, numpy as np
from scipy import stats

D = json.load(open("x005-data.json"))   # USGS ComCat: hours since 2014-01-01 00:00 UTC

def em_two_exponentials(g, iters=3000):
    w, r1, r2 = 0.5, 10 / g.mean(), 0.5 / g.mean()        # weight of the fast part, two rates
    for _ in range(iters):
        a = w * r1 * np.exp(-r1 * g); b = (1 - w) * r2 * np.exp(-r2 * g)
        z = a / (a + b)                                     # responsibility of the fast part
        w, r1, r2 = z.mean(), z.sum() / (z * g).sum(), (1 - z).sum() / ((1 - z) * g).sum()
    pdf = w * r1 * np.exp(-r1 * g) + (1 - w) * r2 * np.exp(-r2 * g)
    cdf = lambda x: w * (1 - np.exp(-r1 * x)) + (1 - w) * (1 - np.exp(-r2 * x))
    return w, 1 / r1, 1 / r2, np.log(pdf).mean(), stats.kstest(g, cdf).statistic

Two honest caveats. The window is a blunt tool: real seismology removes aftershocks with windows that grow with the main shock’s magnitude (Gardner & Knopoff, 1974), and a window as wide as 24 hours also deletes genuine independent quakes and forces a minimum spacing, which is why the counts then become more regular than Poisson. And aftershocks decay as a power law in time (Omori’s law), so two exponentials are only an approximation: good for the world catalogue, rough for Ridgecrest.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Discrete variables have a pmf whose bars sum to one; continuous ones have a density whose area is one, which may be taller than one. The CDF F(z) = P(x ≤ z) works for both.
  • Under y = g(x), probability is conserved, not height: divide by the stretch |g′(x)| (or the Jacobian determinant in many dimensions). Peaks can move.
  • Expectation is a probability-weighted average and is linear; variance is the expected squared deviation; covariance measures linear togetherness, and zero covariance does not mean independence. Monte Carlo averages converge like 1/√N.
  • Bernoulli and binomial count successes, multinoulli and multinomial count categories, and their maximum-likelihood estimates are observed frequencies. Uniform, exponential (memoryless) and Laplace (sharp peak, heavy tails) are the basic continuous shapes; Dirac deltas turn a data set into the empirical distribution; mixtures build bigger shapes from a hidden choice.
  • Sigmoid and softplus turn any real number into a valid probability or a positive scale. On real earthquakes, a single exponential fails because aftershocks cluster; a two-component mixture captures most of it.

Check your understanding

A density takes the value 3 at some point. Is something wrong?

No. A density is probability per unit length, so it can exceed 1; only its total area must be 1. The uniform density on [0, ⅓] equals 3 everywhere on that interval.

x is uniform on (0, 1) and y = x². Why is py(y) not simply 1 on (0, 1)?

Because g(x) = x² squeezes small x-values together: the slice (0, 0.1) of x lands in (0, 0.01) of y. The same mass in a shorter interval means a taller density. The correct answer is py(y) = 1/(2√y), which piles up near 0.

Two variables have zero covariance. Are they independent?

Not necessarily. Covariance only detects linear association. If x is symmetric about 0 and y = x², the covariance is zero but y is completely determined by x. (Independence does imply zero covariance.)

You toss a coin four times and get four tails. What is μML, and why might you not trust it?

μML = 0/4 = 0, which says heads is impossible. With so little data the estimate overfits; a prior (for example a Beta distribution) pulls it towards plausible values, which is the subject of the Bayesian inference chapter.

Daily earthquake counts have a mean of 0.69 and a variance of 107. What does that tell you?

A Poisson process would have variance equal to the mean. A variance 155 times the mean means the counts are hugely overdispersed: quakes come in bursts (here the Ridgecrest aftershocks), so a single rate cannot describe them, which is why a mixture of a fast and a slow component fits the waiting times much better.
