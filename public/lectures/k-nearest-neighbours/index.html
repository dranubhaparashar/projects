k-Nearest Neighbours

No training, no formula: just look at who's nearby

To guess what a new thing is, find the k most similar things you've already seen and let them vote. That's the whole algorithm. It has no equations to fit and learns nothing in advance — it simply remembers. And it reads handwriting with 98% accuracy. Draw a digit in the pad and meet the neighbours who decide.

Foundations

Judged by the company you keep

Every algorithm so far built a model: a line, a pair of Gaussians, a table of word probabilities. k-NN builds nothing. It is a lazy learner (also called instance-based or memory-based): it keeps every training example and does all its work at prediction time. That has a striking consequence — adding a new example “retrains” it instantly — and a cost: every prediction must compare against everything it remembers.

It is also non-parametric: there's no fixed set of weights, so the boundary can take any shape the data suggests. With enough data, the error of 1-NN is at most twice the best possible error (Cover & Hart, 1967) — a remarkable guarantee for something so simple.

The algorithm

Ask your k nearest neighbours

Drag the black ring anywhere. The lines connect it to its k nearest training points; their classes vote. Turn on distance weighting and closer neighbours get a louder voice.

\[ \hat{y}(\mathbf{x}) = \arg\max_{c} \sum_{i \in N_k(\mathbf{x})} w_i \,\mathbb{1}[\,y_i = c\,], \qquad w_i = 1 \;\;\text{or}\;\; w_i = \frac{1}{d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_i)} \]
Read it as: Nk(x) is the set of the k training points closest to x. Each one votes for its own class (the 1[…] is 1 when yi = c, else 0) with weight wi; the class with most votes wins. Uniform weights: one neighbour, one vote. Distance weights: the inverse distance.
Say it“Predict the class with the largest total weight among the k nearest neighbours.”

The ballot

What “near” means

Distance, three ways

“Nearest” depends on how you measure. As the crow flies (Euclidean), along city blocks (Manhattan), or by the single biggest difference (Chebyshev) — all three are members of one family, the Minkowski distance with power p. The shaded shape is every point at the same distance from Q: watch it morph from a diamond into a circle into a square, and watch which point is nearest change. Both points can be dragged.

\[ d_p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) = \Big(\sum_{j=1}^{d} |x_j - z_j|^{\,p}\Big)^{1/p} \qquad p = 1:\ \text{Manhattan}, \quad p = 2:\ \text{Euclidean}, \quad p \to \infty:\ \max_j |x_j - z_j| \]
Read it as: take the difference on every feature, raise to the power p, add, take the p-th root. Small p adds up all differences fairly; large p lets the biggest single difference dominate.
Say it“The Minkowski distance is the p-th root of the sum of absolute differences to the power p.”

The one dial

Choosing k: from nervous to numb

k = 1 trusts a single neighbour: every noisy point carves out its own island — low bias, high variance. Large k averages over half the dataset and smears real structure away — high bias, low variance. Slide k and watch the regions, and the training and test error curves below.

Rules of thumb

Use odd k for two classes (no ties). Start near √n. Then let cross-validation choose: it estimates test error without touching the test set.

A trap

Why scaling matters

Distance adds up differences across features — so a feature measured in big numbers drowns out one measured in small numbers. Below, an insurer predicts who buys a health policy from age (20–70 years) and income (₹2–20 lakh, stored in thousands). Buying really depends on age. But in raw units a difference of ₹50,000 counts as much as fifty years of age. The plot is drawn the way the distance sees it: equal scale on both axes.

\[ z_j = \frac{x_j - \mu_j}{\sigma_j} \]
Read it as: standardise every feature: subtract its mean, divide by its standard deviation. Now every feature has spread 1 and gets an equal say. (Compute μ and σ on training data only, then apply them to new data.)
Say it“z equals x minus its mean, over its standard deviation.”

The big weakness

The curse of dimensionality

In 2 dimensions, your nearest neighbour is much nearer than your farthest. Add dimensions and something strange happens: everyone becomes roughly equally far away. 500 random points in a d-dimensional cube; the histogram shows each one's distance from a query, divided by the farthest. When all the bars crowd near 1, “nearest” stops meaning “similar”.

\[ \text{edge needed to capture a fraction } f \text{ of uniform data in } d \text{ dimensions} = f^{1/d} \]
Read it as: to enclose 10% of the data in a sub-cube, in 1-D you need 10% of the range; in 10-D you need 79% of every axis. “Local” neighbourhoods stop being local.
Say it“The edge length is f to the power one over d.”
nearest ÷ farthest
edge for 10% of data

The remedy: fewer, better features (feature selection, PCA), or learned embeddings where distance does mean similarity — which is what vector search in LLM systems relies on.

Numbers too

k-NN for numbers too

For a numeric target, replace the vote with an average: predict the mean y of the k nearest neighbours. The prediction is a staircase that follows the data — jumpy for small k, flat for large k. Distance weighting smooths the steps.

\[ \hat{y}(x) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i \in N_k(x)} y_i \qquad\text{or, weighted:}\qquad \hat{y}(x) = \frac{\sum_{i} w_i y_i}{\sum_i w_i} \]
Read it as: the average of the neighbours' answers. It can never predict outside the range of the training y values — k-NN cannot extrapolate a trend.
Say it“Y-hat is the average y of the k nearest neighbours.”

The same project in scikit-learn

from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)            # 1797 digits, 64 features each (0–16)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=42, stratify=y)

for k in (1, 3, 5, 15, 51):
    for w in ("uniform", "distance"):
        acc = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=k, weights=w).fit(X_tr, y_tr).score(X_te, y_te)
        print(f"k={k:2d} {w:8s} accuracy {acc:.4f}")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • k-NN stores the training data and predicts by a vote (or average) of the k closest examples — no training, any boundary shape.
  • k is the bias–variance dial: small k is jumpy, large k is blurry; choose it by cross-validation.
  • “Close” depends on the distance (Manhattan, Euclidean, Chebyshev…) and above all on feature scaling: standardise first.
  • In high dimensions all points look equally far apart; reduce dimensions or use learned embeddings.
  • Prediction is the expensive part (compare with everything); real systems use trees or approximate nearest-neighbour indexes.

Check your understanding

What is the training error of 1-NN, and why does that tell you nothing?

Zero (unless two identical points have different labels): each training point is its own nearest neighbour. It has memorised the data; only test or cross-validation error says anything about generalisation.

You add a feature “annual salary in rupees” next to “age” and accuracy collapses. Why?

Salary differences are in the lakhs, age differences in tens; unscaled, distance is almost entirely salary. Standardise each feature (subtract mean, divide by standard deviation, both computed on training data).

Why prefer an odd k for a two-class problem?

With an even k the vote can tie, k/2 against k/2. An odd k always produces a winner (with uniform weights).

Why can k-NN regression never predict a house price higher than the most expensive house it has seen?

Its prediction is an average of training targets, and an average can't exceed the largest value. It can't extrapolate trends the way linear regression can.

Where does k-NN show up inside modern LLM applications?

Retrieval-augmented generation: documents are turned into embedding vectors, and for each question the system retrieves the k nearest documents (with fast approximate search) to give the model context.
