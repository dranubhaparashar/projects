Monte Carlo Basics

From the inverse CDF to importance sampling

Most of the quantities a probabilistic model cares about are integrals: a mean, a probability, a normalising constant, a gradient. Most of those integrals have no formula. Monte Carlo methods replace each one with an average over random draws, and this chapter is about how to make the draws. The figure throws darts under a dashed envelope built from an easy Gaussian, and keeps the ones that land under the target curve, whose area it never needs to know. The kept darts are exact samples from the target; every thrown dart, reweighted, also feeds an importance-sampling estimate. Drag the envelope’s peak to move it and its shoulder to widen it, and watch how many darts are wasted.

Why sample? Averages instead of integrals

There are two reasons to want random samples. Sometimes the samples are the goal: a generative model is judged by what it produces. More often they are a means to an integral. A posterior mean, the probability of a rare event, the normalising constant of a model, the expected gradient of a loss: each is a sum or integral of some function f weighted by a distribution p. When that sum has too many terms to add up, or the integral has no closed form, we can still estimate it. Draw n independent samples from p, evaluate f at each, and take the average.

That average is the Monte Carlo estimate. Its two properties make it useful. It is unbiased: on average over the random draws it equals the true value exactly. And its variance is the variance of f under p divided by n, so the typical error shrinks like 1/√n. Nothing in that rate depends on the dimension of x, which is why sampling survives where grids of quadrature points die. The catch is hidden in Var[f]: when f is large where p rarely visits, that variance is huge, and the rest of this chapter is largely about making it smaller. Pick an integrand and slide n, then try the spike, whose variance is infinite.

\[ \begin{aligned} s &= \sum_x p(x)\, f(x) = \mathbb{E}_p[f(x)] \\ s &= \int p(x)\, f(x)\, dx \\ \hat s_n &= \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f\big(x^{(i)}\big), \quad x^{(i)} \sim p \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the quantity s is an expectation of f under p, written as a sum for discrete x or an integral for continuous x. The estimate ŝn draws n samples from p and averages f over them. Here p is uniform on [0, 1], so s is simply the area under f.
Say it“s-hat-n is the average of f over n draws from p.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\hat s_n] &= s \\ \operatorname{Var}[\hat s_n] &= \frac{\operatorname{Var}_p[f(x)]}{n} \\ \hat s_n &\;\dot\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\Big(s,\ \frac{\operatorname{Var}_p[f(x)]}{n}\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: unbiased, with a variance that falls as 1/n. The law of large numbers says ŝn converges to s as n grows; when Var[f] is finite, the central limit theorem adds that its error is roughly normal, so ŝn ± 1.96 σ̂/√n is a 95% confidence interval. With infinite variance the average still converges, but slowly and in jumps, and the interval means nothing.
Say it“s-hat-n is unbiased, with variance the variance of f over n, and roughly normal for large n.”

PRML 11.1.1 · standard distributions

Uniforms in, any distribution out

A computer’s random-number generator gives one thing: (pseudo-)random numbers spread evenly over (0, 1). Every other distribution is made from those by a transformation. If z is uniform and y = f(z), the density of y follows from the change-of-variables rule: probability is conserved, so p(y) = p(z) |dz/dy|. Choose f so that this comes out as the density you want, and you have a sampler.

The choice that always works in one dimension is the inverse CDF. Let h(y) be the cumulative distribution function of the target, the area under its density to the left of y. Draw z uniformly, then solve h(y) = z. A uniform height on the CDF’s vertical axis comes out as a y that lands where the CDF is steep, which is exactly where the density is high. For the exponential this gives y = −ln(1 − z)/λ; for the Cauchy, y = tan(π(z − ½)). For a data set, the CDF is a staircase, and inverting it just picks one of the observed values at random, which is how the bootstrap resamples. Pick a distribution and draw a thousand; then try the Cauchy, which has no mean, and watch its sample mean refuse to settle.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(y) &= p(z)\,\Big|\frac{dz}{dy}\Big| \\ z = h(y) &= \int_{-\infty}^{y} p(\hat y)\, d\hat y \\ y &= h^{-1}(z), \quad z \sim \mathcal U(0,1) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a transformation squeezes or stretches probability; the factor |dz/dy| keeps the total at 1. With z uniform, p(z) = 1, so p(y) = dh/dy exactly when z = h(y) is the target’s CDF. Inverting the CDF turns a uniform draw into a draw from the target.
Say it“Draw z uniform, and return the y whose CDF value is z.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{exponential:}\;& y = -\lambda^{-1}\ln(1-z) \\ \text{Cauchy:}\;& y = \tan\!\big(\pi(z - \tfrac12)\big) \\ \text{Gumbel:}\;& y = -\ln(-\ln z) \\ \text{data:}\;& y = x_{(\lceil N z \rceil)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each line solves h(y) = z for one distribution. h(y) = 1 − e−λy for the exponential, ½ + arctan(y)/π for the standard Cauchy, exp(−e−y) for the standard Gumbel. For N data points sorted as x(1) ≤ … ≤ x(N), the staircase CDF steps up by 1/N at each, so its inverse returns the ⌈Nz⌉-th smallest.
Say it“Minus log of one minus z over lambda; tan of pi times z minus a half; minus log of minus log z.”

Two at a time: Box–Muller and correlated Gaussians

The Gaussian CDF has no closed-form inverse, so Gaussians are usually made two at a time. The polar Box–Muller method draws a point uniformly in the square [−1, 1]² and throws it away unless it lies inside the unit circle (a small rejection sampler: π/4 of the points survive). A surviving point (z1, z2) with r² = z1² + z2² becomes two independent standard normals. Working out the Jacobian of this two-variable transformation shows that the joint density of (y1, y2) factorises into two standard normal densities. For a Gaussian with mean μ and covariance Σ, factor Σ = LL⊤ (Cholesky) and set y = μ + Lz: the covariance of Lz is L I L⊤ = Σ. Slide the correlation and see the same independent pairs sheared into shape.

\[ \begin{aligned} r^2 &= z_1^2 + z_2^2 \le 1 \\ y_i &= z_i \sqrt{\frac{-2 \ln r^2}{r^2}} \\ p(y_1, y_2) &= p(z_1, z_2) \left| \frac{\partial(z_1, z_2)}{\partial(y_1, y_2)} \right| \\ &= \mathcal N(y_1)\,\mathcal N(y_2) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: keep only the points inside the circle, then stretch each one along its own radius so that the radius follows the right distribution. The Jacobian determinant generalises |dz/dy| to several variables; here it makes the joint density come out as two independent standard normals.
Say it“Each y is z times the square root of minus two log r-squared over r-squared.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \Sigma &= LL^{\top}, \quad y = \mu + Lz \\ \operatorname{cov}[y] &= L\, \mathbb{E}[zz^{\top}]\, L^{\top} = \Sigma \\ L &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \rho & \sqrt{1-\rho^2} \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: independent unit-variance z have E[zz⊤] = I, so multiplying by any L with LL⊤ = Σ gives covariance Σ. The bottom line is the Cholesky factor for two unit-variance variables with correlation ρ: y1 = z1, y2 = ρz1 + √(1 − ρ²) z2.
Say it“Factor Sigma as L L-transpose; mu plus L z has covariance Sigma.”

PRML 11.1.2 · rejection sampling

Throw darts, keep the hits

The inverse CDF needs a CDF you can invert. Often you have much less: a function p̃(z) you can evaluate that is proportional to the density you want, p(z) = p̃(z)/Zp, with the constant Zp unknown. That is the normal situation for a posterior, which is a prior times a likelihood, divided by an evidence nobody can compute.

Rejection sampling needs a proposal q(z) you can sample and a constant k with kq(z) ≥ p̃(z) everywhere. Draw z0 from q, then a height u0 uniformly between 0 and kq(z0). The pair is a point spread evenly under the envelope kq. Keep z0 if the point also lies under p̃, otherwise throw it away. The kept points are spread evenly under p̃, so their z values are exact, independent draws from p. The price is the rejections: the fraction kept is the area under p̃ over the area under kq, so k should be as small as the envelope condition allows, and q should hug the target’s shape. Move and widen the proposal; make it narrower than the target’s widest bump and no k can cover the tails.

\[ \begin{aligned} &z_0 \sim q, \quad u_0 \sim \mathcal U\big(0,\ k q(z_0)\big) \\ &\text{keep } z_0 \text{ if } u_0 \le \tilde p(z_0) \\ &k \ge \max_z \frac{\tilde p(z)}{q(z)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: propose a position from q and a height under the envelope; accept when the height is under the target curve. That happens with probability p̃(z0)/kq(z0), so regions where the envelope sits far above the target waste the most darts. The envelope must cover p̃ everywhere, so k is at least the largest ratio p̃/q.
Say it“Draw z-nought from q and u-nought up to k q of z-nought; keep z-nought if u-nought is under p-tilde.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(\text{accept}) &= \int \frac{\tilde p(z)}{k q(z)}\, q(z)\, dz \\ &= \frac{Z_p}{k} \\ p_D(\text{accept}) &= \Big(\frac{\sigma_p}{\sigma_q}\Big)^{D} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: average the acceptance probability over proposals: the q’s cancel and what is left is the target’s area over k. For a Gaussian target with spread σp in each of D dimensions and a Gaussian proposal of spread σq ≥ σp, the best k is (σq/σp)D times the target’s area, so the acceptance rate falls exponentially with D.
Say it“The acceptance rate is Z-p over k; in D dimensions, sigma-p over sigma-q to the power D.”

The curse of dimensionality

In one dimension, a proposal a little too wide costs little. In many dimensions the cost multiplies. Take a Gaussian target and a Gaussian proposal only slightly wider in each direction: the envelope must rise above the target by the ratio of the peak heights, which is the per-dimension ratio raised to the power D. Push D to a thousand with a proposal just 1% wider than the target.

PRML 11.1.3 · adaptive rejection sampling

An envelope that builds itself

Finding a tight envelope by hand is the hard part of rejection sampling. For one large family of targets it can be built automatically and improved as you go. A density is log-concave when ln p(z) is concave: it bends downward everywhere, like a Gaussian’s parabola. Then every tangent line to ln p lies above it. Take the tangents at a few points and the lowest of them at each z, and you have a piecewise-linear upper bound on ln p. Exponentiate it, and the envelope is a chain of exponential pieces, each of which can be sampled by the inverse CDF of the earlier section.

Propose from that envelope and accept or reject as before. When a point is rejected, the envelope was loose there, so add that point to the set of tangents: the envelope tightens exactly where it was worst, and the acceptance rate climbs toward 1. Gilks and Wild’s version also keeps a squeeze, the chords between neighbouring points, which lie below ln p; a proposal under the squeeze can be accepted without evaluating p at all, which matters when p is expensive. The target here is the Gumbel density, p̃(z) = exp(−z − e−z), whose area is exactly 1, so the envelope’s area is the expected number of proposals per kept sample. Propose a few points one at a time and watch where new tangents appear.

\[ \begin{aligned} u(z) &= \min_j \big[\, h(x_j) \\ &\qquad + h'(x_j)(z - x_j) \,\big] \\ h(z) &= \ln \tilde p(z) \le u(z) \\ \text{accept if } v &\le e^{\,h(z) - u(z)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: h is the log target; x1, …, xm are the current tangent points; u(z) is the lowest tangent at z, which can only lie above h when h is concave. Draw z from the density proportional to eu(z) and v uniformly on (0, 1); accept when v is below the ratio of the target to the envelope.
Say it“u of z is the lowest tangent of log p at z; accept with probability p over e to the u.”
\[ \begin{aligned} A_j &= \int_{z_{j-1}}^{z_j} e^{\,h_j + s_j (z - x_j)}\, dz \\ &= \frac{e^{\,h_j - s_j x_j}}{s_j}\big(e^{s_j z_j} - e^{s_j z_{j-1}}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: piece j of the envelope is the exponential of tangent j (height hj, slope sj), between the points zj−1 and zj where it meets its neighbours. Pick a piece with probability Aj/ΣA, then invert that piece’s CDF. The first piece must slope up and the last slope down, so that the end pieces, which run off to ±∞, have finite area.
Say it“A-j is the area of the j-th exponential piece of the envelope.”

Two limits are worth knowing. The construction needs log-concavity; for other targets a Metropolis–Hastings correction step can be bolted on (adaptive rejection Metropolis sampling), which is a taste of the next chapter. And it is a one-dimensional tool: in many dimensions any rejection sampler drowns in rejections, as the last section showed. Its main use is inside Gibbs sampling, which updates one variable at a time and so needs exactly this: fast exact draws from awkward one-dimensional conditionals.

PRML 11.1.4 · Deep Learning 17.2 · importance sampling

Sample the wrong thing, then reweight

Often you do not need samples from p at all; you need an expectation under p. Importance sampling draws from a convenient proposal q instead and corrects each draw with its importance weight p(z)/q(z): a point q over-produces gets a weight below 1, a point q under-produces a weight above 1. Nothing is thrown away. The estimate stays unbiased for any q that is positive wherever p f is non-zero, but its variance depends entirely on q.

The best possible proposal is q*(z) ∝ p(z)|f(z)|: sample where the integrand is big. When f does not change sign this gives every draw the same value, and the variance is zero (q* needs the answer to normalise it, so this is a target to aim at, not something you can use directly). The worst proposals have thinner tails than p: then a rare draw far out gets a gigantic weight and the variance can be infinite, while most runs look calm and wrong. If p is known only up to a constant, normalise the weights to sum to 1; this self-normalised estimator is slightly biased but consistent, and the same average of raw weights estimates the ratio of normalising constants. Estimate a tail probability: first with q = p, then move q out into the tail; then shrink its spread below 0.71 and watch the variance blow up.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_p[f] &= \int f(z) \frac{p(z)}{q(z)}\, q(z)\, dz \\ &\approx \frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L} r_l\, f\big(z^{(l)}\big) \\ r_l &= \frac{p(z^{(l)})}{q(z^{(l)})} \\ \operatorname{Var}[\hat s_q] &= \frac{1}{L}\Big(\mathbb{E}_q\Big[\frac{p^2 f^2}{q^2}\Big] - s^2\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: multiply and divide by q inside the integral, and the expectation under p becomes an expectation under q of f times the ratio p/q. Average that over L draws from q. The variance has the same 1/L as before, but the per-draw term now depends on q: it is smallest when p f/q is nearly constant.
Say it“Average f times the weight p over q, over draws from q.”
\[ \begin{aligned} q^*(z) &= \frac{p(z)\,|f(z)|}{\int p(z')\,|f(z')|\,dz'} \\ \frac{Z_p}{Z_q} &\approx \frac{1}{L}\sum_l \tilde r_l, \quad \tilde r_l = \frac{\tilde p(z^{(l)})}{\tilde q(z^{(l)})} \\ \mathbb{E}_p[f] &\approx \sum_l w_l\, f\big(z^{(l)}\big) \\ w_l &= \frac{\tilde r_l}{\sum_m \tilde r_m} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the zero-variance proposal follows p times |f|. With unnormalised p̃ and q̃, the average raw weight estimates the ratio of their normalising constants, and dividing by it gives weights wl that sum to 1. The effective sample size (Σr̃)²/Σr̃² says how many equally weighted draws they are worth.
Say it“Weights normalised to one: each w-l is its raw weight over the sum of all of them.”

Two warnings from the books, made concrete. First, a bad proposal fails quietly. With q far from where p f lives, most draws carry weights near zero and the estimate is near zero too, with a small sample variance that looks reassuring. Nothing in the samples says the answer is wrong. Second, importance sampling does not escape dimension. Each weight is a product of per-dimension ratios, so in high dimensions the weights spread over many orders of magnitude and a handful of draws carry almost all the weight; the effective sample size collapses just as the acceptance rate did for rejection sampling.

Deep Learning 12.4.3.3 · importance sampling for big softmaxes

A 10,000-word softmax on a budget

Here is importance sampling earning its keep inside deep learning. A neural language model scores every word i of a vocabulary with a logit ai and turns the scores into probabilities with a softmax. The gradient of the log-probability of the word y that actually came next splits into two parts: a positive phase that pushes up ay, cheap to compute, and a negative phase that pushes down every word in proportion to its probability. The negative phase is an expectation under the model’s own distribution over the whole vocabulary, so computing it exactly touches all V output units for every training word.

Bengio and Senécal’s idea was to estimate that expectation by importance sampling. Draw m words from a cheap proposal, such as the unigram distribution (how often each word appears in the corpus), and weight them by eai/qi. The model’s normaliser is the expensive part, so the weights are normalised to sum to 1: the self-normalised, slightly biased estimator from the last section. The proposal matters: the unigram distribution already puts most of its mass on the words the model rates highly. The vocabulary below is synthetic: 10,000 “words” with Zipf frequencies, logits equal to the log-frequency plus a context effect whose size you set, and one gradient coordinate gi per word. Raise the context strength and see the unigram proposal’s advantage shrink.

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \log P(y \mid C)}{\partial \theta} &= \frac{\partial a_y}{\partial \theta} \\ &\quad - \sum_{i=1}^{V} P(i \mid C)\, \frac{\partial a_i}{\partial \theta} \\ P(i \mid C) &= \frac{e^{a_i}}{\sum_{j} e^{a_j}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: C is the context, y the word that came next, θ the network’s parameters. The first term needs only the logit of the right word. The second is the expectation of the gradient of a logit, taken under the model’s own next-word distribution, and is a sum over all V words.
Say it“Gradient of the log-probability: push up the right word’s logit, push down every word’s logit weighted by its probability.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_i P(i \mid C)\, g_i &\approx \sum_{k=1}^{m} w_k\, g_{n_k} \\ n_k &\sim q \\ w_k &= \frac{e^{a_{n_k}}/q_{n_k}}{\sum_{k'} e^{a_{n_{k'}}}/q_{n_{k'}}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: gi stands for ∂ai/∂θ. Draw m word indices nk from the proposal q, weight each by its unnormalised model probability over its proposal probability, normalise the weights, and average. The cost is m output units instead of V.
Say it“The negative phase is about a weighted average of g over m words drawn from q.”

AIMA 13.4, 13.4.1 · Deep Learning 16.3 · PRML 11.1, 11.1.4

Sampling a Bayes net: prior, rejection and likelihood weighting

A directed graphical model comes with a built-in sampler. Visit the nodes in topological order, parents before children, and draw each one from its conditional distribution given the values its parents just received. One pass gives one joint sample, exactly distributed as the model says, at a cost linear in the number of nodes. This is ancestral sampling (AIMA calls it prior sampling), and the fraction of samples in which an event occurs is a consistent estimate of its probability.

Queries come with evidence: what is the probability of a storm, given that the gauge alarm went off? The blunt fix is rejection sampling (also called logic sampling): sample as before and throw away every sample that disagrees with the evidence. It is correct, but the share of samples kept is P(evidence), which falls exponentially as evidence piles up. Likelihood weighting never wastes a sample: it fixes the evidence variables at their observed values, samples only the others, and weights each sample by how likely the evidence was given its sampled parents. It is importance sampling with the network minus its evidence as the proposal. Its weakness shows when evidence sits at the bottom of the network: the upstream variables are sampled ignoring it, and a few samples carry nearly all the weight. The network and its probabilities are a made-up flood example, not a fitted model. Click nodes to set evidence (unknown → true → false), pick a query, and compare the two estimators with the exact answer.

\[ \begin{aligned} S_{PS}(\mathbf x) &= \prod_{i=1}^{n} P\big(x_i \mid \text{pa}(X_i)\big) \\ \hat P(X = x \mid e) &= \frac{N(x, e)}{N(e)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: pa(Xi) are the parents of Xi. Ancestral sampling produces each full assignment x = (x1, …, xn) with exactly its joint probability under the network. Rejection sampling estimates the conditional by counting: among the N(e) samples that agree with the evidence, the fraction that also have X = x. Of N samples, about N·P(e) survive.
Say it“The estimate of x given e is the count of samples with both, over the count with e.”
\[ \begin{aligned} w(\mathbf z, \mathbf e) &= \prod_{j} P\big(e_j \mid \text{pa}(E_j)\big) \\ \hat P(X = x \mid e) &= \frac{\sum_{s:\, x_s = x} w_s}{\sum_{s} w_s} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: z are the sampled non-evidence variables, each drawn given its parents; e are the fixed evidence values. The weight multiplies the probabilities of the evidence values given their parents, which is exactly the ratio of the true joint to the probability of the sampling distribution. The estimate is a self-normalised weighted count.
Say it“Each sample’s weight is the product of the evidence probabilities given their parents.”

Ancestral sampling has limits, and Goodfellow et al. list them. It works only for directed models; it gives samples from the joint, and conditioning on evidence is exactly what it cannot do cheaply, hence the two fixes above. An undirected model has no topological order to follow: its variables depend on each other in loops, so there is no first variable to sample. Converting it to a directed model is usually intractable, and the general answer is a Markov chain that repeatedly resamples variables given their neighbours, which is the subject of the next chapter.

PRML 11.1.6 · sampling and the EM algorithm

EM with a sampled E-step

The EM algorithm alternates two steps. The E-step works out the posterior over the hidden variables Z under the current parameters; the M-step maximises the expected complete-data log-likelihood Q(θ, θold), an integral over that posterior. When the integral is intractable, replace it with a Monte Carlo average over L samples of Z drawn from the posterior. That is Monte Carlo EM. With L = 1 it is stochastic EM: one sampled completion of the data per iteration, and the parameters wander around the optimum instead of settling.

Going fully Bayesian gives the IP algorithm (data augmentation, Tanner and Wong): instead of maximising, the imputation step draws parameters from the current posterior approximation and then hidden variables given those parameters; the posterior step rebuilds the posterior over θ as an average over the imputed data sets. With one imputation per round, it simply alternates a draw of Z given θ and a draw of θ given Z, which is Gibbs sampling. The model here is a two-component Gaussian mixture with unit variances and equal weights, fitted to 150 synthetic points, so the only parameters are the two means and the log-likelihood can be drawn as a landscape. Drag the start point, pick a method and run it.

\[ \begin{aligned} Q(\theta, \theta^{\text{old}}) &= \int p(Z \mid X, \theta^{\text{old}}) \\ &\qquad \times \ln p(Z, X \mid \theta)\, dZ \\ &\approx \frac{1}{L} \sum_{l=1}^{L} \ln p\big(Z^{(l)}, X \mid \theta\big) \\ Z^{(l)} &\sim p(Z \mid X, \theta^{\text{old}}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the M-step objective is an expectation over the hidden labels. Draw L complete labellings of the data from their posterior and average the complete-data log-likelihoods. For the mixture, the maximiser is then each mean set to the average of the points assigned to it, pooled over the L labellings.
Say it“Q is approximately the average complete-data log-likelihood over L sampled labellings.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{I-step: } & \theta^{(l)} \sim p(\theta \mid X) \\ & Z^{(l)} \sim p(Z \mid \theta^{(l)}, X) \\ \text{P-step: } & p(\theta \mid X) \\ &\quad \approx \frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L} p\big(\theta \mid Z^{(l)}, X\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: impute hidden data from parameters drawn from the current posterior, then update the posterior as the average of the complete-data posteriors. Here, with a flat prior, each mean’s complete-data posterior is a Gaussian centred on the average of its assigned points, with variance 1 over their count.
Say it“Impute Z from a sampled theta, then average the posteriors given each imputed Z.”

Real-world project

Project: how rare is a flood bigger than any on record?

The U.S. Geological Survey publishes the largest flow of each water year at its stream gauges. This page embeds the complete annual-peak record of gauge 01646500, listed by USGS as “POTOMAC RIVER NEAR WASH, DC LITTLE FALLS PUMP STA”: 96 water years, 1931 to 2026, in cubic feet per second (cfs). It was retrieved from the USGS National Water Information System on 2026-11-19; USGS data are in the public domain, and the most recent values may still be provisional. The record’s highest peak is 484,000 cfs on 1936-03-19, and the file notes it as the highest since 1889.

Flood planning asks about events rarer than anything in the record: the flow exceeded once in 500 or 10,000 years on average. That needs a model. We fit two textbook flood-frequency distributions to the 96 peaks by matching their mean and spread: a Gumbel for the flows, and a lognormal (a Gaussian for the log-flows). Then we estimate P(annual peak > t) by plain Monte Carlo and by importance sampling, with the proposal moved out to the threshold. Both models have exact tail formulas, which is the point: they let us grade the two estimators. The same importance-sampling code works unchanged for models with no formula at all.

2 · Estimate a rare exceedance

\[ \begin{aligned} p_t &= P(Q > t) = \mathbb{E}_p\big[\mathbb 1(Q > t)\big] \\ \hat p_{\text{MC}} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb 1(Q_i > t), \ \ Q_i \sim p \\ \hat p_{\text{IS}} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb 1(Q_i > t)\, \frac{p(Q_i)}{q(Q_i)} \\ Q_i &\sim q \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the exceedance probability is the expectation of an indicator. Plain Monte Carlo counts hits among draws from the fitted model; importance sampling draws from a proposal q, the same family shifted so that its centre sits at the threshold, and weights each hit. Draws are made by the inverse CDF (Gumbel) or Box–Muller (lognormal).
Say it“p-hat-IS averages the indicator of exceeding t, times the weight p over q, over draws from q.”
\[ \begin{aligned} n_{\text{MC}} &= \frac{1 - p_t}{p_t\, \varepsilon^2} \\ n_{\text{IS}} &= \frac{\mathbb{E}_q\big[\mathbb 1(Q>t)\, (p/q)^2\big] - p_t^2}{p_t^2\, \varepsilon^2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the number of draws each method needs for a relative standard error ε (here 10%). For plain Monte Carlo the variance of one indicator is pt(1 − pt), so the cost grows like 1/pt: rarer events need proportionally more draws. Importance sampling replaces that variance with its own, computed exactly here, which for a well-placed proposal grows far more slowly.
Say it“Plain Monte Carlo needs about one over p epsilon-squared draws.”

The same computation in Python

The script fits both models, computes the exact tails with SciPy, and reruns both estimators with the page’s own random-number generator (mulberry32, with the same Box–Muller and inverse-CDF steps), so the estimates can be compared digit for digit. It also checks the exact importance-sampling variance against numerical integration.

import json, sys, math
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats, integrate
S = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "./"
M32 = 0xFFFFFFFF
def rng(a):                                   # mulberry32, as on the page
    st = [a & M32]
    def r():
        st[0] = (st[0] + 0x6D2B79F5) & M32; a = st[0]
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & M32
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & M32)) & M32) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M32) / 4294967296
    return r
def gauss(r):                                 # Box-Muller, as on the page
    u = 0.0
    while u == 0.0: u = r()
    return math.sqrt(-2 * math.log(u)) * math.cos(2 * math.pi * r())

rows = json.load(open(S + "x110-peaks.json"))["rows"]
Q = np.array([q for _, _, q in rows], float); n = len(Q)
s = Q.std(ddof=1); beta = s * math.sqrt(6) / math.pi; mu = Q.mean() - np.euler_gamma * beta
ly = np.log(Q); m, sg = ly.mean(), ly.std(ddof=1)
print(f"{n} water years {rows[0][0]}-{rows[-1][0]}; mean {Q.mean():.1f} cfs, sd {s:.1f}, max {Q.max():.0f} on {rows[int(Q.argmax())][1]}")
print(f"Gumbel (moments): location {mu:.2f}, scale {beta:.2f};  scipy ML fit: {stats.gumbel_r.fit(Q)[0]:.2f}, {stats.gumbel_r.fit(Q)[1]:.2f}")
print(f"lognormal: mean of ln Q {m:.6f}, sd {sg:.6f}")
print(f"years above 300,000 cfs: {int((Q > 3e5).sum())} observed; expected {n * stats.gumbel_r.sf(3e5, mu, beta):.2f} (Gumbel), {n * stats.norm.sf((math.log(3e5) - m) / sg):.2f} (lognormal)")

def exact(model, t):
    return stats.gumbel_r.sf(t, mu, beta) if model == "g" else stats.norm.sf((math.log(t) - m) / sg)
def centre(model, t, k):                      # proposal centre: k = 0 is the model itself, k = 1 sits at t
    return mu + k * (t - mu) if model == "g" else m + k * (math.log(t) - m)
def draw(model, r, c):                        # one draw from the family with centre c; returns x on the model's scale
    if model == "g":
        u = 0.0
        while u == 0.0: u = r()
        return c - beta * math.log(-math.log(u))
    return c + sg * gauss(r)
def logw(model, x, c):                        # log p(x) - log q(x)
    if model == "g":
        zp, zq = (x - mu) / beta, (x - c) / beta
        return -(zp + math.exp(-zp)) + (zq + math.exp(-zq))
    return (-(x - m) ** 2 + (x - c) ** 2) / (2 * sg * sg)
def estimate(model, t, nn, k, seed):
    r = rng(seed); c = centre(model, t, k); th = t if model == "g" else math.log(t); s1 = s2 = 0.0; hits = 0
    for _ in range(nn):
        x = draw(model, r, c)
        if x > th:
            w = math.exp(logw(model, x, c)) if k else 1.0; hits += 1; s1 += w; s2 += w * w
    est = s1 / nn; se = math.sqrt(max(0.0, s2 / nn - est * est) / nn)
    return est, se, hits
def is_var(model, t, k):                      # exact per-draw variance of the IS estimator
    c = centre(model, t, k); p = exact(model, t)
    if model == "l":
        lt = math.log(t); e2 = math.exp((m - c) ** 2 / sg ** 2) * stats.norm.sf((lt - 2 * m + c) / sg)
        num = integrate.quad(lambda y: math.exp(2 * stats.norm.logpdf(y, m, sg) - stats.norm.logpdf(y, c, sg)), lt, lt + 40 * sg)[0]
        return e2 - p * p, num - p * p
    f = lambda x: stats.gumbel_r.pdf(x, mu, beta) ** 2 / stats.gumbel_r.pdf(x, c, beta)
    num = integrate.quad(f, t, t + 60 * beta, limit=200)[0]
    return num - p * p, num - p * p

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A Monte Carlo estimate replaces an expectation by an average over samples: it is unbiased, and its error shrinks like √(Var[f]/n) whatever the dimension; everything else in this chapter is about getting samples and shrinking Var[f].
  • Uniforms become any one-dimensional distribution through the inverse CDF; Gaussians come two at a time from Box–Muller, and correlated ones from y = μ + Lz with Σ = LL⊤.
  • Rejection sampling needs only an unnormalised target and an envelope kq ≥ p̃; it keeps Zp/k of its proposals, which collapses exponentially with dimension. For log-concave targets, adaptive rejection builds and tightens the envelope from tangents automatically.
  • Importance sampling draws from q and reweights by p/q; the ideal q follows p|f|, a thin-tailed q can give infinite variance, and self-normalised weights work with unknown constants. It powers sampled softmaxes and rare-event estimates like the flood tails.
  • In a Bayes net, ancestral sampling is exact and cheap; rejection wastes all but P(e) of its samples, likelihood weighting fixes the evidence and weights instead. Inside EM, sampled E-steps give Monte Carlo EM, stochastic EM and, drawing the parameters too, the IP algorithm.

Check your understanding

A Monte Carlo estimate with 100 samples has standard error 0.04. Roughly how many samples would give 0.01?

The standard error falls like 1/√n, so a four-times-smaller error needs sixteen times the samples: about 1,600. That is the honest weakness of plain Monte Carlo: every extra decimal place costs a hundred times more work.

Why does the inverse-CDF method fail for a Gaussian in practice, and what is used instead?

It does not fail in principle, but the Gaussian CDF has no closed-form inverse, so each draw would need a numerical root-find or a special-function approximation. Box–Muller makes two exact Gaussians from two uniforms with a logarithm, a square root and (in the polar form) a rejection step that keeps π/4 of the points.

In rejection sampling, what goes wrong if kq(z) dips below p̃(z) somewhere?

The accepted points are then spread evenly under min(p̃, kq), not under p̃, so the region where the envelope is too low is under-sampled and the draws no longer come from p. No warning appears; the samples are simply biased. That is why a proposal with thinner tails than the target cannot be used at all.

An importance-sampling estimate has a small sample variance across its weights. Does that mean it is accurate?

No. If q almost never visits the region where p f is large, every draw has a tiny weight and the estimate and its sample variance are both small and wrong. The variance estimate is itself an average over the same draws, so it misses the same region. Check the effective sample size, try several proposals, and prefer proposals with heavier tails than the target.

Why does likelihood weighting struggle when the evidence is a leaf far below the query, and rejection sampling struggle when the evidence is unlikely?

Likelihood weighting samples the upstream variables from their priors, ignoring evidence downstream, so most samples have upstream values that make the evidence improbable and get tiny weights; a few samples dominate. Rejection sampling keeps only the fraction P(e) of its samples, so rare evidence leaves almost nothing to count. Both are symptoms of the same mismatch between the sampling distribution and the posterior.
