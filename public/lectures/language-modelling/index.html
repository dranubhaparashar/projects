Language Modelling

Predicting the next word, one probability at a time

Every large language model is, at heart, a machine that answers one question over and over: given the words so far, how likely is each word to come next? Answer it well and you can autocomplete, score, compare and write text. This chapter builds such machines from nothing but counting, using the 115,000 words of Pride and Prejudice, then shows why networks took over. On the right, type the start of a sentence, or click a word to accept it: the bars are the model’s bets on what Austen would write next.

The task

Guess the next word

In 1951 Claude Shannon measured how predictable English is by asking people to guess a text one letter at a time. A language model plays the same game, with one difference: it never commits to a single guess. For every word in its vocabulary it states a probability, and those probabilities add up to one. Below, you play against a model that has read 49 of the novel’s 61 chapters, on sentences from chapters it has never seen. It offers its six favourite words, in alphabetical order. Pick the word you think Austen wrote next, then see what probability the model gave to the truth.

\[ \begin{gathered} P(w \mid h) \ \ge\ 0 \\ \sum_{w \in V} P(w \mid h) = 1 \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: h is the history, the words so far; w is any word in the vocabulary V (here 3,578 entries: words, punctuation marks, and one ‘rare word’ token). A language model is a whole probability distribution over V for every possible history, not a single prediction. Good models put most of the probability on words that really come next.
Say it“The probability of w given h is never negative, and summed over every word in the vocabulary it comes to one.”

Whole sentences

A sentence is a chain of guesses

Next-word probabilities are enough to give a probability to a whole sentence. The chain rule of probability says so exactly, with no approximation: the chance of the sentence is the chance of its first word, times the chance of the second given the first, and so on. The product of forty numbers below one is unimaginably small, so we add logarithms instead. Base-2 logs give bits: a word with probability ½ costs 1 bit of surprise, ¼ costs 2 bits. Below, the model scores Austen’s opening line. Now shuffle the same words, or type a sentence of your own.

\[ \begin{aligned} P(w_{1:N}) &= \prod_{t=1}^{N} P(w_t \mid h_t) \\ \log_2 P(w_{1:N}) &= \sum_{t=1}^{N} \log_2 P(w_t \mid h_t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: w1:N is the whole sentence; the history ht = w1:t−1 is everything before position t. The first line is true for any probability distribution over sequences; it just takes the sentence one word at a time. The second line takes logs, turning the product into a sum. Each term −log₂ P is that word’s surprise in bits; a sentence’s total surprise is the sum of its words’.
Say it“The probability of the sentence is the product, over t, of the probability of word t given all the words before it. Its log is the sum of the logs.”

The Markov assumption

n-grams: remember only the last few words

To estimate P(w | everything before it) by counting, we would need to have seen the whole history before, and almost every long history is new. Andrey Markov’s way out, first used on the letters of Pushkin’s Eugene Onegin in 1913, is to forget all but the most recent part. An n-gram model keeps only the last n−1 words: a bigram (n = 2) looks at one word, a trigram at two. The probabilities then come straight from counts: of all the times the history appeared in the book, what fraction were followed by w? That is the maximum-likelihood estimate. Lengthen the history and watch the counts thin out.

\[ \begin{aligned} &P(w_t \mid w_{1:t-1}) \\ &\quad\approx\ P(w_t \mid w_{t-n+1:t-1}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: pretend the next word depends only on the previous n−1 words. It is a deliberate approximation: it is what makes counting possible, and also what stops an n-gram model from ever tracking a topic across a paragraph.
Say it“The probability of the next word given everything is approximated by its probability given just the last n-minus-one words.”
\[ P_{\text{MLE}}(w \mid h) = \frac{c(h, w)}{c(h)} \]
Read it as: c(h, w) is how many times history h was followed by w in the training text; c(h) is how many times h appeared before anything at all. At the start of a sentence the history is padded with a start marker <s>.
Say it“The estimate is the count of h followed by w, over the count of h.”

Generation

Writing with an n-gram

A language model can write: draw the next word from its distribution, append it, and repeat. Shannon did exactly this by hand in 1948, with a book in place of a computer: open it at random, find the current word, and copy whatever word follows it. A unigram model writes word salad with the right word frequencies; a bigram gets local grammar; a trigram produces plausible phrases. Raise n to 4, and look at how much of the ‘new’ text is copied straight from the book.

\[ w_t \;\sim\; P_{\text{MLE}}\big(\,\cdot \mid w_{t-n+1}, \dots, w_{t-1}\big) \]
Read it as: the tilde means “is drawn at random from”. Each word is a weighted lottery among the words that followed the current history in the book, with weights equal to their counts. A sentence ends when a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark is drawn.
Say it“Word t is sampled from the model’s distribution given the previous n-minus-one words.”

Underlined words sit in a run of six or more words that appears, word for word, in the training chapters. ‘‹rare›’ is the token for words seen only once.

The yardstick

Perplexity: how torn is the model?

To compare models, score them on text they have never seen. The average surprise per word, in bits, is the model’s cross-entropy on that text. Two to the power of it is the perplexity: the number of equally likely words the model is, on average, choosing between. A model that knew nothing and spread its bets evenly over all 3,578 entries would have perplexity 3,578; a model that always knew the next word would have perplexity 1. Lower is better. Switch models, and watch what one unseen word pair does to the counting models.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{PP} &= P(w_1, \dots, w_N)^{-1/N} = 2^{\,H} \\ H &= -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N} \log_2 P(w_t \mid h_t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: take the probability the model gave the whole held-out text, and undo the product: the N-th root gives the probability of a ‘typical’ word, and its inverse is the perplexity. Equivalently, average the surprise H (bits per word) and raise 2 to it. If a model spreads its probability evenly over k words every time, H = log₂ k and PP = k exactly.
Say it“Perplexity is the probability of the text to the power minus one over N; equivalently, two to the average surprise in bits.”

The zero problem

Zeros, and add-k smoothing

Language is full of rare events. In the six validation chapters, more than a fifth of the word pairs never occur in the 49 training chapters, and most triples are new. A maximum-likelihood model gives every one of them probability zero, so a single new pair makes the whole text impossible. Smoothing takes a little probability from what was seen and gives it to what was not. The oldest recipe is Laplace’s: pretend every possible pair was seen once more than it was. Add-k generalises it to a fraction k. Slide k and find the best amount on the validation chapters.

\[ P_{\text{add-}k}(w \mid h) = \frac{c(h, w) + k}{c(h) + k\,|V|} \]
Read it as: add k imaginary sightings to every one of the |V| possible next words, so the denominator grows by k|V|. With k = 1 (Laplace) and |V| = 3,578, a history seen 20 times gets 3,598 imaginary-plus-real sightings, and the real ones are swamped. A small k steals less. The best k is chosen on validation text, never on the test.
Say it“Add-k probability is the count of h followed by w plus k, over the count of h plus k times the vocabulary size.”

The best way to count

Kneser–Ney: discount, interpolate, count contexts

Add-k is a blunt instrument: it hands the same extra mass to every unseen word, whether it is ‘the’ or ‘Pemberley’. For decades the strongest counting method has been Kneser–Ney smoothing (Kneser & Ney, 1995; Chen & Goodman, 1999). It rests on two ideas. First, absolute discounting: subtract a fixed amount D from every seen count, and pass the freed probability down to a shorter history, so a trigram model leans on bigrams, and bigrams on single words. Second, continuation counts: when the shorter model is used, it is because the longer history was unhelpful, so it should ask not “how common is w?” but “after how many different words does w appear?”. ‘Bourgh’ appears 33 times, always after ‘de’; it is a terrible guess for a new context. Slide the discount, then switch the continuation counts off.

\[ \begin{aligned} P_{\text{KN}}(w \mid h) &= \frac{\max\big(c(h,w) - D,\ 0\big)}{c(h)} \\ &\quad + \lambda(h)\, P_{\text{KN}}(w \mid h') \\ \lambda(h) &= \frac{D\, N_{1+}(h\,\bullet)}{c(h)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: h′ is h without its oldest word. Each of the N1+(h •) different words seen after h gives up D, and the pooled mass is spread according to the shorter model. λ(h) is the share of probability freed by discounting. The recursion ends with a uniform distribution over V, so no word ever gets zero. At the top level c is the ordinary count; below it, the continuation count.
Say it“The discounted count over the history count, plus lambda of h times the shorter model; lambda is D times the number of different followers, over the history count.”
\[ P_{\text{cont}}(w) = \frac{N_{1+}(\bullet\, w)}{N_{1+}(\bullet\,\bullet)} \]
Read it as: N1+(• w) is the number of different words that come just before w in the training text; the denominator is the same total over all words (the number of different word pairs). The lower levels of a Kneser–Ney model use these counts in place of c. The discount D = n₁ / (n₁ + 2n₂) comes from how many n-grams were seen once (n₁) and twice (n₂): Ney’s estimate, with nothing to tune.
Say it“The continuation probability of w is the number of different words that precede w, over the number of different word pairs.”

What came next

From counts to networks

A table of counts has one row per history. Write that table as a network instead: feed in the previous symbol as a one-hot vector, multiply by a weight matrix W to get a score for every next symbol, and turn the scores into probabilities with a softmax. Train W by gradient descent on the average surprise and something neat happens: it rediscovers the count ratios exactly. Below, the same book as a stream of 28 characters (letters, space and full stop), so the whole 29 × 28 matrix fits on screen; the extra row is the start of a chapter. Press Train, then add weight decay: penalising large weights pulls every row towards uniform, which is what smoothing does.

\[ \begin{aligned} P(b \mid a) &= \frac{e^{W_{ab}}}{\sum_{c} e^{W_{ac}}} \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{ab}} &= \frac{c(a)\,P(b \mid a) - c(a, b)}{N} \\ &\quad + \lambda W_{ab} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: row a of W holds the scores after symbol a. L is the average surprise (in nats) over all N training pairs, plus a weight-decay penalty λ⁄2 times the sum of squared weights. With λ = 0 the gradient is zero exactly when P(b | a) = c(a, b)/c(a): the network’s optimum is the count model.
Say it“P of b given a is the softmax of row a. The gradient is c-of-a times the predicted probability minus the actual count, over N, plus lambda W.”
\[ \begin{aligned} x &= [\,e_{t-n+1}; \dots; e_{t-1}\,] \\ z &= \tanh(W x + b) \\ P(\cdot \mid h) &= \operatorname{softmax}(U z + d) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Bengio et al. (2003): look up a learned vector e (an embedding) for each word of the history h, stack them into x, pass x through a hidden layer z, and score every next word. Similar words get similar vectors, so evidence about ‘the cat sat’ helps with ‘a dog sat’. Counts can’t share like that, and the size of the network grows only linearly with the history length.
Say it“x stacks the embeddings; z is tanh of W x plus b; the next-word distribution is the softmax of U z plus d.”

Why networks won

For characters, a count table with one character of history has 29 rows. With nine characters of history the training chapters already contain 293,502 different histories, each needing its own row, and almost all of them seen only a handful of times. A network that embeds each character and shares one hidden layer grows by a fixed amount per extra character. At this book’s scale, a well-smoothed table is still hard to beat; with billions of words, networks win decisively. On the One Billion Word benchmark, the best Kneser–Ney 5-gram scores a perplexity of 67.6 (Chelba et al., 2013) and a large LSTM 30.0 (Jozefowicz et al., 2016). Chapter 2.5 built such recurrent networks; chapter 4.3 builds the transformer that replaced them.

Real-world project

Project: a Kneser–Ney model of Pride and Prejudice

The text is Jane Austen’s novel (1813) as published by Project Gutenberg (eBook #1342, the 1894 George Allen edition). We removed the illustration captions, lower-cased everything, and split it into words and the punctuation marks , ; : . ! ? (quotation marks and dashes dropped; ‘Mr.’ becomes ‘mr’). That leaves 139,434 tokens in 61 chapters. Every tenth chapter, 10, 20, …, 60, is sealed as the test set (12,024 tokens); chapters 5, 15, …, 55 are for validation (12,815 tokens); the other 49 chapters (114,595 tokens) are for training. Words seen only once in training become <unk>, giving a vocabulary of 3,578. Every number below is computed in your browser from those counts, and checked against an independent Python program.

\[ P_T(w \mid h) = \frac{P(w \mid h)^{1/T}}{\sum_{v \in V} P(v \mid h)^{1/T}} \]
Read it as: T = 1 samples from the model as it is. As T falls towards 0 the most likely word takes all the probability (greedy decoding); large T flattens the distribution towards uniform. Chapter 4.5 treats decoding properly.

The same models in Python

An independent reference in plain Python, using dictionaries of counters where the page uses typed arrays and maps. It reads the same 61 tokenised chapters, chooses k on the validation chapters from the same grid of 41 values, and prints validation and test perplexity. Its output is below, and its test column is repeated in the table above.

import json, math, collections
D = json.load(open("chapters.json"))                      # 61 chapters, tokenised like the page
def sentences(which):
    out = []
    for k, ch in enumerate(D["chapters"]):
        if D["split"][k] != which: continue
        cur = []
        for w in ch:
            cur.append(w)
            if w in ".!?": out.append(cur); cur = []
        if cur: out.append(cur)
    return out
train, val, test = sentences("train"), sentences("val"), sentences("test")
freq = collections.Counter(w for s in train for w in s)
V = {w for w in freq if freq[w] >= 2} | {"<unk>"}         # words seen once become <unk>
unk = lambda S: [[w if w in V else "<unk>" for w in s] for s in S]
train, val, test = unk(train), unk(val), unk(test)

def counts(n):                                           # c[h][w] for every (n-1)-word history h
    c = collections.defaultdict(collections.Counter)
    for s in train:
        p = ["<s>"] * (n - 1) + s
        for i in range(n - 1, len(p)): c[tuple(p[i - n + 1:i])][p[i]] += 1
    return c

def add_k(n, k):
    c = counts(n)
    return lambda h, w: (c[h][w] + k) / (sum(c[h].values()) + k * len(V)) if h in c else 1 / len(V)

def kneser_ney(n):
    lev = {n: counts(n)}
    for o in range(n - 1, 0, -1):                        # continuation counts N1+(. h w)
        lev[o] = collections.defaultdict(collections.Counter)
        for h, ws in lev[o + 1].items():
            for w in ws: lev[o][h[1:]][w] += 1
    tot = {o: {h: sum(ws.values()) for h, ws in lev[o].items()} for o in lev}
    Dis = {}
    for o in lev:                                        # Ney's discount n1 / (n1 + 2 n2)
        cc = collections.Counter(v for ws in lev[o].values() for v in ws.values())
        Dis[o] = cc[1] / (cc[1] + 2 * cc[2])
    def p(h, w, o=n):
        lower = p(h, w, o - 1) if o > 1 else 1 / len(V)
        h = h[len(h) - o + 1:] if o > 1 else ()
        if h not in lev[o]: return lower
        c, d = tot[o][h], Dis[o]
        return max(lev[o][h][w] - d, 0) / c + d * len(lev[o][h]) / c * lower
    return p

def perplexity(p, n, S):
    lp, N = 0.0, 0
    for s in S:
        q = ["<s>"] * (n - 1) + s
        for i in range(n - 1, len(q)):
            pr = p(tuple(q[i - n + 1:i]), q[i]); N += 1
            if pr == 0: return math.inf
            lp += math.log(pr)
    return math.exp(-lp / N)

uni = counts(1)[()]; N1 = sum(uni.values())
print(f"|V| = {len(V)}; tokens: train {sum(map(len, train))}, validation {sum(map(len, val))}, test {sum(map(len, test))}")
print(f"{'model':34s} {'validation':>10s} {'test':>10s}")
row = lambda name, p, n: print(f"{name:34s} {perplexity(p, n, val):10.4f} {perplexity(p, n, test):10.4f}")
row("uniform", lambda h, w: 1 / len(V), 1)
row("unigram (MLE)", lambda h, w: uni[w] / N1, 1)
c2 = counts(2); row("bigram (MLE)", lambda h, w: c2[h][w] / sum(c2[h].values()) if h in c2 else 0, 2)
row("bigram add-1", add_k(2, 1), 2)
grid = [10 ** (-4 + 0.1 * i) for i in range(41)]       # k = 0.0001 ... 1, chosen on validation
for n in (2, 3, 4):
    k = min(grid, key=lambda k: perplexity(add_k(n, k), n, val))
    row(f"{n}-gram add-k (k = {k:.4g})", add_k(n, k), n)
for n in (2, 3, 4):
    row(f"{n}-gram Kneser-Ney", kneser_ney(n), n)

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A language model gives a probability to every possible next word; by the chain rule, that is enough to score, compare and generate whole texts.
  • An n-gram model keeps only the last n−1 words and estimates probabilities by counting: c(h, w)/c(h).
  • Perplexity, 2 to the average surprise in bits on held-out text, is the number of words the model is effectively torn between. Lower is better; a single zero makes it infinite.
  • Most n-grams in new text were never seen, so smoothing is essential. Kneser–Ney (discount, interpolate, continuation counts) beats add-k by a wide margin: 106 against 177 on Austen’s test chapters.
  • A softmax network trained on the same loss rediscovers the counts; with embeddings it shares evidence between similar histories and grows gracefully with context. That is the road to today’s LLMs.

Check your understanding

A model has perplexity 50 on some text. What does that mean in plain words?

On average, it is as uncertain about each next word as if it were choosing uniformly among 50 words. Equivalently, it spends log₂ 50 ≈ 5.6 bits of surprise per word.

Your 4-gram has lower perplexity than your trigram on the training text, but higher on held-out text. What is happening, and what would you change?

Overfitting: the 4-gram has memorised long, rare histories that don’t recur. Smooth more (or better, e.g. Kneser–Ney with interpolation), get more data, or use the trigram. Always compare on held-out text.

Why does an unsmoothed bigram model have infinite perplexity on almost any new chapter?

New text contains word pairs never seen in training; maximum likelihood gives them probability 0, the product over the text is 0, and its inverse N-th root is infinite.

‘Francisco’ is a common word in a newspaper corpus but almost always follows ‘San’. How does Kneser–Ney treat it when backing off from an unseen bigram ‘the Francisco’?

Its lower-order probability uses the continuation count: the number of different words before it, which is tiny. So it gets little backed-off probability, unlike add-k or a raw unigram, which would rate it as common.

Can perplexities of two models be compared if they use different vocabularies or tokenisers?

Not directly. Perplexity is per token, and mapping rare words to <unk> or splitting words into pieces changes both the number of tokens and what is predicted. Compare on the same tokens, or convert to bits per character of the original text.
