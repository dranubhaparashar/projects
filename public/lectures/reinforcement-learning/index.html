Foundations

Learning from interaction, not from labels

Supervised learning is a classroom: for every input, someone hands you the right answer. Reinforcement learning is life: you act, something happens, and all you get is a score. Two things make this genuinely hard:

Delayed consequences

Actions pay off many steps later. Which of the forty moves won the chess game? Assigning credit backwards through time is the central puzzle.

The agent shapes its own data

What the agent does determines what it sees next. Its data is not a fixed dataset but a consequence of its own behaviour — explore differently, learn different things.

Three paradigms of machine learning

Supervised

Learn x → y from labelled pairs

Feedback: the correct answer. I.i.d. data, one-shot prediction. Full lecture on this paradigm — LDA & QDA live there too.

Unsupervised

Find structure in unlabelled data

Feedback: none. Clustering, density estimation, dimensionality reduction.

Reinforcement

Learn behaviour from interaction

Feedback: a scalar reward, possibly delayed. Sequential, non-i.i.d., and the agent influences its own data.

Instructive vs evaluative feedback: supervision tells you the right action; reward only scores the one you took. That single difference forces everything that follows.

Rewards: one scalar to rule the objective

The reward hypothesis (Sutton & Barto): all goals can be described as maximisation of expected cumulative reward. It sounds too simple. In practice it is how every RL system is specified:

Chess / Go

+1 win, −1 loss

0 otherwise — nothing about moves or style. AlphaGo was told only this.

Robot locomotion

+ forward velocity

− energy used, − falling over.

Recommender

+ long-term retention

+ engagement, − churn.

LLM assistant (RLHF)

Preference score

A reward model's rating of a human-preferred response.

Reward defines what to achieve — never how to achieve it. The how is the agent's problem; that is the whole point.

Simplest RL

Warm-up: the k-armed bandit

Strip away states and transitions and one hard problem remains: k slot machines, each with an unknown average payout \(q_*(a)\). You have a limited budget of pulls. Earn as much as you can.

\[ q_*(a) = \mathbb{E}\left[ R_t \mid A_t = a \right], \qquad \text{maximise } \sum_{t=1}^{T} R_t \]
Read it as: each arm has a true average payout the agent never sees. The sub-goal is to estimate \(q_*(a)\) while still earning reward — estimation and control at once. This isolates the exploration-vs-exploitation dilemma, and it runs daily in A/B tests, ad serving and trial design.

Your turn: beat the bandit

You get 20 pulls across 4 machines. Payouts are noisy. Earn as much as you can, then reveal the truth and see your regret — then let ε-greedy play the same machines on the same budget. Who wins?

Regret = what the best arm would have paid, minus what your choices were worth in expectation.

Estimating action values, one pull at a time

\[ Q_{n+1} \;=\; Q_n + \tfrac{1}{n}\left[ R_n - Q_n \right] \]
Where it comes from: the average of \(n\) rewards is \(\frac{1}{n}\sum R_i = Q_n + \frac{1}{n}(R_n - Q_n)\) after a little algebra — so you never need to store old rewards, just nudge the estimate toward each new one. A 1/n step gives the true average (right for fixed arms); a constant α gives a recency-weighted average that tracks arms whose payouts drift.
The master template
\[ \text{NewEstimate} \;\leftarrow\; \text{OldEstimate} \;+\; \text{StepSize}\cdot\left[\, \text{Target} - \text{OldEstimate} \,\right] \]
Remember this line. Every update on this page — bandits, Monte Carlo, TD, SARSA, Q-learning, even deep RL — is this template with a different Target. Keep asking: what is the target, and is it trustworthy?

Explore or exploit? The ε-greedy compromise

\[ A_t = \begin{cases} \arg\max_a Q_t(a) & \text{with probability } 1-\varepsilon \;\;\text{(exploit)}\\[2pt] \text{a random arm} & \text{with probability } \varepsilon \;\;\text{(explore)} \end{cases} \]
Why not pure greedy? Greedy locks onto the first arm that happens to look good and never corrects its estimates of the others — run it a few times above and watch it sometimes flatline on a mediocre arm. Smarter exploration exists: optimistic initialisation (start every estimate high, let disappointment drive exploration), the UCB bonus \(c\sqrt{\ln t / N(a)}\) (uncertainty as a bonus), Thompson sampling, and decaying ε.
Formalism

Adding state: the Markov Decision Process

A bandit is one situation, repeated. In chess, driving, inventory control or dialogue, the state evolves under your actions: today's choice shapes tomorrow's options, and reward may arrive far in the future. The state is the memory of the problem. The formal object is the MDP \(\langle \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma \rangle\):

  • \(\mathcal{S}\) — statesAll situations the agent can be in. \(s \in \mathcal{S}\).
  • \(\mathcal{A}\) — actionsChoices available in each state. \(a \in \mathcal{A}(s)\).
  • \(P\) — transitionsThe dynamics: \(P(s' \mid s, a)\), a distribution over next states.
  • \(R\) — rewardsExpected immediate payoff \(r(s,a) = \mathbb{E}[R \mid s, a]\).
  • \(\gamma\) — discount\(\gamma \in [0,1)\): how much the future is worth today. Gets its own section below.

\(P\) and \(R\) together are the model. Known model → planning. Unknown model → learning. This one distinction organises every algorithm on this page.

The Markov property: the state is enough

\[ P\left( S_{t+1}, R_{t+1} \mid S_t, A_t \right) \;=\; P\left( S_{t+1}, R_{t+1} \mid S_1, A_1, \dots, S_t, A_t \right) \]
Read it as: the future is independent of the past, given the present. The current state must carry everything that matters.

Markov ✓

  • A chess position — the board summarises all past moves
  • Inventory level — current stock is what matters

Not Markov ✗

  • A single camera frame — velocity is invisible (stack 4 frames: the DQN trick)
  • Poker — hidden cards make it a POMDP, needing belief states

The fix is engineering: build the state until it is (approximately) Markov — frame stacking, recurrent networks, belief states.

The policy: the agent's behaviour, made explicit

\[ \pi(a \mid s) \;=\; P\left( A_t = a \mid S_t = s \right) \]
Read it as: a (possibly stochastic) rule — in state \(s\), act like this. Deterministic \(\pi(s)=a\) suffices for optimal play in any finite MDP; stochastic policies are needed while exploring, and under partial observability or adversaries.
The objective

The return: adding up the future, discounted

\[ G_t \;=\; R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \gamma^2 R_{t+3} + \cdots \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k R_{t+k+1} \]
Read it as: the agent maximises not the next reward but the whole discounted future. A reward \(k\) steps away is worth \(\gamma^k\) of itself today.
\[ G_t \;=\; R_{t+1} + \gamma\, G_{t+1} \]
Where it comes from: factor \(\gamma\) out of every term after the first: \(G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma(R_{t+2} + \gamma R_{t+3} + \cdots)\) — and the bracket is exactly \(G_{t+1}\). Remember this recursive line: it becomes the Bellman equation.
\[ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k = \frac{1}{1-\gamma} \qquad (0 \le \gamma < 1) \]
Where it comes from: call the sum \(S\); then \(S - \gamma S = 1\), so \(S = \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\). This keeps returns finite for bounded rewards, and it gives \(\gamma\) a meaning: an effective look-ahead of about \(1/(1-\gamma)\) steps. \(\gamma = 0\): myopic. \(\gamma \to 1\): far-sighted. (Episodic tasks allow \(\gamma = 1\).)

The math core

Value functions and the Bellman equations

How good is a state? How good is an action? Fix a policy \(\pi\) and ask for the expected return from there on:

\[ V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\left[ G_t \mid S_t = s \right], \qquad Q^\pi(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\left[ G_t \mid S_t = s, A_t = a \right] \]
Read it as: \(V\) scores a state, \(Q\) scores an action taken in a state. They are tied by \(V^\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a|s)\, Q^\pi(s,a)\) — V is the policy-weighted average of Q. Why Q matters more in practice: acting greedily on Q needs no model, \(a^* = \arg\max_a Q(s,a)\); acting on V alone still requires \(P(s'|s,a)\) to look ahead.

The Bellman equation: value = reward + discounted next value

Take the recursion \(G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1}\) and push an expectation through it. The left side becomes \(V^\pi(s)\); the right side averages over the policy's action choices and the environment's transitions:

\[ V^\pi(s) \;=\; \sum_a \pi(a|s) \sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)\left[\, r(s,a) + \gamma\, V^\pi(s') \,\right] \]
Read it as: expected one-step reward, plus the discounted value of wherever you land. Consistency, not magic: this is \(|\mathcal{S}|\) linear equations in \(|\mathcal{S}|\) unknowns — with a known model, evaluating a policy is linear algebra.
The most important line on this page
\[ Q^*(s,a) \;=\; r(s,a) \;+\; \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)\; \max_{a'} Q^*(s', a') \]
Bellman optimality: replace the policy's average with a max — assume you act optimally from the next state on. Then \(V^*(s) = \max_a Q^*(s,a)\) and \(\pi^*(s) = \arg\max_a Q^*(s,a)\): greedy on \(Q^*\) is globally optimal. The max makes the system nonlinear — no closed form, solve iteratively (value iteration, Q-learning). Every finite MDP has a deterministic optimal policy.

Known model: value iteration

\[ V_{k+1}(s) \;=\; \max_a \left[\, r(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s'|s,a)\, V_k(s') \,\right] \]
Read it as: repeat the optimality backup until nothing changes. Why it works — contraction: each sweep shrinks the error by \(\gamma\): \(\lVert V_{k+1}-V^*\rVert \le \gamma \lVert V_k - V^*\rVert\), geometric convergence from any start. (Its sibling, policy iteration, ping-pongs evaluate → improve → evaluate to \(\pi^*\).) This is planning, not learning: no samples, no interaction.

Two curses: why we can't just plan

Curse of modelling

P(s′|s,a) is unknown

For robots, markets, users… writing a simulator is itself the hard research problem, and model errors compound over long horizons.

Curse of dimensionality

Tables don't scale

DP sweeps all states: Go has ≈ 10170. State spaces grow exponentially with state variables — tables cannot even be stored, let alone swept.

Escape 1: sample

Learn from experience

Monte Carlo and TD replace the exact expectation with sampled interaction — no model needed.

Escape 2: generalise

Function approximation

Share estimates across similar states — the road to deep RL, later on this page.

Model-free prediction: Monte Carlo and TD

\[ V(S_t) \;\leftarrow\; V(S_t) + \alpha \left[\, G_t - V(S_t) \,\right] \]
Monte Carlo: wait for the episode to end, then nudge each visited state toward the actual return that followed it. Unbiased, no model, no Markov assumption — but high variance, episodic tasks only, and it learns nothing mid-episode.
\[ V(S_t) \;\leftarrow\; V(S_t) + \alpha \underbrace{\left[\, R_{t+1} + \gamma V(S_{t+1}) - V(S_t) \,\right]}_{\delta_t,\ \text{the TD error}} \]
Temporal difference: don't wait — bootstrap. The target leans on the current estimate of the next state. \(\delta_t\) is the “surprise”: reality minus expectation (dopamine neurons in the brain encode exactly this signal). Online, low variance, some bias — the workhorse of modern RL.

Control — the famous algorithms

SARSA and Q-learning

Prediction scores a fixed policy. Control finds a better one: learn \(Q\), act (mostly) greedily on it, repeat. Two one-line algorithms do this, and they differ by a single symbol.

On-policy · SARSA
\[ Q(S,A) \;\leftarrow\; Q(S,A) + \alpha\left[\, R + \gamma\, Q(S', A') - Q(S,A) \,\right] \]
Read it as: \(A'\) is the action the policy actually takes in \(S'\) — the quintuple \((S, A, R, S', A')\) names the algorithm. SARSA evaluates the ε-greedy policy it is really running, so exploration noise is priced into the values. Converges to \(Q^*\) if every pair is visited forever, \(\alpha\) decays suitably (Robbins–Monro), and ε decays to 0 (GLIE).
Off-policy · Q-learning (Watkins 1989)
\[ Q(S,A) \;\leftarrow\; Q(S,A) + \alpha\left[\, R + \gamma\, \max_{a'} Q(S', a') - Q(S,A) \,\right] \]
Read it as: the most famous update in RL. The behaviour policy stays ε-greedy (keeps exploring) while the target inside the update is pure greedy — it learns about optimal play while behaving sub-optimally. Exploration and optimality, decoupled. Watch out: the max causes overestimation bias (fix: Double Q-learning), and off-policy learning can diverge with function approximation.

Worked example: a 4×4 gridworld, traced by hand

Setup: goal G pays +10 and ends the episode; every other step costs −1. Actions: up, down, left, right — deterministic, walls bounce back. Protocol: \(\gamma = 0.9\), \(\alpha = 0.5\), ε-greedy with ε = 0.2, \(Q(s,a) = 0\) initially. 16 states × 4 actions = a 64-entry Q-table.

  1. ① A → G  (A is the cell left of the goal)
    r = +10, terminal ⇒ maxₐ′ Q(G, a′) = 0
    Q(A, →) ← 0 + 0.5 · [ 10 + 0.9·0 − 0 ] = 5.0
  2. ② B → A  (B is two cells left of the goal)
    r = −1, maxₐ′ Q(A, a′) = 5.0
    Q(B, →) ← 0 + 0.5 · [ −1 + 0.9·5.0 − 0 ] = 1.75
  3. Each later visit to A halves the remaining error
    Q(A, →):  5 → 7.5 → 8.75 → 9.38 → … → 10

Value leaks backwards from the goal — one bootstrap per visit. The goal pays once; the Q-table spreads that payment through the maze.

The three dials: α, γ, ε

α · step size

How far each update moves

Theory wants ∑α = ∞, ∑α² < ∞ (e.g. 1/n). Practice: constant 0.05–0.5, or Adam in deep RL.

γ · horizon

Effective look-ahead ≈ 1/(1−γ)

0.99 ≈ a 100-step horizon — match it to the task. Higher γ means bigger values, usually a smaller α.

ε · exploration

Start high, decay low

Start ≈0.2–1.0, decay toward ≈0.01. GLIE: explore forever, greedy in the limit. Alternatives: UCB, softmax, entropy bonuses.

Guarantee: tabular Q-learning converges to \(Q^*\) with probability 1 — if every (s, a) is visited infinitely often and α obeys the conditions above.

Scaling up

When tables die: deep RL, policy gradients, RLHF

Function approximation — and its price

\[ Q(s, a;\, \mathbf{w}), \qquad \mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} + \alpha\, \delta\, \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} Q(s,a;\mathbf{w}) \]
Read it as: replace the table with a parametrised function — similar states now share estimates, which is the win called generalisation. The price is the deadly triad: function approximation + bootstrapping + off-policy learning together can diverge. No tabular guarantee survives; deep RL is engineering around this triad.

DQN: Q-learning meets deep networks (Atari, 2015)

\[ L(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \left( r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a'; \mathbf{w}^-) - Q(s,a;\mathbf{w}) \right)^2 \right] \]
Read it as: regression toward a frozen bootstrap target. Two tricks made it work: an experience replay buffer (store transitions, train on random mini-batches — breaks correlation, reuses data) and a target network \(\mathbf{w}^-\) (a frozen copy — stops the target chasing itself). Double DQN fixes the max's overestimation; Rainbow stacks the tricks. Input: raw pixels. Output: superhuman play on dozens of Atari games.

The other road: optimise the policy directly

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_\pi\left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi(A \mid S;\, \theta)\; G_t \right] \]
The policy gradient theorem: make good actions more probable, in proportion to the return that followed them. Why bother? Continuous actions (no argmax needed), stochastic policies for free, smooth updates with no value flip-flops. Subtracting a baseline \(b\) from \(G_t\) cuts variance without adding bias — and an actor–critic takes that further: the actor \(\pi(a|s;\theta)\) proposes, the critic \(V(s;\mathbf{w})\) grades via the TD error. Lineage: REINFORCE → A2C/A3C → TRPO → PPO (clipped updates; powers RLHF) · SAC for continuous control.

Wrap-up

The lecture in six lines

  • RL learns behaviour from evaluative, delayed feedback — no labels, only consequences.
  • The MDP ⟨S, A, P, R, γ⟩ formalises the problem; the Markov state carries the burden.
  • Bellman: value = reward + γ · next value; optimality replaces the average with a max.
  • DP plans with a model; MC averages real returns; TD bootstraps online — one template update.
  • SARSA learns the policy it runs; Q-learning learns the optimum it doesn't — cliff walking tells them apart.
  • Tables die at scale: function approximation → DQN, policy gradients, actor–critic, RLHF.

Check your understanding

What should Q(state-left-of-G, right) converge to in the 4×4 gridworld?

Exactly +10. That move enters the terminal goal: the target is r + γ·0 = 10, with no future to discount. Every visit halves the remaining error: 5 → 7.5 → 8.75 → … → 10.

What is the difference between reward and value?

Reward is immediate and given by the environment; value is the expected cumulative discounted reward from here on, and must be estimated. Agents act on value, not reward — a step can pay −1 and still be the best move.

γ = 0.99 corresponds to roughly what planning horizon?

About 1/(1−γ) = 100 steps. γ = 0 makes the agent myopic (maximise the next reward only); γ → 1 makes it far-sighted, and usually requires a smaller step size.

Same ε-greedy exploration — why does Q-learning hug the cliff while SARSA detours?

Q-learning's max-target evaluates pure greedy play, ignoring that its ε-greedy behaviour sometimes slips off the edge — off-policy. SARSA evaluates the ε-greedy policy it actually runs, so the risk of slipping is priced into its values and the safe path scores higher — on-policy. Decay ε to 0 and both converge to optimal.

Why explore at all? Greedy already maximises the current estimates.

Because the estimates are wrong at first. Pure greedy locks onto the first arm or path that happens to look good and never corrects the others. Exploration buys information; ε-greedy, optimism and UCB are three prices for it.

A single camera frame isn't a Markov state. Why, and what's the fix?

Velocity is invisible in one frame — the past matters, violating the Markov property. Fix: engineer the state until it is approximately Markov. DQN stacks 4 frames; recurrent networks and belief states are the general tools.
