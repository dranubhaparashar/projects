Bayesian Model Comparison

The evidence, the Occam factor and empirical Bayes

A curve of degree 9 always fits the training points at least as well as a curve of degree 3. So how can the training data alone ever prefer the simpler one? Bayes has an answer: score each model by the evidence, the probability it gave to the data before seeing it, averaged over everything the model could have believed. A flexible model spreads its bets over so many possible datasets that it gives yours very little. On the right, drag any dot and watch the bars, the probability of each degree given the data, move.

The idea

Occam’s razor, computed

Think of a model as a machine that, before you collect anything, says how likely each possible dataset is. Those probabilities must add up to 1 over all datasets. A rigid model bets heavily on a few datasets. A flexible model can explain almost anything, so it has to spread the same total over many more, and gives each one less. When the data land where the rigid model bet, it wins; when they land elsewhere, only the flexible one can catch them. That number, the probability a model gave to the data you actually saw, is its evidence, or marginal likelihood.

Start with the simplest case: 20 tosses of a coin. Three models compete. Fair says the chance of heads is exactly ½. Nearly fair says it is somewhere near ½ (a Beta(10, 10) prior). Anything goes says every chance from 0 to 1 is equally plausible (a uniform prior). Slide the number of heads and see which model the data favour.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathcal{D}\mid \mathcal{M}_i) = \int & p(\mathcal{D}\mid \mathbf{w}, \mathcal{M}_i) \\ & \times p(\mathbf{w}\mid \mathcal{M}_i)\; d\mathbf{w} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a model ℳi has parameters w (here, one number: the chance of heads) and a prior over them. Its evidence is the likelihood of the data, averaged over that prior. It is not the best fit; it is the average fit, weighted by how much the model believed each setting beforehand. A fixed model with no free parameters (Fair) has no integral: its evidence is just its likelihood.
Say it“The evidence for model i is the integral of the likelihood times the prior over the weights.”

Comparing models

Posterior odds, Bayes factors and averaging

Bayes’ rule works on models just as it works on parameters. Give each model a prior probability (often equal), multiply by its evidence, and normalise. The ratio of two evidences is the Bayes factor: how far the data alone move the odds from one model to the other. A Bayes factor above about 20 (a log above 3) is usually called strong.

Choosing the single best model is itself an approximation. The full Bayesian answer keeps every model and averages their predictions, each weighted by its posterior probability. In practice one model often dominates the mixture, and picking it changes little.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathcal{M}_i\mid\mathcal{D}) &\propto p(\mathcal{M}_i)\, p(\mathcal{D}\mid\mathcal{M}_i) \\ \text{Bayes factor} &= \frac{p(\mathcal{D}\mid\mathcal{M}_1)}{p(\mathcal{D}\mid\mathcal{M}_2)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the posterior probability of a model is its prior probability times its evidence, rescaled so all the models add up to 1. With equal priors, the posterior odds equal the Bayes factor.
Say it“p of model i given D is proportional to p of model i times the evidence for model i.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(t\mid \mathbf{x}, \mathcal{D}) = \sum_{i} \,& p(t\mid \mathbf{x}, \mathcal{M}_i, \mathcal{D}) \\ & \times p(\mathcal{M}_i\mid\mathcal{D}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: to predict, ask every model and weight its answer by how much the data now trust it. This is a mixture, so it can be broader or even two-peaked when models disagree.
Say it“The predictive distribution is the sum over models of each model’s prediction times its posterior probability.”

Does the evidence pick the right model? Not on every dataset: an unlucky sample can favour the wrong one. But on average it does. If the data really come from ℳ1, the expected log Bayes factor in its favour is a Kullback–Leibler divergence, which is never negative. Choose where the data come from, then draw a thousand datasets and look at the spread of log Bayes factors.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}\sim p(\mathcal{D}\mid\mathcal{M}_1)}\!\left[\ln \frac{p(\mathcal{D}\mid\mathcal{M}_1)}{p(\mathcal{D}\mid\mathcal{M}_2)}\right] \\ &\quad = \mathrm{KL}\big(p(\mathcal{D}\mid\mathcal{M}_1)\,\big\Vert\, p(\mathcal{D}\mid\mathcal{M}_2)\big) \\ &\quad \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: average the log Bayes factor over all the datasets the true model would generate. The result is the KL divergence between the two models’ distributions over datasets: zero only if they are the same model, positive otherwise. This guarantee holds only if one of the candidates really did generate the data.
Say it“The expected log Bayes factor, under the true model, is the KL divergence from the true model’s evidence to the other’s, and it is at least zero.”

Why it works

The Occam factor

Why does averaging punish flexibility? Picture one parameter with a flat prior of width Δwprior. After the data arrive the likelihood is a narrow peak of width Δwpost. The evidence averages the likelihood over the prior, and the peak fills only a sliver of it, so the evidence is about the best-fit likelihood times the fraction Δwpost/Δwprior. That fraction, always below 1, is the Occam factor: the penalty for how much of its prior the model had to throw away to fit the data.

Here the data are n draws from a Gaussian with unknown mean μ and known spread 1, and the prior on μ is flat on −L to L. Widen the prior, then add data, and see the two parts of the evidence pull against each other.

\[ p(\mathcal{D}) \simeq p(\mathcal{D}\mid w_{\text{MAP}})\, \frac{\Delta w_{\text{post}}}{\Delta w_{\text{prior}}} \]
Read it as: evidence ≈ best fit × Occam factor. A sharper peak (more data) or a wider prior (a vaguer model) makes the factor smaller.
Say it“The evidence is roughly the likelihood at the best w, times delta-w-posterior over delta-w-prior.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \ln p(\mathcal{D}) \simeq {} & \ln p(\mathcal{D}\mid \mathbf{w}_{\text{MAP}}) \\ & + M \ln\!\left(\frac{\Delta w_{\text{post}}}{\Delta w_{\text{prior}}}\right) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with M parameters, each roughly equally constrained, the log penalty is M times as big. Adding parameters improves the first term (better fit) but makes the second more negative (each new parameter is another negative log). The best model balances them, at an intermediate complexity.
Say it“Log evidence is about the log best-fit likelihood plus M times the log of the posterior-to-prior width ratio.”

Two warnings fall out of this picture. First, the evidence depends on the prior’s width even when the fit does not: make the prior a thousand times wider and the evidence drops by a factor of a thousand per parameter. So the priors in a model comparison must be chosen with care. Second, an improper prior (flat over the whole line, with infinite width) gives zero evidence, and comparisons become meaningless; you can take a proper prior and let it widen only if you compare models with the same limit.

Linear regression

Evaluating the evidence function

Now the real model of chapter 1.2d: a linear model y(x, w) = wᵀφ(x) with a Gaussian prior on the weights, precision α, and Gaussian noise of precision β. Here φ(x) holds the powers 1, x, …, xd, so a polynomial of degree d has M = d + 1 weights. Because everything is Gaussian, the integral over w can be done exactly by completing the square, and the evidence has a closed form. The posterior mean mN and precision A are the ones from the last chapter.

The points below come from sin(πx) plus noise of spread 0.25, so the true β = 16; for now α is fixed at 0.005. Step through the degrees and compare two curves: the best-fit likelihood, which only ever climbs, and the evidence, which rises and then falls. You can drag the points.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{t}\mid\alpha,\beta) &= \int p(\mathbf{t}\mid\mathbf{w},\beta) \\ &\qquad \times p(\mathbf{w}\mid\alpha)\,d\mathbf{w} \\ E(\mathbf{w}) &= E(\mathbf{m}_N) \\ &\quad + \tfrac12(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{m}_N)^{\!\top} \\ &\qquad \times \mathbf{A}\,(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{m}_N) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the evidence integrates the weights out. The exponent of likelihood × prior, −E(w), is a quadratic bowl in w: its value at the bottom mN plus a Gaussian-shaped part with curvature A. Integrating a Gaussian is easy: it leaves (2π)M/2|A|−1/2.
Say it“E of w equals E at m-N plus a half times w minus m-N, transposed, A, w minus m-N.”
\[ \begin{aligned} E(\mathbf{m}_N) &= \tfrac{\beta}{2}\lVert \mathbf{t}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{m}_N\rVert^2 \\ &\quad + \tfrac{\alpha}{2}\,\mathbf{m}_N^{\!\top}\mathbf{m}_N \\ \mathbf{A} &= \alpha\mathbf{I} + \beta\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\!\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi} \\ \mathbf{m}_N &= \beta\mathbf{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\!\top}\mathbf{t} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: E at the bottom of the bowl is the regularised sum-of-squares error: misfit to the targets plus a weight penalty. A is the posterior precision, and mN is the posterior mean (the ridge solution).
Say it“E at m-N is beta over two times the squared residual plus alpha over two times m-N squared.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \ln p(\mathbf{t}\mid\alpha,\beta) = {} & \tfrac{M}{2}\ln\alpha + \tfrac{N}{2}\ln\beta \\ & - E(\mathbf{m}_N) - \tfrac12 \ln\lvert\mathbf{A}\rvert \\ & - \tfrac{N}{2}\ln(2\pi) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the log evidence. −E(mN) rewards a good regularised fit. −½ ln|A| is the Occam factor in disguise: |A| measures how sharply the data pin the weights down, and every well-determined weight adds to it. M/2 ln α and N/2 ln β come from the normalising constants of the prior and the noise.
Say it“Log evidence is M over two log alpha, plus N over two log beta, minus E at m-N, minus a half log det A, minus N over two log two pi.”

The evidence approximation

Empirical Bayes: let the data set α

The fully Bayesian treatment would put priors on α and β too and integrate over them, along with w. That integral has no closed form. The evidence approximation takes a shortcut: if the data pin the hyperparameters down well, their posterior is a sharp peak at some (α̂, β̂), and we can simply plug those values in. To find the peak with a flat-ish hyperprior, maximise the evidence p(t | α, β). Statisticians call this empirical Bayes or type-2 maximum likelihood: maximum likelihood, one level up.

The striking part: α is a regularisation strength, which you would normally tune on held-out data. Here it is chosen from the training data alone. Slide α and watch the evidence and the error on 400 fresh test points (which the evidence never sees). Here β stays at its true value, 16.

\[ \begin{aligned} &p(t\mid\mathbf{t}) = \iiint p(t\mid\mathbf{w},\beta) \\ &\quad \times p(\mathbf{w}\mid\mathbf{t},\alpha,\beta) \\ &\quad \times p(\alpha,\beta\mid\mathbf{t})\,d\mathbf{w}\,d\alpha\,d\beta \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the honest prediction averages over everything we don’t know: the weights, and the hyperparameters too. (Inputs are left out of the notation to keep it short.)
Say it“The predictive for t is the triple integral of the noise model, the weight posterior and the hyperparameter posterior.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(t\mid\mathbf{t}) &\simeq p(t\mid\mathbf{t},\hat\alpha,\hat\beta) \\ (\hat\alpha,\hat\beta) &= \arg\max_{\alpha,\beta}\ p(\mathbf{t}\mid\alpha,\beta) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: if p(α, β | t) is a narrow spike, replace the outer integrals by the value at the spike. With a nearly flat prior on the hyperparameters, the spike is where the evidence peaks.
Say it“The predictive is approximately the one at alpha-hat and beta-hat, the values that maximise the evidence.”

The algorithm

Maximising the evidence: a fixed-point loop

Set the derivatives of the log evidence to zero. The tricky term is ln|A|. Write the eigenvalues of βΦᵀΦ as λi; then A has eigenvalues α + λi, so ln|A| = Σ ln(α + λi) and its derivative in α is Σ 1/(α + λi). Gather the terms and one quantity keeps appearing, γ. The conditions for the peak don’t give α and β directly, because mN and γ depend on them. So iterate: guess, compute mN and γ, update, repeat. This is MacKay’s re-estimation, and it is what scikit-learn’s BayesianRidge runs.

The map shows ln p(t | α, β) for the 12 points above. Click anywhere to start the loop there, then press Step.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\left(\beta\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\!\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)\mathbf{u}_i = \lambda_i\mathbf{u}_i \\ &\gamma = \sum_{i} \frac{\lambda_i}{\alpha+\lambda_i} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: λi measures how strongly the data constrain the weights along eigen-direction ui. Each direction contributes between 0 and 1 to γ. Because the λi scale with β, they must be recomputed whenever β changes (by rescaling, not re-solving).
Say it“Gamma is the sum over i of lambda-i over alpha plus lambda-i.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha &= \frac{\gamma}{\mathbf{m}_N^{\!\top}\mathbf{m}_N} \\ \frac{1}{\beta} &= \frac{1}{N-\gamma}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big(t_n - \mathbf{m}_N^{\!\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_n)\big)^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the new α is the number of well-determined weights divided by the squared size of the weights. The new noise variance is the mean squared residual, but dividing by N − γ instead of N, because γ of the N data points’ worth of information was spent fitting the weights.
Say it“Alpha is gamma over m-N dot m-N; one over beta is the sum of squared residuals over N minus gamma.”

Interpretation

The effective number of parameters

What is γ? Look at the posterior in weight space. The prior is a round bowl centred on zero, the same in every direction. The likelihood is a long ellipse: narrow along directions the data measure well (large λi), wide along directions they barely touch (small λi). Along a well-measured direction, λi ≫ α, so the posterior sits at the maximum-likelihood value and the direction counts almost 1 toward γ. Along a poorly measured one, λi ≪ α, so the prior pulls the weight to zero and it counts almost 0. So γ is the number of weights the data, not the prior, have determined.

Here a straight line t = w₀ + w₁x is fitted to 8 points whose x values are centred on zero, so the two axes are the eigen-directions: the intercept is measured by every point, the slope only by how spread out the x values are. Squeeze the x values together, then raise α.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\gamma = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \frac{\lambda_i}{\alpha+\lambda_i}, \qquad 0 \le \gamma \le M \\ &\text{at the peak: } 2\alpha E_W(\mathbf{m}_N) = \gamma \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each direction’s ratio is between 0 (set by the prior) and 1 (set by the data); γ adds them up. At the evidence peak, the update α = γ/mNᵀmN says that the weight penalty 2αEW, with EW = ½mNᵀmN, equals the number of well-determined weights.
Say it“Gamma, between zero and M, is the sum of lambda-i over alpha plus lambda-i.”
γ, of 2 weights
noise estimate 1/β

The β update now reads naturally. Each well-determined weight uses up one data point’s worth of freedom, just as the sample mean uses one when you estimate a variance with N − 1 instead of N. When there are many more data points than weights, every direction becomes well determined, γ → M, and the updates simplify to the ones below. They need no eigenvalues at all, which makes them easy to use on big data.

\[ \begin{aligned} &N \gg M: \quad \gamma \to M \\ &\alpha = \frac{M}{2E_W(\mathbf{m}_N)} \\ &\beta = \frac{N}{2E_D(\mathbf{m}_N)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with plenty of data, α is the number of weights over twice the weight penalty, and β is N over the sum of squared residuals (ED = ½‖t − ΦmN‖²): the plain maximum-likelihood noise estimate.
Say it“For large N, alpha is M over two E-W, and beta is N over two E-D.”
Choosing

Evidence versus cross-validation

Cross-validation (chapter 1.10) answers a similar question a different way: hold some data out, train on the rest, and score the predictions. It assumes almost nothing about the model, but it costs a refit per fold, wastes data in each fold, and is noisy when data are few. The evidence uses every data point at once, needs one fit per model, and can tune many hyperparameters by gradient. The price is trust: it is only as good as the model’s assumptions, including the prior. If no candidate is close to the truth, the evidence can be confidently wrong.

Below, each experiment draws a fresh dataset from sin(πx) + noise, picks a degree from 0 to 9 twice, by maximum evidence (with α and β tuned by the loop above) and by leave-one-out cross-validation of least squares, then scores both picks on 400 fresh test points. Run 200 experiments, then change the amount of data.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{LOO} &= \sqrt{\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\frac{t_n - y(\mathbf{x}_n)}{1-H_{nn}}\right)^{\!2}} \\ \mathbf{H} &= \boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\!\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\!\top} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for least squares, leave-one-out needs no refitting. Hnn says how much point n pulls the fit towards itself; dividing its residual by 1 − Hnn gives exactly the error it would have had if it had been left out.
Say it“LOO is the root mean of each residual divided by one minus its leverage, squared.”

Real-world project

Project: how wiggly is the global temperature record?

NASA’s GISTEMP v4 is one of the standard records of Earth’s surface temperature. For every year since 1880 it gives the global mean anomaly: how much warmer or cooler than the 1951–1980 average the planet was, combining thousands of land stations with sea-surface measurements. We take the 146 annual means from 1880 to 2025 and ask a model-comparison question: how much of the shape is a smooth trend, and which polynomial degree does the data support?

The years are rescaled to z between −1 and 1 and the basis is the Legendre polynomials P₀(z), …, Pd(z), which span the same curves as the powers of z but are far better conditioned at high degree. For every degree from 0 to 15 the page runs the re-estimation loop to find α̂ and β̂, reports the log evidence, and also runs 10-fold cross-validation (with the same folds as the Python reference). The same project in Python

import json, sys
import numpy as np
from numpy.polynomial import legendre
from sklearn.linear_model import BayesianRidge, LinearRegression

D = json.load(open(sys.argv[1] + "x015-data.json"))   # NASA GISTEMP v4, global annual means 1880-2025
year, t, fold = np.array(D["year"]), np.array(D["t"]), np.array(D["fold"])   # fold: KFold(10, shuffle, seed 0)
z = (year - 1952.5) / 72.5                             # years rescaled to [-1, 1]

def evidence_fit(X, y):                                # MacKay's fixed-point updates (Bishop 3.5.2)
    N, M = X.shape; d = np.linalg.eigvalsh(X.T @ X); a, b = 1.0, 1 / np.var(y)
    for _ in range(1000):
        A = a * np.eye(M) + b * X.T @ X; m = b * np.linalg.solve(A, X.T @ y)
        g = np.sum(b * d / (a + b * d))                # effective number of parameters
        a2, b2 = g / (m @ m), (N - g) / np.sum((y - X @ m) ** 2)
        if abs(np.log(a2 / a)) + abs(np.log(b2 / b)) < 1e-12: break
        a, b = a2, b2
    E = b / 2 * np.sum((y - X @ m) ** 2) + a / 2 * m @ m
    lnev = M / 2 * np.log(a) + N / 2 * np.log(b) - E - np.linalg.slogdet(A)[1] / 2 - N / 2 * np.log(2 * np.pi)
    return lnev, g, a, b, m

print("deg   ln p(t|a,b)    gamma    alpha     beta   sklearn ln ev   CV RMSE: LS    Bayes   forecast RMSE")
for M in range(16):
    X = legendre.legvander(z, M)                       # Legendre polynomials P0..PM
    lnev, g, a, b, m = evidence_fit(X, t)
    br = BayesianRidge(fit_intercept=False, compute_score=True, max_iter=1000, tol=1e-12,
                       alpha_1=0, alpha_2=0, lambda_1=0, lambda_2=0).fit(X, t)   # sklearn: alpha=beta, lambda=alpha
    se = np.zeros(2)
    for k in range(10):
        tr, te = fold != k, fold == k
        ls = LinearRegression(fit_intercept=False).fit(X[tr], t[tr])
        se += [np.sum((t[te] - ls.predict(X[te])) ** 2), np.sum((t[te] - X[te] @ evidence_fit(X[tr], t[tr])[4]) ** 2)]
    old = year <= 2005                                 # fit 1880-2005, forecast 2006-2025
    mf = evidence_fit(X[old], t[old])[4]
    fc = np.sqrt(np.mean((t[~old] - X[~old] @ mf) ** 2))
    print(f"{M:3d} {lnev:12.4f} {g:9.4f} {a:8.3f} {b:8.3f} {br.scores_[-1]:13.4f} {np.sqrt(se[0] / len(t)):12.5f} "
          f"{np.sqrt(se[1] / len(t)):8.5f} {fc:12.4f}")

Two honest caveats. Both the evidence and random 10-fold cross-validation treat the yearly noise as independent, but temperature anomalies are autocorrelated (El Niño years cluster), so neighbouring years leak information into each other’s folds and the evidence is over-confident: the Bayes factors in the table are larger than the data truly justify. And both criteria answer an interpolation question. The forecast column shows that the degree they prefer, 9, extrapolates badly; the best twenty-year forecasts come from low degrees, and a polynomial is the wrong tool for forecasting a physical system anyway.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • The evidence p(D | ℳ) is the likelihood averaged over the model’s prior. Its probabilities add up to 1 over all datasets, so a flexible model must spread them thin: Occam’s razor, automatically.
  • Evidence ≈ best fit × Occam factor Δwpost/Δwprior, once per well-determined parameter. Priors matter: wider priors always cost, and improper ones break the comparison.
  • For Bayesian linear regression the evidence is exact: ln p(t | α, β) = M/2 ln α + N/2 ln β − E(mN) − ½ ln|A| − N/2 ln 2π.
  • Empirical Bayes maximises it over α and β with a simple loop: γ = Σ λi/(α + λi), α = γ/mNᵀmN, 1/β = residuals/(N − γ). It tunes regularisation without a validation set.
  • γ counts the weights the data, not the prior, have pinned down. The evidence is efficient but trusts the model; cross-validation is costly but trusts only the data. On 146 years of global temperatures both pick degree 9, and neither tells you how to forecast.

Check your understanding

A degree-9 polynomial always fits the training points at least as well as a degree-3 one. How can its evidence be lower?

The evidence is not the best fit but the fit averaged over the prior. Most of the degree-9 prior describes wild curves that fit the data badly, so the average is low. In Occam-factor terms, the better fit is outweighed by the extra penalty for six more parameters, each squeezed from its prior width to a much narrower posterior width.

You double the width of a flat prior on one parameter, and the likelihood peak lies well inside both priors. What happens to the evidence?

It halves. The best fit and Δwpost are unchanged, but Δwprior doubles, so the Occam factor halves (ln evidence drops by ln 2 ≈ 0.69).

The loop converges with γ = 3.2 for a model with 10 weights. What does that tell you?

Only about three directions in weight space are determined by the data; the other seven are held near zero by the prior. The noise estimate uses N − 3.2 degrees of freedom.

On average, does the evidence favour the model that generated the data? Always?

On average yes: the expected log Bayes factor in favour of the true model is a KL divergence, which is never negative. But for individual datasets the wrong model can win, and if none of the candidates generated the data the guarantee does not apply at all.

Name one situation where you would trust cross-validation over the evidence.

When you doubt the model’s assumptions: for example correlated noise, a wrong noise model, or a prior chosen for convenience. The evidence scores models under their own assumptions; cross-validation measures real predictive error on held-out data.
