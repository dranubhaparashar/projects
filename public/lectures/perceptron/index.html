The Perceptron & the Neuron

A weighted vote, a threshold, and a rule that learns from its mistakes

Every deep network is built from one small part: a unit that adds up its inputs, each multiplied by a weight, and answers according to the total. In 1958 Frank Rosenblatt showed that such a unit can learn its own weights from examples, nudging them only when it gets an example wrong. He called it the perceptron. On the right, a perceptron is learning to split two classes with a line. Click anywhere on the plot to drop a new point and watch it learn again.

The idea

One cell, one decision

A nerve cell collects signals from other cells through its branches (the dendrites). Some connections excite it, some inhibit it, and some are stronger than others. If the combined signal in the cell body is strong enough, the cell fires a pulse down its axon; otherwise it stays quiet. In 1943 Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts reduced this to arithmetic: multiply each input by a weight (its strength and sign), add them up, add a bias, and fire if the total is above zero.

Here is one such neuron deciding whether to cycle to work. Its three inputs are how dry, how warm and how windy the morning is, each between 0 and 1. Move the inputs, then change the weights and see how the same morning can get a different answer.

\[ \begin{aligned} z &= w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + w_3x_3 + b \\ &= \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b \\ \hat y &= \operatorname{step}(z) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } z > 0 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: z is a weighted vote. A positive weight makes its input argue for “yes”, a negative weight argues for “no”, and a large weight shouts. The bias b is the neuron’s mood before it sees anything: a negative bias is a threshold the vote must beat. The step function turns the vote into a decision.
Say it“z is w-one x-one plus w-two x-two plus w-three x-three plus b, the dot product of w and x plus b. The output y-hat is one if z is positive and zero otherwise.”

Neurons as logic

Logic gates from a single neuron

McCulloch and Pitts’ point was that threshold neurons can do logic. Give a neuron two inputs that are either 0 or 1. With w₁ = w₂ = 1 and b = −1.5 it fires only when both inputs are on: that’s AND. Lower the threshold to b = −0.5 and one input is enough: OR. Geometrically, the neuron draws a straight line across the square of four possible inputs and fires on one side of it. Pick a target gate and find weights that compute it. Then try XOR.

\[ \begin{aligned} w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + b &= 0 \\ \Longleftrightarrow\quad x_2 &= -\frac{w_1}{w_2}\,x_1 - \frac{b}{w_2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the neuron’s boundary, where z = 0, is a straight line. The weights (w₁, w₂) point at right angles to it, towards the side that fires; the bias slides it, and it sits at distance |b| / ‖w‖ from the origin. Whatever the weights, one neuron can only ever cut the plane with one straight line.
Say it“w-one x-one plus w-two x-two plus b equals zero; solved for x-two, a line with slope minus w-one over w-two and intercept minus b over w-two.”

Activation functions

How a neuron answers: activation functions

The step is all or nothing. That suits logic, but it has a hidden cost: its slope is zero everywhere (and undefined at the jump). Nudge a weight a little and the output almost never changes, so there is no gradient to tell a learning algorithm which way to move. Modern networks replace the step with a smooth activation function φ, so the neuron computes φ(w·x + b). Each choice trades off a little differently. Pick an activation and slide z; the dashed curve is its slope, the number gradient descent will live on in the next chapter.

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{step}(z) &= [\,z > 0\,] \\ \sigma(z) &= \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} \\ \sigma'(z) &= \sigma(z)\big(1-\sigma(z)\big) \\ \tanh(z) &= \frac{e^{z}-e^{-z}}{e^{z}+e^{-z}} \\ \tanh'(z) &= 1-\tanh^2(z) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the sigmoid σ is a smoothed step from 0 to 1 (it is logistic regression’s S-curve from chapter 1.3); tanh is the same shape stretched to run from −1 to 1, centred on zero. Both flatten out for large |z|, where their slopes all but vanish.
Say it“Sigma of z is one over one plus e to the minus z, and its slope is sigma times one minus sigma. Tanh’s slope is one minus tanh squared.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{ReLU}(z) &= \max(0, z) \\ \operatorname{ReLU}'(z) &= [\,z > 0\,] \\ \operatorname{GELU}(z) &= z\,\Phi(z) \\ \Phi(z) &= \tfrac12\Big(1 + \operatorname{erf}\tfrac{z}{\sqrt 2}\Big) \\ \operatorname{GELU}'(z) &= \Phi(z) + z\,\varphi(z) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the rectified linear unit passes positive votes straight through and silences negative ones. Its slope is exactly 1 on the positive side, so it never saturates there. GELU weights z by the probability Φ(z) that a standard normal falls below it: a smooth ReLU that is the default in Transformers such as BERT and GPT-2.
Say it“ReLU of z is the max of zero and z. GELU of z is z times capital phi of z, the normal distribution’s cumulative probability.”

1958 · learning

The perceptron rule: learn from mistakes

McCulloch and Pitts set their weights by hand. Rosenblatt’s idea was to let the data set them. Label the two classes y = +1 and y = −1 and feed the examples in one at a time. If the neuron gets an example right, do nothing. If it gets it wrong (or the example sits exactly on the line), add y·x to the weights and y to the bias. A missed +1 point drags w towards itself; a missed −1 point pushes w away. Press Step to feed the next point, or Play to watch a whole run.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{if } y_i\,(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i + b) \le 0: \\ &\qquad \mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} + \eta\, y_i\, \mathbf{x}_i \\ &\qquad b \leftarrow b + \eta\, y_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: yi(w·xi + b) is positive exactly when point i is on its own side. When it isn’t, move the weights by the point itself, signed by its label, times a step size η. Starting from w = 0, η only rescales every weight by the same factor: the line, and every mistake along the way, come out identical.
Say it“If y-i times w-dot-x-i plus b is at most zero, add eta y-i x-i to w and eta y-i to b.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &y_i\big(\mathbf{w}_{\text{new}}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i + b_{\text{new}}\big) \\ &\quad = y_i\big(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i + b\big) + \eta\,\big(\lVert\mathbf{x}_i\rVert^2 + 1\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: why the rule helps. After an update, the point that was missed has its score raised by η(‖xi‖² + 1), which is always positive (yi² = 1). One update may not be enough to fix it, and it can break other points, so the perceptron keeps cycling through the data until a whole pass makes no mistake.
Say it“The new score of point i is its old score plus eta times the squared length of x-i plus one.”

A guarantee

Why it must stop: the convergence theorem

Does this cycling ever end? Albert Novikoff proved in 1962 that it must, whenever some line separates the classes. Fold the bias into the weights by giving every point a third coordinate that is always 1, so x̃ = (x₁, x₂, 1) and the rule becomes w̃ ← w̃ + y x̃. Let R be the length of the longest x̃, and suppose a unit vector u separates the data with room to spare: y u·x̃ ≥ γ for every point. Then the perceptron makes at most (R/γ)² mistakes in total, in any order. Narrow the gap, then run the perceptron in 200 random orders and compare the worst case with the bound.

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{\mathbf w}_k\cdot\mathbf u &\ge k\gamma \\ \lVert\tilde{\mathbf w}_k\rVert^2 &\le kR^2 \\ k\gamma \le \tilde{\mathbf w}_k\cdot\mathbf u \le \lVert\tilde{\mathbf w}_k\rVert &\le \sqrt{k}\,R \\ \Rightarrow\quad k &\le (R/\gamma)^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: line one: each mistake adds y x̃·u ≥ γ to w̃·u, so after k mistakes the weights have moved at least kγ in the good direction u. Line two: each mistake adds ‖x̃‖² + 2y w̃·x̃ ≤ R² to the squared length, because a mistake means y w̃·x̃ ≤ 0; so the length grows only like √k. Something growing like k cannot stay below something growing like √k for ever, so k is capped. A narrow gap (small γ) means a large cap.
Say it“w-k dot u is at least k gamma; the squared length of w-k is at most k R squared; so k gamma is at most root-k R, and k is at most R over gamma, squared.”

1969 · the limit

The XOR wall

Exclusive-or fires when exactly one input is on. On the square, its two “yes” corners, (0, 1) and (1, 0), sit diagonally opposite each other, with the two “no” corners on the other diagonal. No straight line can put both yes-corners on one side and both no-corners on the other, so no single neuron computes XOR, and the convergence theorem no longer applies. Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert’s book Perceptrons (1969) worked out such limits in detail; it is often blamed for a decade in which neural networks fell out of fashion. Pick XOR and let the perceptron try.

\[ \begin{aligned} (0,0)\mapsto 0 &:\; b \le 0 \\ (0,1)\mapsto 1 &:\; w_2 + b > 0 \\ (1,0)\mapsto 1 &:\; w_1 + b > 0 \\ (1,1)\mapsto 0 &:\; w_1 + w_2 + b \le 0 \\ \text{(2) + (3)} &:\; w_1 + w_2 + b > -b \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a proof by contradiction. The four rows of XOR’s truth table each demand an inequality of the weights. Adding rows (2) and (3) and using row (1) forces w₁ + w₂ + b to be positive, and row (4) forbids it. No weights satisfy all four, so at least one row is always wrong. The best any single neuron can do is 3 of 4.
Say it“b at most zero, w-two plus b positive, w-one plus b positive; adding the last two, w-one plus w-two plus b exceeds minus b, which is at least zero, yet the last row needs it at most zero.”

The way out

Over the wall: lift the inputs, or stack neurons

There are two ways round the wall, and both lead to the rest of this book. Lift: give the neuron a new input, the product x₁x₂. In three dimensions a flat plane can separate XOR, and back on the square its boundary curves (it is the kernel trick of chapter 1.9). Stack: let two neurons draw two lines, one for OR and one for NAND, and feed their answers to a third neuron that ANDs them together. That middle row of neurons is a hidden layer, and the result is a two-layer network. Switch between the two fixes and move the sliders until all four corners are right.

\[ \hat y = \operatorname{step}\big(x_1 + x_2 + w_3\,x_1x_2 - 0.5\big) \]
Read it as: the lift. The corners (0, 1) and (1, 0) score 0.5 and fire; (0, 0) scores −0.5 and doesn’t. Only (1, 1) depends on w₃: it scores 1.5 + w₃, so XOR needs w₃ ≤ −1.5.
Say it“Y-hat is the step of x-one plus x-two plus w-three x-one x-two minus a half.”
\[ \begin{aligned} h_1 &= \operatorname{step}(x_1 + x_2 - t_1) \\ h_2 &= \operatorname{step}(t_2 - x_1 - x_2) \\ \hat y &= \operatorname{step}(h_1 + h_2 - 1.5) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the stack. h₁ fires above one line, h₂ below another, and the output neuron fires only when both do: inside the band between the lines. With the inputs’ sum s = x₁ + x₂, the network answers [t₁ < s < t₂], which is XOR whenever 0 ≤ t₁ < 1 < t₂ ≤ 2.
Say it“h-one is the step of x-one plus x-two minus t-one, h-two the step of t-two minus x-one minus x-two, and y-hat the step of h-one plus h-two minus one and a half.”
corners right
noisy points right

Who sets the hidden weights? Not the perceptron rule: it needs a target for every neuron, and nobody tells us what h₁ and h₂ should have said. The answer, back-propagation, needs slopes to pass the blame backwards through the layers, which is why the smooth activations above replaced the step. That is chapter 2.2.

Real-world project

Project: mines or rocks? Listening to sonar

In 1988 Paul Gorman and Terrence Sejnowski trained neural networks to tell the sonar echo of a metal cylinder (a stand-in for a mine) from that of a rock on the sea floor. Each echo of a rising-frequency chirp was turned into 60 numbers: the energy in 60 frequency bands, from low to high, each between 0 and 1. The data, 111 mine echoes and 97 rock echoes from many angles, is on the UCI repository. We use Gorman & Sejnowski’s own split: 104 echoes to learn from (49 mines) and 104 to test on (62 mines), balanced across viewing angles.

The model is one perceptron with 60 weights and a bias, trained by Rosenblatt’s rule with η = 1, exactly as scikit-learn’s Perceptron does it. Because the rule depends on the order of the examples, we shuffle the training echoes with a fixed seed. Change the seed, then drag the epoch slider through the whole run.

The same project in scikit-learn

import re, sys, numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize
from sklearn.linear_model import Perceptron, SGDClassifier, LogisticRegression
DIR = "sonar/"   # sonar.mines and sonar.rocks from the UCI repository

def load(name, label):   # "*CM078: {60 numbers}"; a leading * marks Gorman & Sejnowski's training half
    rows = re.findall(r"^(\*?)C[MR]\d+:\s*\{([^}]*)\}", open(DIR + name).read(), re.M)
    return [(star == "*", [float(v) for v in vals.split()], label) for star, vals in rows]
data = load("sonar.mines", 1) + load("sonar.rocks", 0)       # 1 = mine, 0 = rock
X_tr = np.array([x for tr, x, _ in data if tr]); y_tr = np.array([y for tr, _, y in data if tr])
X_te = np.array([x for tr, x, _ in data if not tr]); y_te = np.array([y for tr, _, y in data if not tr])
print(f"train {len(y_tr)} ({y_tr.sum()} mines), test {len(y_te)} ({y_te.sum()} mines)")

print("seed  epochs  train   test | to zero mistakes: train  test | averaged: test")
for seed in range(10):
    o = np.random.RandomState(seed).permutation(len(y_tr)); X, y = X_tr[o], y_tr[o]
    p = Perceptron(shuffle=False).fit(X, y)                                # Rosenblatt's rule, eta = 1
    q = Perceptron(shuffle=False, max_iter=6000, tol=None).fit(X, y)      # no early stop
    a = SGDClassifier(loss="perceptron", learning_rate="constant", eta0=1, penalty=None,
                      average=True, shuffle=False, max_iter=p.n_iter_, tol=None).fit(X, y)
    print(f"{seed:4d} {p.n_iter_:7d} {p.score(X, y):6.3f} {p.score(X_te, y_te):6.3f} |"
          f" {q.score(X, y):23.3f} {q.score(X_te, y_te):5.3f} | {a.score(X_te, y_te):14.3f}")

lr = LogisticRegression().fit(X_tr, y_tr)                                  # one sigmoid neuron
print(f"logistic regression: train {lr.score(X_tr, y_tr):.3f}, test {lr.score(X_te, y_te):.3f}")

Xa = np.c_[X_tr, np.ones(len(y_tr))]; A = np.where(y_tr == 1, 1, -1)[:, None] * Xa   # bias as a weight on 1
u = minimize(lambda w: w @ w / 2, np.zeros(61), jac=lambda w: w, method="SLSQP", options={"ftol": 1e-14, "maxiter": 2000},
             constraints=[{"type": "ineq", "fun": lambda w: A @ w - 1, "jac": lambda w: A}]).x
R, gamma = np.linalg.norm(Xa, axis=1).max(), 1 / np.linalg.norm(u)
print(f"R = {R:.4f}, gamma = {gamma:.5f}, Novikoff bound (R/gamma)^2 = {(R / gamma) ** 2:,.0f} mistakes")

The page runs the same arithmetic as scikit-learn’s SGD loop (zero start, η = 1, an update whenever y·z ≤ 0, and its stopping rule: quit once the epoch’s total perceptron loss has failed to improve by 0.001 × 104 for five epochs in a row), so its weights agree with coef_ to rounding error. Logistic regression is solved here by Newton’s method to full precision, while scikit-learn’s L-BFGS stops at a tolerance, so those weights differ in the third decimal place; every prediction is the same. Gorman & Sejnowski’s figures are averages of 10 runs of their own back-propagation networks, quoted from their paper, not re-run here. The margin γ behind the Novikoff bound comes from a quadratic program solved offline with SciPy and is embedded in the page.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • An artificial neuron computes a weighted vote z = w·x + b and passes it through an activation; with a step, it fires on one side of a straight line (a hyperplane in more dimensions).
  • The perceptron rule learns from mistakes only: when y(w·x + b) ≤ 0, add ηyx to w and ηy to b. Starting from zero, η changes nothing but the scale.
  • If a separating line exists with margin γ and the points fit in radius R, the perceptron stops after at most (R/γ)² mistakes, whatever the order of the data.
  • One neuron cannot compute XOR or any other pattern that no line separates. Lifting the inputs or stacking neurons into a hidden layer gets over the wall.
  • Smooth activations (sigmoid, tanh, ReLU, GELU) give neurons slopes, which is what lets gradients train hidden layers. On sonar, a single perceptron gets roughly 75–85% of the test echoes right, but where it stops depends on the order of the data; averaging its weights steadies it.

Check your understanding

A neuron has w = (2, −1) and b = −1. What does it output for x = (1, 0.5)?

z = 2×1 + (−1)×0.5 − 1 = 0.5 > 0, so it fires: ŷ = 1.

The perceptron misclassifies a point with y = −1 and x = (3, 1). What happens to w and b (with η = 1)?

w loses (3, 1) and b drops by 1. The point’s score y(w·x + b) rises by ‖x‖² + 1 = 11, pushing it towards its own side.

Why does doubling η not change anything when the weights start at zero?

Every update adds η times ±x, so after any sequence of updates the weights are η times what they would be with η = 1. Multiplying w and b by a positive number leaves the sign of w·x + b, and so every decision and every later update, unchanged.

Novikoff’s bound is 400 mistakes for some data. You halve the gap between the classes. Roughly what does the bound become?

γ halves, so (R/γ)² quadruples: about 1,600 (a little less if R also shrinks). The bound says nothing about the actual count, which is usually far smaller.

Why can’t the perceptron rule train the hidden neurons of the XOR network?

The rule needs to know the right answer for the neuron it updates, and only the output neuron has a target. Hidden neurons need a way to share the output’s error, and with step activations there is no slope to share it through. Back-propagation with smooth activations solves this.
