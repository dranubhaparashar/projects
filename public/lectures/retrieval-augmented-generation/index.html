Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Look it up first, then answer, and show your sources

A language model answers from whatever it absorbed during training: it can be out of date, it cannot point to a source, and when it doesn’t know it may simply make something up. Retrieval-augmented generation hands it the right pages first. Every dot on the right is one passage from 25 real Wikipedia articles about the Solar System, laid out so that passages about similar things sit together. Ask the library a question: the retriever lights up the passages it would hand to the model, and the page quotes the sentence that answers you, with its source.

The idea

Retrieve, then read

Think of the difference between a closed-book and an open-book exam. A model answering from its weights is sitting the closed-book version: everything it knows was squeezed into its parameters months ago. Retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al., 2020) makes it open-book. A retriever scores every passage in a library against the question and keeps the top k; a reader (in practice an LLM) answers from those passages and cites them. The whole system can only be as good as what the retriever hands over: if the answer is not in the top k, the reader never sees it.

Below, the retriever’s scores are turned into probabilities over the passages it keeps. Change k and the temperature and watch how much of the probability lands on passages that actually contain the answer.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(y \mid q) &= \sum_{z \in \text{top-}k} p(z \mid q)\, p(y \mid q,z) \\ p(z \mid q) &= \frac{e^{\,s(q,z)/\tau}}{\sum_{z'} e^{\,s(q,z')/\tau}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: q is the question, z a retrieved passage, y the answer. The retriever gives each kept passage a score s(q, z); a softmax with temperature τ turns the scores into weights p(z | q). The reader then answers from each passage, p(y | q, z), and the two are mixed. Here no LLM is called, so p(y | q, z) is the best any reader could do: 1 if passage z contains the known answer, 0 if not. The sum is then the share of the retriever’s belief that sits on useful passages.
Say it“The chance of the answer is the sum over retrieved passages of how much the retriever trusts the passage times how well the reader answers from it.”

Step 1

Cutting documents into chunks

A retriever doesn’t hand over whole articles: a 1,600-word article would drown the answer and burn the reader’s context window. So each document is cut into chunks, and chunks are what get scored and retrieved. Two common recipes: fixed windows of W words, which may slice a sentence (or an answer) in half, and sentence packing, which fills each chunk with whole sentences up to W words. An overlap repeats the end of one chunk at the start of the next, so a fact near a boundary appears whole somewhere. Many systems also paste the document’s title onto every chunk, so “the planet” in a sentence still matches “Saturn” in a question. Try sizes from 20 to 300 words and watch recall and the reading bill pull against each other.

\[ n_{\text{chunks}} = \left\lceil \frac{L - O}{W - O} \right\rceil \qquad (L > W) \]
Read it as: a document of L words cut into windows of W words that overlap by O words starts a new window every W − O words, so it needs about (L − O)/(W − O) of them, rounded up. Overlap buys safety at the boundaries and pays for it in more chunks to index and more repeated text in the results.
Say it“The number of chunks is L minus O over W minus O, rounded up.”

Step 2 · matching words

BM25: scoring by shared words

The oldest good idea in search is still hard to beat: a chunk is relevant if it contains the question’s words, especially the rare ones. “Canyon” appears in one chunk of this library; “planet” appears in 213 of the 452, so sharing it proves little. That rarity is the inverse document frequency. BM25 (Robertson and colleagues, 1994) adds two refinements: the tenth repetition of a word adds far less than the first (saturation, set by k₁), and a long chunk is expected to contain more words by chance (length normalisation, set by b). Before any of this, both the question and the chunks are lower-cased, stripped of words like “the” and lightly stemmed (“moons” → “moon”). Pick a question, then move k₁ and b.

\[ \text{idf}(t) = \ln\!\Big(1 + \frac{N - n_t + 0.5}{n_t + 0.5}\Big) \]
Read it as: N chunks in the library, nt of them contain term t. A term in almost every chunk gets an idf near 0; a term in one chunk gets the most. (This is the form Lucene and Elasticsearch use; it never goes negative.)
Say it“The idf of t is the log of one plus N minus n-t plus a half, over n-t plus a half.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{BM25}(q,c) &= \sum_{t \in q} \text{idf}(t)\; g_{t,c} \\ g_{t,c} &= \frac{f_{t,c}\,(k_1+1)}{f_{t,c} + k_1 B_c} \\ B_c &= 1 - b + b\,|c| \,/\, \overline{|c|} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ft,c is how often t occurs in chunk c, |c| the chunk’s length in terms and the barred |c| the average length over all chunks (avgdl), so Bc is the length factor. g rises with f but levels off at k₁ + 1; b = 1 fully penalises long chunks, b = 0 ignores length.
Say it“BM25 is the sum over query terms of idf times a saturating, length-normalised term count.”

Keeping score

Recall@k and MRR: grading a retriever

“It found the right thing for my test question” is not an evaluation. Write questions whose answers you know, say which chunks count as relevant, and measure. Here a chunk is relevant if it comes from the right article and contains the known answer string, the convention used by Karpukhin et al. (2020) for open-domain question answering. Two numbers tell most of the story. Recall@k: for what share of questions is a relevant chunk in the top k? That is what the reader gets to see. MRR: on average, how high is the first relevant chunk? It rewards putting the answer first. Compare three scoring rules, from crude word overlap to BM25, and move k.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{recall@}k &= \frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q \in Q} \mathbb{1}\big[\,r_q \le k\,\big] \\ \text{MRR} &= \frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q \in Q} \frac{1}{r_q} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: rq is the rank of the first relevant chunk for question q (1 is the top). Recall@k counts the questions where that rank is k or better. MRR averages 1/r: a hit at rank 1 earns 1, rank 2 earns ½, rank 10 earns 0.1. Neither cares how many relevant chunks there are, only about the first.
Say it“Recall at k is the share of questions answered within the top k; MRR is the average of one over the rank of the first right answer.”

Step 2, again · matching meaning

Embeddings: scoring by meaning

Word matching has a blind spot: “How old is the Sun?” shares no useful word with “The Sun formed approximately 4.6 billion years ago.” An embedding maps every chunk and every question to a vector so that texts about the same thing point the same way, whatever words they use; retrieval becomes “find the nearest vectors”. Modern systems use neural encoders trained on millions of question–passage pairs, which this page cannot run. It builds the classic ancestor instead, right here in your browser: latent semantic analysis (Deerwester et al., 1990). Words that keep appearing in the same chunks (lava, flows, volcanic) get folded onto shared directions, the k strongest patterns of co-occurrence in the library. Type a word and slide k to see which words LSA thinks belong together.

\[ \begin{aligned} X &\approx U_k\,\Sigma_k\,V_k^{\top} \\ \hat{\mathbf q} &= \mathbf x_q V_k, \qquad \cos(\mathbf a,\mathbf b) = \frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\Vert\mathbf a\Vert\,\Vert\mathbf b\Vert} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: X is the chunk × term TF-IDF matrix (452 × 2,226). The singular value decomposition keeps its k strongest directions: row i of UkΣk is chunk i’s embedding, and a question’s TF-IDF vector xq is projected into the same space by Vk. Similarity is the cosine of the angle between vectors. With every direction kept (k = 452), LSA is exactly TF-IDF cosine again.
Say it“X is approximately U-k Sigma-k V-k transpose; the query vector is x-q times V-k, and similarity is the cosine of the angle.”

Step 3 · at scale

Vector search: finding neighbours fast

Comparing a question with all 452 chunks takes a blink. Comparing it with a billion, each a 768-number vector, does not. Vector databases therefore search approximately. The simplest scheme is the inverted file (IVF): cluster the vectors once with k-means into C cells; at query time, find the cells whose centres are nearest the question, and scan only the vectors inside those nprobe cells. Below, the library is split into 16 cells (coloured on the map). Probe more or fewer cells and compare what IVF finds with the exact top 10.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{exact:}\quad & n\,d \\ \text{IVF:}\quad & C\,d \;+\; \frac{n_{\text{probe}}}{C}\,n\,d \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: n vectors of d numbers; an exact search costs n × d multiply-adds per question. IVF first compares the question with the C cell centres, then scans only the probed cells, on average nprobe/C of the library. The price is recall: a true neighbour sitting just across a cell border is never looked at. FAISS, the library behind many vector databases, offers exactly this index (IndexIVFFlat), plus compressed and graph-based (HNSW) variants.
Say it“Exact search costs n times d; IVF costs C times d plus n-probe over C times n times d.”

Two opinions

Hybrid search: fusing two rankings

BM25 and embeddings fail on different questions: BM25 on paraphrases, embeddings on rare names and exact numbers. Their scores live on different scales (BM25 is unbounded, cosine sits between −1 and 1), so adding them is awkward. Reciprocal rank fusion (Cormack, Clarke & Büttcher, 2009) ignores the scores and fuses the ranks: a chunk near the top of either list floats up. It needs no training, which is why Elasticsearch, OpenSearch and several vector databases offer it for hybrid search. Shift the weight between the two lists and change the constant k.

\[ \text{RRF}(c) = \sum_{m} \frac{w_m}{k + \text{rank}_m(c)} \]
Read it as: each method m (here BM25 and LSA) ranks every chunk; a chunk earns wm/(k + its rank) from each list, and the earnings are added. The constant k (60 in the original paper) flattens the reward: with a small k, rank 1 is worth far more than rank 5; with a large k they are nearly equal, so agreement between the lists matters more than either list’s top pick.
Say it“The fused score of a chunk is the sum over methods of the weight over k plus that method's rank.”

Step 4 · a closer look

Re-ranking: a second, slower opinion

First-stage retrieval must be fast enough to score everything, so it looks at each chunk crudely. A re-ranker only has to look at the top 20, so it can afford to look harder. Production systems use a cross-encoder, a transformer that reads the question and the chunk together (Nogueira & Cho, 2019). This page uses an honest, small stand-in: a logistic regression on four features of each (question, chunk) pair, trained on the question set. f₁ is BM25 scaled to 0–1, f₂ the LSA cosine, f₃ the share of the question’s idf weight that the chunk covers, and f₄ the same share inside the single best sentence (with the article title counted in), a crude test of whether the words sit together. Set the weights yourself, then let logistic regression fit them.

\[ p(\text{rel} \mid q, c) = \sigma\big(\mathbf w \cdot \mathbf f(q,c) + w_0\big) \]
Read it as: f(q, c) holds the four features; σ(z) = 1/(1 + e−z) squashes the weighted sum into a probability that the chunk is relevant. Sorting the 20 candidates by it gives the new order. The bias w0 does not change the order, but it makes the probabilities meaningful, which the next section needs.
Say it“The probability of relevance is the sigmoid of w dot f plus w-nought.”
\[ \min_{\mathbf w,\,w_0}\; \tfrac12\Vert\mathbf w\Vert^2 + C\sum_i \ell\big(y_i,\,p_i\big) \]
Read it as: yi = 1 when candidate i contains the answer. ℓ is the log-loss −[y ln p + (1 − y) ln(1 − p)]; the ½‖w‖² term (C = 1, as in scikit-learn) keeps the weights modest. Newton’s method finds the minimum in a few steps.
Say it“Minimise half the squared weights plus C times the total log-loss.”

Step 5 · the answer

Grounded answers, citations, and knowing when to stop

The last step assembles a prompt: an instruction to answer only from the numbered sources and to cite them, the sources themselves, and the question. A real system would send it to an LLM. This page does not call one, so a transparent stand-in plays the reader: an extractive reader that quotes the single sentence of the sources that covers most of the question’s idf weight, and cites where it came from. It cannot paraphrase or combine facts, but it can never invent one either. The re-ranker’s top probability also tells us when the library probably has no answer: below a threshold τ, the honest reply is “I can’t find that in my sources”. Ten of the 97 questions are deliberately unanswerable from this library. Move the threshold and watch the trade between answering more and being wrong less.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{answer} &\iff \max_c\, p(\text{rel}\mid q,c) \ge \tau \\ \text{coverage} &= \frac{\#\,\text{answered}}{|Q|} \\ \text{accuracy} &= \frac{\#\,\text{correct}}{\#\,\text{answered}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the system answers only when its most confident source clears τ. Coverage is the share of all questions it attempts; accuracy is measured on the attempted ones, and any attempt at an unanswerable question counts as wrong. Raising τ trades coverage for accuracy; this is called selective answering.
Say it“Answer only if the best source's probability is at least tau; coverage is answered over all, accuracy is correct over answered.”

Nothing here was tuned on a held-out set except the re-ranker, which is cross-fitted: questions are split into two halves by index, and each half is re-ranked by a model trained on the other half.

The same pipeline in Python


import json, re, math, numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
docs, qs, stop = (json.load(open(f, encoding="utf8")) for f in ("rg-docs.json", "rg-qs.json", "rg-stop.json"))
stop = set(stop)

def stem(w):                                  # Harman (1991) S-stemmer
    if len(w) <= 3: return w
    if w.endswith("ies") and not w.endswith(("eies", "aies")): return w[:-3] + "y"
    if w.endswith("es") and not w.endswith(("aes", "ees", "oes")): return w[:-1]
    if w.endswith("s") and not w.endswith(("us", "ss")): return w[:-1]
    return w
def tok(t): return [stem(w) for w in re.findall(r"[^\W_]+", t.lower()) if w not in stop and not (len(w) == 1 and w.isalpha())]

chunks = []                                   # pack whole sentences, up to 100 words, title on every chunk
for d, doc in enumerate(docs):
    ss = [s for _, sents in doc["p"] for s in sents]; i = 0
    while i < len(ss):
        j, n = i, 0
        while j < len(ss) and (j == i or n + len(ss[j].split()) <= 100): n += len(ss[j].split()); j += 1
        chunks.append(dict(d=d, sents=ss[i:j], text=" ".join(ss[i:j]), tok=tok(doc["t"] + " " + " ".join(ss[i:j])))); i = j
N = len(chunks); dl = np.array([len(c["tok"]) for c in chunks]); avgdl = dl.mean()
df = {}
for c in chunks:
    for t in set(c["tok"]): df[t] = df.get(t, 0) + 1
idf = lambda t: math.log(1 + (N - df.get(t, 0) + 0.5) / (df.get(t, 0) + 0.5))
def bm25(q, k1=1.2, b=0.75):
    s = np.zeros(N)
    for t in set(tok(q)):
        for i, c in enumerate(chunks):
            f = c["tok"].count(t)
            if f: s[i] += idf(t) * f * (k1 + 1) / (f + k1 * (1 - b + b * dl[i] / avgdl))
    return s

vocab = sorted(t for t in df if df[t] >= 2); ix = {t: i for i, t in enumerate(vocab)}   # LSA on TF-IDF
sidf = np.array([math.log((1 + N) / (1 + df[t])) + 1 for t in vocab])
def tfidf(ts):
    v = np.zeros(len(vocab))
    for t in ts:
        if t in ix: v[ix[t]] += 1
    v *= sidf; n = np.linalg.norm(v); return v / n if n else v
X = np.array([tfidf(c["tok"]) for c in chunks])
U, sig, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X, full_matrices=False); K = 128
E = U[:, :K] * sig[:K]
def lsa(q): qh = tfidf(tok(q)) @ Vt[:K].T; return E @ qh / (np.linalg.norm(E, axis=1) * np.linalg.norm(qh) + 1e-300)
order = lambda s: np.lexsort((np.arange(len(s)), -s))
def rrf(*orders, k=60):
    s = np.zeros(N)
    for o in orders: s[o] += 1 / (k + np.arange(1, N + 1))
    return s

def rel(q):
    ds = q[0] if isinstance(q[0], list) else [q[0]]
    pat = re.compile("|".join(r"(?<!\w)" + re.escape(a.lower()) + r"(?!\w)" for a in q[2]))
    return np.array([ds[0] >= 0 and c["d"] in ds and bool(pat.search(c["text"].lower())) for c in chunks])
def first(o, r): h = np.nonzero(r[o])[0]; return h[0] + 1 if len(h) else None
def report(name, ranks):
    R = lambda k: np.mean([r is not None and r <= k for r in ranks])
    print(f"{name:20s} R@1 {R(1):.4f}  R@3 {R(3):.4f}  R@5 {R(5):.4f}  R@10 {R(10):.4f}  MRR {np.mean([1 / r if r else 0 for r in ranks]):.4f}")

rows, ranks = [], {"BM25": [], "LSA (k=128)": [], "RRF fusion": []}
for qi, q in enumerate(qs):
    sb, sl = bm25(q[1]), lsa(q[1]); ob, ol = order(sb), order(sl); oh = order(rrf(ob, ol)); r = rel(q)
    if q[0] != -1:
        for name, o in zip(ranks, (ob, ol, oh)): ranks[name].append(first(o, r))
    qt = set(tok(q[1])); w = {t: idf(t) for t in qt}; tot = sum(w.values()) or 1
    cov = lambda ts: sum(w[t] for t in qt if t in ts) / tot
    for i in oh[:20]:                         # re-ranker features of the top 20 candidates
        title = set(tok(docs[chunks[i]["d"]]["t"]))
        f = [sb[i] / (tot * 2.2), sl[i], cov(set(chunks[i]["tok"])), max(cov(set(tok(s)) | title) for s in chunks[i]["sents"])]
        rows.append((qi, i, f, int(r[i])))
for name, rk in ranks.items(): report(name, rk)

F = np.array([x[2] for x in rows]); y = np.array([x[3] for x in rows]); qid = np.array([x[0] for x in rows])
ans = np.array([qs[x[0]][0] != -1 for x in rows]); P = np.zeros(len(y))
for h in (0, 1):                              # cross-fitting: train on one half of the questions, score the other
    m = LogisticRegression(C=1.0, tol=1e-10, max_iter=10000).fit(F[(qid % 2 != h) & ans], y[(qid % 2 != h) & ans])
    P[qid % 2 == h] = m.predict_proba(F[qid % 2 == h])[:, 1]
    print(f"re-ranker fitted on half {1 - h}: w = {np.round(m.coef_[0], 4)}, w0 = {m.intercept_[0]:.4f}")
rr, top, ok = [], [], []
for qi, q in enumerate(qs):
    s = np.nonzero(qid == qi)[0]; o = s[np.lexsort((np.arange(len(s)), -P[s]))]; top.append(P[o[0]])
    if q[0] != -1: rr.append(int(np.nonzero(y[o])[0][0]) + 1 if y[o].any() else None)
    qt = set(tok(q[1])); w = {t: idf(t) for t in qt}; tot = sum(w.values()) or 1; best = None
    for x in o[:3]:                           # extractive reader: best-covered sentence of the top 3 sources
        c = chunks[rows[x][1]]; title = set(tok(docs[c["d"]]["t"]))
        for sent in c["sents"]:
            sc = sum(w[t] for t in qt if t in set(tok(sent)) | title) / tot
            if best is None or sc > best[0] + 1e-12: best = (sc, c, sent)
    ok.append(q[0] != -1 and rel([q[0], q[1], q[2]])[chunks.index(best[1])] and any(a.lower() in best[2].lower() for a in q[2]))
report("fusion + re-ranker", rr)
top, ok, una = np.array(top), np.array(ok), np.array([q[0] == -1 for q in qs])
print(f"reader quotes a correct sentence for {ok.sum()} of {(~una).sum()} answerable questions")
for tau in (0.0, 0.15, 0.3):
    a = top >= tau
    print(f"tau {tau:.2f}: answers {a.sum()}/{len(qs)}, correct {(a & ok).sum()}, accuracy {(a & ok).sum() / max(a.sum(), 1):.4f}, unanswerable declined {(~a & una).sum()}/{una.sum()}")
i = order(bm25("What is the tallest volcano on Mars?"))[:3]
print("BM25, 'What is the tallest volcano on Mars?':", [(int(c), round(float(bm25("What is the tallest volcano on Mars?")[c]), 6)) for c in i])
print(f"chunks {N}, LSA vocabulary {len(vocab)}, top singular values {np.round(sig[:3], 6)}")

The browser’s pipeline is a separate JavaScript implementation of the same recipe, not a translation of this listing. Its chunks, tokens and vocabulary are identical; its BM25 scores agree with Python to within 4×10−15 and its LSA cosines to within 10−14 (the browser gets the singular vectors from an eigendecomposition of X XT, Python from an SVD of X); its logistic-regression weights match scikit-learn’s to 5 decimal places, so every rank and every score in the table is the same. Two honest caveats. The question set is small (87 answerable questions), so a difference of one or two questions, about 2 points of recall, is noise. And LSA is a 1990 method: modern neural embeddings would close much of the gap to BM25 on this kind of question, but they need a model this page cannot load.

  







  

    
Wrap-up

    
Five lines to remember

    
    
      
  • RAG makes a model sit an open-book exam: retrieve the top k chunks, answer only from them, and cite them. The reader can never use what the retriever missed.
    • 
      
  • Chunking is a real design choice: bigger chunks raise recall@k but cost more context per result; whole sentences and a title on every chunk keep facts intact.
    • 
      
  • BM25 (idf × a saturating, length-normalised count) is a strong, cheap baseline; embeddings catch paraphrases but can miss rare names and numbers.
    • 
      
  • Fuse the two with reciprocal rank fusion, then let a slower re-ranker re-read the top 20; at scale, approximate search (IVF, HNSW) trades a little recall for a lot of speed.
    • 
      
  • Measure with questions whose answers you know (recall@k, MRR, answer accuracy), and let the system say “not in my sources” when its best evidence is weak.
    • 
    

    
Check your understanding

    

      
Your RAG chatbot gives a wrong answer. How do you tell whether retrieval or generation is to blame?
Look at what was retrieved. If no retrieved chunk contains the answer, the retriever failed and no reader could have succeeded (recall@k is the ceiling). If the answer was in the sources and the reader still got it wrong, the reading or prompting failed. This is why retrieval is evaluated on its own.

      
Why does BM25 give “Triton” much more weight than “planet” in “Which planet does Triton orbit?”
Because idf rewards rarity: “planet” appears in nearly half of the chunks, so sharing it says little about relevance, while “Triton” appears in few. A chunk that shares only common words gets a low score.

      
Doubling the chunk size raised recall@5. Why might you still not do it?
Each retrieved chunk is twice as long, so five of them cost twice the context tokens (money, latency) and bury the answer among more irrelevant text, which can hurt the reader. Compare recall at an equal reading budget, not at an equal k.

      
Why fuse BM25 and embedding results by rank rather than by adding their scores?
The scores are on incomparable scales (BM25 is unbounded and varies with the question; cosine lies in [−1, 1]), so any sum needs per-query normalisation. Ranks are on the same scale for every method, so reciprocal rank fusion works without tuning.

      
An IVF index probes 2 of 16 cells. Roughly what fraction of the vectors is scanned, and what can go wrong?
About 2/16 = 12.5% (more if those cells are large). A true nearest neighbour just across a cell border is never examined, so recall of the exact top-k drops; probing more cells recovers it at higher cost.
