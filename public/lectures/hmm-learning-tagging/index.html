Learning HMMs: Baum–Welch, Extensions & POS Tagging

Where the transition and emission numbers come from, and what they buy you

Chapter 6.14 took a hidden Markov model as given and asked what its hidden states had been doing. This chapter asks where the model comes from. When the states are written down, learning is counting; when they are hidden, the Baum–Welch algorithm counts them in expectation, and that one idea carries from a crooked casino to speech and to grammar. The figure is a real part-of-speech tagger: an HMM whose 17 hidden states are the Universal Dependencies word classes, learned by counting 204,578 hand-tagged words of English web text. Each column is a word; the shading is the posterior probability of each tag from forward–backward, and the line is the single best tag sequence from Viterbi. Learning when the states are hidden

A hidden Markov model has three sets of numbers: the initial probabilities π, the transition matrix A, and the emission probabilities φ that say how each state produces observations. Chapter 6.14 assumed someone handed them over. In practice they are learned from data, and learning a probabilistic model, in the framing of AIMA chapter 20, means choosing parameters that make the observed data probable. That splits into two very different cases.

With complete data every variable is observed: you see the hidden state at every step as well as the observation. Then maximum likelihood is plain counting. The estimate of Ajk is the number of times state j was followed by state k, divided by the number of times j was followed by anything; the emission estimate is the same with states and symbols. With hidden variables the states are never seen, the likelihood becomes a sum over every possible state path, and its logarithm no longer splits into counts. The general tool for that situation is the EM algorithm (AIMA 20.3); applied to an HMM it is called Baum–Welch (AIMA 20.3.3), after Leonard Baum and Lloyd Welch, who developed it around 1970.

The running example is the dishonest casino from Durbin and co-authors’ Biological Sequence Analysis. A dealer usually rolls a fair die, but now and then swaps in a loaded die that shows a six half the time, and later swaps back. Here the fair die is kept with probability 0.95 per roll and the loaded one with probability 0.90. Slide the number of rolls, then hide the dealer’s log: with the log you count; without it, Baum–Welch has to work out which rolls were loaded at the same time as it learns how loaded the die is.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat A_{jk} &= \frac{n_{jk}}{\sum_{l} n_{jl}} \\ \hat\phi_k(v) &= \frac{m_k(v)}{\sum_{u} m_k(u)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: njk is the number of steps where state j was followed by state k, and mk(v) the number of times state k showed symbol v. Each estimate is a count divided by the total of its row, so every row sums to 1. This is the maximum-likelihood answer when the states are observed, and it is all an HMM part-of-speech tagger needs, because a tagged corpus is complete data.
Say it“A-hat j k is the number of j-to-k moves over the number of moves out of j.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf X \mid \theta) &= \sum_{\mathbf Z} p(\mathbf X, \mathbf Z \mid \theta) \\ &= \sum_{\mathbf Z} p(\mathbf z_1 \mid \boldsymbol\pi) \\ &\quad \times \prod_{n=2}^{N} p(\mathbf z_n \mid \mathbf z_{n-1}, \mathbf A) \\ &\quad \times \prod_{n=1}^{N} p(\mathbf x_n \mid \mathbf z_n, \boldsymbol\phi) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: when the state path Z is hidden, the likelihood of the observations X is the complete-data likelihood summed over every path. There are KN paths, and the logarithm of a sum does not break into separate counts, so there is no closed-form maximiser. The forward algorithm still evaluates the sum in O(NK²) time; Baum–Welch climbs it.
Say it“The likelihood of X is the sum over all state paths Z of the joint probability of X and Z.”

Baum–Welch: expected counts instead of counts

The way out is a bargain. If we knew the states, we could count. If we knew the parameters, forward–backward (chapter 6.14) would tell us, for every step, how probable each state was. EM alternates the two. The E-step runs forward–backward with the current parameters θold and keeps two kinds of posterior: γ(zn), the probability of each state at step n, and ξ(zn−1, zn), the probability of each pair of consecutive states. The M-step treats those probabilities as fractional counts and re-estimates every parameter with the counting formulas from the last section. A roll that is 70% likely to have come from the loaded die adds 0.7 to the loaded die’s tally for that face.

Why does that climb the likelihood? The M-step maximises the function Q below: the expected complete-data log-likelihood, where the expectation uses the posterior under θold. Q splits into three independent pieces, one for π, one for A and one for the emissions, and each is maximised by normalised expected counts (with Lagrange multipliers keeping each row summing to 1). The general EM argument (chapter 1.12a) shows that raising Q can never lower the true log-likelihood, so the likelihood rises or stays put at every round. It converges to a local maximum, which depends on where it starts. Two starts teach the most: a symmetric start, where both states are identical and stay identical forever, and a start with a zero in A, which stays zero because every ξ for that transition is multiplied by it. That is how left-to-right models used in speech keep their shape. Press E-step and M-step in turn, then run to convergence from each start.

\[ \begin{aligned} & Q(\theta, \theta^{\text{old}}) = \sum_{k} \gamma(z_{1k}) \ln \pi_k \\ &\;+ \sum_{n=2}^{N} \sum_{j,k} \xi(z_{n-1,j}, z_{nk}) \ln A_{jk} \\ &\;+ \sum_{n=1}^{N} \sum_{k} \gamma(z_{nk}) \ln p(\mathbf x_n \mid \boldsymbol\phi_k) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: take the complete-data log-likelihood, which would be a sum of logs of counted things, and replace each indicator “the chain was in state k at step n” by its posterior probability γ(znk), and each “moved from j to k at step n” by ξ(zn−1,j, znk). The three lines involve different parameters, so the M-step maximises each separately.
Say it“Q is gamma-weighted log pi, plus xi-weighted log A, plus gamma-weighted log emission.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \gamma(\mathbf z_n) &= \hat\alpha(\mathbf z_n)\, \hat\beta(\mathbf z_n) \\ \xi(\mathbf z_{n-1}, \mathbf z_n) &= \frac{\hat\alpha(\mathbf z_{n-1})\, p(\mathbf x_n \mid \mathbf z_n)}{c_n} \\ &\quad \times p(\mathbf z_n \mid \mathbf z_{n-1})\, \hat\beta(\mathbf z_n) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the scaled forward and backward messages of chapter 6.14 (with cn the scaling factors) give both posteriors directly. ξ joins the forward message arriving at the earlier step to the backward message leaving the later one, through the transition and the new observation.
Say it“Gamma is alpha-hat times beta-hat; xi is alpha-hat before, times emission, times transition, times beta-hat after, over c n.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \pi_k &= \frac{\gamma(z_{1k})}{\sum_{j} \gamma(z_{1j})} \\ A_{jk} &= \frac{\sum_{n=2}^{N} \xi(z_{n-1,j}, z_{nk})}{\sum_{l} \sum_{n=2}^{N} \xi(z_{n-1,j}, z_{nl})} \\ \mu_{ik} &= \frac{\sum_{n} \gamma(z_{nk})\, x_{ni}}{\sum_{n} \gamma(z_{nk})} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the counting formulas again, with expected counts. For a discrete emission, xni is 1 when roll n shows face i, so μik is the expected number of times state k showed face i, over the expected number of visits to k.
Say it“A j k is the expected number of j-to-k moves over the expected number of moves out of j.”

PRML 13.2.1 · Gaussian emissions, initialisation and many sequences

Continuous emissions, restarts and many sequences

Nothing in the E-step cares what the emissions look like; it only needs the number p(xn | zn = k) for each step and state. The M-step for the emissions is the one place the choice matters, and for Gaussian emissions it is the familiar weighted mean and weighted covariance from a Gaussian mixture, with the responsibilities γ as weights. In fact an HMM whose transition matrix has identical rows is a mixture model: the state no longer depends on the previous one.

Three practical points come with it. First, initialisation: Bishop suggests random positive values for π and A, and for the emissions something data-driven, such as K-means on the observations treated as independent. Second, local maxima: different starts converge to different answers, so run several and keep the one with the highest likelihood. Third, many sequences: with R independent sequences, run forward–backward on each and add their expected counts before normalising; their log-likelihoods add too. (The same holds for the tagger, whose training data is thousands of separate sentences.) A Gaussian state can also cheat by shrinking its variance onto a single data point and sending the likelihood to infinity, as in mixtures; the lab keeps every variance above a small floor. The signal below was drawn from a three-state model with levels near −1.5, 0 and 2. Choose K, then press 10 restarts and look at how the final likelihoods spread.

\[ \begin{aligned} N_k &= \sum_{n=1}^{N} \gamma(z_{nk}) \\ \boldsymbol\mu_k &= \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \gamma(z_{nk})\, \mathbf x_n \\ \boldsymbol\Sigma_k &= \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \gamma(z_{nk}) \\ &\quad \times (\mathbf x_n - \boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf x_n - \boldsymbol\mu_k)^{\top} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Nk is the expected number of steps spent in state k. Each state’s mean is the average of the data weighted by how responsible that state is for each point, and its covariance is the weighted spread around that mean. In one dimension Σk is just the variance σk².
Say it“Mu k is the gamma-weighted average of x; Sigma k is the gamma-weighted spread about mu k.”
\[ \begin{aligned} A_{jk} &= \frac{\sum_{r=1}^{R} \sum_{n=2}^{N_r} \xi^{(r)}_n(j,k)}{\sum_{l} \sum_{r} \sum_{n} \xi^{(r)}_n(j,l)} \\ \ln p(\mathbf X^{(1:R)}) &= \sum_{r=1}^{R} \ln p(\mathbf X^{(r)}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ξ(r)n(j, k) is the posterior probability that sequence r moved from state j to state k at step n. For R independent sequences, every expected count is summed over sequences before dividing, and the total log-likelihood is the sum of the separate ones. No transition is counted across the boundary between two sequences, and every sequence contributes its own γ(z1) to π.
Say it“Add the expected counts over all sequences, then normalise.”

PRML 13.2.3

The same algorithm as message passing

The HMM’s graph is a tree: a chain of hidden states with one observation hanging off each. So the sum-product algorithm of chapter 6.5 applies to it directly, and it turns out to be forward–backward. Bishop’s derivation in two moves: draw the factor graph, with one factor for p(z1), one for each transition and one for each emission; then, because every xn is observed, absorb each emission into the transition factor that ends at the same step. What is left is a chain of hidden variables joined by factors fn(zn−1, zn).

On a chain, a variable node with two neighbours simply passes along the message it receives, so only the factor-to-variable messages matter. The message travelling right into zn satisfies exactly the forward recursion, so it is α(zn); the one travelling left is β(zn). The product of the two messages arriving at a node is the joint p(zn, X), and summing it over zn gives the same p(X) at every node. The max-sum version, with sums replaced by maxima, is Viterbi. Click a roll to change it and click a node to read its messages; the numbers are checked against brute force over all 64 state paths.

\[ \begin{aligned} h(\mathbf z_1) &= p(\mathbf z_1)\, p(\mathbf x_1 \mid \mathbf z_1) \\ f_n(\mathbf z_{n-1}, \mathbf z_n) &= p(\mathbf z_n \mid \mathbf z_{n-1}) \\ &\quad \times p(\mathbf x_n \mid \mathbf z_n) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the reduced factor graph has one factor at the start and one factor per transition, and the observed emission at step n is folded into the factor that arrives at step n. The joint p(X, Z) is the product of all of them.
Say it“f n of z n-minus-1 and z n is the transition into z n times the emission from z n.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_{f_n \to z_n}(z_n) &= \sum_{z_{n-1}} f_n(z_{n-1}, z_n) \\ &\quad \times \mu_{f_{n-1} \to z_{n-1}}(z_{n-1}) \\ &= \alpha(z_n) \\ \mu_{f_{n+1} \to z_n}(z_n) &= \sum_{z_{n+1}} f_{n+1}(z_n, z_{n+1}) \\ &\quad \times \mu_{f_{n+2} \to z_{n+1}}(z_{n+1}) \\ &= \beta(z_n) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the rightward message multiplies the previous message by the factor and sums out the earlier variable: the forward recursion, started from h(z1). The leftward one is the backward recursion, started from 1 at the last node.
Say it“The message from f n into z n is alpha; the message from f n-plus-1 into z n is beta.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(z_n, \mathbf X) &= \mu_{f_n \to z_n}\, \mu_{f_{n+1} \to z_n} \\ &= \alpha(z_n)\,\beta(z_n) \\ p(\mathbf X) &= \sum_{z_n} \alpha(z_n)\,\beta(z_n) \\ \xi(z_{n-1}, z_n) &= \alpha(z_{n-1})\, f_n(z_{n-1}, z_n) \\ &\quad \times \beta(z_n) \,/\, p(\mathbf X) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a node’s marginal is the product of the messages arriving from both sides, and summing it over the node’s values gives p(X) whichever n you pick; dividing by p(X) makes it the posterior γ. The pairwise posterior ξ needed by Baum–Welch is the factor-graph rule for the marginal of the variables attached to one factor: the factor times the messages arriving at it from both ends.
Say it“The joint of z n and X is alpha times beta; p of X is its sum, the same at every n.”

AIMA 23, 23.1, 23.1.6 · Universal Dependencies

Part-of-speech tagging with an HMM

Natural language processing (AIMA chapter 23) begins with language models: probability distributions over strings of words (23.1). The bag-of-words and n-gram models assign those probabilities from word counts alone. A step up is to give each word a part of speech (a lexical category such as noun, verb or determiner) and let the model reason about categories, which generalise far better than individual words. Many words are ambiguous: in the training data below, that is a subordinating conjunction 987 times, a pronoun 787 times and a determiner 160 times. Choosing the right category for each word in context is part-of-speech tagging (23.1.6).

An HMM tagger treats the tags as the hidden states and the words as the observations. It has four parts. The tag set: here the 17 Universal Dependencies tags (UPOS). The transition model P(tn | tn−1): a determiner is very often followed by a noun or an adjective, almost never by a verb. The sensor model P(w | t), confusingly called the emission model: how likely a noun is to be the word table. And the prior P(t1) for the first tag. A treebank is complete data, so all four are learned by counting, smoothed with add-k so that no unseen transition or word gets probability zero. Words the tagger never learned are replaced by a class built from their spelling (a digit, a capital letter, endings such as -ing, -ed, -ly), whose emission counts come from the training words seen only once: rare words are the best guide to unknown ones. Decoding is Viterbi. Click a row of the transition matrix, and try ambiguous words.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat t_{1:N} &= \arg\max_{t_{1:N}} P(t_{1:N} \mid w_{1:N}) \\ &= \arg\max_{t_{1:N}} P(t_1)\, P(w_1 \mid t_1) \\ &\quad \times \prod_{n=2}^{N} P(t_n \mid t_{n-1})\, P(w_n \mid t_n) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the best tag sequence is the one with the highest posterior given the words. Bayes’ rule drops the denominator P(w1:N), which is the same for every tag sequence, and the HMM factorisation turns what is left into one transition and one emission per word. Viterbi finds the maximiser in O(NK²) time instead of checking all 17N sequences.
Say it“t-hat is the arg max over tag sequences of prior times emissions times transitions.”
\[ \begin{aligned} P(k \mid j) &= \frac{C(j \to k) + k_t}{C(j) + k_t K} \\ P(w \mid t) &= \frac{C(t, w) + k_e}{C(t) + k_e V} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: count how often tag j was followed by tag k, and how often tag t was the word w, in the training sentences; add a small pseudo-count to every cell so that nothing is impossible; normalise. K = 17 tags; V is the vocabulary size: here 9,873 words seen at least twice plus 11 unknown-word classes.
Say it“P of k given j is the count of j-to-k plus k-t, over the count of j plus K times k-t.”
\[ \begin{aligned} P(w_{1:N}) &= \sum_{t_{1:N}} P(t_{1:N}, w_{1:N}) \\ &= \sum_{k} \alpha_N(k) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same HMM is also a language model. The joint P(t1:N, w1:N) is the product of prior, transitions and emissions above; summing it over every tag sequence (the forward algorithm) gives the probability of the words themselves: a “class-based” model that predicts the next word through its likely categories.
Say it“The probability of the words is the sum over all tag sequences, which is the sum of the last forward message.”

AIMA 23.1.6 · Viterbi on a real sentence

A worked example, column by column

The simplest tagger ignores context and gives every word its most frequent training tag. On this test set that baseline is right 89.5% of the time, which sounds good until you notice that most words are not ambiguous at all. Its errors are exactly where context matters: to before a noun phrase is a preposition, before a verb it is the infinitive marker. The HMM can fix those because the transition model knows that a determiner rarely follows the infinitive to.

Viterbi works through the sentence left to right. For every tag k at word n it keeps δn(k), the log-probability of the best tag sequence that ends with k at that word, and a back-pointer ψn(k) to the tag before it on that best sequence. Each new column needs only the previous one: for each k, try every predecessor j, add the log transition, keep the best, and add the log emission of the new word. At the end it takes the best final tag and follows the back-pointers home. The sentences below are real test sentences the HMM gets completely right and the baseline does not. Press Step and watch each column’s winner, then hover the numbers in the running example.

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta_1(k) &= \ln P(t_1 = k) \\ &\quad + \ln P(w_1 \mid k) \\ s_n(j, k) &= \delta_{n-1}(j) + \ln P(k \mid j) \\ \delta_n(k) &= \max_{j}\, s_n(j, k) \\ &\quad + \ln P(w_n \mid k) \\ \psi_n(k) &= \arg\max_{j}\, s_n(j, k) \\ \hat t_N &= \arg\max_k \delta_N(k) \\ \hat t_{n-1} &= \psi_n(\hat t_n) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: δ is the score of the best partial tagging ending in each tag, and sn(j, k) the score of reaching tag k at word n through tag j; working in logs turns the products into sums and avoids underflow on long sentences. ψ remembers which predecessor won, so the best whole sequence is read off backwards from the best last tag.
Say it“Delta n of k is the best of delta n-minus-1 of j plus log transition, plus log emission; psi n of k is the j that won.”

PRML 13.2.6

Beyond the plain HMM

The plain HMM makes four strong assumptions, and each has a well-known repair. Durations: the chance of staying in a state is the same at every step, so the time spent in a state is geometric, most likely one step and then ever less likely. Speech sounds and casino dealers are not like that; a semi-Markov model draws each state’s duration from its own distribution and emits that many observations before moving on. Memory in the outputs: given the state, each observation is independent of the last; an autoregressive HMM lets xn also depend on xn−1 (and further back), which keeps long-range state structure but adds short-range smoothness. Inputs: an input–output HMM conditions the transitions and emissions on an observed input sequence u1, …, uN, making it a model of how an input drives an output, and still trainable by EM. One chain: a factorial HMM uses M independent chains whose states jointly produce each observation, like several slow and fast causes added together.

The price of the factorial model is inference. The M chains are independent a priori, but once you observe their combined output they are coupled (explaining away, chapter 6.2), so the exact E-step must treat them as one chain with KM joint states and costs O(NK2M); in practice one uses variational or sampling methods. Finally, an HMM need not be trained to maximise likelihood at all. For classifying whole sequences, such as which word was spoken, discriminative training maximises the probability of the correct class given each sequence instead, comparing the class models against each other. Pick a variant and move its sliders; the trace is a fresh sample from that model.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(T) &= (A_{kk})^{T-1} (1 - A_{kk}) \\ \mathbb E[T] &= \frac{1}{1 - A_{kk}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: to spend exactly T steps in state k, the chain stays T − 1 times and then leaves once. The probabilities fall geometrically, so a duration of 1 is always the most likely, whatever the mean.
Say it“p of T is A k k to the T minus 1, times one minus A k k.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{AR: } p(x_n \mid z_n, x_{n-1}) \\ &\quad = \mathcal N(x_n \mid m_n, \sigma^2) \\ &\quad m_n = \mu_{z_n} + a\,(x_{n-1} - \mu_{z_n}) \\ &\text{IO: } p(\mathbf z_n \mid \mathbf z_{n-1}, \mathbf u_n) \\ &\quad \text{and } p(\mathbf x_n \mid \mathbf z_n, \mathbf u_n) \\ &\text{FHMM: } p(x_n \mid \mathbf z^{(1)}_n, \dots, \mathbf z^{(M)}_n) \\ &\quad = \mathcal N\big(x_n \mid \textstyle\sum_{m} \mathbf w^{(m)\top} \mathbf z^{(m)}_n,\ \sigma^2\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the autoregressive model pulls each observation part of the way, a, back toward the previous one; with a = 0 it is the plain HMM. The input–output model lets the observed input un change both the transition and the emission. The factorial model adds one contribution w(m) per chain, chosen by that chain’s current state.
Say it“Autoregressive: x n is Gaussian around mu plus a times the last deviation. Factorial: the means of all chains add.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\sum_{r=1}^{R} \ln p(m_r \mid \mathbf X_r) \\ &= \sum_{r=1}^{R} \ln \frac{p(\mathbf X_r \mid \theta_{m_r})\, p(m_r)}{\sum_{l=1}^{M} p(\mathbf X_r \mid \theta_l)\, p(l)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: discriminative training, for R training sequences Xr with known classes mr, each class l having its own HMM θl. Each term rewards the correct model for explaining its sequence better than the rival models do, not merely well. Every p(X | θ) is a forward-algorithm likelihood, but the objective no longer has EM’s simple counting M-step and is usually optimised by gradient methods.
Say it“Sum over sequences of the log posterior of the right class.”

Deep Learning 10.2.3

RNNs as directed graphical models

The tagger’s transition model is a bigram: the next tag depends only on the current one. That Markov assumption is what makes HMM inference cheap, and it is also a real limitation; whether a verb is coming can depend on several tags back. The chain rule of probability needs no such assumption: any distribution over a sequence y(1), …, y(τ) is the product of each element’s probability given all of the ones before. As a directed graphical model that is a complete graph: every y(t) has every earlier element as a parent. Stored as tables, it would need about kτ numbers for k symbols and length τ.

A recurrent network trained to predict the next symbol represents exactly this fully connected model, but compactly. It introduces a state h(t) that is a deterministic function of the past, so drawn with h the graph is a sparse chain again, and the same weights are used at every step. That parameter sharing makes the number of parameters independent of the sequence length, at the price of assuming that the conditional distribution of the next symbol given the state is the same at every time step (stationarity). Two contrasts with the HMM matter. The HMM’s hidden state is a random variable, so its likelihood needs the forward sum over paths; the RNN’s state is computed, so its likelihood is a single forward pass. But the RNN pays in training difficulty and interpretability: h is not a tag, and there is nothing like Viterbi’s exact search over it. Finally, a sequence model must decide when to stop. The options are an end-of-sequence symbol, an extra Bernoulli “stop now?” output at every step, or a model of the length τ itself. The models here all predict an END symbol after the last tag. Build a tag sequence by clicking tags and compare what a bigram, a trigram and a small RNN expect next.

\[ \begin{aligned} P(\mathbb Y) &= \prod_{t=1}^{\tau} P\big(y^{(t)} \mid y^{(<t)}\big) \\ y^{(<t)} &= \big(y^{(1)}, \dots, y^{(t-1)}\big) \\ L &= -\sum_{t} \log P\big(y^{(t)} \mid y^{(<t)}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the chain rule, with no independence assumed; the training loss is the negative log-likelihood, summed over positions. A bigram model replaces each condition by y(t−1) alone, a trigram by the last two.
Say it“The probability of the sequence is the product over t of y t given everything before it.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf a^{(t)} = \mathbf W \mathbf e_{y^{(t-1)}} \\ &\qquad + \mathbf U \mathbf h^{(t-1)} + \mathbf b \\ &\mathbf h^{(t)} = \tanh\big(\mathbf a^{(t)}\big) \\ &\mathbf o^{(t)} = \mathbf V^{\top} \mathbf h^{(t)} + \mathbf c \\ &P\big(y^{(t)} \mid y^{(<t)}\big) = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf o^{(t)})_{y^{(t)}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the state mixes the previous symbol (as a one-hot vector e) with the previous state; the output layer turns it into a distribution over the next symbol. The same W, U, b, V, c at every step: here 18 symbols (17 tags plus the boundary) and 32 hidden units, 2,226 numbers in all.
Say it“h t is tanh of W times the last symbol plus U times the last state plus b; the next symbol is the softmax of V-transpose h plus c.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{table:}\;& k^{\tau} - 1 \\ \text{Markov:}\;& (k-1) + k(k-1) \\ \text{RNN:}\;& \text{fixed, any } \tau \\ \text{perplexity:}\;& \exp\big(L / N\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: free parameters of a distribution over sequences of length τ with k symbols: a complete table needs one number per sequence (less one, since they sum to 1); a first-order Markov chain (“Markov”) needs a start distribution and one row per symbol; an RNN’s count depends only on its layer sizes. Perplexity turns the average loss over N predictions into an effective number of equally likely choices.
Say it“k to the tau minus one for the table; constant for the RNN; perplexity is e to the average loss.”

How the RNN was trained

Offline, in NumPy: an Elman network trained by backpropagation through time with Adam (learning rate 0.01, batches of 32, 10 epochs, about 30 seconds on one CPU core) on the 12,544 training tag sequences, each followed by END. Project · UD English Web Treebank

Project: an HMM tagger for English web text

The data is the Universal Dependencies English Web Treebank (UD_English-EWT; Silveira et al., 2014): 16,622 sentences from weblogs, newsgroups, emails, reviews and Yahoo! Answers, each word hand-annotated with one of the 17 universal part-of-speech tags. Its official split gives 12,544 training sentences (204,578 words) and 2,077 test sentences (25,094 words). The annotations are licensed CC BY-SA 4.0; the underlying web texts come from the Linguistic Data Consortium’s English Web Treebank (LDC2012T13), with copyrights held by their original owners as the treebank’s README describes. The extract embedded in this page (the word list, the tag counts, the test sentences and 3,000 unlabelled training sentences) is shared under the same CC BY-SA 4.0 licence.

Part 1 is supervised learning: count, smooth and decode, exactly as in the last three sections. Part 2 asks the question this chapter is really about: can Baum–Welch learn tags from untagged text? It starts from a tagger counted on only the first L labelled sentences and then runs EM on 3,000 other training sentences with their tags removed. Merialdo (1994) ran this experiment on English newswire and found something surprising, and you can reproduce it here. Move the smoothing sliders, then run EM from each seed size.

The same computation in Python

The script reads the three CoNLL-U files, builds the same vocabulary and unknown-word classes, tags the test set with NumPy, and runs every Baum–Welch configuration for eight iterations. import sys, re, collections, numpy as np
D = sys.argv[1]                                # folder holding the three en_ewt-ud-*.conllu files (UD v2.19)
TAGS = 'ADJ ADP ADV AUX CCONJ DET INTJ NOUN NUM PART PRON PROPN PUNCT SCONJ SYM VERB X'.split(); K = 17
def read(f):                                   # (word, UPOS index) per token; skip multiword ranges and empty nodes
    out, cur = [], []
    for line in open(D + f, encoding='utf8'):
        c = line.rstrip('\n').split('\t')
        if len(c) < 4: out += [cur] if cur else []; cur = []; continue
        if '-' not in c[0] and '.' not in c[0]: cur.append((c[1], TAGS.index(c[3])))
    return out + ([cur] if cur else [])
tr, te = read('en_ewt-ud-train.conllu'), read('en_ewt-ud-test.conllu')
CLASSES = '<num> <sym> <hyph> <Cap> <-ing> <-ed> <-ly> <-tion> <-adj> <-s> <other>'.split()
def shape(w):                                  # the unknown-word class of a word the tagger has not learned
    for cl, rx in [('<num>', '[0-9]'), ('<sym>', '^[^A-Za-z]*$'), ('<hyph>', '-'), ('<Cap>', '^[A-Z]'), ('<-ing>', 'ing$'), ('<-ed>', 'ed$'),
                   ('<-ly>', 'ly$'), ('<-tion>', '(tion|sion|ment|ness|ity)$'), ('<-adj>', '(able|ible|al|ous|ive|ful|ic|less)$'), ('<-s>', 's$')]:
        if re.search(rx, w): return cl
    return '<other>'
wc = collections.Counter(w for s in tr for w, _ in s)
vocab = sorted([w for w, c in wc.items() if c >= 2], key=lambda w: (-wc[w], w)) + CLASSES; VI = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}; V = len(vocab)
def wid(w): return VI[w] if w in VI else VI[w.lower()] if w.lower() in VI else VI[shape(w)]
def counts(sents):
    C0, CA, CE = np.zeros(K), np.zeros((K, K)), np.zeros((K, V))
    for s in sents:
        for i, (w, t) in enumerate(s):
            CE[t, wid(w)] += 1
            if i: CA[s[i - 1][1], t] += 1
            else: C0[t] += 1
    return C0, CA, CE
def params(C0, CA, CE, kt, ke):                # add-k smoothed maximum likelihood
    return (C0 + kt) / (C0.sum() + kt * K), (CA + kt) / (CA.sum(1, keepdims=True) + kt * K), (CE + ke) / (CE.sum(1, keepdims=True) + ke * V)
def viterbi(o, lpi, lA, lB):
    d, bp = lpi + lB[:, o[0]], []
    for x in o[1:]: sc = d[:, None] + lA; bp.append(sc.argmax(0)); d = sc.max(0) + lB[:, x]
    p = [int(d.argmax())]
    for b in reversed(bp): p.append(int(b[p[-1]]))
    return p[::-1]
def accuracy(pi, A, B, detail=False):
    lp = [np.log(pi), np.log(A), np.log(B)]; ok = collections.Counter(); n = collections.Counter(); conf = collections.Counter()
    for s in te:
        for (w, t), q in zip(s, viterbi([wid(w) for w, _ in s], *lp)):
            k = 'unknown' if wid(w) >= V - len(CLASSES) else 'known'; n[k] += 1; ok[k] += t == q
            if t != q: conf[(TAGS[t], TAGS[q])] += 1
    acc = (ok['known'] + ok['unknown']) / (n['known'] + n['unknown'])
    return (acc, ok, n, conf) if detail else acc
C0, CA, CE = counts(tr)
print(f"train {len(tr)} sentences, {sum(map(len, tr))} tokens; test {len(te)} sentences, {sum(map(len, te))} tokens; vocabulary {V - len(CLASSES)} words + {len(CLASSES)} classes")
acc, ok, n, conf = accuracy(*params(C0, CA, CE, 1, 0.001), detail=True)
print(f"HMM tagger (kt = 1, ke = 0.001): {acc:.4%}; known {ok['known']}/{n['known']} = {ok['known'] / n['known']:.4%}; unknown {ok['unknown']}/{n['unknown']} = {ok['unknown'] / n['unknown']:.4%}")
mft = CE.argmax(0); base = np.mean([mft[wid(w)] == t for s in te for w, t in s])
print(f"most-frequent-tag baseline: {base:.4%};  commonest confusions (gold -> guess):", ', '.join(f'{a}->{b} {c}' for (a, b), c in conf.most_common(5)))
for kt in (0.01, 1, 10):
    print(f"  kt = {kt:<5}", '  '.join(f"ke = {ke:g}: {accuracy(*params(C0, CA, CE, kt, ke)):.4%}" for ke in (1e-5, 1e-3, 1e-1)))

def estep(obs, pi, A, B):                      # scaled forward-backward; expected counts and log-likelihood
    G0, XI, GE, ll = np.zeros(K), np.zeros((K, K)), np.zeros((K, V)), 0.0
    for o in obs:
        n = len(o); al = np.zeros((n, K)); c = np.zeros(n); be = np.ones((n, K))
        a = pi * B[:, o[0]]; c[0] = a.sum(); al[0] = a / c[0]
        for i in range(1, n): a = (al[i - 1] @ A) * B[:, o[i]]; c[i] = a.sum(); al[i] = a / c[i]
        for i in range(n - 2, -1, -1): be[i] = (A @ (B[:, o[i + 1]] * be[i + 1])) / c[i + 1]
        g = al * be; ll += np.log(c).sum(); G0 += g[0]; np.add.at(GE.T, o, g)
        for i in range(1, n): XI += np.outer(al[i - 1], B[:, o[i]] * be[i]) * A / c[i]
    return G0, XI, GE, ll
U = [[wid(w) for w, _ in s] for s in tr[3000:] if len(s) <= 30][:3000]; NU = sum(map(len, U))
print(f"\nBaum-Welch on {len(U)} unlabelled training sentences ({NU} tokens), started from L labelled ones (kt = 0.1, ke = 0.001 in every M-step)")
for L in (20, 100, 500, 3000):

            if keep: G0, XI, GE = G0 + S0, XI + SA, GE + SE
            th = params(G0, XI, GE, 0.1, 0.001)
        print(f"L = {L:4d} {'keep labels' if keep else 'EM only    '}  accuracy % " + ' '.join(f'{100 * a:.2f}' for a in accs))
        print(f"{'':23}LL / token " + ' '.join(f'{l:.3f}' for l in lls))

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • With complete data, maximum likelihood for an HMM is counting and normalising; with hidden states the likelihood is a sum over KN paths, and EM (Baum–Welch) climbs it by replacing counts with expected counts from forward–backward.
  • The E-step needs γ and ξ, which come straight from the scaled α̂ and β̂; the M-step normalises expected counts, with weighted means and covariances for Gaussian emissions and sums over sequences when there are many. Zeros stay zero, symmetric starts stay stuck, and different starts reach different local maxima.
  • Forward–backward is the sum-product algorithm on the HMM’s factor graph: α and β are the two factor-to-variable messages, and Viterbi is max-sum.
  • An HMM tagger has tags as states and words as observations; learned by counting a treebank, smoothed, with spelling classes for unknown words and Viterbi decoding, it tags 92.1% of this web English correctly against 89.5% for the most-frequent-tag baseline. Baum–Welch on untagged text raises the likelihood but, from a good start, lowers the accuracy.
  • Extensions relax one assumption each: semi-Markov durations, autoregressive outputs, input–output conditioning, factorial chains (at K2M exact cost) and discriminative training. An RNN represents the fully connected chain-rule model with a deterministic state and shared parameters.

Check your understanding

Why can’t the likelihood of an HMM with hidden states be maximised by counting, as it can with complete data?

With complete data the log-likelihood is a sum of logs of individual parameters weighted by counts, and each row of A or φ can be maximised on its own. With hidden states the likelihood is a sum over all state paths, and the log of a sum does not split into terms for separate parameters. EM restores the counting structure by working with the expected complete-data log-likelihood Q, whose counts are posterior expectations.

You start Baum–Welch with A21 = 0. What will A21 be after 100 iterations, and why might you want that?

Still 0. Every ξ(zn−1,2, zn1) contains the factor A21, so the expected count for that transition is zero and the M-step sets the new A21 to zero again. Left-to-right models for speech use this deliberately: set the backward transitions to zero at the start and EM will never introduce them.

How does the sum-product algorithm on the HMM’s factor graph give the quantity ξ that Baum–Welch needs?

The marginal of the variables attached to a factor is the factor times the messages arriving at it from all other directions, normalised. For fn(zn−1, zn) those incoming messages are α(zn−1) from the left and β(zn) from the right, so ξ = α(zn−1) fn β(zn) / p(X).

Baum–Welch on untagged sentences raises the likelihood at every iteration, yet the tagging accuracy falls. Is EM broken?

No: EM is doing exactly its job, which is to make the untagged words probable. Nothing in the likelihood says the hidden states must mean the treebank’s tags; the model can explain the word sequence better by re-purposing states, for example splitting frequent nouns and merging rare tags. A good seed puts EM near the linguists’ solution, and climbing the likelihood moves it away. This is Merialdo’s (1994) finding, and the reason modern taggers are trained on labelled data.

Both an HMM and an RNN have a hidden state. What is the key difference, and what does it cost or buy?

The HMM’s state is a random variable with a distribution, so computing the likelihood means summing over all state paths (the forward algorithm), but it supports exact posterior inference and Viterbi. The RNN’s state is a deterministic function of the past inputs, so the likelihood is one forward pass and the model can capture dependencies far beyond one step, with a parameter count that does not grow with the length; but it has no exact search over hidden values and is harder to train and interpret.
