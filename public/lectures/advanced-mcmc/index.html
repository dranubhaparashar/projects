Slice Sampling & HMC

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, and how to estimate a partition function

The Metropolis sampler of the last chapter proposes blindly and pays for it with a slow random walk. This chapter’s samplers look at the target first. Slice sampling picks its step from the height of the density; Hamiltonian Monte Carlo turns the log density into a landscape and lets a frictionless marble glide across it, using the gradient to travel far in a single move. The second half turns to a quantity that every sampler avoids: the partition function, the total mass of an unnormalised density, which you need to compare models. PRML 11.4 · slice sampling

Sampling under the curve

Metropolis needs a step size, and the right one depends on the target: too small and the chain crawls, too large and it is always rejected. Slice sampling chooses its own step. It rests on a simple observation: if you scatter points uniformly over the region under the graph of p̃(z), the z coordinates of those points are distributed as p(z). So add an auxiliary height u and sample the pair (z, u) uniformly under the curve; then throw u away.

Uniform sampling under the curve is done by alternating two easy moves, a Gibbs sampler on (z, u). Given z, draw the height u uniformly between 0 and p̃(z). Given u, draw z uniformly from the slice, the set of z where p̃(z) is above u. Finding the slice exactly is usually impossible, so Neal’s method finds an interval around it: place a window of width w at random around the current z, step it out by w at either end until both ends are outside the slice, then draw from the interval, and each time a draw lands outside the slice, shrink the interval to that point and try again. Every move keeps the uniform distribution on the slice invariant, so the chain is correct for any w; w only changes the cost. In many dimensions the same update is applied to one variable at a time, like a Gibbs sampler that never needs the conditional distributions in closed form. Press One update to see a step, then change w and watch the evaluation count.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat p(z, u) &= \begin{cases} 1/Z_p & 0 \le u \le \tilde p(z) \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \\ \int \hat p(z, u)\, du &= \int_0^{\tilde p(z)} \frac{1}{Z_p}\, du \\ &= \frac{\tilde p(z)}{Z_p} = p(z) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the joint distribution of (z, u) is flat over the region under the curve p̃, and zero above it. Integrating out the height u leaves exactly the length of the vertical strip at z, which is p̃(z), divided by the total area Zp: the target. We never need Zp, because each move only asks whether a point is under the curve.
Say it“Points spread evenly under the curve have z distributed as p.”
\[ \begin{aligned} u &\sim \mathcal U\big(0,\ \tilde p(z^{(\tau)})\big) \\ v &\sim \mathcal U(0,1) \\ [L, R] &= [z^{(\tau)} - w v,\ z^{(\tau)} - w v + w] \\ &\text{while } \tilde p(L) > u:\ L \leftarrow L - w \\ &\text{while } \tilde p(R) > u:\ R \leftarrow R + w \\ z' &\sim \mathcal U(L, R) \\ &\text{if } \tilde p(z') \le u: \text{ shrink, redraw} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: draw a height under the current point, place a window of width w at a random offset around z, widen it in steps of w until both ends poke out of the slice, then draw uniformly inside. A draw outside the slice replaces the end of the interval on its side of z, so the interval shrinks towards the current point and the next draw is more likely to succeed.
Say it“Pick a height, step out until you are outside, then shrink in until you land inside.”

PRML 11.5, 11.5.1 · dynamical systems

A marble on an energy landscape

Slice sampling still moves one coordinate at a time. The hybrid Monte Carlo algorithm (now usually called Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, HMC) moves all of them at once, a long way, using the gradient of the log density. Its trick is borrowed from physics. Write the target as p(z) = exp(−E(z))/Zp, so that the potential energy E(z) = −log p̃(z) is low where the density is high. Give the state a momentum r of the same dimension, with kinetic energy K(r) = ½‖r‖², and call the total H(z, r) = E(z) + K(r) the Hamiltonian.

Hamilton’s equations say how a frictionless particle moves: position changes in the direction of the momentum, and momentum is pushed downhill by the force −∇E. Two facts about this motion make it useful for sampling. First, H is conserved: the particle trades potential for kinetic energy but never gains or loses total energy. Second, the flow preserves volume in the (z, r) phase space (Liouville’s theorem): a blob of starting points may be sheared and stretched, but its area never changes. Together they mean that the joint distribution exp(−H)/ZH is left unchanged by the dynamics. Since H is a sum, that joint distribution is p(z) times an independent standard Gaussian on r, so the z-marginal is our target. Press Play and watch the blob, then switch to the double well.

\[ \begin{aligned} H(z, r) &= E(z) + K(r) \\ K(r) &= \tfrac12 \textstyle\sum_i r_i^2 \\ \frac{dz_i}{d\tau} &= \frac{\partial H}{\partial r_i} = r_i \\ \frac{dr_i}{d\tau} &= -\frac{\partial H}{\partial z_i} = -\frac{\partial E}{\partial z_i} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the position moves with velocity equal to the momentum; the momentum changes by the force, minus the slope of the energy. τ is a fictitious time, nothing to do with the chain’s iteration count. The particle speeds up as it rolls into the valleys of E, which are the peaks of p, and slows down as it climbs.
Say it“z moves with r; r is pushed by minus the gradient of E.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{dH}{d\tau} &= \sum_i \Big( \frac{\partial H}{\partial z_i}\frac{dz_i}{d\tau} + \frac{\partial H}{\partial r_i}\frac{dr_i}{d\tau} \Big) = 0 \\ \operatorname{div} V &= \sum_i \Big( \frac{\partial}{\partial z_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial r_i} - \frac{\partial}{\partial r_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial z_i} \Big) = 0 \\ p(z, r) &= \frac{1}{Z_H} e^{-H(z, r)} = p(z)\, \mathcal N(r \mid 0, I) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: substituting Hamilton’s equations, the two terms in dH/dτ cancel pair by pair, so energy is constant along every path. The flow field V = (dz/dτ, dr/dτ) has zero divergence, because mixed second derivatives are equal: nothing is compressed or expanded. A density that depends only on H is therefore carried onto itself.
Say it“H is constant along a path, the flow keeps volume, so e to the minus H is invariant.”

PRML 11.5.1 · the leapfrog discretisation

Leapfrog: the integrator that keeps its word

A computer cannot follow Hamilton’s equations exactly; it takes steps of size ε. The obvious scheme, Euler’s method, updates z and r together from their old values. For sampling it is a disaster: each step inflates phase-space volume, so the energy drifts steadily and the path spirals away. Updating the momentum first and then moving with the new momentum (a modified Euler step) fixes the volume, because each half is a shear that slides one coordinate by an amount depending only on the other. But that scheme is lopsided in time.

The leapfrog integrator makes it symmetric: half a step for the momentum, a full step for the position, another half step for the momentum. It is built from three shears, so it preserves volume exactly, and it is time-reversible: negate the momentum at the end and the same steps carry you back to the start. Its energy is not conserved exactly, but the error oscillates with amplitude of order ε² instead of growing. HMC needs exactly these two properties, volume preservation and reversibility, and it will correct the leftover energy error with an accept/reject step. Compare the three integrators, then push ε past 2.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat r_i(\tau + \tfrac{\epsilon}{2}) &= \hat r_i(\tau) - \frac{\epsilon}{2} \frac{\partial E}{\partial z_i}\big(\hat z(\tau)\big) \\ \hat z_i(\tau + \epsilon) &= \hat z_i(\tau) + \epsilon\, \hat r_i(\tau + \tfrac{\epsilon}{2}) \\ \hat r_i(\tau + \epsilon) &= \hat r_i(\tau + \tfrac{\epsilon}{2}) - \frac{\epsilon}{2} \frac{\partial E}{\partial z_i}\big(\hat z(\tau + \epsilon)\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: kick the momentum by half a step of force, drift the position for a full step at that momentum, kick again with the force at the new position. Consecutive half kicks merge, so L leapfrog steps cost L gradient evaluations. The hats mark the discrete approximations to the true path.
Say it“Half kick, drift, half kick.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Euler: } & \begin{pmatrix} z \\ r \end{pmatrix} \leftarrow \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \epsilon \\ -\epsilon & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} z \\ r \end{pmatrix} \\ & \det = 1 + \epsilon^2 \\ \text{leapfrog: } & \det = 1 \\ & \text{stable iff } \epsilon < 2\sigma \\ & H(\hat z, \hat r) - H(z, r) = O(\epsilon^2) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for the Gaussian E = z²/2σ² with σ = 1, one Euler step is a linear map whose determinant 1 + ε² exceeds 1, so volume and energy grow by that factor every step. Each leapfrog shear has determinant 1. For this Gaussian the leapfrog stays bounded only while ε < 2σ; in many dimensions the narrowest direction sets the limit.
Say it“Euler inflates by one plus epsilon squared; leapfrog keeps volume and is stable below two sigma.”

PRML 11.5.2 · hybrid Monte Carlo

Hybrid Monte Carlo

Now combine the pieces. One HMC iteration has two stages. First, refresh the momentum by drawing r afresh from its Gaussian; that is a Gibbs step on r, it leaves the joint distribution invariant, and it moves the state to a new energy level. Second, run L leapfrog steps of size ε from (z, r) to a proposal (z*, r*), and accept it with probability min(1, exp(H(z, r) − H(z*, r*))). If the integrator were exact, H would not change and every proposal would be accepted; the Metropolis test removes the bias left by the energy error.

Why is that test valid when the proposal is a deterministic path? Because the leapfrog map is reversible and volume-preserving. Bishop’s argument picks the direction of integration (±ε) at random with probability ½, so that the proposal from a small region R of phase space to its image R′ is exactly as likely as the reverse. Then the probability flow from R to R′ is the symmetric expression in the second formula, and detailed balance holds. (Because a fresh Gaussian momentum is as likely to point one way as the other, randomising the direction changes nothing in practice; the momentum flip is still needed in the proof.) Accepted trajectories can carry the state across the whole distribution, which is what defeats the random walk. Run both chains, then shorten L until HMC also crawls.

\[ \begin{aligned} r &\sim \mathcal N(0, I) \\ (z^*, r^*) &= \text{LEAPFROG}_{\epsilon, L}(z, r) \\ A &= \min\big(1,\ e^{H(z, r) - H(z^*, r^*)}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: draw a fresh momentum, follow the discretised dynamics for L steps, and accept the end point with probability A. A proposal that lowers H is always accepted; one that raises it by ΔH is accepted with probability e−ΔH. If it is rejected, the chain keeps z (the momentum is discarded either way).
Say it“Accept with probability the minimum of one and e to the H-before minus H-after.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{Z_H} e^{-H(\mathcal R)}\, \delta V\, \tfrac12 \min\!\big\{1,\ e^{H(\mathcal R) - H(\mathcal R')}\big\} \\ &= \frac{1}{Z_H}\, \delta V\, \tfrac12 \min\!\big\{e^{-H(\mathcal R)},\ e^{-H(\mathcal R')}\big\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the probability of being in the small region R (density times volume δV), choosing the direction that maps it to R′ (the ½) and accepting. Multiply the e−H(R) inside the minimum and the result is symmetric in R and R′. Volume preservation means R′ has the same δV, and reversibility means the opposite direction maps R′ back to R, so the reverse flow is the same number: detailed balance.
Say it“The flow from R to R-prime is a half times the smaller of the two densities: symmetric.”

PRML 11.5.2 · tuning ε, L and the dimension

Step size, path length and dimension

HMC has two knobs. The step size ε controls the energy error and so the acceptance rate; it must stay below the stability limit of the narrowest direction. The number of steps L controls how far a trajectory travels: too short and HMC degenerates into a random walk (with L = 1 it is the Langevin sampler), too long and the path curls back on itself and wastes gradients. Practical samplers adapt both during a warm-up phase, and also rescale each coordinate by a mass matrix, a guess at the posterior’s spread; the project below does that with a pilot run.

The dimension is where HMC earns its keep. For a random-walk proposal in D dimensions every coordinate adds its own risk of landing somewhere worse, so the step must shrink like 1/√D to keep the acceptance rate fixed, and the classical optimum keeps about 23% of proposals. The leapfrog’s energy errors in different coordinates partly cancel, and the total error grows only like Dε⁴, so its step needs to shrink only like D−1/4, and the optimal acceptance rate is about 65%. The lab measures both on a D-dimensional standard Gaussian. Slide D from 1 to 1,024.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{RW: } A &\approx 2\,\Phi\!\Big(-\frac{s\sqrt D}{2}\Big) \\ s^* &\approx \frac{2.38}{\sqrt D}, \quad A^* \approx 0.234 \\ \text{HMC: } \mathbb E[\Delta H] &\propto D\,\epsilon^4 \\ \epsilon^* &\propto D^{-1/4}, \quad A^* \approx 0.65 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for a random walk on a standard Gaussian the acceptance rate is a function of s√D alone; the dashed curve in the lab is this formula. The best trade-off between step length and acceptance (Roberts, Gelman and Gilks, 1997) is at about 23%. For HMC the average energy error grows like Dε⁴, so keeping it fixed allows ε to shrink much more slowly (Neal, 2011).
Say it“Random-walk steps shrink like one over root D; leapfrog steps like D to the minus a quarter.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{cost}_{\text{RW}} &\sim \frac{1}{A\, s^{*2}} \propto D \\ \text{cost}_{\text{HMC}} &\sim \frac{1}{\epsilon^*} \propto D^{1/4} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a random walk with steps of length s needs about 1/s² accepted moves to drift one standard deviation, and only a fraction A of its moves are accepted. A Hamiltonian trajectory of unit time moves about one standard deviation per coordinate and costs about 1/ε gradients. These are rough, order-of-magnitude costs, but the gap between D and D1/4 is what makes HMC the default for models with many parameters.
Say it“Random walk costs grow like D, HMC costs like the fourth root of D.”

PRML 11.6 · Deep Learning 18.7 · estimating the partition function

Weighing a distribution: ratios of partition functions

Every sampler so far has avoided the normalising constant Z = ∫ p̃(z) dz. Sometimes we need it. In Bayesian model comparison it is the evidence p(y | model), the posterior’s normaliser. For an energy-based model such as a restricted Boltzmann machine it is the partition function, and without it you cannot report a test log-likelihood or compare two models: their probabilities differ by p̃A/p̃B times ZB/ZA. Absolute values of Z are rarely available, but ratios can be estimated by sampling.

Take a distribution p0 whose constant Z0 is known and which we can sample. The ratio Z1/Z0 is the expected value, under p0, of the importance weight p̃1/p̃0, so average that weight over draws from p0. In practice one works with logarithms and the log-sum-exp trick, because the weights span many orders of magnitude. The estimate of Z is unbiased; its logarithm is biased low. It works only when p0 covers p1 well: a few draws in the right place carry all the weight otherwise, and in many dimensions the weights degenerate exponentially fast. Bishop adds that p0 need not be a simple distribution: samples from a Markov chain can define one, through the chain’s own transition probabilities. Move and widen the proposal, then raise D.

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{Z_1}{Z_0} &= \frac{\int \tilde p_1(z)\, dz}{Z_0} = \int p_0(z)\, \frac{\tilde p_1(z)}{\tilde p_0(z)}\, dz \\ \frac{\hat Z_1}{Z_0} &= \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{\tilde p_1(z^{(k)})}{\tilde p_0(z^{(k)})}, \quad z^{(k)} \sim p_0 \\ \log \hat Z_1 &= \log Z_0 - \log K \\ &\quad + \operatorname{logsumexp}_k \log w_k \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: write Z1 as an integral, multiply and divide by p0, and it becomes an expectation under p0 of the weight w = p̃1/p̃0. Bishop writes the same thing with energies: ZE/ZG = EG[exp(−E + G)]. The log-sum-exp form subtracts the largest log weight before exponentiating, so nothing overflows. Here p0 is normalised, Z0 = 1.
Say it“Z-one over Z-nought is the average weight p-tilde-one over p-tilde-nought under p-nought.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}\Big[\frac{\hat Z_1}{Z_0}\Big] &= \frac{1}{K}\,\operatorname{Var}_{p_0}\!\Big[\frac{\tilde p_1}{\tilde p_0}\Big] \\ \text{ESS} &= \frac{(\sum_k w_k)^2}{\sum_k w_k^2} \\ \mathbb E[\log \hat Z] &\le \log Z \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the variance of the estimate is the variance of a single weight over K; when p0 rarely visits places where p̃1 is large, that variance is huge. The effective sample size counts how many equally weighted draws the weights are worth. Because log is concave, the average log estimate falls below log Z (Jensen’s inequality): a stochastic lower bound.
Say it“The variance is the weight variance over K; the effective sample size is sum of w squared over sum of w-squared.”

Deep Learning 18.7.1 · PRML 11.6 · annealed importance sampling

Annealed importance sampling

When p0 and p1 are far apart, one big jump is hopeless, but many small ones are not. Chain a sequence of intermediate distributions between them, pη0 = p0, pη1, …, pηT = p1, and write Z1/Z0 as a product of ratios between neighbours. Each neighbouring pair overlaps well, so each ratio is easy. The usual choice is a weighted geometric average, p̃η = p̃1η p̃01−η, which Bishop writes with energies as Eα = (1 − α)E1 + αEM.

Annealed importance sampling (Neal, 2001) estimates all the ratios with one walk per sample. Start a chain with a draw from p0. At each rung, multiply its weight by p̃ηj/p̃ηj−1 at the current point, then move the point with any MCMC transition that leaves pηj invariant. The average of the final weights over K chains estimates Z1/Z0. The proof is to see the whole walk as one importance sample in a space of T points, with a proposal (the forward walk) and a target (the same walk run backwards from p1 using reversed transitions) whose ratio is exactly the AIS weight; the transitions cancel. The chains need not mix: a poor transition increases the variance but not the bias of Ẑ. Raise the number of temperatures and watch the log weights close in on log Z.

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde p_{\eta}(x) &= \tilde p_1(x)^{\eta}\, \tilde p_0(x)^{1-\eta} \\ 0 &= \eta_0 < \eta_1 < \dots < \eta_T = 1 \\ \frac{Z_1}{Z_0} &= \prod_{j=1}^{T} \frac{Z_{\eta_j}}{Z_{\eta_{j-1}}} \\ w^{(k)} &= \prod_{j=1}^{T} \frac{\tilde p_{\eta_j}(x_j^{(k)})}{\tilde p_{\eta_{j-1}}(x_j^{(k)})} \\ \frac{\hat Z_1}{Z_0} &= \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} w^{(k)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the path of distributions blends p0 into p1. The ratio of the end constants telescopes into a product of neighbouring ratios. For chain k, x1 is a draw from p0 and xj+1 comes from xj by a transition Tηj that keeps pηj invariant. With the geometric path each factor is exp((ηj − ηj−1)(log p̃1 − log p̃0)).
Say it“Multiply the weight by the next distribution over the current one, then take a step; average the weights.”
\[ \begin{aligned} q(x_{1:T}) &= p_0(x_1) \prod_{j=1}^{T-1} T_{\eta_j}(x_{j+1} \mid x_j) \\ \tilde p(x_{1:T}) &= \tilde p_1(x_T) \prod_{j=1}^{T-1} \tilde T_{\eta_j}(x_j \mid x_{j+1}) \\ \tilde T_{\eta}(x \mid x') &= T_{\eta}(x' \mid x)\, \frac{\tilde p_{\eta}(x)}{\tilde p_{\eta}(x')} \\ \frac{\tilde p}{q} &= w \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the forward walk is an ordinary proposal in the extended space of the whole path. The extended target starts at p̃1 and runs the reversed transitions; integrating over the path leaves exactly Z1, because each reversed transition sums to 1. Substituting the reversal formula, every T cancels and the ratio is the AIS weight, so the weights are ordinary importance weights for Z1/Z0.
Say it“AIS is importance sampling on whole paths: forward walk as proposal, backward walk as target.”

Deep Learning 18.7.2 · bridge sampling

Bridge sampling

AIS walks from p0 to p1 through many rungs. Bridge sampling (Bennett, 1976) uses a single intermediate distribution, the bridge p*, and samples from both ends. Draws from p0 estimate how much of p* sits under p0, draws from p1 how much sits under p1, and the ratio of the two averages is Z1/Z0. The bridge only has to overlap each end, not the ends each other, which is why it beats one-sided importance sampling when the two distributions are some distance apart.

Which bridge is best? The geometric mean √(p̃0p̃1) is simple and usually good. The variance-minimising bridge (Meng & Wong, 1996) is p̃0p̃1/(r p̃0 + p̃1), which needs the unknown ratio r itself; start from a rough guess and iterate, and the estimate converges in a few rounds. When the two ends are very far apart even the optimal bridge fails, and you are back to chains of intermediate distributions. Linked importance sampling (Neal, 2005) combines the two ideas, using bridges to link the rungs of an AIS-like chain. A related practical trick is to track the partition function during training of an energy-based model: consecutive parameter vectors give similar models, so each new log Z can be estimated from the last by a short bridge or AIS step (Desjardins et al., 2011). Pull p1 away and narrow it, and compare the four estimators.

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{Z_1}{Z_0} &\approx \frac{\sum_{k} \tilde p_*(x_0^{(k)}) / \tilde p_0(x_0^{(k)})}{\sum_{k} \tilde p_*(x_1^{(k)}) / \tilde p_1(x_1^{(k)})} \\ x_0 &\sim p_0, \quad x_1 \sim p_1 \\ \tilde p_* = \tilde p_1 &\Rightarrow \text{IS from } p_0 \\ \tilde p_* = \sqrt{\tilde p_0\, \tilde p_1} &\Rightarrow \text{geometric bridge} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the numerator estimates Z*/Z0 by importance sampling from p0, the denominator Z*/Z1 from p1; dividing cancels the unknown Z*. Choosing the bridge equal to one end recovers one-sided importance sampling, so bridge sampling contains it as a special case.
Say it“Bridge over nought from the nought-samples, divided by bridge over one from the one-samples.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde p_*^{(\text{opt})}(x) &= \frac{\tilde p_0(x)\, \tilde p_1(x)}{r\, \tilde p_0(x) + \tilde p_1(x)} \\ r &= Z_1 / Z_0 \\ \frac{\hat r^{(t+1)}}{\hat r^{(t)}} &= \frac{\sum_k \sigma\big(\ell(x_0^{(k)}) - \log \hat r^{(t)}\big)}{\sum_k \sigma\big(\log \hat r^{(t)} - \ell(x_1^{(k)})\big)} \\ \ell(x) &= \log \tilde p_1(x) - \log \tilde p_0(x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the optimal bridge (for equal sample sizes) is a harmonic-mean-like blend weighted by the ratio being estimated. Substituting it into the bridge formula and simplifying gives the iteration on the second line, written with the logistic function σ; each round uses the previous estimate, starting from the geometric bridge.
Say it“The best bridge is p-nought p-one over r p-nought plus p-one; iterate r until it stops moving.”

Real-world project

Project: radon in 919 Minnesota homes

Radon is a radioactive gas that seeps out of the ground into houses; long exposure raises the risk of lung cancer. In the late 1980s the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency measured it in homes across the country, in its State Residential Radon Survey. Gelman and Hill (2007) made the Minnesota part of that survey a standard teaching example for multilevel models, and this page embeds it: 919 measurements from 85 counties, the floor on which each was taken (0 for basement, 1 for first floor; 153 are first-floor readings), and a county-level uranium measurement supplied with the data. The response is log radon (zero readings set to 0.1 pCi/L before taking logs, as Gelman and Hill do). Counties are very unequal: some contributed one house, one contributed 116.

The model gives each county its own intercept αj, drawn from a statewide distribution with mean μ and spread σα, so that counties with few houses borrow strength from the rest (partial pooling). It has 89 parameters, strong correlations between the intercepts and μ, and a scale parameter σα on which everything else depends: a good test for a sampler. We fit it with HMC and with random-walk Metropolis at the same cost, and then use annealed importance sampling to estimate the evidence of three models (no county effects, county intercepts, county intercepts explained in part by uranium) and check every estimate against a direct numerical integral.

\[ \begin{aligned} y_i &\sim \mathcal N\big(\alpha_{j[i]} + \beta x_i,\ \sigma_y^2\big) \\ \alpha_j &\sim \mathcal N\big(\mu + \gamma u_j,\ \sigma_\alpha^2\big) \\ \mu, \beta, \gamma &\sim \mathcal N(0, 10^2) \\ \sigma_\alpha, \sigma_y &\sim \text{Exp}(1) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: house i in county j[i] has log radon yi around its county’s intercept, lowered by β if measured on the first floor (xi = 1). The intercepts scatter around a statewide line in log uranium uj; without uranium, γ = 0. HMC samples log σα and log σy, with the Jacobian of that change of variable included.
Say it“Houses vary around their county; counties vary around the state.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{centred: } & \alpha_j \text{ sampled directly} \\ \text{non-centred: } & \alpha_j = \mu + \gamma u_j + \sigma_\alpha \eta_j \\ & \eta_j \sim \mathcal N(0, 1) \\ p(y \mid \mathcal M) &= \int p(y \mid \theta)\, p(\theta \mid \mathcal M)\, d\theta \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same model can be sampled in two coordinate systems. In the non-centred one the sampler sees standardised county effects ηj, whose prior does not depend on σα. The evidence p(y | M) is the model’s partition function: the posterior’s normaliser, the quantity AIS estimates below.
Say it“Alpha is mu plus gamma u plus sigma-alpha times a standard normal eta.”

1 · The race: HMC against random-walk Metropolis

Both samplers get 20,000 evaluations of the log density (HMC also gets its gradient, which costs about the same here because the model reduces to per-county sums), start from the same point and rescale every coordinate by its posterior standard deviation from a pilot run, the diagonal “mass matrix” a warm-up phase would learn. The first 10% of each chain is discarded. Changing a slider reruns the race when you let go.

2 · Which model does the data prefer? The evidence by AIS

For each model, AIS starts 16 chains from p0, a Gaussian with the pilot run’s means and 1.5 times its standard deviations, and anneals along η = (j/T)² to the posterior, with one HMC move (ε = 0.3, five leapfrog steps, in units of p0’s spread) per rung. The exact log evidence comes from a separate Python computation: with the county intercepts integrated out analytically, each model has only 3 to 5 parameters left, few enough for Gauss–Hermite quadrature (checked to four decimals by refining the grid).

The same computation in Python

The script rebuilds the model, the two samplers and AIS with the page’s own random-number generator (mulberry32 and the same Box–Muller step), in the same order of operations, so every chain and every estimate can be compared with the page digit for digit. Its output is below; the default settings above reproduce the first block and the T = 1000 column.

import json, math, sys, time
import numpy as np
S = sys.argv[1]
M32 = 0xFFFFFFFF
def rng(a):                                    # mulberry32, as on the page
    st = [a & M32]
    def r():
        st[0] = (st[0] + 0x6D2B79F5) & M32; a = st[0]
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & M32
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & M32)) & M32) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & M32) / 4294967296
    return r
def gauss(r):                                  # Box-Muller, as on the page
    u = 0.0
    while u == 0.0: u = r()
    return math.sqrt(-2 * math.log(u)) * math.cos(2 * math.pi * r())

D = json.load(open(S + "x112-radon.json")); P = json.load(open(S + "x112-model.json"))
U = [c[2] for c in D["counties"]]; J = len(U); N = len(D["houses"]); L2P = math.log(2 * math.pi)
n, Sx, Sy, Sxy, Syy = ([0.0] * J for _ in range(5))
for j, x, y in D["houses"]:                    # per-county sufficient statistics (x is 0 or 1)
    n[j] += 1; Sx[j] += x; Sy[j] += y; Sxy[j] += x * y; Syy[j] += y * y
LP0 = -math.log(10) - 0.5 * L2P                # log N(0, 10^2) normaliser

def lpg(model, th):
    """log joint density p(y, theta) and its gradient. Exp(1) priors on the two sds, sampled as logs."""
    g = [0.0] * len(th)
    if model == "m1":                          # complete pooling: y ~ N(mu + beta x, sy)
        mu, be, b = th
        if not abs(b) < 30: return -math.inf, g
        sy2 = math.exp(2 * b); Q = gm = gb = 0.0
        for j in range(J):
            Q += Syy[j] - 2 * mu * Sy[j] - 2 * be * Sxy[j] + n[j] * mu * mu + 2 * mu * be * Sx[j] + be * be * Sx[j]
            gm += Sy[j] - n[j] * mu - be * Sx[j]; gb += Sxy[j] - mu * Sx[j] - be * Sx[j]
        lp = -N * b - N / 2 * L2P - Q / (2 * sy2) + (-mu * mu / 200 + LP0) + (-be * be / 200 + LP0) - math.exp(b) + b
        g[0] = gm / sy2 - mu / 100; g[1] = gb / sy2 - be / 100; g[2] = -N + Q / sy2 - math.exp(b) + 1
        return lp, g
    ur = model != "m2c"; nc = model == "m3n"   # uranium predictor? non-centred?
    mu = th[J]; k = J + 1; gam = 0.0
    if ur: gam = th[k]; k += 1
    be, a, b = th[k], th[k + 1], th[k + 2]
    if not (abs(a) < 30 and abs(b) < 30): return -math.inf, g
    sa = math.exp(a); sa2 = sa * sa; sy2 = math.exp(2 * b)
    Q = s1 = s2 = s3 = gb = 0.0
    for j in range(J):
        mj = mu + gam * U[j]; v = th[j]
        al = mj + sa * v if nc else v
        Q += Syy[j] - 2 * al * Sy[j] - 2 * be * Sxy[j] + n[j] * al * al + 2 * al * be * Sx[j] + be * be * Sx[j]
        ga = (Sy[j] - n[j] * al - be * Sx[j]) / sy2          # d loglik / d alpha_j
        gb += Sxy[j] - al * Sx[j] - be * Sx[j]
        if nc: g[j] = sa * ga - v; s1 += ga; s2 += ga * U[j]; s3 += ga * v
        else: dv = al - mj; g[j] = ga - dv / sa2; s1 += dv; s2 += dv * U[j]; s3 += dv * dv
    lp = -N * b - N / 2 * L2P - Q / (2 * sy2) + (-mu * mu / 200 + LP0) + (-be * be / 200 + LP0) - sa + a - math.exp(b) + b
    if ur: lp += -gam * gam / 200 + LP0
    if nc:
        lp += -sum(v * v for v in th[:J]) / 2 - J / 2 * L2P
        g[J] = s1 - mu / 100; g[k + 1] = sa * s3 - sa + 1
        if ur: g[J + 1] = s2 - gam / 100
    else:
        lp += -J * a - J / 2 * L2P - s3 / (2 * sa2)
        g[J] = s1 / sa2 - mu / 100; g[k + 1] = -J + s3 / sa2 - sa + 1
        if ur: g[J + 1] = s2 / sa2 - gam / 100
    g[k] = gb / sy2 - be / 100; g[k + 2] = -N + Q / sy2 - math.exp(b) + 1
    return lp, g

def hmc(model, th, s, eps, L, iters, r):       # leapfrog in coordinates scaled by the pilot sds s
    d = len(th); lp, g = lpg(model, th); out = []; acc = 0
    for it in range(iters):
        p = [gauss(r) for _ in range(d)]
        H0 = -lp + 0.5 * sum(x * x for x in p)
        q = th[:]; p = [p[i] + 0.5 * eps * g[i] * s[i] for i in range(d)]; ok = True
        for l in range(L):
            q = [q[i] + eps * p[i] * s[i] for i in range(d)]
            lq, gq = lpg(model, q)
            if not math.isfinite(lq): ok = False; break
            if l < L - 1: p = [p[i] + eps * gq[i] * s[i] for i in range(d)]
        pp = [p[i] + 0.5 * eps * gq[i] * s[i] for i in range(d)] if ok else []
        H1 = -lq + 0.5 * sum(x * x for x in pp) if ok else math.inf
        u = r(); dH = H1 - H0
        if math.isfinite(dH) and (dH <= 0 or u < math.exp(-dH)): th, lp, g = q, lq, gq; acc += 1
        out.append(th)
    return out, acc / iters

def rwm(model, th, s, step, iters, r):
    d = len(th); lp, _ = lpg(model, th); out = []; acc = 0
    for it in range(iters):
        q = [th[i] + step * s[i] * gauss(r) for i in range(d)]
        lq, _ = lpg(model, q); u = r(); dl = lq - lp
        if math.isfinite(lq) and (dl >= 0 or u < math.exp(dl)): th, lp = q, lq; acc += 1
        out.append(th)
    return out, acc / iters

def ess(x):                                    # Geyer's initial positive sequence, autocovariance by FFT
    x = np.asarray(x, float) - np.mean(x); nn = len(x); m = 1
    while m < 2 * nn: m *= 2
    f = np.fft.fft(x, m); ac = np.fft.ifft(f * np.conj(f)).real; s = 0.0
    for k in range(0, nn - 1, 2):
        pr = (ac[k] + ac[k + 1]) / ac[0]
        if pr < 0: break
        s += pr
    return nn / max(2 * s - 1, 1 / math.log10(nn))   # cap for antithetic chains, as Stan does

def tracked(model, ch):                        # mu, (gamma), beta, sigma_alpha (log), sigma_y (log), alpha_1..J
    d = len(ch[0]); cols = {"mu": [t[J] for t in ch], "beta": [t[d - 3] for t in ch], "log sa": [t[d - 2] for t in ch], "log sy": [t[d - 1] for t in ch]}
    if model == "m3n": al = [[t[J] + t[J + 1] * U[j] + math.exp(t[d - 2]) * t[j] for t in ch] for j in range(J)]
    else: al = [[t[j] for t in ch] for j in range(J)]
    return cols, al

def ais(model, T, K, eps, L, seed, c=1.5):     # from N(pilot mean, (c * pilot sd)^2) to the posterior, HMC moves
    m0, s = P["pilots"][model]["m"], P["pilots"][model]["s"]; d = len(m0); s0 = [c * v for v in s]
    jac = sum(math.log(v) for v in s0); r = rng(seed); lws = []
    def f1(z):
        lp, g = lpg(model, [m0[i] + s0[i] * z[i] for i in range(d)])
        return lp + jac, [s0[i] * g[i] for i in range(d)]
    for k in range(K):
        z = [gauss(r) for _ in range(d)]; l1, g1 = f1(z); lw = 0.0
        for j in range(1, T + 1):
            ep, en = ((j - 1) / T) * ((j - 1) / T), (j / T) * (j / T)
            l0 = -0.5 * sum(x * x for x in z) - d / 2 * L2P
            lw += (en - ep) * (l1 - l0)
            if j == T: break
            p = [gauss(r) for _ in range(d)]
            H0 = -((1 - en) * l0 + en * l1) + 0.5 * sum(x * x for x in p)
            gr = [-(1 - en) * z[i] + en * g1[i] for i in range(d)]
            q = z[:]; p = [p[i] + 0.5 * eps * gr[i] for i in range(d)]; ok = True
            for l in range(L):
                q = [q[i] + eps * p[i] for i in range(d)]
                lq1, gq1 = f1(q)
                if not math.isfinite(lq1): ok = False; break
                gr = [-(1 - en) * q[i] + en * gq1[i] for i in range(d)]
                if l < L - 1: p = [p[i] + eps * gr[i] for i in range(d)]
            if ok:
                lq0 = -0.5 * sum(x * x for x in q) - d / 2 * L2P
                pp = [p[i] + 0.5 * eps * gr[i] for i in range(d)]
                H1 = -((1 - en) * lq0 + en * lq1) + 0.5 * sum(x * x for x in pp)
            else: H1 = math.inf
            u = r(); dH = H1 - H0
            if math.isfinite(dH) and (dH <= 0 or u < math.exp(-dH)): z, l1, g1 = q, lq1, gq1
        lws.append(lw)
    mx = max(lws); w = [math.exp(x - mx) for x in lws]
    est = mx + math.log(sum(w) / K); essw = sum(w) ** 2 / sum(x * x for x in w)
    se = math.sqrt(max(0.0, sum(x * x for x in w) / K - (sum(w) / K) ** 2) / K) / (sum(w) / K)
    return est, se, essw

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Slice sampling draws uniformly from the region under the curve: a height u under p̃(z), then a point in the slice p̃ > u, found by stepping out and shrinking. It is correct for any window w, and w only changes the cost.
  • Hamiltonian dynamics on H = E(z) + ½‖r‖² conserves energy and phase-space volume, so it leaves exp(−H) invariant; the leapfrog integrator keeps volume exactly, is reversible, and has an energy error of order ε².
  • Hybrid Monte Carlo alternates a fresh Gaussian momentum with L leapfrog steps and a Metropolis test on the energy error. It moves far in one iteration, its step needs to shrink only like D−1/4, and it fails in funnels, where reparameterising helps.
  • Ratios of partition functions are expectations of importance weights, Z1/Z0 = Ep0[p̃1/p̃0]; log Ẑ is biased low, and the weights degenerate exponentially with dimension unless p0 is close to p1.
  • AIS walks from p0 to p1 through a ladder of geometric averages, multiplying weights and taking MCMC steps; bridge sampling uses one bridge sampled from both ends, optimally p̃0p̃1/(r p̃0 + p̃1). Both turn the evidence, and the partition function of an energy-based model, into something you can measure.

Check your understanding

Slice sampling has a window width w. Why does a bad choice of w not bias the samples?

Every part of the update leaves the uniform distribution on the slice invariant: the random placement of the window, stepping out (which stops by the same rule whichever point of the final interval you started from) and shrinkage towards the current point. A poor w only changes how many evaluations each update costs: many stepping-out steps if w is small, many shrinkage steps if it is large, and the latter cost grows only logarithmically.

If Hamiltonian dynamics conserves H, how does HMC ever visit states of different energy?

Through the momentum refresh. Drawing r afresh from its Gaussian at the start of each iteration changes the kinetic energy and so moves the state to a new level of H; the dynamics then explores along that level. Without the refresh the chain would stay on one energy contour forever and would not sample p(z).

Why can HMC not use the plain Euler integrator, even with a Metropolis correction?

The acceptance rule min(1, exp(H − H*)) is only valid for a proposal map that preserves volume and is reversible. Euler’s step expands volume by 1 + ε² per step and is not reversible, so the rule would need a Jacobian correction and a reverse proposal that does not exist. In practice the energy also drifts so fast that almost nothing would be accepted.

Annealed importance sampling uses MCMC transitions that may not mix. Why is the estimate of Z still unbiased?

Because AIS is importance sampling on the whole path: the weight is the ratio of an extended target to the forward-walk proposal, and the transitions cancel out of that ratio whatever they are, as long as each leaves its own intermediate distribution invariant. Poor transitions make the weights more variable, so the estimate is noisier and its logarithm more biased low, but the expectation of Ẑ itself is still Z1/Z0.

In the radon project, HMC struggles with the centred uranium model but not with the varying-intercept model. Why?

Uranium explains most of the variation between counties, so σα becomes small and its posterior reaches towards zero. When σα is small the 85 intercepts must sit very close to the statewide line, so the posterior has a narrow neck joined to a wide mouth: a funnel. No single step size suits both regions. The non-centred form samples standardised effects whose scale does not depend on σα, and the funnel disappears.
