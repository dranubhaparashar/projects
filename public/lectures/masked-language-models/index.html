BERT & Masked Language Models

Read the whole sentence, hide a few words, learn to put them back

The previous chapter built a language model that reads left to right and predicts the next token. That is one way to use a transformer, and not the only one. Drop the causal mask and every token can look at every other token, in both directions; you lose the ability to generate text, and in exchange you get the best possible representation of each word in its context. BERT is that idea, trained by hiding words and guessing them back. The figure shows the one difference that matters. Toggle the mask and watch which words the blank is allowed to read; a small masked language model is meanwhile training in your browser, and its guesses for the blank appear as it learns.

Three architectures · 11.1

Encoder, decoder, encoder–decoder

Every transformer language model is built from the same block: multi-head self-attention, a small per-token feed-forward network, residual connections and layer normalisation (the attention chapter wires it up). What differs between the families is almost entirely one matrix: the attention mask that says which positions a position may look at. Three choices give the three architectures of the field.

A decoder (GPT, Llama, the model of the previous chapter) uses a causal mask: position i may attend to positions 1…i only. That is what makes it a generator: the prediction at position i never peeks at the future, so the model can be trained to predict token i+1 everywhere at once, then run one token at a time. An encoder (BERT, RoBERTa) has no mask at all, apart from hiding padding: every position attends to every position, left and right. An encoder–decoder (the original transformer for translation, T5, BART) uses both: the encoder reads the source bidirectionally, the decoder generates the target causally and additionally attends to the whole encoder output through cross-attention. Pick an architecture below and hover the grid: a filled cell means the row may read the column.

\[ \text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\right)V, \qquad M_{ij} = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } i \text{ may attend to } j \\ -\infty & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]
Read it as: the same scaled dot-product attention as always, plus a mask matrix M added to the scores before the softmax. A score of −∞ becomes a weight of exactly zero, so the masked positions contribute nothing to the weighted sum. Causal: Mij = −∞ for j > i. Bidirectional: M is all zeros (except for padding columns). Cross-attention: queries come from the decoder, keys and values from the encoder, and M is all zeros.

Encoder-only

BERT, RoBERTa, ALBERT, DeBERTa, ModernBERT

Reads the whole input at once, both directions. Trained by masked language modelling. Best at producing one vector per token or per sentence: classification, tagging, span extraction, retrieval and reranking. Cannot generate text in any natural way.

Decoder-only

GPT-2, GPT-4-class models, Llama, Claude

Reads left to right under a causal mask. Trained by next-token prediction, which uses every token of every sequence as a target. Generates text one token at a time; does every task by prompting. The dominant design since 2020.

Encoder–decoder

The 2017 transformer, T5, BART, mT5, Whisper

A bidirectional encoder for the input, a causal decoder for the output, joined by cross-attention. Natural for sequence-to-sequence tasks where input and output are different things: translation, summarisation, speech to text.

Bidirectional encoders · 11.1

The bidirectional encoder: reading both ways

Why would anyone give up generation? Because for many tasks the right context for a word is on both sides of it. In “the bank of the river” the word that settles what bank means comes three tokens later; a left-to-right model has to represent bank before it has seen river, and can only fix things up at later positions. A bidirectional encoder represents bank with the river already in view. Peters et al. (2018) showed with ELMo that word vectors computed from both directions (two separate LSTM language models, concatenated) improve almost every task they touched; BERT’s contribution was to make the two directions interact inside one transformer stack.

The encoder block is the transformer block you know, with the mask removed. BERT uses the post-LN arrangement of the 2017 transformer (normalise after each residual add) and the GELU activation in the feed-forward layer; the toy model later in this chapter keeps post-LN and uses ReLU to keep its hand-written backward pass short. The figure traces information flow: pick a token and a depth, and see which input tokens can have influenced its vector. Under the causal mask the reachable set is everything to the left, at any depth. Without the mask, one layer already reaches everything.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf h' &= \text{LayerNorm}\big(\mathbf x + \text{MultiHead}(\mathbf x,\mathbf x,\mathbf x)\big) \\ \mathbf h &= \text{LayerNorm}\big(\mathbf h' + \text{FFN}(\mathbf h')\big), \qquad \text{FFN}(\mathbf u) = \text{GELU}(\mathbf u W_1 + \mathbf b_1)\,W_2 + \mathbf b_2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: x is the matrix of input vectors (one row per token). Self-attention lets every row gather from every other row with no mask, the result is added back to x and normalised; then the same two-layer network is applied to every row separately (W1 is H × 4H, W2 is 4H × H in BERT), added back and normalised again. L such blocks are stacked; BERT-base has L = 12 with H = 768 and 12 heads, BERT-large L = 24, H = 1024, 16 heads.
Say it“h-prime is layer-norm of x plus multi-head self-attention of x; h is layer-norm of h-prime plus the feed-forward network applied to h-prime.”

Why the objective has to change. Tick the box above. A decoder is trained to predict token i+1 from position i, and that works because position i cannot see i+1. In a bidirectional encoder, position i sees token i+1 directly after one layer, so the “prediction” is a copy: the training signal collapses to nothing. If you want to train a bidirectional encoder as a language model you must remove the target from the input, and that is exactly what masking does. The price is that you only get a training signal at the hidden positions, about one token in seven, instead of at every position; section 9 returns to that cost.

Input layout · 11.2

WordPiece, [CLS], [SEP] and three embeddings

BERT’s vocabulary has 30,522 WordPiece tokens: whole common words plus sub-word pieces, with a continuation piece written with a leading ##. Tokenising a word is a greedy longest-match-first walk: take the longest vocabulary entry that matches from the current position, then continue with ##-pieces until the word is used up; a word that cannot be covered becomes [UNK], which is rare because every single character is in the vocabulary. The vocabulary itself is learned much like BPE (merge the pair that most improves the likelihood of the training text rather than the most frequent pair); the words chapter has the background on words and morphemes. BERT’s standard English model is uncased: everything is lower-cased and accents are stripped before tokenising. Type a word or two to see a toy WordPiece tokeniser at work on a 150-entry vocabulary.

    The input sequence and its three embeddings

    A BERT input is one or two segments packed into a single sequence. It always starts with a special [CLS] token whose final vector is used as a summary of the whole input for classification, and each segment ends with [SEP]. The vector fed into the first block is the sum of three learned embeddings: a token embedding (which WordPiece this is), a segment embedding (first or second segment, so that sentence-pair tasks can tell the two apart) and a position embedding (BERT learns one vector per position, up to 512, instead of the sinusoids of the 2017 transformer). After the sum come layer normalisation and dropout. Edit the two segments and the figure redraws the layout live.

    \[ \mathbf x_i = E_{\text{tok}}[w_i] + E_{\text{seg}}[s_i] + E_{\text{pos}}[i], \qquad s_i \in \{A, B\},\; i = 0,1,\dots,n-1 \]
    Read it as: the input vector for position i is the token embedding of its WordPiece wi, plus the embedding of its segment id si (A for everything up to and including the first [SEP], B after it), plus the embedding of its position i. All three tables are trained with the rest of the model. Etok is 30,522 × H, Eseg is 2 × H, Epos is 512 × H; together they are a fifth of BERT-base’s parameters.

    Masking words · 11.2

    The masked language modelling objective

    The trick that makes a bidirectional encoder trainable is a cloze test, the fill-in-the-blank exercise of language classes. Take a sentence, pick a random 15% of its tokens, hide them, and ask the model to recover each hidden token from everything that is left. Since the target is gone from the input, the model cannot copy it; it has to infer it from both sides of the gap. The loss is ordinary cross-entropy over the vocabulary at the hidden positions, and nothing is asked of the model at the other positions. Devlin et al. called this the masked language model (MLM) objective; because the whole sequence is processed in one parallel pass, a training step costs the same as for a decoder, but only the masked positions contribute to the loss.

    Hiding a token means replacing it with a special [MASK] token, but not always. If every selected token were replaced by [MASK], the model would learn to represent real words well only when a [MASK] is nearby, and no downstream task ever contains a [MASK]. BERT’s recipe for the selected 15% is therefore 80 / 10 / 10: 80% become [MASK], 10% are replaced by a random vocabulary token, 10% are left unchanged. In all three cases the target is the original token. The random replacements teach the model that any visible token might be wrong and should be checked against its context; the unchanged ones teach it to keep a good representation of a word even when nothing looks hidden. Change the rate and redraw to see the recipe on one sentence and in the long run.

    \[ \mathcal L_{\text{MLM}} = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal M|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal M} \log p_\theta\big(w_i \mid \tilde{\mathbf w}\big), \qquad p_\theta(w \mid \tilde{\mathbf w})_i = \text{softmax}\big(\mathbf h_i^{L} W_{\text{out}} + \mathbf b\big)_w \]
    Read it as: M is the set of selected positions, wi the original token there, and w̃ the corrupted sequence (with its [MASK]s, random tokens and kept tokens). hiL is the final-layer vector at position i; a linear layer turns it into a score for every vocabulary entry and the softmax turns scores into probabilities. The loss is the average negative log-probability of the right answer over the selected positions only. In BERT, Wout is tied to the token-embedding table (the same 30,522 × H matrix, transposed) after a small extra transform; our toy keeps a separate output matrix.

    Training regimes · 11.2

    Next-sentence prediction, RoBERTa and ALBERT

    BERT was trained with a second objective alongside MLM. For next-sentence prediction (NSP), half the training inputs were two consecutive segments from the corpus and half were a segment followed by a random one; a classifier on the [CLS] vector had to say IsNext or NotNext. The motivation was downstream: question answering and natural-language inference take pairs of texts, and NSP was meant to teach the model about the relation between two segments and to give [CLS] a job during pretraining. Devlin et al. reported that removing it hurt those tasks.

    Later work disagreed. Liu et al. (2019) re-ran BERT’s recipe far more carefully (RoBERTa, “robustly optimised BERT approach”) and found that dropping NSP while feeding full contiguous sentences up to 512 tokens matched or improved every downstream result. Their diagnosis: with NSP, half the inputs were pairs of unrelated short segments, so the model saw less coherent long text; and the task itself is largely solvable by topic (two random segments are usually about different things), which is easy and teaches little. Lan et al. (2019) made the same point from the other side in ALBERT: they kept a pair task but made it sentence-order prediction (SOP), two consecutive segments either in order or swapped, so that topic cannot help and the model must learn coherence; SOP helped where NSP did not.

    RoBERTa’s other changes are the ones every later encoder adopted: dynamic masking (a fresh mask every time a sentence is seen, instead of BERT’s ten fixed masks per sentence generated at preprocessing), much larger batches (8,000 sequences against 256), more data (160 GB of text against 16 GB) and longer training, plus a byte-level BPE vocabulary of 50,000. None of these changes the architecture. The figure draws static against dynamic masking for one sentence over ten epochs; toggle and redraw.

    \[ \mathcal L_{\text{BERT}} = \mathcal L_{\text{MLM}} + \mathcal L_{\text{NSP}}, \qquad \mathcal L_{\text{NSP}} = -\log p\big(y \mid \mathbf h_{[\text{CLS}]}\big),\; y\in\{\text{IsNext},\text{NotNext}\} \]
    Read it as: the two losses are simply added. The NSP term is a two-way softmax classifier on the final [CLS] vector (BERT first passes that vector through a tanh “pooler” layer). RoBERTa sets the NSP term to zero; ALBERT replaces it by a swap-or-not classifier on consecutive segments.

    Contextual embeddings · 11.3

    The same word, different vectors

    A word2vec or GloVe table (the tokens chapter) assigns one vector to each word type: every bank in every sentence gets the same 300 numbers, a blend of the river bank and the savings bank. The vector that comes out of the top of a BERT stack is different in kind: it belongs to a word token, this occurrence in this sentence, and it has already absorbed the words around it through twelve layers of attention. These are contextual embeddings, and they are what BERT is really for. ELMo introduced the idea with two LSTMs; BERT made the vectors bidirectional at every layer and far better.

    The figure shows it on the toy model. Our grammar uses bank in two senses (on the bank of the river, at the bank for the money) and bat in two (a bat hangs in the barn at night, hits the ball with a bat). We take 40 sentences of each sense, run them through the model, read the vector at the ambiguous word, and project all 80 vectors onto their two leading principal components (computed live by power iteration). At the input embedding they are one point: same token, same vector. After one block they spread; after two the two senses sit apart, and a nearest-centroid rule fitted on half of them labels the other half almost perfectly. The colours come from the grammar, not from the model: the model was never told there were two senses. Switch word and layer; hover a point for its sentence.

    \[ \cos(\mathbf h_i, \mathbf h_j) = \frac{\mathbf h_i^{\top}\mathbf h_j}{\lVert\mathbf h_i\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf h_j\rVert}, \qquad \text{sense}(\mathbf h) = \arg\max_{s\in\{1,2\}} \cos(\mathbf h, \bar{\mathbf h}_s) \]
    Read it as: similarity between two contextual vectors is the cosine of the angle between them. The nearest-centroid rule computes the mean vector of each sense from 20 labelled examples and assigns a new vector to the closer mean. For a static embedding this rule cannot beat chance, because every occurrence has the same vector. One caution from Ethayarajh (2019): contextual vectors from real BERT layers all live in a narrow cone, so even unrelated words have high cosines; it is the differences between cosines that carry the information, exactly as in the readouts here.

    What people do with these vectors. Frozen, they are features: average the top-layer vectors of a sentence and you have a sentence embedding for search and clustering, which is where today’s retrieval encoders come from (the RAG chapter). Probed, they tell us what the network has learned: Rogers, Kovaleva and Rumshisky (2020) survey more than a hundred “BERTology” papers finding syntax in the middle layers, word sense and semantics towards the top, and attention heads that track specific relations. Fine-tuned, they become the input of a task head, which is the next section.

    Fine-tuning · 11.4–11.6

    Fine-tuning: one encoder, four kinds of head

    Pretraining produces an encoder that knows the language but has no task. Fine-tuning adds a small, randomly initialised head on top of the contextual vectors and trains head and encoder together on labelled data, for a few epochs at a small learning rate (BERT used 2 to 5 × 10−5 with Adam, 2 to 4 epochs). The encoder changes a little; the head learns to read it. Almost every supervised NLP task fits one of four layouts, which differ only in which vectors the head reads. Pick a task to see where the head attaches. (The transfer-learning chapter covers freezing against fine-tuning in general; fine-tuning & LoRA covers the same moves for decoders.)

    Sequence classification and sentence pairs

    For sentiment, topic, spam or acceptability the input is one segment and the head is a single softmax layer on the final [CLS] vector: y = softmax(W h[CLS] + b), with W of size C × H for C classes. Sentence-pair tasks (does sentence B follow from sentence A? are these two questions paraphrases? how similar are they, 0–5?) use exactly the same head on exactly the same vector; the only change is the input, which packs both sentences as [CLS] A [SEP] B [SEP] with the segment embeddings telling them apart. Since every token of A attends to every token of B inside the encoder, this is a cross-encoder: the comparison happens in the layers, not in the head, which is why it is accurate and why it is expensive (every pair needs its own forward pass). Without NSP, [CLS] arrives at fine-tuning untrained; it still works, because fine-tuning teaches the encoder to write a summary there, but mean-pooling the token vectors is a common and often better alternative.     Sequence labelling: one label per token, BIO style

    Part-of-speech tagging and named-entity recognition want one label per word. The head is the same softmax layer, applied to every position: yi = softmax(W hi + b). Entities span several words, so the labels use the BIO scheme: B-type for the first token of an entity, I-type for the tokens inside it, O for everything else. “Marie Curie” becomes B-PER I-PER; two adjacent entities stay distinct because the second starts with a fresh B. Two practical wrinkles. First, WordPiece splits words into pieces, but labels are per word: the convention is to label the first piece and ignore the others when computing the loss, then copy the word’s label at prediction time. Second, the per-token softmax can produce illegal sequences (an I-LOC right after O); a CRF layer or a simple repair pass fixes those.     Span-based tasks: question answering as start and end

    Extractive question answering (SQuAD style) gives the model a question and a passage, packed as a pair, and asks for the span of the passage that answers. BERT’s head is two vectors, S and E, each of size H. The start score of position i is S·hi, the end score of position j is E·hj; a softmax over the passage positions turns each into a distribution, and training maximises the log-probability of the true start and the true end. At prediction time the answer is the pair (i, j) with the highest S·hi + E·hj subject to j ≥ i and a maximum length. The figure draws this live: drag a bar to change a start or end score and watch the best span move; the heat map shows the score of every legal (i, j) pair. The passage and question are our own: the 1866 transatlantic cable ran from Valentia Island in Ireland to Heart’s Content in Newfoundland.

    \[ P_{\text{start}}(i) = \frac{e^{S\cdot \mathbf h_i}}{\sum_{k} e^{S\cdot \mathbf h_k}}, \qquad P_{\text{end}}(j) = \frac{e^{E\cdot \mathbf h_j}}{\sum_{k} e^{E\cdot \mathbf h_k}}, \qquad (\hat\imath,\hat\jmath) = \arg\max_{i \le j \le i + \ell_{\max}} \big(S\cdot\mathbf h_i + E\cdot\mathbf h_j\big) \]
    Read it as: two independent softmaxes over the passage positions, one for where the answer starts and one for where it ends, each driven by a single learned vector dotted with the token’s contextual embedding. The training loss is the sum of the two cross-entropies. The answer is the best-scoring legal pair; the sum of scores, not of probabilities, is what BERT maximises, and the two softmax denominators do not affect which pair wins.

    Where BERT’s 110 million parameters live

    “BERT-base has 110 M parameters” is a number you can derive on the back of an envelope, and doing so tells you what a transformer encoder is made of. Write H for the hidden size, L for the number of blocks, A for the number of heads, V for the vocabulary, P for the maximum position and 4H for the feed-forward width. Each block has four H × H projections for attention (queries, keys, values, output) with their biases, two feed-forward matrices of H × 4H and 4H × H with biases, and two layer norms with a scale and a shift each. The embeddings add V·H + P·H + 2H, plus one layer norm. BERT also carries a pooler, one more H × H layer with tanh that feeds the [CLS] classifier. Notice what is not in the formula: the number of heads A. Splitting H into A heads of size H/A changes how the projections are read, not how many numbers they contain.

    \[ \begin{aligned} N_{\text{block}} &= \underbrace{4H^2 + 4H}_{\text{attention}} + \underbrace{8H^2 + 5H}_{\text{feed-forward}} + \underbrace{4H}_{\text{2 layer norms}} = 12H^2 + 13H \\ N_{\text{total}} &= L\,N_{\text{block}} + \underbrace{(V + P + 2)H + 2H}_{\text{embeddings + LN}} + \underbrace{H^2 + H}_{\text{pooler}} \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: a block is dominated by twelve H × H matrices’ worth of weights (four in attention, eight in the feed-forward layer, because 2 × H × 4H = 8H2); everything else in the block is linear in H and negligible. The embedding table is V·H and is the one large term that does not scale with depth. The MLM output head is tied to the embeddings, so it adds only a transform (H2 + 3H) and a bias of size V; most parameter counts, including the 110 M figure, leave it out.

      \( 4H^2 + 4H = 4\cdot 768^2 + 4\cdot 768 = 2{,}359{,}296 + 3{,}072 = 2{,}362{,}368 \)
      how exactly?
      7682 = 589,824. Four of those (WQ, WK, WV, WO) = 2,359,296, plus four bias vectors of 768 = 3,072. With 12 heads each head’s WQ slice is 768 × 64, and twelve of them make the same 768 × 768.
    2. 2 · Feed-forward network
      \( (H\cdot 4H + 4H) + (4H\cdot H + H) = 2{,}359{,}296 + 3{,}072 + 2{,}359{,}296 + 768 = 4{,}722{,}432 \)
      how exactly?
      W1 is 768 × 3072 = 2,359,296 with a 3,072 bias; W2 is 3072 × 768 = 2,359,296 with a 768 bias. The feed-forward layer holds two thirds of a block’s parameters.
    3. 3 · Two layer norms
      \( 2\cdot(H + H) = 4\cdot 768 = 3{,}072 \)
      how exactly?
      Each LayerNorm has a scale γ and a shift β of size H. Two per block.
    4. 4 · One block, twelve blocks
      \( N_{\text{block}} = 7{,}087{,}872, \qquad 12\,N_{\text{block}} = 85{,}054{,}464 \)
      how exactly?
      2,362,368 + 4,722,432 + 3,072 = 7,087,872. Check against the shortcut 12H2 + 13H = 7,077,888 + 9,984 = 7,087,872. Times 12 layers: 85,054,464.
    5. 5 · Embeddings and pooler
      \( (30{,}522 + 512 + 2)\cdot 768 + 2\cdot 768 = 23{,}837{,}184, \qquad 768^2 + 768 = 590{,}592 \)
      how exactly?
      Token table 30,522 × 768 = 23,440,896; positions 512 × 768 = 393,216; segments 2 × 768 = 1,536; embedding LayerNorm 1,536. Sum 23,837,184. The pooler is one 768 × 768 layer with a bias.
    6. 6 · Total
      \( 85{,}054{,}464 + 23{,}837{,}184 + 590{,}592 = 109{,}482{,}240 \approx 110\text{ M} \)
      how exactly?
      This is exactly the count reported for the released bert-base-uncased checkpoint without its MLM head. For BERT-large (H = 1024, L = 24, 16 heads): a block is 12,596,224, 24 of them 302,309,376, embeddings 31,782,912, pooler 1,049,600, total 335,141,888, which is the “340 M” of the paper. ALBERT-base replaces the 23.4 M token table by 30,000 × 128 + 128 × 768 = 3.94 M and keeps one shared block: 12.0 M in all.
    Encoders vs decoders

    Masked LMs after the decoder-only turn

    Between 2018 and 2020 BERT-style encoders held the top of every NLP leaderboard. Then GPT-3 showed that a decoder, scaled far enough, does most of those tasks from a prompt, and the field’s investment moved almost entirely to decoder-only models. It is worth being precise about why, and about what did not change.

    Why decoders won. Generation is the product: a model that can only label text cannot write it, and most of the economic value turned out to be in writing. Next-token prediction extracts a training signal from every token of every sequence, where MLM uses about 15%; at equal compute the decoder sees roughly six times as many targets. One objective serves every task through prompting, with no per-task head, no labelled set, and no separate copy of the weights per task. And scaling laws for decoders were established early (the next chapter), so the path to improvement was clear. Slide the mask rate in the figure to see the signal gap.

    What encoders still do best. Anything whose output is a vector or a label rather than text. Embeddings for search, clustering and retrieval (bi-encoders) and rerankers (cross-encoders) are overwhelmingly BERT-descended encoders, because bidirectional context gives better token representations and one forward pass over a passage is all you pay. Classification and tagging at scale (moderation, routing, NER over millions of documents) are cheaper and often more accurate with a fine-tuned 100–400 M encoder than with a prompted multi-billion decoder, and the encoder’s latency is a few milliseconds. The design is also not frozen: ModernBERT (Warner et al., 2024) retrained the encoder recipe with 2024 ingredients (8,192-token context, rotary positions, GeGLU, flash attention, 2 trillion tokens) and is state of the art on retrieval and classification at 150 M and 400 M parameters. The honest summary: masked LMs lost the race to be the general model, and remain the right tool for the representation half of the field.

    Reach for an encoder when

    • the output is a label, a tag per token, a span, or a vector
    • you have (or can make) a few thousand labelled examples
    • you must run over millions of texts cheaply, or in milliseconds
    • you are building retrieval: bi-encoder for recall, cross-encoder for reranking
    • the text is domain-specific and a domain encoder exists (biomedical, legal, code)

    Reach for a decoder when

    • the output is text: answers, summaries, code, dialogue
    • you have no labelled data and a prompt will do
    • the task needs world knowledge or multi-step reasoning
    • one model must cover many tasks without retraining
    • you want in-context learning from a handful of examples
    Project

    Project: a tiny masked language model, trained in your tab

    Real BERT took four days on 16 TPU chips. A model four thousand times smaller, trained on a language a million times poorer, still shows every mechanism of this chapter, and you can watch it happen. The project builds a two-layer transformer encoder (hidden size 32, 4 heads, feed-forward width 64, 121-token vocabulary, 25,337 parameters), pretrains it with the MLM objective on 4,000 sentences from a seeded grammar, then fine-tunes a sentiment head on 48 labelled sentences, all in this page, with the same model written again in numpy below.     5 · Does pretraining transfer? Fine-tuning four ways

    The labelled set contains 48 reviews that use only four adjectives: wonderful, lovely, dull, awful. The 300 test reviews use only the other six (brilliant, delightful, superb, boring, terrible, bland), which never appear with a label. A classifier trained from scratch cannot do better than chance on them: it has never been told whether superb is good. A pretrained encoder can, if and only if pretraining placed superb near wonderful, which the cloze objective encourages because the grammar’s reviews pair adjectives of one polarity. def mulberry32(a):
    def nxt():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return nxt

def mask_batch(r, X):                     # BERT's 80/10/10 recipe (here at a 20% rate); at least one target per sentence

# ---------- 2. a two-layer transformer encoder, forward and backward by hand ----------
d, H, F, L = 32, 4, 64, 2; dk = d // H
def init(r):
    g = lambda *s: np.array([r() + r() + r() + r() + r() + r() - 3 for _ in range(int(np.prod(s)))]).reshape(s) * 0.1   # roughly N(0, 0.07^2)
    P = {"E": g(V, d), "P": g(T, d), "Wo": g(d, V), "bo": np.zeros(V), "Wc": g(d, 2), "bc": np.zeros(2)}
    for l in range(L):
        for n in ("q", "k", "v", "o"): P[f"{l}W{n}"], P[f"{l}b{n}"] = g(d, d), np.zeros(d)
        P[f"{l}W1"], P[f"{l}b1"], P[f"{l}W2"], P[f"{l}b2"] = g(d, F), np.zeros(F), g(F, d), np.zeros(d)
        for n in ("1", "2"): P[f"{l}g{n}"], P[f"{l}be{n}"] = np.ones(d), np.zeros(d)
    return P

def ln_f(x, g, b): mu = x.mean(-1, keepdims=True); xc = x - mu; s = np.sqrt((xc * xc).mean(-1, keepdims=True) + 1e-5); xh = xc / s; return xh * g + b, (xh, s, g)
def ln_b(dy, c): xh, s, g = c; dxh = dy * g; return (dxh - dxh.mean(-1, keepdims=True) - xh * (dxh * xh).mean(-1, keepdims=True)) / s, (dy * xh).sum((0, 1)), dy.sum((0, 1))

def forward(P, X):                        # X: (B, T) token ids -> final hidden states (B, T, d) and a cache for backward
    B = len(X); keymask = np.where(X == PAD, -1e9, 0.0)[:, None, None, :]
    x = P["E"][X] + P["P"][None, :, :]; cache = []
    for l in range(L):
        q, k, v = (((x @ P[f"{l}W{n}"] + P[f"{l}b{n}"]).reshape(B, T, H, dk).transpose(0, 2, 1, 3)) for n in "qkv")
        s = q @ k.transpose(0, 1, 3, 2) / np.sqrt(dk) + keymask           # every position sees every non-pad position
        a = np.exp(s - s.max(-1, keepdims=True)); a /= a.sum(-1, keepdims=True)
        o = (a @ v).transpose(0, 2, 1, 3).reshape(B, T, d)
        x1, c1 = ln_f(x + o @ P[f"{l}Wo"] + P[f"{l}bo"], P[f"{l}g1"], P[f"{l}be1"])
        h = x1 @ P[f"{l}W1"] + P[f"{l}b1"]; hr = np.maximum(h, 0)
        x2, c2 = ln_f(x1 + hr @ P[f"{l}W2"] + P[f"{l}b2"], P[f"{l}g2"], P[f"{l}be2"])
        cache.append((x, q, k, v, a, o, x1, c1, hr, c2)); x = x2
    return x, (X, cache)

def backward(P, dx, cache, grads):        # dx: gradient at the final hidden states; accumulates into grads
    X, cs = cache; B = len(X)
    for l in reversed(range(L)):
        x, q, k, v, a, o, x1, c1, hr, c2 = cs[l]
        dz, grads[f"{l}g2"], grads[f"{l}be2"] = ln_b(dx, c2)
        grads[f"{l}W2"] = hr.reshape(-1, F).T @ dz.reshape(-1, d); grads[f"{l}b2"] = dz.sum((0, 1))
        dh = (dz @ P[f"{l}W2"].T) * (hr > 0)
        grads[f"{l}W1"] = x1.reshape(-1, d).T @ dh.reshape(-1, F); grads[f"{l}b1"] = dh.sum((0, 1))
        dx1 = dz + dh @ P[f"{l}W1"].T
        dz, grads[f"{l}g1"], grads[f"{l}be1"] = ln_b(dx1, c1)
        grads[f"{l}Wo"] = o.reshape(-1, d).T @ dz.reshape(-1, d); grads[f"{l}bo"] = dz.sum((0, 1))
        do = (dz @ P[f"{l}Wo"].T).reshape(B, T, H, dk).transpose(0, 2, 1, 3)
        da = do @ v.transpose(0, 1, 3, 2); dv = a.transpose(0, 1, 3, 2) @ do
        ds = a * (da - (da * a).sum(-1, keepdims=True)) / np.sqrt(dk)
        dq = ds @ k; dkk = ds.transpose(0, 1, 3, 2) @ q
        dx = dz
        for n, dn in (("q", dq), ("k", dkk), ("v", dv)):
            dn = dn.transpose(0, 2, 1, 3).reshape(-1, d)
            grads[f"{l}W{n}"] = x.reshape(-1, d).T @ dn; grads[f"{l}b{n}"] = dn.sum(0); dx = dx + (dn @ P[f"{l}W{n}"].T).reshape(B, T, d)
    grads["P"] = dx.sum(0); grads["E"] = np.zeros_like(P["E"]); np.add.at(grads["E"], X, dx)

def softmax_ce(logits, y):                # mean cross-entropy and its gradient wrt the logits
    z = logits - logits.max(-1, keepdims=True); p = np.exp(z); p /= p.sum(-1, keepdims=True)
    loss = -np.log(p[np.arange(len(y)), y] + 1e-12).mean(); dl = p; dl[np.arange(len(y)), y] -= 1
    return loss, dl / len(y)

def adam(P, grads, M, Vv, t, lr, skip=()):
    for k in grads:
        if k in skip: continue
        M[k] = 0.9 * M[k] + 0.1 * grads[k]; Vv[k] = 0.999 * Vv[k] + 0.001 * grads[k] ** 2
        P[k] -= lr * (M[k] / (1 - 0.9 ** t)) / (np.sqrt(Vv[k] / (1 - 0.999 ** t)) + 1e-8)

def mlm_step(P, X, Y, M, Vv, t, lr):      # one masked-LM update; returns the loss
    hs, cache = forward(P, X); sel = Y >= 0
    loss, dl = softmax_ce(hs[sel] @ P["Wo"] + P["bo"], Y[sel])
    grads = {"Wo": hs[sel].T @ dl, "bo": dl.sum(0)}; dx = np.zeros_like(hs); dx[sel] = dl @ P["Wo"].T
    backward(P, dx, cache, grads); adam(P, grads, M, Vv, t, lr); return loss

def predict(P, ws):                       # top-5 fill-ins for every [MASK] in a list of words
    X = encode(ws)[None]; hs, _ = forward(P, X); out = []
    for i in np.where(X[0] == MASK)[0]:
        z = hs[0, i] @ P["Wo"] + P["bo"]; p = np.exp(z - z.max()); p /= p.sum(); top = np.argsort(-p)[:5]
        out.append([(VOCAB[j], float(p[j])) for j in top])
    return out

    What this toy leaves out of BERT. GELU (we use ReLU), tied input and output embeddings, segment embeddings and NSP (single sentences only), dropout, a WordPiece vocabulary (our tokens are whole words), 12 or 24 layers of width 768 or 1024 instead of 2 of width 32, and a corpus of 3.3 billion words instead of 4,000 sentences of a grammar. The masking rate is 20% instead of 15% to give the small corpus more targets per step. Everything else, the bidirectional attention, the post-LN block, the 80/10/10 corruption, the cross-entropy at masked positions only, the Adam schedule with warm-up, the heads for fine-tuning, is the real thing at toy scale.

    Wrap-up · 11.7

    Five lines to remember

    • Encoder, decoder and encoder–decoder are the same transformer block under three attention masks: none, causal, and both joined by cross-attention. No mask means every token reads every token, in both directions, and the model cannot generate.
    • A bidirectional encoder cannot be trained to predict the next token (it can see it), so BERT hides tokens and predicts them: 15% selected, of which 80% become [MASK], 10% a random token, 10% stay; the loss is cross-entropy at the selected positions only.
    • Input is [CLS] segment A [SEP] segment B [SEP] over WordPiece tokens, each position the sum of a token, a segment and a learned position embedding. Next-sentence prediction trained [CLS] on segment pairs; RoBERTa showed it was unnecessary and that dynamic masking, bigger batches and more text matter more.
    • What comes out is a contextual embedding per token: the same word in two contexts gets two vectors, and the senses separate. Fine-tuning reads those vectors through a small head: [CLS] for labels and sentence pairs, every position for tags, two dot products for the start and end of a span.
    • Parameters are 12H2 per block plus a V × H embedding table: 110 M for BERT-base, 340 M for large. Decoders won the race to generate; encoders remain the cheaper and often better choice for embeddings, retrieval, classification and tagging.

    Check your understanding

    Why can a decoder be trained on every token of a sequence at once, while a bidirectional encoder cannot be trained the same way?

    Under the causal mask, position i has seen only tokens 1…i, so asking it to predict token i+1 is a genuine prediction at every position simultaneously. Without the mask, position i attends to token i+1 directly after the first layer, so the target is in the input and the model would learn to copy. The encoder therefore needs the target removed from the input, which is what masking does, and only the removed positions can carry a loss.

    What goes wrong if the selected tokens are always replaced by [MASK], and how does 80/10/10 address it?

    The model would only ever have to build good representations of words next to a [MASK], and it would never need to doubt a visible word. At fine-tuning there is no [MASK], so the representations would be computed in a regime the model never trained in. Replacing 10% of selected tokens by random words forces the model to check every visible word against its context; leaving 10% unchanged forces it to represent even an apparently normal word well enough to reproduce it.

    RoBERTa dropped next-sentence prediction and did not lose accuracy on sentence-pair tasks. What two reasons did its authors and ALBERT’s give?

    First, the task is mostly solvable by topic: two random segments are usually about different things, so the classifier learns a shallow topic detector rather than discourse coherence. Second, the NSP input format itself hurts: half the training inputs are pairs of unrelated segments, so the model sees less long, coherent text than it would with full contiguous passages. ALBERT’s sentence-order prediction removes the topic shortcut by using consecutive segments in original or swapped order.

    Explain why the number of attention heads does not appear in the parameter-count formula, and what it does change.

    The Q, K, V and output projections are H × H regardless of A: with A heads each projection is read as A slices of size H × H/A, which together contain the same H2 numbers. What changes is the geometry of attention: A separate softmaxes over A smaller dot products, which lets the block attend to several things at once at the cost of each head working in a lower-dimensional space. Compute per block also barely changes.

    In the project, a randomly initialised encoder stays at chance on the test reviews while the pretrained one reaches near-perfect accuracy. What exactly did pretraining contribute?

    The test adjectives never carry a label. The only way to classify a review containing superb correctly is to already know that superb behaves like wonderful, and that knowledge came from pretraining: the cloze objective had to predict one adjective from another of the same polarity, which pushes same-polarity adjectives to similar embeddings. The 48 labelled examples then teach the head which side of that existing axis is “positive”. The random encoder has no such axis and nothing in the labelled data can create one for words it never sees.

    For span question answering, why does BERT pick the pair maximising the sum of the start and end scores rather than running one softmax over all (i, j) pairs?

    A softmax over pairs would need n2 scores and a parameter for the interaction of every start with every end. Two independent softmaxes over n positions cost 2n scores and two vectors of size H, and because the score of a pair is additive, the softmax denominators are constants that do not change which pair wins; so the argmax over legal pairs of S·hi + E·hj is both cheap and consistent with the training objective. The independence assumption is a real approximation, which is why the legality constraints (j ≥ i, bounded length) are enforced at decoding time.
