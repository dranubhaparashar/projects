Probabilistic PCA & Factor Analysis

PCA, retold as a story of how the data were made

In chapter 1.13, PCA was geometry: find the directions where the cloud is widest and project onto them. Here we turn it into a model. Pick a few hidden numbers at random, stretch them through a matrix onto a flat sheet, then add a little noise. That story gives PCA a likelihood, so it can be fitted by EM, can choose its own dimension, can fill gaps, and has a close cousin, factor analysis, that gives every feature its own noise level. On the right, tap the cloud to make a new point the way the model does, and drag to turn it.

The idea

PCA as a generative story

Ordinary PCA describes data; it never says where the data came from. Tipping & Bishop (1999) and, independently, Roweis (1998) asked for a recipe that produces data like ours and whose best fit is PCA. The recipe has a hidden, or latent, variable z with only M numbers in it, far fewer than the D features we see. Draw z from a standard Gaussian, map it through a D×M matrix W onto an M-dimensional flat sheet, shift it by μ, and add Gaussian noise of the same size σ in every direction.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{z} &\sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\, \mathbf{I}_M) \\ \mathbf{x} &= \mathbf{W}\mathbf{z} + \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}_D) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the hidden factors z are M independent standard normal numbers. W turns them into a point on a flat M-dimensional sheet through μ; the noise ε then knocks it off the sheet by the same amount, on average, in every one of the D directions.
Say it“z is standard normal in M dimensions; x is W z plus mu plus noise with variance sigma squared in every direction.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{x}) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu},\, \mathbf{C}) \\ \mathbf{C} &= \mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T} + \sigma^2 \mathbf{I} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: integrate z out and x is just a Gaussian. Its covariance has two parts: WWT, the spread the factors create along the sheet, plus σ2I, a small round blur everywhere. A full covariance needs D(D+1)/2 numbers; this one needs only about DM + 1.
Say it“x is Gaussian with mean mu and covariance W W-transpose plus sigma squared times the identity.”

variance across

Turn the noise down to its minimum and the cloud collapses onto the line: that is PCA’s world, where everything off the sheet is thrown away. Turn it up and the factor drowns. Probabilistic PCA is one member of a family that Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville call linear factor models: every member draws hidden factors, maps them linearly, and adds noise. They differ only in what they assume about the factors and the noise.

Inference

Where did this point come from?

The story runs forwards, from z to x. Bayes’ theorem runs it backwards: given a point x, which hidden z probably made it? Because everything is Gaussian and linear, the answer is a Gaussian too, with a closed form. Its mean is PCA’s projection with a twist: it is pulled towards the centre, because the prior says z is usually small and the noise might explain part of where x sits. Drag the black point, then raise the noise and watch the guess shrink inwards and grow less certain.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}) &= \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{m},\ \sigma^2\mathbf{M}^{-1}\big) \\ \mathbf{m} &= \mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}) \\ \mathbf{M} &= \mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{W} + \sigma^2\mathbf{I}_M \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: M is a small M×M matrix. The best guess of z, the posterior mean m, is WT(x − μ), the plain projection, divided by M. The σ2 inside M is what shrinks the guess; with σ2 → 0 it becomes exactly PCA’s orthogonal projection, (WTW)−1WT(x − μ). The uncertainty, σ2M−1, does not depend on x at all.
Say it“Given x, z is Gaussian with mean M-inverse W-transpose times x minus mu, and covariance sigma squared M-inverse, where M is W-transpose W plus sigma squared I.”

Fitting

Maximum likelihood, in closed form

Now fit the model: choose μ, W and σ2 to make the data as probable as possible. μ is the sample mean. For W and σ2 the log-likelihood depends on the data only through its covariance S. Try to beat the maximum by hand: steer the direction, the length and the noise until your ellipse hugs the 80 points, then press the button to see how close you got.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\ln p(\mathbf{X}\mid \boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf{W}, \sigma^2) \\ &\quad = -\tfrac{N}{2}\Big\{ D\ln(2\pi) + \ln\lvert\mathbf{C}\rvert + \operatorname{Tr}\!\big(\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{S}\big) \Big\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the log-probability of all N points. ln|C| punishes a covariance that is too big (a blurry model spreads its probability thin); Tr(C−1S) punishes one that is too small compared with the data’s own covariance S. The best C balances the two. The lab shows the value per point, divided by N.
Say it“The log-likelihood is minus N over two, times D log two pi, plus log det C, plus the trace of C-inverse S.”
your log-likelihood per point
the maximum

Tipping & Bishop showed that the maximum has a closed form, built from the eigenvalues λ1 ≥ … ≥ λD and eigenvectors of S. The columns of W point along the top M eigenvectors, exactly PCA’s directions; every other stationary point of the likelihood is a saddle. Below, the spectrum is real: the ten standardised chemistry measurements of 4,500 wines from this chapter’s project. Slide M and watch the noise level settle at the average of what is left over.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{W}_{\text{ML}} &= \mathbf{U}_M\big(\mathbf{L}_M - \sigma^2\mathbf{I}\big)^{1/2}\mathbf{R} \\ \sigma^2_{\text{ML}} &= \frac{1}{D-M}\sum_{i=M+1}^{D}\lambda_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: UM holds the top M eigenvectors and LM their eigenvalues. Each column of W has squared length λi − σ2: the variance in that direction minus the part the noise already explains. σ2 is the average variance in the D − M directions the model throws away. R is any M×M rotation: it never changes C.
Say it“W is the top eigenvectors times the square root of their eigenvalues minus sigma squared, times any rotation; sigma squared is the mean of the discarded eigenvalues.”
\[ \underbrace{DM + 1 - \tfrac{M(M-1)}{2}}_{\text{free numbers in } \mathbf{C}} \ \text{ vs } \ \underbrace{\tfrac{D(D+1)}{2}}_{\text{full covariance}} \]
Read it as: W has DM numbers and σ2 one more, but a rotation R of the latent space changes W without changing C, and a rotation in M dimensions has M(M − 1)/2 degrees of freedom, so those are not really free. M = 0 is a single round Gaussian; M = D − 1 is a full Gaussian.
Say it“D M plus one minus M times M minus one over two free parameters, against D times D plus one over two for a full covariance.”

An algorithm

EM: a rod held by springs

If there is a closed form, why iterate? Because the closed form needs the D×D covariance and its eigenvectors, which costs O(ND2) and more. With thousands of features (pixels, genes, words) EM costs O(NDM) per sweep. It also copes with missing values, which the closed form cannot. EM, from chapter 1.12a, alternates two easy steps. The E-step fills in the hidden z for each point using the posterior from the section above. The M-step treats those guesses as if they were data and refits W and σ2 by least squares.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}_n] &= \mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_n \\ \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}_n\mathbf{z}_n^{\mathsf T}] &= \sigma^2\mathbf{M}^{-1} + \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}_n]\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}_n]^{\mathsf T} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the E-step. x̃n = xn − x̄ is point n minus the mean. For every point, the posterior mean of its factors, and their second moment: the square of the mean plus the leftover uncertainty σ2M−1.
Say it“The expected z-n is M-inverse W-transpose times x-n minus x-bar; the expected z z-transpose adds sigma squared M-inverse to the outer product of that mean.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{W}_{\text{new}} &= \Big[\textstyle\sum_n \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_n\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}_n]^{\mathsf T}\Big] \\ &\quad \times \Big[\textstyle\sum_n \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}_n\mathbf{z}_n^{\mathsf T}]\Big]^{-1} \\ \sigma^2_{\text{new}} &= \tfrac{1}{ND}\textstyle\sum_n \big\{ \lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_n\rVert^2 \\ &\quad - 2\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}_n]^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}}^{\mathsf T}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_n \\ &\quad + \operatorname{Tr}\!\big(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}_n\mathbf{z}_n^{\mathsf T}]\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}}\big) \big\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the M-step. W is a least-squares regression of the data on the guessed factors, with the uncertainty added to the denominator. σ2 is the average squared distance from each point to its reconstruction, again counting the uncertainty.
Say it“New W is the sum of x-tilde times expected z-transpose, times the inverse of the summed second moments; new sigma squared is the average squared reconstruction error per feature.”

Let σ2 → 0 and the E-step becomes an orthogonal projection: Roweis’s EM for plain PCA. Bishop draws it as a rod held by springs. Each point is tied to the rod by a spring. In the E-step the rod is fixed and each spring slides along it to its shortest length; in the M-step the attachment points are fixed and the rod swings to lower the total spring energy. Step through E and M from a bad start, then switch to full EM and see the springs stop being perpendicular.

\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma^2 \to 0: \quad \boldsymbol{\Omega} &= (\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{W})^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\mathsf T} \\ \mathbf{W}_{\text{new}} &=\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\mathsf T}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\mathsf T})^{-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: X̃ is the centred data, one row per point. Ω holds every point’s coordinates along the rod (an orthogonal projection); the new W is the least-squares fit of the data on those coordinates. It converges to the span of the top M principal components.
Say it“Omega is W-transpose-W inverse, W-transpose, X-tilde-transpose; the new W is X-tilde-transpose Omega-transpose, times Omega Omega-transpose inverse.”

Choosing M

Bayesian PCA picks the dimension

How many factors? The training likelihood is no help: it can only go up as M grows, just as the spectrum above showed. You could hold data out (the project does), but Bishop (1999) found a neater route. Put a Gaussian prior on each column of W, with its own precision αi, and learn the αs from the data. This is automatic relevance determination (ARD), the idea behind the relevance vector machine of chapter 1.9c. A column the data cannot justify gets a huge αi, which squeezes it to zero: the factor switches itself off. Generate data with a known number of factors and press Run; then cut the number of points and see weak factors vanish.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{W}\mid\boldsymbol\alpha) = \prod_{i=1}^{M} &\Big(\frac{\alpha_i}{2\pi}\Big)^{D/2} \\ &\times \exp\!\Big\{-\tfrac12\,\alpha_i\,\mathbf{w}_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{w}_i\Big\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each column wi of W is a D-vector drawn from N(0, αi−1I). A large αi says “this column should be tiny”. Start with as many columns as you dare (here M = D − 1 = 9) and let the data decide.
Say it“The prior on W is a product over columns of Gaussians, each with its own precision alpha-i.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha_i^{\text{new}} &= \frac{D}{\mathbf{w}_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{w}_i} \\ \mathbf{W}_{\text{new}} &= \Big[\textstyle\sum_n \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_n\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}_n]^{\mathsf T}\Big] \\ &\quad \times \Big[\textstyle\sum_n \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}_n\mathbf{z}_n^{\mathsf T}] + \sigma^2\mathbf{A}\Big]^{-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: αi is re-estimated by maximising the evidence (type-II maximum likelihood): the inverse of the column’s mean squared entry. The M-step is the EM update with a ridge penalty σ2A, A = diag(αi), added. Short columns get bigger α, a stronger penalty, and shrink further: a rich-get-richer race that ends with each column either clearly useful or exactly zero.
Say it“Alpha-i becomes D over the squared length of column i; W's update adds sigma squared A to the second-moment sum.”

Minka’s choice

Bayesian PCA here is only half Bayesian: W gets a point estimate, and only the αs are integrated over in spirit. A fully Bayesian treatment would average over W too, by Gibbs sampling or variational inference. Minka (2000) took another path: a Laplace approximation to the evidence of each M, the same idea as BIC in chapter 1.10a. Its pick appears beside the ARD answer above, and both reappear on real data in the project.

The cousin

Factor analysis: a private noise for each feature

Probabilistic PCA’s weakest assumption is that every feature is equally noisy. Real measurements rarely are. Factor analysis, far older than PCA’s probabilistic form (Spearman used it in 1904 to argue for a general intelligence factor), keeps the same story but gives feature j its own noise variance ψj. Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman describe it as the latent-variable view of the SVD: the data matrix is factors times loadings, plus noise that is unique to each column. The shared factors only have to explain what features have in common, their covariance; each feature’s private wobble goes into ψj.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{x} &= \mathbf{W}\mathbf{z} + \boldsymbol\mu + \boldsymbol\epsilon, \quad \boldsymbol\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol\Psi) \\ \mathbf{C} &= \mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T} + \boldsymbol\Psi \\ \boldsymbol\Psi &= \operatorname{diag}(\psi_1,\dots,\psi_D) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the only change from probabilistic PCA is σ2I → Ψ. In the old language of the field, the columns of W are loadings, ψj is feature j’s uniqueness, and its share of variance explained by the factors, Σkwjk2, is its communality.
Say it“x is W z plus mu plus noise with diagonal covariance Psi, so C is W W-transpose plus Psi.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}_n] &= \mathbf{G}\,\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\Psi^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_n \\ \mathbf{G} &= (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\Psi^{-1}\mathbf{W})^{-1} \\ \boldsymbol\Psi_{\text{new}} &= \operatorname{diag}\Big\{ \mathbf{S} - \mathbf{W}_{\text{new}}\tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_n \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}_n]\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_n^{\mathsf T} \Big\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: there is no closed form any more, so EM is the standard tool (Rubin & Thayer, 1982). The E-step weights each feature by 1/ψj, trusting reliable features more. Wnew has the same formula as in PPCA; Ψ takes whatever variance of each feature the factors leave unexplained.
Say it“The expected z is G W-transpose Psi-inverse times x minus x-bar; the new Psi is the diagonal of S minus the new W times the average of expected z times x-transpose.”

A test bench: four thermometers in one room, all following the same hidden temperature (one factor) with loading 0.8 and private noise 0.36. Thermometer D is cheap; raise its noise. Thermometer A can report in other units; change its scale. We fit both models to the exact covariance, as if we had unlimited readings, so any error is the model’s, not the sample’s.

Interpretation

Rotations, and why factor names are a choice

Both models share an awkward freedom. Spin the latent space by any rotation R and the covariance does not move, so the data cannot tell W from WR. For PCA we break the tie by taking eigenvectors, ordered by variance. In factor analysis the tradition is different: choose the rotation that makes the loadings easiest to read. Hastie and colleagues flag this as the method’s weak spot: the same fit can be told as different stories, so naming factors is partly a choice by the analyst. Below, six exam scores from a classic textbook-style example: three sciences and three languages. Turn the rotation, then press varimax.

\[ \begin{aligned} (\mathbf{W}\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{R})^{\mathsf T} &= \mathbf{W}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T} \\ &= \mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T} \quad \text{when } \mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\mathsf T} = \mathbf{I} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: rotated loadings give exactly the same covariance, so exactly the same likelihood. Each feature’s communality, the squared length of its row of W, is unchanged too; only how it is split between the factors moves.
Say it“W R times its transpose is W R R-transpose W-transpose, which is W W-transpose for any rotation R.”
\[ V = \sum_{k=1}^{M}\Bigg[\frac1D\sum_{j=1}^{D} \ell_{jk}^4 - \Big(\frac1D\sum_{j=1}^{D}\ell_{jk}^2\Big)^{\!2}\Bigg] \]
Read it as: Kaiser’s (1958) varimax criterion: for each factor, the variance of its squared loadings ℓjk2. It is large when each factor has a few big loadings and many near zero, a “simple structure” that is easy to name. Varimax chooses R to maximise V.
Say it“V sums, over factors, the variance across features of the squared loadings.”

Another view

The manifold view: a thin pancake

Goodfellow and colleagues offer one more way to see all this. Probabilistic PCA puts nearly all its probability in a thin, flat pancake around a d-dimensional sheet: wide along the sheet, only σ thick across it. That makes it the simplest example of a manifold model, the picture behind autoencoders later in the book: an encoder maps x to a short code h, a decoder maps h back. For a linear encoder and decoder, the pair with the smallest reconstruction error is exactly PCA, and its error is exactly the variance it throws away. Choose d, reshape the pancake and drag to turn it; the two numbers below always agree.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{h} &= f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu) \\ \hat{\mathbf{x}} &= g(\mathbf{h}) = \boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf{W}\mathbf{h} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the encoder f compresses x to d numbers; the decoder g puts them back on the sheet. With W’s columns orthonormal, x̂ is the orthogonal projection of x onto the sheet.
Say it“The code h is W-transpose times x minus mu; the reconstruction is mu plus W h.”
\[ \min_{\mathbf{W},\,\boldsymbol\mu}\ \mathbb{E}\,\lVert\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}\rVert^2 = \sum_{i=d+1}^{D}\lambda_i \]
Read it as: the best possible average squared error equals the sum of the eigenvalues PCA discards. It is reached when μ is the mean and W spans the top d eigenvectors. If the data really lie on a d-dimensional sheet, those eigenvalues are zero and the reconstruction is perfect.
Say it“The smallest expected reconstruction error is the sum of the eigenvalues from d plus one to D.”

Real-world project

Project: latent factors in wine chemistry

Cortez and colleagues (2009) collected laboratory measurements of 6,497 Portuguese vinho verde wines, 1,599 red and 4,898 white, for a study of wine taste; the data are on the UCI repository under CC BY 4.0. We use ten chemistry measurements: fixed and volatile acidity, citric acid, residual sugar, chlorides, free and total sulphur dioxide, pH, sulphates and alcohol. (We drop the eleventh, density: 94% of its variance is predictable from the other ten, and keeping it pushes factor analysis to give density zero private noise, a “Heywood case” that stalls EM.) The models never see colour or quality. The wines are shuffled once; the first 4,500 form a training pool and the other 1,997 are the test set. Each model is fitted to the first N wines of the pool, standardised with those wines’ own means and standard deviations, and judged by its held-out log-likelihood per test wine. Slide N and watch how many factors each method thinks the data can support.

The same project in Python

import json, numpy as np
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA, FactorAnalysis
d = json.load(open("wine.json"))                            # 6,497 UCI wines, shuffled once (seed 0)
X = np.array(d["X"]).reshape(-1, 10); red = np.array([c == "1" for c in d["red"]])
pool, X_te = X[:4500], X[4500:]                             # training pool, 1,997 test wines

def ppca_ll(Z, M, Zt):                                      # closed-form maximum-likelihood PPCA
    S = Z.T @ Z / len(Z); lam, U = np.linalg.eigh(S); lam, U = lam[::-1], U[:, ::-1]
    s2 = lam[M:].mean(); W = U[:, :M] * np.sqrt(lam[:M] - s2)
    C = W @ W.T + s2 * np.eye(10)
    return -0.5 * (10 * np.log(2 * np.pi) + np.linalg.slogdet(C)[1] + np.mean(np.sum(Zt @ np.linalg.inv(C) * Zt, 1)))

def bpca(Z, it=2000):                                       # Bishop's Bayesian PCA (ARD), start with M = D - 1
    N, D = Z.shape; M = D - 1; S = Z.T @ Z / N; lam, U = np.linalg.eigh(S); lam, U = lam[::-1], U[:, ::-1]
    W = U[:, :M] * np.sqrt(lam[:M]); s2 = lam[M:].mean(); a = D / (W**2).sum(0)
    for _ in range(it):
        Mi = np.linalg.inv(W.T @ W + s2 * np.eye(M)); Sxz = S @ W @ Mi; Szz = s2 * Mi + Mi @ W.T @ S @ W @ Mi
        W = Sxz @ np.linalg.inv(Szz + s2 * np.diag(a) / N)
        s2 = (np.trace(S) - 2 * np.trace(W.T @ Sxz) + np.trace(Szz @ W.T @ W)) / D; a = D / (W**2).sum(0)
    return int(((W**2).sum(0) > 1e-3).sum()), W, s2

for N in [50, 200, 1000, 4500]:
    mu, sd = pool[:N].mean(0), pool[:N].std(0); Z, Zt = (pool[:N] - mu) / sd, (X_te - mu) / sd
    pp = [ppca_ll(Z, M, Zt) for M in range(10)]
    fa = [FactorAnalysis(M, svd_method="lapack", tol=1e-10, max_iter=20000).fit(Z).score(Zt) for M in range(1, 5)]
    kb, W, s2 = bpca(Z)                                     # ... held-out score of C = W W^T + s2 I
    mk = PCA(n_components="mle").fit(Z).n_components_       # Minka's Laplace evidence
    ...
fa = FactorAnalysis(3, rotation="varimax", ...).fit(Z)      # N = 4500: loadings, scores, correlations

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Probabilistic PCA tells a story: z ~ N(0, I), x = Wz + μ + ε with ε ~ N(0, σ2I). Integrating z out gives a Gaussian with covariance WWT + σ2I.
  • The maximum-likelihood fit is PCA: W along the top eigenvectors with squared lengths λi − σ2, and σ2 the average discarded eigenvalue. The posterior mean of z is PCA’s projection, shrunk towards the centre.
  • EM fits the same model without forming the covariance, handles missing values, and in the zero-noise limit is a rod pulled into place by springs.
  • A likelihood lets the data choose M: by held-out likelihood, by ARD (Bayesian PCA switches surplus columns off) or by Minka’s Laplace evidence. More data supports more factors.
  • Factor analysis gives each feature its own noise ψj, so it explains covariance rather than variance and is unfazed by units. Its rotation is not fixed by the data; varimax picks a readable one.

Check your understanding

Your PPCA fit with M = 2 on 10 features gives σ2 = 0.4. What does that number mean?

It is the average of the eight eigenvalues PCA would discard: the variance per direction that the two factors leave unexplained. Every column of W then has squared length equal to its eigenvalue minus 0.4.

Why does the posterior mean E[z | x] sit closer to the centre than PCA’s projection?

The prior says z is usually small, and some of x’s distance from μ may be noise. The posterior balances the two, dividing the projection WT(x − μ) by M = WTW + σ2I instead of WTW. As σ2 → 0 the shrinkage disappears.

Can you pick M by maximising the training log-likelihood?

No. A bigger M nests a smaller one, so the training likelihood never goes down. Use held-out likelihood, Bayesian PCA’s ARD, or an evidence approximation such as Minka’s.

One feature is measured in grams, the others in kilograms. Which model cares?

Probabilistic PCA: its single σ2 and its eigenvectors change when one feature is rescaled, so its first factor chases the large-unit feature. Factor analysis just rescales that feature’s loading and ψj; everything else is unchanged.

Two analysts fit the same factor analysis but report different factors. Can both be right?

Yes, if their loadings differ by a rotation: WR gives exactly the same covariance and likelihood. The data cannot choose between them; criteria such as varimax are conventions for readability.
