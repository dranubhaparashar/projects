Curvature: Jacobians, Hessians, Newton, CG & BFGS

Read the shape of the loss, not just its slope

Gradient descent only knows which way is downhill. It cannot tell whether the bottom of the valley is a step away or a mile away, or whether the ground is about to curve back up. Second derivatives can: they describe the local bowl, and a method that knows the bowl can jump straight to its bottom. On the right is a loss surface with two valleys and a pass between them. Drag the white point. The dashed rings inside the lens are the quadratic bowl the Hessian predicts, the orange arrow is a gradient step, and the purple arrow is Newton’s jump to the bowl’s centre. Drag it onto the pass and watch Newton head for the saddle instead of a valley.

The local picture

Close up, every loss is a bowl (or a saddle)

Zoom in far enough on any smooth loss and it looks quadratic. Taylor’s theorem says how: around a point ŵ, the error is its value there, plus a tilt set by the gradient b, plus a bend set by the Hessian H, the W × W matrix of second derivatives. The tilt says which way is down. The bend says how fast the slope itself changes, in every direction and every pair of directions. The model is only trustworthy nearby: walk too far and the real surface curves away from it.

The surface below has two weights, so H is a 2 × 2 matrix that you can see. Drag the expansion point and turn the slice: the lower plot cuts both the real loss and the quadratic model along the line, so you can see where the model stops being honest.

\[ \begin{aligned} E(\mathbf{w}) &\approx E(\hat{\mathbf{w}}) + (\mathbf{w}-\hat{\mathbf{w}})^{\top}\mathbf{b} \\ &\quad + \tfrac12 (\mathbf{w}-\hat{\mathbf{w}})^{\top}\mathbf{H}\,(\mathbf{w}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}) \\ b_i &= \frac{\partial E}{\partial w_i}\Big|_{\hat{\mathbf{w}}} \\ H_{ij} &= \frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial w_i\,\partial w_j}\Big|_{\hat{\mathbf{w}}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: three terms, each one order higher than the last: the height at ŵ, the change you would get if the slope stayed constant, and a correction for how the slope changes as you move. The terms left out (cubic and higher) shrink like the cube of the distance, so close to ŵ the model is excellent and far away it is a guess. H is symmetric because the order of differentiation does not matter for a smooth function.
Say it“E of w is about E of w-hat, plus the step dotted with the gradient, plus a half the step times H times the step.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{H}\mathbf{u}_i = \lambda_i \mathbf{u}_i \\ &\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^\star = \textstyle\sum_i \alpha_i \mathbf{u}_i \\ &E(\mathbf{w}) \approx E(\mathbf{w}^\star) + \tfrac12 \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i\, \alpha_i^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: at a stationary point w⋆ the gradient vanishes, so only the bend is left. Write the step in the Hessian’s eigenvectors ui and the bend splits into independent parabolas, one per direction, with curvature λi. The contours are ellipses whose axes point along the ui and have lengths proportional to 1/√λi. w⋆ is a minimum exactly when every λi > 0 (H is positive definite); one negative λ makes it a saddle.
Say it“Near w-star, E is E of w-star plus a half the sum of lambda-i times alpha-i squared.”

Counting the cost

What a gradient is worth

Why do we bother with gradients at all? Count. The quadratic model has W numbers in b and W(W+1)/2 distinct numbers in H (it is symmetric), so W(W+3)/2 unknowns pin down where its minimum is. A method that only looks at values of E learns one number per evaluation, so it needs on the order of W² evaluations, and each forward pass costs on the order of W operations: O(W³) in all. A gradient from backpropagation hands over W numbers at once for a few times the cost of a forward pass, so about W/2 of them are enough: O(W²). The gap is a factor of about W, and W is large. Slide W from this chapter’s toy network up to a billion weights.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{unknowns} &= \tfrac{W(W+3)}{2} \\ \text{values:}\ \ & \tfrac{W(W+3)}{2} \times W = O(W^3) \\ \text{gradients:}\ \ & \tfrac{W+3}{2} \times 3W = O(W^2) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: W counts the weights (and so the cost of one forward pass, in multiply-adds). A gradient costs about three forward passes (one forward, a backward pass about twice as expensive), but tells you W numbers instead of one. That trade is why every serious training method uses gradients, and why the next question is how much of H we can afford on top.
Say it“W times W plus three over two unknowns: order W cubed from values alone, order W squared with gradients.”

Derivatives with respect to inputs

The Jacobian: how outputs move when inputs move

The gradient asks how the error responds to the weights. The Jacobian asks how each output responds to each input: Jki = ∂yk/∂xi. You need it whenever a network is one block inside a bigger system (the error signal has to pass back through it to whatever feeds it), to measure how sensitive a prediction is to its inputs, and to push input uncertainty through to the outputs: a small wobble Δx becomes Δy ≈ JΔx. It comes from the same backward pass as the gradient, started at an output instead of at the error.

Below is a small network with two inputs, six tanh hidden units and two outputs (fixed weights: the six units face every 30°, so the network squeezes the far-out parts of the plane like a fish-eye lens). The left panel is the input plane, the right panel is where the network sends it. Drag the input point and grow the circle: J predicts that the circle becomes an ellipse; the network’s real image of the circle is drawn on top.

\[ J_{ki} = \frac{\partial y_k}{\partial x_i} = \sum_j w^{(2)}_{kj}\, h'(a_j)\, w^{(1)}_{ji} \]
Read it as: input i reaches output k along every hidden unit j: through weight wji into unit j, scaled by that unit’s slope h′(aj) at the current input, then through wkj out. Add up the paths. Because h′ depends on x, the Jacobian is different at every input.
Say it“J k i is the sum over hidden units of w-k-j times h-prime of a-j times w-j-i.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\Delta\mathbf{y} \approx \mathbf{J}\,\Delta\mathbf{x} \\ &\lVert\Delta\mathbf{x}\rVert = r \;\Rightarrow\; \text{an ellipse} \\ &\text{with semi-axes } \sigma_1 r,\ \sigma_2 r \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: near x the network acts like the matrix J. A matrix turns a circle into an ellipse whose semi-axes are the circle’s radius times J’s singular values σ1 ≥ σ2; |det J| = σ1σ2 is how much area is stretched.
Say it“Delta y is about J times delta x: a circle of radius r becomes an ellipse with semi-axes sigma-one r and sigma-two r.”

Second derivatives of a network

The Hessian, and three cheaper stand-ins

The Hessian of a network’s error turns up all over the place: in second-order optimisers, in pruning (Optimal Brain Damage and Optimal Brain Surgeon delete the weights whose removal the Hessian says will cost least), in quickly re-training after a small change to the data, and in Laplace approximations for Bayesian networks, where its inverse becomes the posterior covariance. With W weights it has W² entries, so practical work usually swaps in something cheaper.

To see whole matrices we use a toy: one input, four tanh hidden units and one output (13 weights), fitted by sum-of-squares to 20 points of a noisy sine wave (synthetic, generated in the page). Move the training slider and switch between the matrices: the exact Hessian, its diagonal, the outer-product approximation, and the inverse built one data point at a time.

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial^2 E_n}{\partial w_{ji}^2} &= \frac{\partial^2 E_n}{\partial a_j^2}\, z_i^2 \\ \frac{\partial^2 E_n}{\partial a_j^2} &\approx h'(a_j)^2 \sum_k w_{kj}^2 \frac{\partial^2 E_n}{\partial a_k^2} \\ &\quad + h''(a_j) \sum_k w_{kj} \frac{\partial E_n}{\partial a_k} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the diagonal approximation keeps only Hii and throws every off-diagonal entry away, which makes the matrix trivial to invert. Becker and LeCun’s recipe gets the diagonal in one extra backward pass: second derivatives with respect to a unit’s input aj are passed back like ordinary errors, ignoring the cross terms between different units k. With a single output and one hidden layer there are no cross terms to ignore, so on this toy the recipe gives the exact diagonal; what you lose is everything off it.
Say it“The second derivative with respect to w-j-i is the second derivative with respect to a-j times z-i squared, and that is passed back from the outputs.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{H} &= \sum_n \nabla y_n \nabla y_n^{\top} \\ &\quad + \sum_n (y_n - t_n)\, \nabla\nabla y_n \\ &\approx \sum_n \mathbf{b}_n \mathbf{b}_n^{\top} \\ \mathbf{b}_n &= \nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\, y_n \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for a sum-of-squares error the exact Hessian has two parts. The second is weighted by the residuals yn − tn; for a well-trained model on clean data they are small and roughly uncorrelated with the second derivatives, so the sum nearly cancels. Drop it and you keep the outer-product (Levenberg–Marquardt, or Gauss–Newton) approximation: first derivatives only, and positive semi-definite by construction. It is only good near a good fit.
Say it“H is about the sum over data points of b-n times b-n transpose.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{H}_{L+1}^{-1} &= \mathbf{H}_L^{-1} - \frac{\mathbf{H}_L^{-1}\mathbf{b}_{L+1}\mathbf{b}_{L+1}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_L^{-1}}{1 + \mathbf{b}_{L+1}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_L^{-1}\mathbf{b}_{L+1}} \\ \mathbf{H}_0 &= \alpha \mathbf{I} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the outer-product form adds one rank-one term per data point, and the inverse of “matrix plus rank one” has a closed form (the Sherman–Morrison identity). So the inverse Hessian can be grown point by point with no matrix inversion at all. Starting from a small αI keeps every step invertible; after the last point you have (αI + Σbnbn⊤)−1 exactly.
Say it“The new inverse is the old inverse minus old-inverse b, b transpose old-inverse, over one plus b transpose old-inverse b.”

Getting the numbers

Three ways to compute curvature

There are three honest ways to get second derivatives out of a network. Finite differences nudge the weights and watch what changes: simple, almost impossible to get wrong, and the standard way to check the other two. Exact evaluation extends backpropagation to second order and builds the whole H in O(W²) operations. And Hessian-vector products skip H entirely and compute Hv for one direction v in O(W), the price of about two gradients. That last one is the workhorse of everything later in this chapter.

Finite differences have a catch: the step ε must be small for the Taylor error to vanish, but not so small that the difference drowns in rounding error (double precision keeps about 16 digits). Slide ε and watch the three schemes’ errors on the toy network from the last section.

\[ \begin{aligned} H_{ij} &\approx \frac{E_{++} - E_{+-} - E_{-+} + E_{--}}{4\epsilon^2} \\ E_{\pm\pm} &= E(\mathbf{w} \pm \epsilon\mathbf{e}_i \pm \epsilon\mathbf{e}_j) \\ H_{ij} &\approx \frac{\partial_i E(\mathbf{w}{+}\epsilon\mathbf{e}_j) - \partial_i E(\mathbf{w}{-}\epsilon\mathbf{e}_j)}{2\epsilon} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ei is the unit vector for weight i, so E+− means “nudge weight i up by ε and weight j down by ε, then evaluate the error”; ∂iE is the i-th entry of the backpropagated gradient. Both are central differences, so their truncation error shrinks like ε² (a one-sided difference only manages ε). The first uses error values only: about 2W² evaluations of an O(W) forward pass, O(W³) for the whole matrix. The second differences backpropagated gradients: only 2W gradient evaluations, O(W²) in all. Rounding error grows like 10−16/ε² in the first and 10−16/ε in the second, so the best ε differs.
Say it“H i j is about four nudged errors combined, over four epsilon squared; or the change in the i-th gradient when you nudge weight j both ways, over two epsilon.”

Exact evaluation. For a two-layer network the second derivatives fall into three blocks, depending on whether the two weights sit in the second layer, the first layer, or one in each. Each block is a short formula in quantities that backpropagation already computes: hidden activations z, slopes h′ and h″, and the output errors δk = yk − tk. With a sum-of-squares error and linear outputs, the output-layer second derivatives are simply 1 on the diagonal (Ikk′, the identity).

\[ \begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial^2 E_n}{\partial w^{(2)}_{kj}\,\partial w^{(2)}_{k'j'}} = z_j z_{j'}\, I_{kk'} \\[6pt] &\frac{\partial^2 E_n}{\partial w^{(1)}_{ji}\,\partial w^{(1)}_{j'i'}} \\ &\quad = x_i x_{i'} \Big[ h''(a_j)\, I_{jj'} \textstyle\sum_k w^{(2)}_{kj}\delta_k \\ &\qquad + h'(a_j)\,h'(a_{j'}) \textstyle\sum_k w^{(2)}_{kj} w^{(2)}_{kj'} \Big] \\[6pt] &\frac{\partial^2 E_n}{\partial w^{(1)}_{ji}\,\partial w^{(2)}_{kj'}} \\ &\quad = x_i\, h'(a_j) \big[ \delta_k I_{jj'} + z_{j'}\, w^{(2)}_{kj} \big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: two output weights interact only through the hidden activations they multiply. Two input weights interact through the curvature of tanh (only if they feed the same unit j) and through every output they both reach. A mixed pair couples through the error δk (only when the second-layer weight leaves the same unit) and through the path they share. Biases are weights whose input is a constant 1. Summed over the data, this is the exact H in O(W²) work.
Say it“Second-layer pairs: z-j z-j-prime. First-layer pairs: x-i x-i-prime times a curvature term plus a path term. Mixed pairs: x-i h-prime times delta-k plus z-j-prime w-k-j.”

Fast multiplication by the Hessian. Pearlmutter’s trick defines an operator R{·} = v⊤∇(·): “how much does this quantity change if the weights move along v?”. Apply it to every line of the forward and backward passes and the last line, R{∇E}, is exactly Hv. It is one forward and one backward sweep with a few extra multiplications: no W² anywhere. Pick a direction v and compare the three routes.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{R}\{a_j\} &= \textstyle\sum_i v_{ji} x_i \\ \mathcal{R}\{z_j\} &= h'(a_j)\,\mathcal{R}\{a_j\} \\ \mathcal{R}\{y_k\} &= \textstyle\sum_j \big( w_{kj}\mathcal{R}\{z_j\} + v_{kj} z_j \big) \\ \mathcal{R}\{\delta_k\} &= \mathcal{R}\{y_k\} \\ \mathcal{R}\{\delta_j\} &= h''(a_j)\mathcal{R}\{a_j\} \textstyle\sum_k w_{kj}\delta_k \\ &\quad + h'(a_j) \textstyle\sum_k v_{kj}\delta_k \\ &\quad + h'(a_j) \textstyle\sum_k w_{kj}\mathcal{R}\{\delta_k\} \\ (\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v})_{kj} &= \mathcal{R}\{\delta_k\}\, z_j + \delta_k\, \mathcal{R}\{z_j\} \\ (\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v})_{ji} &= \mathcal{R}\{\delta_j\}\, x_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first three lines run forward alongside the ordinary forward pass, the next two run backward alongside backpropagation, and the last two read off the answer in the same places the gradient would be read off. vji and vkj are v’s entries for each weight; biases again use an input of 1.
Say it“Push v through the forward pass and the backward pass with the R operator; what comes out where the gradient usually appears is H times v.”

Second-order optimisation

Newton’s method: jump to the bottom of the bowl

If the quadratic model were the whole truth, its lowest point would be at ŵ − H−1b. Newton’s method steps there, rebuilds the model and repeats. On a true quadratic one step is enough. Near a minimum the number of correct digits roughly doubles with every step, which is why Newton is the method of choice for small, smooth, convex problems.

Deep networks break all three of those conditions. The update needs H to be positive definite: where some λi < 0 the model has no bottom, and Newton happily walks towards the stationary point, which near a saddle is the saddle itself. High-dimensional losses are full of saddles. Storing H costs W² numbers and inverting it costs W³ operations. And with minibatches the Hessian estimate is noisy, so second-order methods need much bigger batches than SGD. The standard first-aid is damping: add α to every eigenvalue. Pick a method, drag the start and press Step.

\[ \mathbf{w}_{t+1} = \mathbf{w}_t - \mathbf{H}^{-1}\,\nabla E(\mathbf{w}_t) \]
Read it as: solve HΔw = −g and move by Δw. In the eigenvector picture, the gradient’s component along ui is divided by λi: flat directions get long steps, steep ones short steps. That rescaling is exactly what gradient descent lacks, and why Newton ignores bad conditioning.
Say it“w-next is w minus H inverse times the gradient.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{w}_{t+1} &= \mathbf{w}_t - \big[\mathbf{H} + \alpha\mathbf{I}\big]^{-1} \\ &\qquad\times \nabla E(\mathbf{w}_t) \\ \text{step along } \mathbf{u}_i &= -\frac{\mathbf{g}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_i}{\lambda_i + \alpha} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with α = 0 a negative λi flips the sign of the step along ui, so it goes uphill. Any α > |λmin| makes every denominator positive, so the step goes downhill again. As α grows the step turns into a gradient step with learning rate 1/α: damping slides smoothly from Newton to gradient descent.
Say it“w-next is w minus H plus alpha I, inverted, times the gradient.”

Hessian-free directions

Conjugate gradients: never undo your own work

Steepest descent with an exact line search has an awkward habit: it stops where the new gradient is perpendicular to the direction it just travelled, so its next direction is at right angles to the last. In a long, narrow valley it zig-zags, and each zig partly undoes the previous zag. Conjugate gradients (CG) fix this by choosing each new direction to be conjugate to the previous ones, dt⊤Hdt−1 = 0: minimising along the new direction no longer spoils the minimum already found along the old ones. On a quadratic with W weights, CG reaches the exact minimum in at most W line searches, and it never forms H: it needs only gradients (or Hessian-vector products).

On a real network the loss is not quadratic, so nonlinear CG keeps the same recipe with an inexact line search, and restarts from the plain gradient every so often, or whenever the Polak–Ribière β turns negative. Stretch the bowl and drag the start: the two methods race from the same point. Each time you change the bowl, the start jumps to steepest descent’s worst case.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{d}_t &= -\nabla E(\mathbf{w}_t) + \beta_t\, \mathbf{d}_{t-1} \\ \beta_t^{\text{FR}} &= \frac{\mathbf{g}_t^{\top}\mathbf{g}_t}{\mathbf{g}_{t-1}^{\top}\mathbf{g}_{t-1}} \\ \beta_t^{\text{PR}} &= \frac{(\mathbf{g}_t - \mathbf{g}_{t-1})^{\top}\mathbf{g}_t}{\mathbf{g}_{t-1}^{\top}\mathbf{g}_{t-1}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the new direction is the steepest-descent direction plus a dose β of the old direction. Fletcher–Reeves and Polak–Ribière are two ways to pick β using gradients only; on a quadratic with exact line searches they agree and give exactly conjugate directions. Off a quadratic, Polak–Ribière (with β clipped at 0) is usually the more robust.
Say it“The new direction is minus the gradient plus beta times the old direction; beta is a ratio of squared gradient lengths.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{d}_t^{\top}\mathbf{H}\,\mathbf{d}_{t-1} &= 0 \\ \eta_t^{\star} &= -\frac{\mathbf{g}_t^{\top}\mathbf{d}_t}{\mathbf{d}_t^{\top}\mathbf{H}\,\mathbf{d}_t} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: conjugate means “perpendicular once the bowl is squashed into a round one”. On a quadratic the exact step length along dt is one Hessian-vector product away, so a Hessian-free optimiser can run CG on the quadratic model using nothing but Hv: that is the inner loop of Martens’s Hessian-free training.
Say it“d-t transpose H d-t-minus-one is zero; the best step is minus g dot d over d H d.”

Two dimensions hide how good CG is. Below is a quadratic with up to 80 weights whose eigenvalues are spread evenly on a log scale from 1 to κ. In exact arithmetic CG would finish in at most W steps. In floating point the directions slowly lose their conjugacy, so it can take a little longer, but its speed is set by √κ instead of steepest descent’s κ, and that is the difference that matters. Raise W and κ.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{CG:}\quad \frac{\lVert \mathbf{e}_t \rVert_{\mathbf{H}}}{\lVert \mathbf{e}_0 \rVert_{\mathbf{H}}} &\le 2\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right)^{t} \\ \text{SD:}\quad \frac{\lVert \mathbf{e}_t \rVert_{\mathbf{H}}}{\lVert \mathbf{e}_0 \rVert_{\mathbf{H}}} &\le \left(\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\right)^{t} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: et = wt − w⋆ is the distance still to go, and SD is steepest descent with exact line searches. Both errors are measured in the bowl’s own units, ‖e‖H = √(e⊤He). With κ = 10⁴, steepest descent’s factor is 0.9998 per step, CG’s bound is 0.98: thousands of steps against hundreds. CG often beats its bound, because it also exploits gaps and clusters in the spectrum.
Say it“CG’s error falls at least like root kappa minus one over root kappa plus one, to the t; steepest descent’s like kappa minus one over kappa plus one.”

Quasi-Newton

BFGS and L-BFGS: learn the curvature from the gradients

Newton needs H−1; quasi-Newton methods learn an approximation of it, Mt, from the one thing every step produces anyway: how the gradient changed. After a step st = wt+1 − wt the gradient changes by yt = gt+1 − gt, and for a quadratic y = Hs exactly. So ask the new estimate to reproduce that: Mt+1yt = st (the secant condition). BFGS (Broyden, Fletcher, Goldfarb and Shanno, 1970) makes the smallest symmetric change to M that satisfies it, and keeps M positive definite as long as y⊤s > 0, which a line search satisfying the Wolfe conditions guarantees. It costs W² memory, fine for thousands of weights and hopeless for millions.

L-BFGS (Liu and Nocedal, 1989) never stores M. It keeps only the last m pairs (s, y) and applies M to the gradient on the fly with a two-loop recursion: O(mW) memory and time, with m typically 3 to 20. The test below is the Rosenbrock function, a classic curved valley for optimisers. Drag the start, pick a method and step through it; the convergence plot races all five from the same start.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{M}_{t+1}\mathbf{y}_t = \mathbf{s}_t \\ &\mathbf{M}_{t+1} = (\mathbf{I} - \rho\,\mathbf{s}\mathbf{y}^{\top})\,\mathbf{M}_t\,(\mathbf{I} - \rho\,\mathbf{y}\mathbf{s}^{\top}) \\ &\qquad\quad + \rho\,\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}^{\top} \\ &\rho = 1/(\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{s}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: s is where we went, y is how the gradient answered. The update removes M’s old opinion along the step and installs the one the data just measured: curvature y⊤s/s⊤s along s. Each step adds rank two of information, so after a handful of steps M knows the bowl’s shape in the directions that matter.
Say it“M-next times y equals s; M-next is I minus rho s y-transpose, times M, times I minus rho y s-transpose, plus rho s s-transpose.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{q} \leftarrow \mathbf{g} \\ &\text{for } i = t{-}1, \dots, t{-}m: \\ &\quad \alpha_i = \rho_i\,\mathbf{s}_i^{\top}\mathbf{q},\ \ \mathbf{q} \leftarrow \mathbf{q} - \alpha_i \mathbf{y}_i \\ &\mathbf{r} \leftarrow \gamma\,\mathbf{q}, \quad \gamma = \frac{\mathbf{s}_{t-1}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{t-1}}{\mathbf{y}_{t-1}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{t-1}} \\ &\text{for } i = t{-}m, \dots, t{-}1: \\ &\quad \mathbf{r} \leftarrow \mathbf{r} + (\alpha_i - \rho_i\,\mathbf{y}_i^{\top}\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{s}_i \\ &\mathbf{d}_t = -\mathbf{r} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the two loops apply the BFGS update m times to a simple starting guess γI, without ever writing a matrix down. Every operation is a dot product or a scaled vector sum, so the cost is about 4mW multiply-adds per step. Older pairs fall off the end of the memory, which also lets L-BFGS forget curvature that is out of date.
Say it“Peel the stored pairs off the gradient from newest to oldest, scale by gamma, then add them back from oldest to newest.”

Higher-order derivatives

Higher-order derivatives without building the Hessian

Automatic-differentiation libraries can differentiate their own derivative code, so in principle they can produce the Hessian, and third derivatives after that. In deep learning you almost never want the whole thing: a network with a billion weights has a billion-by-billion Hessian. What you want is its action: H times a vector. Take the gradient, dot it with a fixed v, and you have a single number; differentiate that number once more and out comes Hv, for a small constant multiple of the cost of one gradient. Pearlmutter’s R-operator from earlier is the hand-written version of exactly this.

Krylov methods are algorithms that touch a matrix only through products like these. Conjugate gradients solve Hx = b. Power iteration finds the largest-magnitude eigenvalue. Lanczos builds a small tridiagonal matrix from the space span{v, Hv, H²v, …} whose eigenvalues (the Ritz values) pin down both ends of the spectrum within a few dozen products. Here they run on the project’s network (112 weights, 576 training buildings) at its starting weights, using only Hv. Step k up and watch the edges of the spectrum appear first.

\[ \mathbf{H}\mathbf{v} = \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \big[ \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} E(\mathbf{w})^{\top} \mathbf{v} \big] \]
Read it as: the bracket is an ordinary scalar function of the weights (the slope of E along v), so its gradient is one more pass of backpropagation. The same idea, applied again, gives third-derivative contractions like ∂³E[v, v, ·]; each extra order costs another pass, and in practice deep learning rarely goes past second order.
Say it“H v is the gradient of the gradient dotted with v.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{v}_{k+1} &= \frac{\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_k}{\lVert \mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_k \rVert}, \qquad \lambda \approx \mathbf{v}_k^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}_k \\ \mathcal{K}_k &= \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{H}\mathbf{v}, \dots, \mathbf{H}^{k-1}\mathbf{v}\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: power iteration multiplies by H over and over; the component along the largest-|λ| eigenvector grows fastest and wins, at a speed set by the ratio of the top two |λ|. Lanczos keeps all the vectors it has made (the Krylov space Kk) and finds the best eigenvalue estimates inside it, so it converges much faster and finds the most negative eigenvalue too.

Real-world project

Project: L-BFGS against Adam on building energy

The UCI Energy Efficiency data (Tsanas and Xifara, 2012) describes 768 buildings simulated in the Ecotect software: 12 building shapes, each run with different orientations and glazing. Eight inputs describe each one: X1 relative compactness, X2 surface area, X3 wall area, X4 roof area, X5 overall height, X6 orientation, X7 glazing area and X8 glazing area distribution. Two outputs are to be predicted: Y1 heating load and Y2 cooling load. We shuffle once, train on 576 buildings and test on 192, standardise inputs and outputs with the training set’s means and spreads, and fit an 8–10–2 tanh network (112 weights) by mean squared error. Both optimisers start from the same weights and get the same budget of 1,500 gradient evaluations on the full training set: Adam spends one per step, L-BFGS spends one per line-search trial. import numpy as np, json
from scipy.optimize import minimize

d = json.load(open("x052-data.json"))            # UCI Energy Efficiency (id 242), rows shuffled with RandomState(0)
X, Y, N, H = np.array(d["X"]), np.array(d["Y"]), d["ntr"], d["H"]   # 576 train, 192 test; H = 10 hidden units
mx, sx, my, sy = X[:N].mean(0), X[:N].std(0), Y[:N].mean(0), Y[:N].std(0)
Xs, Ts = (X - mx) / sx, (Y - my) / sy
w0 = np.array(d["w0"])                            # 112 starting weights, drawn once with RandomState(1)

def unpack(w):
    return w[:8*H].reshape(H, 8), w[8*H:9*H], w[9*H:11*H].reshape(2, H), w[11*H:]
def fg(w, X=Xs[:N], T=Ts[:N]):                     # E = mean over rows of 1/2 ||y - t||^2, and its gradient
    W1, b1, W2, b2 = unpack(w); Z = np.tanh(X @ W1.T + b1); R = Z @ W2.T + b2 - T
    D = (R @ W2) * (1 - Z**2)
    return 0.5 * (R**2).sum() / len(X), np.concatenate([(D.T @ X).ravel(), D.sum(0), (R.T @ Z).ravel(), R.sum(0)]) / len(X)
def rmse(w, X, T):                                 # in the targets' own units
    W1, b1, W2, b2 = unpack(w); return np.sqrt(((np.tanh(X @ W1.T + b1) @ W2.T + b2 - T)**2).mean(0)) * sy

def adam(w, budget, lr=0.01):
    m = v = 0 * w; E, g = fg(w)
    for t in range(1, budget):
        m = 0.9*m + 0.1*g; v = 0.999*v + 0.001*g*g
        w = w - lr * (m / (1 - 0.9**t)) / (np.sqrt(v / (1 - 0.999**t)) + 1e-8); E, g = fg(w)
    return w, E

def lbfgs(w, budget, mem=10):
    E, g = fg(w); ev, S, Yk = 1, [], []
    while ev < budget:
        q, al = g.copy(), []
        for s, y in reversed(list(zip(S, Yk))):  # two-loop recursion: q <- H_k g
            a = (s @ q) / (y @ s); al.append(a); q -= a * y
        q *= (S[-1] @ Yk[-1]) / (Yk[-1] @ Yk[-1]) if S else 1 / max(1, np.linalg.norm(g))
        for (s, y), a in zip(zip(S, Yk), reversed(al)):
            q += (a - (y @ q) / (y @ s)) * s
        p, t = -q, 1.0
        if g @ p >= 0: p, S, Yk = -g, [], []
        while True:                                # backtrack until the Armijo condition holds
            En, gn = fg(w + t*p); ev += 1
            if En <= E + 1e-4 * t * (g @ p) or t < 1e-12: break
            t /= 2
        s, y = t*p, gn - g
        if s @ y > 1e-12 * (y @ y): S, Yk = (S + [s])[-mem:], (Yk + [y])[-mem:]
        w, E, g = w + s, En, gn
    return w, E, ev

def hessian(w, eps=1e-5):                          # central differences of the exact gradient, then symmetrise
    Hm = np.array([(fg(w + eps*e)[1] - fg(w - eps*e)[1]) / (2*eps) for e in np.eye(len(w))])
    return (Hm + Hm.T) / 2
def gauss_newton(w):                               # outer-product approximation: mean of J^T J over the rows
    W1, b1, W2, b2 = unpack(w); X = Xs[:N]; Z = np.tanh(X @ W1.T + b1); G = 0
    for k in range(2):
        Dk = W2[k] * (1 - Z**2); J = np.hstack([(Dk[:, :, None] * X[:, None, :]).reshape(N, -1), Dk,
                                                Z * (k == 0), Z * (k == 1), np.eye(2)[[k] * N]])
        G = G + J.T @ J / N
    return G
def spectrum(w):
    lam = np.linalg.eigvalsh(hessian(w))
    return f"lambda in [{lam[0]:.4f}, {lam[-1]:.4f}], {(lam < -1e-6).sum()} negative, {(abs(lam) <= 1e-6).sum()} zero"

print(f"W = {len(w0)} weights; X2 - X3 - 2*X4 = {abs(X[:, 1] - X[:, 2] - 2*X[:, 3]).max()} in every row")
print(f"start: loss = {fg(w0)[0]:.6f}, {spectrum(w0)}")
for B in (100, 300, 1500):
    wa, Ea = adam(w0, B); wl, El, ev = lbfgs(w0, B)
    print(f"budget {B:4d}: Adam loss = {Ea:.8f}   L-BFGS loss = {El:.8f} ({ev} evaluations)")
for name, w in (("Adam", wa), ("L-BFGS", wl)):
    print(f"{name}: test RMSE (heating, cooling) = {np.round(rmse(w, Xs[N:], Ts[N:]), 3)}")
    print(f"   Hessian {spectrum(w)}; Gauss-Newton lambda_max = {np.linalg.eigvalsh(gauss_newton(w))[-1]:.4f}")
r = minimize(fg, w0, jac=True, method="L-BFGS-B", options={"maxfun": 1500, "maxiter": 10**5, "ftol": 0, "gtol": 0})
print(f"SciPy L-BFGS-B, same start, {r.nfev} evaluations: loss = {r.fun:.6f}, test RMSE = {np.round(rmse(r.x, Xs[N:], Ts[N:]), 3)}")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Near any point the loss is a quadratic: value, plus gradient tilt, plus ½Δw⊤HΔw. The Hessian’s eigenvalues are the curvatures along its eigenvectors; all positive means a bowl, any negative means a saddle.
  • Gradients are worth W numbers each, which turns an O(W³) search into O(W²). The Jacobian is the same backward pass started at an output: it tells you how outputs move with inputs.
  • The full Hessian costs W² numbers. Cheaper stand-ins: the diagonal, the outer product Σbnbn⊤ (good only near a good fit), and its inverse grown one point at a time.
  • Finite differences check everything; exact backprop builds H in O(W²); the R-operator gives Hv for the price of about two gradients, which is all that CG, power iteration and Lanczos need.
  • Newton jumps to the bowl’s bottom but is drawn to saddles and costs W³; damping, conjugate gradients and BFGS/L-BFGS keep most of the benefit for O(W) to O(W²). On small full-batch problems such as the energy network, L-BFGS reaches a low loss in a fraction of Adam’s evaluations.

Check your understanding

At a stationary point the Hessian has eigenvalues 4, 0.5 and −0.1. What kind of point is it, and what does Newton’s method do there?

A saddle: one direction curves down. Newton is attracted to stationary points of the quadratic model regardless of type, so near this point its step along the third eigenvector points uphill, towards the saddle. Damping with α > 0.1 makes every step direction downhill again.

Why does the outer-product approximation get better as training proceeds?

The term it drops is weighted by the residuals yn − tn. Early on they are large and that term matters; near a good fit they are small (and roughly uncorrelated with the second derivatives), so the dropped term nearly vanishes.

You check a Hessian by finite differences with ε = 10⁻¹⁰ and the error is huge. Your code may be fine. Why?

Rounding error. E is known to about 16 significant digits, and a four-point difference divided by 4ε² = 4 × 10⁻²⁰ magnifies that error enormously. Use ε around 10⁻⁴ for differences of E, or around 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁶ for differences of the gradient.

On a quadratic with 50 weights, how many line searches does conjugate gradients need in exact arithmetic? And steepest descent?

CG: at most 50, and often far fewer, because it converges at a rate set by √κ and by clusters in the spectrum. Steepest descent has no such limit: its error shrinks by about (κ−1)/(κ+1) per step, which can take thousands of steps.

L-BFGS with m = 10 on a network with 10⁶ weights: how much memory does it need for its curvature, and how much would BFGS need?

L-BFGS stores 10 pairs (s, y), so 2 × 10 × 10⁶ = 2 × 10⁷ numbers (about 160 MB in double precision). BFGS stores a dense 10⁶ × 10⁶ matrix: 10¹² numbers, 8 terabytes.
