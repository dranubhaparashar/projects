Practical Methodology: Baselines, Tuning & Debugging

Knowing the algorithms is half the job; the other half is a disciplined way of using them

A practitioner who knows a few methods well and measures carefully usually beats one who knows many and guesses. This chapter is about the guessing you can replace with measurement: choosing a metric and a target, starting from a default baseline, deciding whether to buy more data, searching for hyperparameters, and finding the bugs that never raise an error. Everything runs on one real problem: reading numbers of one to three digits built from real handwritten MNIST digits. The network in the figure is training in your browser right now, from random weights. Deal new numbers, then raise the confidence bar and watch it refuse the numbers it is unsure about.

Step 1 · the goal

Decide what “good” means before you train anything

Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville boil practical work down to four moves. One: choose an error metric and a target value for it, set by what the application needs. Two: get an end-to-end system working as early as possible, however crude. Three: instrument it so you can see where it falls short: overfitting, underfitting, bad data or a bug. Four: make one change at a time (more data, a bigger model, a different hyperparameter, a bug fix) and measure again. The rest of this chapter is those four moves, done carefully.

The metric comes first because it decides everything else. Plain accuracy is rarely enough. Even a perfect learner has some error left, the Bayes error, when the input does not fully determine the answer (a smudged 4 that is honestly also a 9). For rare events, accuracy flatters a detector that never fires, so we count precision (of the alarms raised, how many were right) and recall (of the real events, how many were caught). And a system that may decline to answer, like a street-number reader that can hand hard cases to a human, is judged by coverage: the share of inputs it answers while keeping its accuracy at the required level. Move the confidence threshold, then switch to the rare-event view.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat S &= \arg\max_{L,\,s}\; P(L\mid x) \\ &\qquad\times \prod_{j=1}^{L} P(s_j\mid x) \\ \text{cov}(\tau) &= \tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_i \mathbb 1[c_i \ge \tau] \\ \text{acc}(\tau) &= \frac{\sum_i \mathbb 1[c_i \ge \tau]\,\text{ok}_i}{\sum_i \mathbb 1[c_i \ge \tau]} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the network has one head for the length L of the number and one head per digit position. The reading Ŝ is the length and digits with the largest joint probability, and that probability is the network’s confidence ci in strip i. With a threshold τ it answers only strips whose confidence reaches τ: coverage is the share it answers, accuracy is measured on those alone (oki = 1 when every digit and the length are right).
Say it“Read the length and digits with the highest joint probability; coverage is the share answered at or above tau, accuracy the share of those that are right.”
\[ \begin{aligned} p &= \frac{TP}{TP+FP} \\ r &= \frac{TP}{TP+FN} \\ F_1 &= \frac{2\,p\,r}{p + r} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: TP are true alarms, FP false alarms and FN missed events. Precision p asks “when it fires, is it right?”, recall r asks “of the real cases, how many did it catch?”. Raising the threshold usually trades recall for precision; the F-score (their harmonic mean) is one number that punishes giving up either. The rare event here: “this number starts with a 7”, true for about one strip in ten.
Say it“Precision is true positives over everything flagged; recall is true positives over everything real; F-one is twice p r over p plus r.”

Step 2 · something that works

Start from a default baseline

The second move is a working pipeline with a sensible default model, not a clever one. The textbook defaults are short. If the problem looks like one that has been solved, copy that model. Otherwise pick by the shape of the data: a fully connected network for a fixed-size vector, a convolutional network for images, a recurrent or attention model for sequences. Use ReLU units, SGD with momentum and a decaying learning rate (or Adam), early stopping from the start, and add regularisation (weight decay, dropout, batch normalisation) when the data is small. Below the defaults sit two cheaper rungs every project should report: a chance or majority answer, so you know what “no learning” scores, and a linear model, so you know what depth is buying you.

Our network reads a 14 × 36 pixel strip and has four softmax heads: one for the length and one for each of the three digit positions. Train the rungs of the ladder one at a time; each run is real training in your browser, checked against the same run in Python.

\[ \begin{aligned} -\log P(S\mid x) &= -\log P(L\mid x) \\ &\quad - \sum_{j=1}^{L} \log P(s_j \mid x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the loss for one strip is the cross-entropy of the length head plus the cross-entropy of each digit head that is actually used. A two-digit number trains the length head and the first two digit heads; the third head gets no gradient from it.
Say it“Minus log P of the sequence is minus log P of the length, minus the sum over used positions of log P of each digit.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf v &\leftarrow \mu\,\mathbf v - \epsilon_t\,\nabla_\theta J \\ \theta &\leftarrow \theta + \mathbf v \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: momentum keeps a running velocity v of past gradients (μ = 0.9 here) and moves the weights by it. The learning rate εt decays linearly to a tenth of its start over the run. Plain SGD is the special case μ = 0.
Say it“Velocity becomes mu times velocity minus epsilon times the gradient; the weights move by the velocity.”

Step 3 · the cheapest fix?

Should we gather more data?

Once a baseline runs, the most common question is whether to collect more data or to change the model. The textbook gives a clean rule. First look at training error. If the model cannot fit the data it already has, more data will not help: make the model bigger, train it longer, or check the data for label noise. If training error is acceptable but validation error is much worse, the model is overfitting, and more data is one of the most reliable cures (regularisation is the other). To decide how much to collect, plot error against training-set size on a log scale and extrapolate. Small multiples of the data rarely change much; think in doublings.

These curves were computed offline: the same network trained with the same recipe on pools of 125 to 16,000 MNIST training digits. Switch between the small and the large model, and choose how many points to fit.

\[ \text{err}(m) \approx c + a\,m^{-b} \]
Read it as: m is the number of labelled digits. Error falls like a power of m towards a floor c that more data cannot remove: the model’s own limit (and the Bayes error). The fit below picks b by a fine search and a, c by least squares on the chosen points; c tells you whether your target is reachable by collecting data at all.
Say it“Error at m examples is roughly c plus a times m to the minus b.”

Step 4 · hyperparameters

Tuning by hand: capacity and the learning rate

Hyperparameters are the settings the learning algorithm does not learn: width, depth, learning rate, weight decay, epochs. Tuning them by hand means understanding what each one does to the model’s effective capacity, its ability to fit the training data, which is limited by the model’s size, by how well the optimiser can use it, and by regularisation. More hidden units, a wider convolution kernel or more zero padding raise capacity; more weight decay or a higher dropout rate lower it. Validation error traces a U as capacity grows: too little and both errors are high, too much and the gap opens.

If you can tune only one thing, tune the learning rate. Training error is U-shaped in it too, and the right side of that U is steep: a little too large and the updates overshoot, much too large and the network breaks down (here the ReLU units die and every input gets the same answer). Model selection, in Russell and Norvig’s terms, is then a loop: train each candidate on the training set, score it on the validation set, keep the best, and touch the test set once at the very end. Below, every cell is a real six-epoch run (110 of them, computed offline in Python). Slide the learning rate and the width, then re-run any cell live to check it.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat h &= \arg\min_{h \in \mathcal H}\; \text{Err}_{\text{val}}(h) \\ \text{gap}(h) &= \text{Err}_{\text{val}}(h) - \text{Err}_{\text{train}}(h) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each setting h of the hyperparameters is trained on the training data and scored on the validation data; we keep the one with the lowest validation error. The gap between validation and training error says which side of the capacity U you are on: a large gap means overfitting, two high errors with a small gap mean underfitting (or an optimiser that is struggling).
Say it“h-hat is the argmin over candidates of validation error; the gap is validation error minus training error.”

Step 4, automated

Grid search and random search

Hand tuning needs experience and attention. An automatic hyperparameter optimiser wraps the whole training run in an outer loop and searches for you. It has hyperparameters of its own (which ranges, how many trials), but they are far easier to set: “learning rate somewhere between 0.001 and 1, on a log scale” is a safe range for almost any network.

Grid search picks a few values for each hyperparameter, usually log-spaced, and trains every combination. It is simple and easy to parallelise, but the number of runs multiplies with every new hyperparameter, and if the best value lands on the edge of the grid you must extend it and search again. Random search (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012) draws each trial’s values independently. Its advantage shows when some hyperparameters matter little: a 4 × 4 grid tries only 4 learning rates, while 16 random trials over a continuous range try 16 different ones. Our map is a harder test for random search: both hyperparameters matter, the best runs sit on the edge of the width range (where every grid puts a whole row of trials), and the 110 cells offer only 11 learning rates. Change the budget and re-draw the random trials; the strip under the map shows how many distinct learning rates each method actually tested.

\[ \begin{aligned} \lambda^\star &= \arg\min_{\lambda}\; \text{Err}_{\text{val}}\big(\theta^\star(\lambda)\big) \\ \theta^\star(\lambda) &= \arg\min_{\theta}\; J_{\text{train}}(\theta;\lambda) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: hyperparameter search is optimisation with a very expensive objective. Each evaluation of the outer function, at a setting λ, means a full training run to find θ*(λ). The outer objective has no useful gradient, is noisy, and costs minutes or days per point, which is why the strategies below spend their trials so carefully.
Say it“Lambda-star minimises validation error of theta-star of lambda, where theta-star minimises the training objective at lambda.”
\[ \begin{aligned} T_{\text{grid}} &= \textstyle\prod_{i=1}^{k} n_i \\ P(\text{hit the top } q) &= 1 - (1-q)^{T} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a grid with ni values for each of k hyperparameters needs the product of the ni runs: 5 values of 6 hyperparameters is 15,625. T random trials each land in the best q-fraction of the space with probability q, independently, so the chance that at least one does depends on T alone, not on how many hyperparameters there are.
Say it“A grid costs the product of the value counts; with T random trials, the chance of hitting the top q is one minus one-minus-q to the T.”

Step 4, with a model

Model-based search: Bayesian optimisation

Grid and random search ignore what they have already seen. Bayesian optimisation does not: it fits a cheap statistical model of the error surface to the trials so far, and uses the model to choose the next trial. A Gaussian process is the usual model; it gives, at every untried setting, a predicted error μ and an uncertainty σ. An acquisition function turns the two into one score that balances exploitation (try where μ is low) against exploration (try where σ is high). Expected improvement asks how far below the best error so far the next trial is likely to go, on average.

It is no silver bullet: the textbook notes that it sometimes reaches expert-level results and sometimes fails badly, and the GP’s own settings matter. A close relative, population-based training (Jaderberg et al., 2017), runs many trainings in parallel and periodically copies the weights of the better ones into the worse ones with perturbed hyperparameters, so the search happens during training. Here the GP searches the same 110 runs as above. Step it trial by trial and change the view to see what it believes and why it picks the next point.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu(\mathbf x) &= m + \mathbf k_*^{\top} A\,(\mathbf y - m) \\ \sigma^2(\mathbf x) &= k(\mathbf x,\mathbf x) - \mathbf k_*^{\top} A\, \mathbf k_* \\ A &= (K+\sigma_n^2 I)^{-1} \\ k(\mathbf x,\mathbf x') &= s^2 \exp\!\big(-\Vert \mathbf x-\mathbf x'\Vert^2 / 2\ell^2\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: x is a setting (log learning rate and log width, both scaled to [0, 1]); y holds the validation errors of the trials so far and m their mean. K compares the trials with each other through the kernel k (A is its inverse after adding the noise variance σn²), and k* compares x with each trial. Near a trial the prediction copies it and σ shrinks; far from all trials μ falls back to m and σ grows to s. The length scale ℓ says how far one trial’s influence reaches.
Say it“Mu is the prior mean plus k-star times K-inverse times the residuals; sigma squared is the prior variance minus k-star K-inverse k-star.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{EI}(\mathbf x) &= (y^{+}-\mu)\,\Phi(z) + \sigma\,\varphi(z) \\ z &= \frac{y^{+}-\mu}{\sigma} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: y+ is the lowest validation error found so far; Φ and φ are the standard normal distribution and density. The first term rewards settings predicted to beat y+; the second rewards uncertainty, because an uncertain point might be much better. The next trial is the untried setting with the largest EI. The alternative LCB rule scores μ − κσ and picks the smallest.
Say it“Expected improvement is the predicted gain times Phi of z plus sigma times phi of z.”

Searching over the model itself

Neural architecture search

Why stop at learning rates? Neural architecture search treats the architecture itself (how many layers, how wide, which operations, how they connect) as the thing to optimise. Russell and Norvig survey the main families. Evolutionary methods keep a population of networks, mutate the good ones (add a layer, widen one, change an operation) and discard the bad. Hill climbing with network morphisms grows a trained network step by step without retraining from scratch. Reinforcement learning trains a controller that writes architectures and is rewarded by their validation accuracy. All of them pay for one training run per candidate, so the field’s real work is making evaluation cheap: share weights between candidates, train each candidate briefly and trust the early result as a proxy, predict the final score from features of the architecture, or relax the discrete choice into a continuous one and use gradients (differentiable architecture search, DARTS).

Our search space: zero to three hidden layers, each 8, 16, 32 or 64 units wide, 85 architectures in all, each trained for six epochs offline. Race evolution against random sampling, then test the cheap proxy: a single epoch of training.

\[ \begin{aligned} a^\star &= \arg\min_{a \in \mathcal A}\; \text{Err}_{\text{val}}\big(\mathbf w^\star(a), a\big) \\ \mathbf w^\star(a) &= \arg\min_{\mathbf w}\; J_{\text{train}}(\mathbf w, a) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same two-level problem as hyperparameter search, but the outer variable a is a whole architecture from a search space 𝒜, and every evaluation trains its weights w*(a) from scratch. Regularised evolution (Real et al., 2019) samples a few members of the population, mutates the best of them, adds the child and retires the oldest member.
Say it“a-star is the architecture with the lowest validation error once its own weights are trained.”
\[ \rho = 1 - \frac{6\sum_i d_i^2}{n(n^2-1)} \]
Read it as: Spearman’s rank correlation between a cheap proxy (validation error after one epoch) and the real score (after six). Rank the n architectures both ways; di is the difference between architecture i’s two ranks. ρ = 1 means the proxy orders them perfectly, which is all a search needs: it only has to know which candidate is better. (This form assumes no ties; ties get average ranks.)
Say it“Rho is one minus six times the sum of squared rank differences over n times n squared minus one.”

When it doesn’t work

Debugging: tests for bugs that never crash

A network with a bug usually still trains. It just trains worse, and since you do not know how well it should do, nothing looks wrong. Many parts of the model also adapt around a mistake: a broken gradient for one layer can be partly compensated by the others. So debugging means designing tests whose answer you know. The textbook’s list: look at the model in action (read its outputs, not just its score), look at its worst mistakes (the confident errors), reason with training and test error (low training but very poor test error points at the test pipeline as much as at overfitting), fit a tiny dataset (any healthy network can memorise a handful of examples), compare back-propagated derivatives with numerical ones, and monitor histograms of activations and gradients (updates should be about 1% of the weights’ size; units that never activate are dead).

Below, plant one of five realistic bugs and run four of those tests. Try every bug and see which test catches it: no single test catches all of them, which is the point.

\[ \begin{aligned} g_{\text{num}} &= \frac{J(\theta+h) - J(\theta-h)}{2h} \\ \text{rel} &= \frac{|g_{\text{bp}} - g_{\text{num}}|}{\max\big(|g_{\text{bp}}|,\,|g_{\text{num}}|\big)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: nudge one weight by ±h, measure the change in the loss, and compare with what back-propagation says. The centred difference is wrong by about h² (curvature), and the subtraction loses digits to rounding at about 10−16/h, so the error is smallest near h ≈ 10−5. Healthy code gives a relative error around 10−7 or less; a bug gives 10−2 or worse.
Say it“The numerical gradient is J at theta plus h minus J at theta minus h, over two h; compare it with backprop relative to the larger of the two.”
\[ \frac{\Vert \Delta W \Vert}{\Vert W \Vert} \approx 10^{-2} \]
Read it as: over one minibatch, the update to a layer’s weights should be a small fraction of the weights themselves, roughly one per cent. Far more and the training is unstable or the inputs are on the wrong scale; far less and the layer is barely learning (a learning rate too low, or units that never switch on). Watch it, with the share of dead units, for the first few dozen steps.
Say it“The size of the update over the size of the weights should be about one in a hundred.”

Real-world project

Project: reading multi-digit numbers, with a tuning and debugging log

The textbook’s worked example is the system Google built to transcribe house numbers from Street View photographs (Goodfellow et al., 2014). The team began with the metric: a transcription is only useful if it is almost always right, so accuracy was fixed at 98%, roughly human level, and the goal became coverage at that accuracy. The first baseline was a convolutional network with ReLU units, later replaced by one with a length head and one head per digit, as here. When training and test error came out nearly equal, so overfitting was not the problem, the team looked at the model’s most confident mistakes. Many turned out to be crops that cut off part of the number: a data-preparation bug, not a modelling one. Widening the crop added about ten points of coverage, and the last few points came from tuning, mostly making the network bigger.

This project replays that story at a scale your browser can train. Each strip holds a number of one to three handwritten MNIST digits (each trimmed to its central 10 of 14 columns, left-aligned with a random shift of 0–6 pixels); the training pool is 2,000 digits from the MNIST training set, the validation pool 1,000 other training digits, the test pool 1,000 digits from the MNIST test set. MNIST is © Yann LeCun and Corinna Cortes, distributed under CC BY-SA 3.0; the 4,000-digit extract embedded in this page (downsampled to 14 × 14, 16 grey levels) is shared under the same licence. Our pipeline started with a planted version of the Street View bug: a crop that shaves five columns off each side of every strip.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{cov}^\star &= \max_{\tau}\ \text{cov}(\tau) \\ \text{subject to}\quad & \text{acc}(\tau) \ge 0.98 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: sort the strips by the network’s confidence, answer from the top down, and stop at the last point where the accuracy of everything answered is still at least 98%. That share is the coverage, the Street View team’s headline metric. The validation set picks every change in the log; the test set is opened once, for the final model.
Say it“Coverage-star is the largest coverage whose accuracy is still at least ninety-eight per cent.”

import numpy as np, sys
d = np.load(sys.argv[1] + "x056_dl/small.npz")          # MNIST at 14x14, 16 grey levels
POOL = {"train": (d["xtr"][:2000], d["ytr"][:2000]), "val": (d["xtr"][d["vi"]], d["ytr"][d["vi"]]),
        "test": (d["xte"][d["ti"]], d["yte"][d["ti"]])}   # 1000 random digits each

def mulberry32(seed, n):                                  # the page's random numbers, n at a time
    s = (seed + 0x6D2B79F5 * np.arange(1, n + 1, dtype=np.uint64)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
    t = ((s ^ (s >> 15)) * (s | 1)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
    t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (t | 61)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
    return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 2.0**32

def compose(pool, n, seed, crop=False):                   # n strips of 1-3 digits, 14 x 36 pixels, left-aligned
    imgs, labs = pool; r = mulberry32(seed, 5 * n).reshape(n, 5); X = np.zeros((n, 14, 36))
    L = (1 + r[:, 0] * 3).astype(int); off = (r[:, 4] * 7).astype(int); K = (r[:, 1:4] * len(imgs)).astype(int)
    for j in range(3):
        i = np.nonzero(L > j)[0]; cols = off[i, None] + 10 * j + np.arange(10)
        X[i[:, None], :, cols] = imgs[K[i, j]][:, :, 2:12].transpose(0, 2, 1) / 15
    if crop: X[:, :, :5] = 0; X[:, :, 31:] = 0               # the "tight crop" bug: 5 columns lost at each side
    return X.reshape(n, 504), L - 1, np.where(np.arange(3) < L[:, None], labs[K], 0)

def init(sizes, seed):
    r = mulberry32(seed, sum(a * b for a, b in zip(sizes[:-1], sizes[1:]))); W, k = [], 0
    for l, (a, b) in enumerate(zip(sizes[:-1], sizes[1:])):
        lim = np.sqrt(6 / a) if l < len(sizes) - 2 else np.sqrt(6 / (a + b))   # He for ReLU layers, Glorot for the heads
        W.append([(2 * r[k:k + a * b] - 1).reshape(a, b) * lim, np.zeros(b)]); k += a * b
    return W

def forward(W, X):
    H = [X]
    for l, (w, b) in enumerate(W): Z = H[-1] @ w + b; H.append(np.maximum(Z, 0) if l < len(W) - 1 else Z)
    return H

def softmax(z): e = np.exp(z - z.max(1, keepdims=True)); return e / e.sum(1, keepdims=True)

def heads(out):                                           # length head (3 classes) + three digit heads (10 each)
    return softmax(out[:, :3]), [softmax(out[:, 3 + 10 * j: 13 + 10 * j]) for j in range(3)]

def loss_grad(W, X, L, D):
    H = forward(W, X); pL, pD = heads(H[-1]); n = len(X); g = np.zeros_like(H[-1])
    loss = -np.log(pL[np.arange(n), L])
    g[:, :3] = pL; g[np.arange(n), L] -= 1
    for j in range(3):
        m = L >= j; loss -= m * np.log(pD[j][np.arange(n), D[:, j]])
        gj = pD[j].copy(); gj[np.arange(n), D[:, j]] -= 1; g[:, 3 + 10 * j: 13 + 10 * j] = gj * m[:, None]
    g /= n; grads = [None] * len(W)
    for l in range(len(W) - 1, -1, -1):
        grads[l] = [H[l].T @ g, g.sum(0)]
        if l: g = (g @ W[l][0].T) * (H[l] > 0)
    return loss.mean(), grads

def read(W, X):                                           # the most probable whole number and its probability
    pL, pD = heads(forward(W, X)[-1]); m = np.stack([p.max(1) for p in pD], 1)
    seq = pL * np.stack([m[:, 0], m[:, 0] * m[:, 1], m[:, 0] * m[:, 1] * m[:, 2]], 1)
    Lh = seq.argmax(1); return Lh, np.stack([p.argmax(1) for p in pD], 1), seq.max(1)

def seq_err(W, data):
    X, L, D = data; Lh, Dh, conf = read(W, X)
    ok = (Lh == L) & np.all((Dh == D) | (np.arange(3)[None] > L[:, None]), 1)
    return 1 - ok.mean(), ok, conf

def coverage_at(ok, conf, target=0.98):                   # largest share answered with accuracy >= target
    o = np.lexsort((np.arange(len(conf)), -conf)); acc = np.cumsum(ok[o]) / np.arange(1, len(ok) + 1)
    good = np.nonzero(acc >= target)[0]; return (good[-1] + 1) / len(ok) if len(good) else 0.0

def train(hidden=(64,), lr=0.05, mom=0.9, wd=0.0, epochs=6, n=3000, seed=1, crop=False, fresh=False, early=False, pool=None, B=32):
    pool = pool or POOL["train"]; val = compose(POOL["val"], 1000, 202, crop)
    W = init([504, *hidden, 33], seed); V = [[np.zeros_like(w), np.zeros_like(b)] for w, b in W]; best = (np.inf, None, 0); log = []
    for e in range(epochs):
        tr = compose(pool, n, 101 + (1000 * e if fresh else 0), crop)
        r = mulberry32(seed + 7919 * (e + 1), n); p = np.arange(n); tot = 0.0
        for i in range(n - 1, 0, -1): j = int(r[i] * (i + 1)); p[i], p[j] = p[j], p[i]
        eta = lr * (1 - 0.9 * e / epochs)                 # learning rate decays linearly to 10%
        for s in range(0, n, B):
            b = p[s:s + B]; l, G = loss_grad(W, tr[0][b], tr[1][b], tr[2][b]); tot += l * len(b)
            if not np.isfinite(l): return dict(diverged=True, epoch=e, log=log)
            for (w, bb), (gw, gb), v in zip(W, G, V):
                v[0] *= mom; v[0] -= eta * (gw + wd * w); w += v[0]; v[1] *= mom; v[1] -= eta * gb; bb += v[1]
        ve = seq_err(W, val)[0]; log.append((tot / n, ve, loss_grad(W, *val)[0]))
        if ve < best[0]: best = (ve, [[w.copy(), b.copy()] for w, b in W], e)
    if early: W = best[1]

The browser’s trainer is a separate JavaScript implementation that follows the same recipe step for step: the same random numbers (mulberry32) for composing strips, initialising weights and shuffling, the same minibatches, momentum and learning-rate decay, all in 64-bit floating point. It loops over one strip at a time and skips zero pixels, where numpy multiplies whole matrices, so sums are added in a different order; the weights still agree to about 10 decimal places, and every error count in the log matches Python exactly. The grid of 110 runs, the learning curves and the 85 architectures were computed offline with the same Python functions (they would take minutes in a browser); the live re-run button in “Tuning by hand” reproduces any grid cell exactly. Honest limits: an MLP is a poor reader of shifted digits (the Street View system used a deep convolutional network trained on millions of images), and 1,000 validation strips measure error to about ±1.5 points, so differences of a point or two between log entries are noise.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Fix the metric and the target first (coverage at 98% accuracy, precision at a given recall), then build the simplest end-to-end system and measure it against majority and linear baselines.
  • Training error decides what to do next: if it is high, grow or fix the model; if only validation error is high, get more data or regularise, and read a log-scale learning curve to see how much data would help.
  • Tune the learning rate first; capacity follows from width, depth and regularisation. Select on validation error and open the test set once.
  • Random search beats grid search when some hyperparameters barely matter; Bayesian optimisation and evolution reuse what earlier trials taught them, and architecture search is the same problem with the model as the variable.
  • Bugs rarely crash: check gradients numerically, overfit a tiny set, compare training with validation error, watch update sizes and dead units, and look at the most confident mistakes.

Check your understanding

Training error is 30%, validation error 32%. Your manager offers to label 10,000 more examples. What do you say?

Not yet. The gap is small, so the model is not overfitting; it cannot even fit the data it has. More data mainly shrinks the gap. First increase capacity (wider or deeper, longer training, a better learning rate) or look for data and code problems; revisit data once training error is acceptable and the gap has opened.

Why does random search with 16 trials usually find a better learning rate than a 4 × 4 grid over learning rate and width?

The grid tests only four distinct learning rates, each repeated at four widths. Random trials test sixteen different learning rates. When one hyperparameter matters much more than the other, the extra distinct values of that one are what count.

Your gradient check passes but the network still cannot overfit 8 examples. Name a bug that fits.

Anything that makes correct gradients useless: all-zero initialisation (every hidden unit is identical and receives zero gradient), inputs on a wildly wrong scale, a learning rate far too small or too large, or labels that do not match their inputs. The gradient check only proves back-propagation matches the loss you wrote.

Training error 5%, validation error 70%, and the same model scored 25% last week. Overfitting?

Unlikely to appear that suddenly. A gap that large, especially after a change, points at the evaluation pipeline: a different preprocessing at test time, labels in a different order, a model that is not loaded properly. Look at individual validation predictions before adding regularisation.

Why does Bayesian optimisation sometimes try a setting that its own model predicts to be worse than the best so far?

Because the acquisition function also rewards uncertainty. Expected improvement adds σφ(z): a region with few trials might hide a much better setting, and occasionally checking it is how the search avoids getting stuck around its first good point.
