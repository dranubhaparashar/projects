Markov Random Fields

When neighbours influence each other and no arrow makes sense

A Bayesian network needs a direction on every edge: this causes that. Neighbouring pixels, interacting atoms and co-expressed genes have no such direction; they simply tend to agree. A Markov random field draws those links as plain lines, scores every joint configuration with a product of local compatibility terms, and still lets you read conditional independence straight off the picture. On the right, three lines from a book printed in 1858 sit under an Ising model, the simplest field there is: the top panel is what a noisy scanner saw, the bottom is the model’s best guess at the clean page. Drag across the top panel to spray on more noise and watch the model put the letters back.

Bishop 8.3 · ESL 17.1

Pixels have no arrows

The last two chapters built joint distributions from arrows: each variable gets a table conditioned on its parents. That works when there is a story of cause and effect. But which way should the arrow point between two neighbouring pixels of a printed letter? Neither pixel causes the other; they are both dark because they are part of the same stroke. Any direction you choose is arbitrary, and a different choice gives a different set of tables to fill in.

An undirected graphical model, also called a Markov random field or Markov network, keeps the graph and drops the arrows. There is one node per random variable, and an edge means that two variables interact directly. A missing edge is a promise: those two are conditionally independent once you know the rest. Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman open their chapter with exactly this reading. The graph is a compact picture of which variables talk to which, and sparse graphs (few edges) are easy to interpret and cheap to compute with.

The cleanest way to feel this is to look at one pixel. In the image model of this chapter, a pixel xi (+1 for ink, −1 for paper) is linked to its four neighbours and to the noisy value yi the scanner reported. Everything the rest of the image can tell you about xi flows through those five numbers. Click the neighbours and the observation to flip them, then move the three sliders.

\[ \begin{aligned} &p(x_i = +1 \mid \text{the rest}) = \sigma(2 f_i) \\ &f_i = \beta \textstyle\sum_{j \in N(i)} x_j + \eta\, y_i - h \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: N(i) is the set of xi’s four neighbours and σ(t) = 1/(1 + e−t) is the logistic function. The local field fi adds three pulls: β times the vote of the neighbours, η times the observation, and a constant bias −h toward paper. Pixels further away do not appear at all: once the neighbours and yi are fixed, they cannot change this probability. (Where the formula comes from is the Ising energy of section 6.)
Say it“The chance this pixel is ink is the logistic of twice its local field: beta times the neighbours’ sum, plus eta times what was observed, minus h.”

Bishop 8.3.1 · conditional independence

Separation: independence you can see

In a directed graph, deciding whether a set C blocks the information between A and B needs d-separation, with its special rule for colliders (two arrows meeting head to head, where observing the middle node opens the path). Undirected graphs have no colliders, so the test is plain graph separation. Delete the nodes in C, together with their edges. If no path is left from any node in A to any node in B, the graph promises that A and B are conditionally independent given C. Observing more nodes can only cut more paths, never open new ones.

One consequence is worth its own name. The Markov blanket of a node, the smallest set that shields it from everything else, is just its set of neighbours. In a Bayesian network it is the parents, the children and the children’s other parents; here there is nothing to add. Paint the sets with the buttons, then click nodes: start by adding node 8 to C and watch the last open path close.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{every path } A \leadsto B \text{ meets } C \\ &\quad\Longrightarrow\; X_A \perp\!\!\!\perp X_B \mid X_C \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: if C separates A from B in the graph, then once XC is known, learning XA tells you nothing more about XB. The arrow goes one way only: a path that is open does not guarantee dependence, because the numbers could happen to cancel.
Say it“If C cuts every path from A to B, then A is independent of B given C.”
\[ p\big(x_i \mid x_{V \setminus \{i\}}\big) = p\big(x_i \mid x_{N(i)}\big) \]
Read it as: V is the set of all nodes and N(i) the neighbours of node i. Conditioning on all the other variables gives the same answer as conditioning on the neighbours alone, so N(i) is the Markov blanket of i.
Say it“Given its neighbours, a node ignores the rest of the graph.”

ESL 17.2 · Bishop 8.3.1

Three Markov properties, checked with numbers

“The graph promises independence” can mean three things, from weakest to strongest. The pairwise property: two nodes with no edge between them are independent given all the other nodes. The local property: a node is independent of everything outside its neighbourhood, given its neighbours. The global property: for any sets A, B and C where C separates A from B, XA is independent of XB given XC. The global one is the useful one, because it lets you condition on small sets.

For a distribution that gives every configuration a probability above zero, the three are equivalent: if the pairwise statements hold, so do all the global ones. That is the setting Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman work in. Without positivity the implication can fail, and the lab has an example. Here five binary variables sit on a 4-cycle a–b–c–d with a tail c–e, each edge carrying an Ising potential exp(J sisj) with s = ±1. The page sums all 32 configurations and measures each statement with the conditional mutual information, which is zero exactly when the independence holds. Move the couplings, add the chord a–c, then try the four-copies model.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{pairwise: } (i,j) \notin E \;\Rightarrow \\ &\qquad x_i \perp\!\!\!\perp x_j \mid x_{V \setminus \{i,j\}} \\ &\text{local: } x_i \perp\!\!\!\perp x_{V \setminus (\{i\} \cup N(i))} \mid x_{N(i)} \\ &\text{global: } C \text{ separates } A, B \;\Rightarrow \\ &\qquad x_A \perp\!\!\!\perp x_B \mid x_C \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: E is the edge set. Each line claims more than the one before: pairwise conditions on everything else, local only on the neighbours, global on any separating set. Global implies local implies pairwise for every distribution; the reverse holds when p(x) > 0 for all x.
Say it“No edge, independent given the rest; given its neighbours, a node ignores the rest; separated sets are independent given the separator.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &I(X_A; X_B \mid X_C) = \textstyle\sum p(x_A, x_B, x_C) \\ &\qquad \times \log_2 \dfrac{p(x_A, x_B \mid x_C)}{p(x_A \mid x_C)\, p(x_B \mid x_C)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the sum runs over every joint value of the three sets. It is the average surprise of the joint compared with what independence would predict, measured in bits. It is never negative and is zero exactly when XA and XB are independent given XC.
Say it“The conditional mutual information is the expected log ratio of the joint to the product of the conditionals.”

Bishop 8.3.2 · Deep Learning 16.2.2 · ESL 17.2

Cliques and the factorisation

Separation says what an undirected model must not do. To build one, you need a way to write p(x) that keeps every promise automatically. The answer uses cliques: sets of nodes in which every pair is joined by an edge. A maximal clique is one you cannot enlarge without losing that property. Give each clique C a potential ψC(xC) ≥ 0, a table that scores how compatible the values of its variables are, multiply the potentials, and divide by the total. Two variables that share no clique never appear in the same factor, so the product cannot tie them together except through the variables between them.

The Hammersley–Clifford theorem says this is not just sufficient but, for positive distributions, the only way: a strictly positive p satisfies the global Markov property of a graph if and only if it factorises over the graph’s cliques. Writing each positive potential as ψC = exp(−EC) turns the product into a sum of energies, the Boltzmann form behind the energy-based models of later chapters. One subtlety from Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman: the graph alone does not fix the factorisation. A triangle allows one three-way potential, or just three pairwise ones (a pairwise Markov field); both have the same graph. Click two nodes to add or remove the edge between them and watch the cliques and the parameter counts change.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{x}) &= \frac{1}{Z} \prod_{C} \psi_C(\mathbf{x}_C) \\ Z &= \sum_{\mathbf{x}} \prod_{C} \psi_C(\mathbf{x}_C) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the product runs over the maximal cliques (any smaller clique can be folded into a maximal one that contains it). The partition function Z sums the product over every joint configuration, so that the probabilities add up to 1. Z is the expensive part: for n binary variables it has 2n terms.
Say it“p of x is one over Z times the product of the clique potentials; Z is that product summed over every x.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \psi_C &= e^{-E_C(\mathbf{x}_C)} \;\Rightarrow \\ p(\mathbf{x}) &= \frac{1}{Z}\, e^{-\sum_C E_C(\mathbf{x}_C)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: any strictly positive potential can be written as the exponential of minus an energy. Multiplying potentials then means adding energies, and low total energy means high probability. This is the Boltzmann distribution of statistical physics.
Say it“Potentials are exponentials of minus energies, so probability falls as total energy rises.”

Bishop 8.3.2 · Deep Learning 16.2.2 · by hand

Potentials are not probabilities

In a Bayesian network every factor is a conditional probability, so you can read it directly. A potential is only a score: it has no need to sum to 1, and on its own it is not the marginal of anything. Take the smallest interesting case, a chain a – b – c of binary variables with two potentials. The unnormalised product p̃ is easy to compute for any single configuration. The normalised p needs Z, the sum over all eight configurations. The marginal p(a, b) depends on ψbc as well, because b is shared: Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville stress the same thing: a clique potential measures how strongly its variables favour each joint value, and is not itself a probability distribution.

Type new numbers into the two tables, then try the two buttons. Multiplying a potential by a constant changes Z and nothing else. Making ψbc flat is the one case where the normalised ψab is the true marginal.

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde p(a,b,c) &= \psi_{ab}(a,b)\, \psi_{bc}(b,c) \\ p(a,b,c) &= \tilde p(a,b,c) / Z \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: p̃ (“p tilde”) is the unnormalised probability: just the product of the potentials for this configuration. Dividing by Z = Σ p̃ over all eight configurations turns the scores into probabilities.
Say it“p tilde is psi-a-b times psi-b-c; divide by Z to get a probability.”
\[ p(a,b) = \frac{1}{Z}\, \psi_{ab}(a,b) \sum_{c} \psi_{bc}(b,c) \]
Read it as: to get the marginal of a and b, sum c out. Because c only appears in ψbc, the sum becomes a message from c to b, one number for each value of b, that reweights ψab. Unless that message is the same for both values of b, p(a, b) is not proportional to ψab.
Say it“p of a and b is psi-a-b times the sum over c of psi-b-c, over Z.”

Bishop 8.3.3 · image de-noising

The Ising model cleans an image

Bishop’s illustration puts all of this to work. A clean binary image x (pixels ±1) is corrupted by flipping each pixel independently with a small probability; you see only the noisy image y. The graph has two kinds of edge. Each hidden pixel xi is joined to its four neighbours, because images are mostly smooth; and to its own observation yi, because the noise is usually small. Every clique is a pair, so the model is a pairwise field: the Ising model of a magnet, with an extra term that tugs each spin toward the data.

Finding the most probable x means minimising the energy. Exactly minimising it looks hard, since there are 2n images, so Bishop uses iterated conditional modes (ICM, Besag 1986): visit the pixels one at a time and set each to the value that lowers the energy most, given its current neighbours. That is just the sign of the local field from the first section. Each change can only lower the energy, so the process stops, but where it stops depends on where it started. Here the pixels are visited in checkerboard order: first every pixel with an even row-plus-column, then every odd one. Pixels of one colour share no edge, so updating them together is the same as updating them one by one. Press One sweep a few times, then click any pixel of the estimate x to see the arithmetic behind its value.

\[ \begin{aligned} E(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) &= h \sum_i x_i \;-\; \beta \sum_{\{i,j\}} x_i x_j \\ &\quad - \eta \sum_i x_i y_i \\ p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) &= \tfrac{1}{Z} \exp\{-E(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the middle sum runs over neighbouring pairs. Agreeing neighbours (xixj = +1) lower the energy by β; a pixel that agrees with its observation lowers it by η; h > 0 makes ink slightly more expensive than paper. The lower the energy, the more probable the pair (x, y).
Say it“Energy is h times the sum of x, minus beta times neighbour agreement, minus eta times agreement with the data.”
\[ \begin{aligned} x_i &\leftarrow \operatorname{sign}\Big(\beta \textstyle\sum_{j \in N(i)} x_j + \eta\, y_i - h\Big) \\ \eta^{\star} &= \tfrac12 \ln \tfrac{1-q}{q} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the ICM update sets a pixel to the sign of its local field (a tie leaves it alone). The second line says what η means: if each pixel is flipped with probability q, then p(yi | xi) is proportional to exp(η* xiyi). For q = 10%, η* = ½ ln 9 ≈ 1.10. With h = 0 only the ratio β/η matters, so Bishop’s β = 1.0, η = 2.1 behaves like β ≈ 0.52 with the matched η*.
Say it“Set each pixel to the sign of its local field; the right eta is half the log of the odds against a flip.”

Bishop 8.3.3 · exact MAP by graph cuts

ICM gets stuck; a minimum cut does not

ICM only ever changes one pixel at a time, and only when that change helps on its own. With Bishop’s settings (β = 1.0, η = 2.1) a wrong pixel inside the image is corrected only if all four of its neighbours disagree with it, because three against one gives a pull of 2β = 2.0, less than η. A clump of two wrong pixels therefore survives forever, even though flipping both together would lower the energy. ICM has found a local minimum.

For this particular energy (binary labels, and couplings β ≥ 0 that reward agreement) the global minimum can be found exactly and quickly, as Greig, Porteous and Seheult showed in 1989 and Bishop mentions. Build a network with a source s (“ink”), a sink t (“paper”) and one node per pixel. Any way of cutting the network into an s side and a t side is a labelling of the image, and the capacities can be chosen so that the cost of the cut equals the energy plus a constant. The cheapest cut is found by a maximum-flow algorithm (the max-flow min-cut theorem), here Dinic’s, in a fraction of a second. Slide β and compare the two energies; then switch the chart to errors and see that lower energy is not always fewer mistakes.

\[ \begin{aligned} E(\mathbf{x}) &= \text{const} + \sum_i c_i\, [x_i \ne \ell_i] \\ &\quad + \sum_{\{i,j\}} 2\beta\, [x_i \ne x_j] \\ c_i &= 2\,|h - \eta\, y_i| \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: [·] is 1 when the condition holds and 0 otherwise, and ℓi is pixel i’s cheaper label on its own (ink if h − ηyi < 0). Each pixel pays ci = 2|h − ηyi| if it takes the other label, and each neighbouring pair pays 2β if it disagrees. Put a pixel on the source side for “ink” and the sink side for “paper”: an arc is cut exactly when its cost is paid, so the minimum cut is the minimum energy. It works because every pair term rewards agreement (β ≥ 0); with β < 0 the capacities would be negative.
Say it“Energy is a constant plus what each pixel pays for its costlier label plus two beta for each disagreeing pair: exactly the cost of a cut.”

Bishop 8.3.4 · Deep Learning 16.2.6

From arrows to edges: moralisation

Every Bayesian network can be rewritten as a Markov random field, and the recipe follows from the factorisations. A directed model is a product of factors p(xi | pai). An undirected model is a product of clique potentials. So make each family {xi} ∪ pai a clique and use the conditional as its potential: Z is then exactly 1. To make the family a clique, join every pair of parents that share a child (“marry the parents”) and then drop the arrowheads. The result is the moral graph, and the operation is moralisation. A collider whose parents are not already joined is what Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville call an immorality; moralisation removes it by adding an edge.

Nothing is lost from the distribution, but something is lost from the picture. In a collider a → c ← b, the parents are independent until you observe c. After marriage, a and b are joined by an edge, so the moral graph can no longer promise that independence. Moralising keeps every promise the undirected graph makes true (it is an I-map) but it can make fewer promises than the DAG did. The page checks every statement “i ⊥ j | C” with up to two nodes in C, using d-separation on the DAG and separation on the moral graph. Pick each network and look at what marriage costs.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{x}) &= \prod_i p(x_i \mid \mathrm{pa}_i) \\ &= \prod_i \psi_i\big(x_i, \mathrm{pa}_i\big), \quad Z = 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each conditional becomes the potential of the clique formed by a node and its parents. Because the factors are already normalised conditionals, their product sums to 1 and no partition function is needed. The moral graph is the smallest undirected graph in which every such family is a clique.
Say it“Each conditional becomes a clique potential on the node and its parents, and Z is one.”

Deep Learning 16.2.6 · Bishop 8.3.4

From edges to arrows: loops need chords

The other direction is harder. Take the 4-cycle a – b – c – d – a. It says a ⊥ c | b, d and b ⊥ d | a, c. Try to orient its four edges. Any acyclic orientation has a sink, a node whose two arrows both point in, and its two neighbours are not joined: an immorality. The DAG then claims that those two parents are independent given the fourth node alone (or given nothing), which the cycle never says, and it loses the cycle’s own statement that they are independent given both other nodes, because observing the sink opens the path through it. To convert faithfully in the safe direction, first add chords until every cycle of four or more nodes has an edge cutting across it. The graph is then chordal (triangulated), and it can be oriented with no immoralities: eliminate nodes one by one, and point every edge from the node eliminated later to the one eliminated earlier. The DAG you get keeps only promises the original graph made, but it has lost some of them, the ones the chords destroyed.

Bishop frames this with maps. A graph is a D-map of a distribution if every independence of the distribution shows in the graph, an I-map if every independence the graph shows is true, and a perfect map if both. Some distributions have a directed perfect map but no undirected one (the collider), some have an undirected one but no directed one (the 4-cycle), and many have neither. Choose a graph and an elimination order and count the chords and the lost statements.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{chordal} \iff \text{every cycle} \\ &\quad v_1 \!-\! v_2 \!-\! \cdots \!-\! v_k \!-\! v_1,\; k \ge 4, \\ &\quad \text{has a chord } v_a \!-\! v_b, \\ &\quad a, b \text{ not consecutive} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a chord is a shortcut across a loop. In a chordal graph the only chordless cycles are triangles. Eliminating a node means joining all of its remaining neighbours into a clique and then removing it; the edges added along the way are the fill-in, and after eliminating every node the original graph plus its fill-in is chordal.
Say it“A graph is chordal if every loop of four or more has a shortcut.”

Only arrows can say it

a → c ← b: a and b are independent, but dependent once c is known. Any undirected graph that allows the dependence given c must join a and b, and then it cannot show their independence.

Only lines can say it

a – b – c – d – a: a ⊥ c given b and d, and b ⊥ d given a and c, and nothing else. Every DAG on these four nodes either misses one of the two statements or adds a claim of its own.

Real-world project

Project: cleaning a page printed in 1858

The page is page 13 of Leonard Jenyns, Observations in Meteorology (London: J. Van Voorst, 1858), from the New York Public Library copy digitised by Google and held by the Internet Archive as item bub_gb_XHIAAAAAMAAJ, marked Public Domain Mark 1.0. The page image (scan n30, 2,500 × 4,034 grey pixels) was cropped to nine lines of text, averaged over 3 × 3 blocks, and thresholded with Otsu’s method (threshold 156 of 255). The result, 636 × 290 = 184,440 pixels with 36,669 of them ink, is embedded in this page and serves as the clean truth.

The scan itself is clean, so the noise is added here: every pixel is flipped independently with probability q, using a seeded generator so that Python can reproduce the same noisy page. Then the Ising model of section 6 restores it, by ICM and by the exact minimum cut. Printed text is a hard case for this prior. Strokes are only a few pixels wide and every letter is mostly edge, while the Ising model prefers large smooth blobs. Run both solvers at Bishop’s settings, then look for the β that makes the fewest mistakes.

The same experiment in Python

The preparation script (Pillow and numpy) turns the downloaded scan into the embedded crop. The reference script (numpy and SciPy only) reads that crop, adds noise with the same generator as the page, runs checkerboard ICM, and finds the exact minimum cut with SciPy’s maximum-flow solver; it also checks two of the small labs. Its output is below.

import sys, json, base64
import numpy as np
from PIL import Image
S = sys.argv[1]
g = np.asarray(Image.open(S + "x106_dl/p30.jpg"), dtype=float)      # 4034 x 2500 grey, 0 = black
r0, r1, c0, c1, f = 370, 1240, 210, 2120, 3                         # nine lines of text, 3x3 box average
g = g[r0:r1, c0:c1]; H, W = g.shape[0] // f, g.shape[1] // f
g = np.round(g[:H * f, :W * f].reshape(H, f, W, f).mean((1, 3))).astype(int)
p = np.bincount(g.ravel(), minlength=256) / g.size                   # Otsu's threshold
best, t = -1, 0
for k in range(1, 256):
    w0 = p[:k].sum(); w1 = 1 - w0
    if w0 == 0 or w1 == 0: continue

def nsum(x):
    s = np.zeros_like(x); s[1:] += x[:-1]; s[:-1] += x[1:]; s[:, 1:] += x[:, :-1]; s[:, :-1] += x[:, 1:]; return s
def energy10(x, y, B, E, Hh):
    pairs = (x[1:] * x[:-1]).sum() + (x[:, 1:] * x[:, :-1]).sum()
    return int(Hh * x.sum() - B * pairs - E * (x * y).sum())
def icm(y, B, E, Hh, maxs=200):               # checkerboard sweeps: even (r + c) pixels, then odd; ties keep x
    x = y.copy(); par = np.add.outer(np.arange(y.shape[0]), np.arange(y.shape[1])) % 2; flips = []

        for c in (0, 1):

            v = np.where(f > 0, 1, np.where(f < 0, -1, x)); ch += int(((v != x) & m).sum()); x = np.where(m, v, x)
        flips.append(ch)
        if ch == 0: break
    return x, flips

    if P > 0:
        for p, q in [(idx[:, :-1].ravel(), idx[:, 1:].ravel()), (idx[:-1].ravel(), idx[1:].ravel())]:
            r += [p, q]; c += [q, p]; k += [np.full(p.size, P), np.full(p.size, P)]
    pos, neg = A > 0, A < 0
    r += [np.where(pos)[0], np.full(neg.sum(), s)]; c += [np.full(pos.sum(), t), np.where(neg)[0]]; k += [A[pos], -A[neg]]
    C = coo_matrix((np.concatenate(k).astype(np.int32), (np.concatenate(r), np.concatenate(c))), shape=(n + 2, n + 2)).tocsr()
    res = maximum_flow(C, s, t, method="dinic")
    R = (C - res.flow).tocsr(); R.data = (R.data > 0).astype(np.int8); R.eliminate_zeros()
    lab = -np.ones(n + 2, int); lab[breadth_first_order(R, s, directed=True, return_predecessors=False)] = 1
    x = lab[:n].reshape(y.shape)
    emin = -np.abs(A).sum() // 2 - B * ((W - 1) * H + (H - 1) * W) + res.flow_value
    return x, int(res.flow_value), int(emin)

def joint(n, factors):                        # unnormalised table over n binary variables, first variable = highest bit
    p = np.ones(2 ** n)
    for s in range(2 ** n):
        for vars_, tab in factors:
            idx = 0
            for u in vars_: idx = idx * 2 + ((s >> (n - 1 - u)) & 1)
            p[s] *= tab[idx]
    return p, p.sum()
def cmi(p, n, A, B, C):                       # I(A; B | C) in bits, A, B, C lists of variables
    p = p / p.sum(); P = {}
    key = lambda s, V: tuple((s >> (n - 1 - u)) & 1 for u in V)
    for s in range(2 ** n):
        if p[s] == 0: continue
        a, b, c = key(s, A), key(s, B), key(s, C)
        for k in [("x", a, b, c), ("a", a, c), ("b", b, c), ("c", c)]: P[k] = P.get(k, 0) + p[s]
    I = sum(v * np.log2(v * P[("c", k[3])] / (P[("a", k[1], k[3])] * P[("b", k[2], k[3])])) for k, v in P.items() if k[0] == "x")
    return max(0.0, I)

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A Markov random field is an undirected graph plus non-negative potentials on its cliques: p(x) = (1/Z) ∏ ψC(xC). Potentials score compatibility; only after dividing by the partition function Z do they give probabilities, and a potential is generally not the marginal of its own variables.
  • Conditional independence is plain graph separation: if C cuts every path from A to B, then XA ⊥ XB | XC. A node’s Markov blanket is just its neighbours. For positive distributions the pairwise, local and global Markov properties are equivalent, and Hammersley–Clifford ties them to the clique factorisation.
  • The Ising model for de-noising has energy hΣxi − βΣxixj − ηΣxiyi. ICM sets each pixel to the sign of its local field, which is fast but stops at a local minimum.
  • For binary labels with agreement-rewarding couplings the exact minimum-energy image is a minimum s–t cut. On the 1858 page it cut the errors from 18,424 to 5,325 pixels, against 9,368 for ICM; but lower energy is not always fewer errors, because the model is not the truth.
  • Directed to undirected: marry co-parents and drop the arrows (moralisation); the distribution survives, independences of colliders do not. Undirected to directed: add chords until the graph is chordal, then orient by an elimination order; the 4-cycle’s independences cannot all survive. Neither family of graphs contains the other.

Check your understanding

In an undirected graph, can observing an extra node ever make two separated sets dependent?

No. Adding a node to C can only delete more paths, so separation is preserved. This is the big difference from directed graphs, where observing a collider (or one of its descendants) opens a path and creates dependence, as in explaining away.

You double every entry of one clique potential. What happens to p(x), to Z and to the energy?

p(x) is unchanged, because the factor of 2 appears in every term of Z as well and cancels. Z doubles. Every energy of that clique falls by ln 2, a constant shift that changes no comparison between configurations.

With β = 1.0, η = 2.1 and h = 0, which wrong pixels can ICM correct, and why does the minimum cut do better?

Only a pixel whose four neighbours all disagree with it: the neighbour pull is then 4β = 4.0, which beats η = 2.1, whereas three against one gives only 2.0. A pair of adjacent wrong pixels each sees three disagreeing neighbours and one agreeing (wrong) one, so neither moves, although flipping both together lowers the energy. The minimum cut considers every labelling at once and finds the global minimum.

Why does moralising a → c ← b add an edge between a and b, and what is lost?

The factor p(c | a, b) involves all three variables, so they must form a clique of the undirected graph; that needs the edge a – b. The distribution is represented exactly, but the undirected graph can no longer show that a and b are marginally independent.

Is the clean page the most probable image under the Ising model given the noisy page?

No. Its energy is higher than that of the minimum-cut image (−616,773.2 against −625,502.6 at Bishop’s settings). The model prefers smoother strokes than real type has, so its best answer differs from the truth. That is a statement about the model, not about the solver: a better prior, such as learned potentials over larger cliques, would be needed to close the gap.
