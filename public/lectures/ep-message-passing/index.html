EP & Message Passing

Variational message passing, expectation propagation and learned inference

A posterior is a product of many factors: a prior and one likelihood term per observation. That product almost never has a name, so we approximate it with something that does, usually a Gaussian, and improve the approximation one piece at a time, passing what each piece learns to its neighbours. Variational message passing does this by minimising KL(q‖p); expectation propagation works the other way round and matches moments; learned inference trains a network to output the approximation directly. The figure is EP’s smallest step. Two players’ skills start as a Gaussian belief (dashed). One game result multiplies in a factor that tilts the plane along the line sA = sB, the exact posterior (shaded) is no longer Gaussian, and EP replaces it with the Gaussian that has the same mean and covariance (solid ellipses). Drag the prior’s centre and pick a result, and watch the upset results move the belief furthest.

PRML 10.4 · exponential-family variational updates

When every piece is exponential-family, the updates write themselves

The previous chapters derived variational updates model by model: take logs, average over the other factors, recognise a Gaussian or a Gamma. There is a large class of models where that recognition is automatic. Suppose the joint distribution of one observation xn and its latent variable zn, given the parameters η, is in the exponential family with natural parameters η and sufficient statistic u(xn, zn), and the prior on η is conjugate, which means it has the same functional form in η. The prior then behaves like ν0 imaginary observations whose average sufficient statistic is χ0.

Now take the mean-field split q(Z, η) = q(Z) q(η), and nothing finer. Two facts drop out of the general variational formula. The optimal q(zn) has the same form as the model’s own conditional, with η replaced by its expectation E[η]. And the optimal q(η) is again conjugate, with ν0 + N pseudo-observations and the observed statistics replaced by their expectations under q(Z). The whole algorithm is: compute expected natural parameters, update the latents; compute expected sufficient statistics, update the parameters; repeat. Each half-step can only raise the lower bound.

The lab uses the smallest interesting case. Each reading xn is a noisy measurement, with known instrument noise s, of a true value zn; the true values scatter around an unknown mean μ with unknown precision τ. The latents are the true values; the parameters are (μ, τ) with a Normal-Gamma prior. Marginalising zn gives xn ~ N(μ, 1/τ + s²), so the exact posterior over (μ, τ) can be drawn on a grid and compared. Step the two updates by hand, then drag a reading or raise the instrument noise and see what the factorisation between Z and (μ, τ) costs.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf x_n, \mathbf z_n \mid \boldsymbol\eta) &= h(\mathbf x_n, \mathbf z_n)\, g(\boldsymbol\eta) \\ &\quad \times e^{\boldsymbol\eta^{\top} \mathbf u(\mathbf x_n, \mathbf z_n)} \\ p(\boldsymbol\eta \mid \nu_0, \boldsymbol\chi_0) &= f(\nu_0, \boldsymbol\chi_0)\, g(\boldsymbol\eta)^{\nu_0} \\ &\quad \times e^{\nu_0\, \boldsymbol\eta^{\top} \boldsymbol\chi_0} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the model for one observed–latent pair is exponential-family in η: the data enter only through u, and g(η) normalises. The conjugate prior has the same shape, as if we had already seen ν0 observations with average statistic χ0. In the lab u = (z, z²) and η = (τμ, −τ/2).
Say it“h times g of eta times e to the eta-transpose u; the prior is g of eta to the nu-nought, times e to the nu-nought eta-transpose chi-nought.”
\[ \begin{aligned} q^\star(\mathbf z_n) &\propto h(\mathbf x_n, \mathbf z_n)\, e^{\mathbb E[\boldsymbol\eta]^{\top} \mathbf u(\mathbf x_n, \mathbf z_n)} \\ q^\star(\boldsymbol\eta) &= f(\nu_N, \boldsymbol\chi_N)\, g(\boldsymbol\eta)^{\nu_N} e^{\nu_N \boldsymbol\eta^{\top} \boldsymbol\chi_N} \\ \nu_N &= \nu_0 + N \\ \nu_N \boldsymbol\chi_N &= \nu_0 \boldsymbol\chi_0 \\ &\quad + \textstyle\sum_n \mathbb E_{\mathbf z_n}\big[\mathbf u(\mathbf x_n, \mathbf z_n)\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each latent’s factor is the model’s own conditional with the expected natural parameter plugged in. The parameter factor is the conjugate posterior you would get from complete data, except that each unseen statistic is replaced by its expectation. Neither update needs any calculus: only expectations.
Say it“q-star of z-n uses the expected eta; q-star of eta adds N to nu and the expected statistics to nu-chi.”
\[ \begin{aligned} q(z_n) &= \mathcal N\big(z_n \mid \hat z_n, \omega_n^{-1}\big) \\ \omega_n &= \mathbb E[\tau] + s^{-2} \\ \hat z_n &= \big(\mathbb E[\tau\mu] + x_n / s^2\big) / \omega_n \\ q(\mu, \tau) &= \mathcal N\big(\mu \mid m_N, (\lambda_N \tau)^{-1}\big) \\ &\quad \times \operatorname{Gam}(\tau \mid a_N, b_N) \\ \lambda_N &= \lambda_0 + N, \quad a_N = a_0 + \tfrac N2 \\ m_N &= \big(\lambda_0 m_0 + \textstyle\sum_n \mathbb E[z_n]\big) / \lambda_N \\ b_N &= b_0 + \tfrac12 \Big(\textstyle\sum_n \mathbb E[z_n^2] \\ &\qquad + \lambda_0 m_0^2 - \lambda_N m_N^2\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the two generic updates written out for the lab. A true value’s estimate is a precision-weighted average of the reading (weight 1/s²) and the group mean (weight E[τ]). The Normal-Gamma update is the textbook complete-data one, fed with E[zn] and E[zn²] = ẑn² + 1/ωn. Prior: m0 = 0, λ0 = 0.1, a0 = b0 = 1.
Say it“Each z-hat is a weighted average of the reading and the mean; the Normal-Gamma update eats the expected z and z-squared.”

PRML 10.4.1 · variational message passing

Variational message passing: every node listens only to its neighbours

Now let the model be a whole directed graph, each node conditionally exponential-family given its parents and conjugate to them, and let q factorise over the nodes (or over groups of nodes). The general variational update for node j averages the log joint over everything else. Only the terms that mention xj survive: its own conditional, and the conditionals of its children, whose other parents are the node’s co-parents. So the update of a node needs only its Markov blanket.

Because of conjugacy, each surviving term is linear in the node’s sufficient statistic, and its coefficient is a vector that can be computed by the neighbour that owns the term. That vector is the message. A parent sends its child the expectations of its own sufficient statistics; a child sends each parent the natural-parameter vector of its conditional, rearranged as a function of that parent, with the other parents averaged out. The node adds its prior’s natural parameters to the sum of its children’s messages and is done. Winn and Bishop called this variational message passing; it is what the VIBES and Infer.NET systems run. The bound is a sum of local terms, so it can be monitored, and no update can lower it.

The lab is a hierarchical model: four groups of five measurements, group means θj around a shared mean m with between-group precision κ, and within-group precision τ. Seven nodes, fully factorised. Click any node to update it and see which messages it reads; the bound must never fall, whatever order you choose.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\ln q_j^\star(\mathbf x_j) = \mathbb E_{\neg j}\big[\ln p(\mathbf x_j \mid \mathrm{pa}_j)\big] \\ &\quad + \textstyle\sum_{k \in \mathrm{ch}_j} \mathbb E_{\neg j}\big[\ln p(\mathbf x_k \mid \mathrm{pa}_k)\big] \\ &\quad + \text{const} \\ &\boldsymbol\eta_j^\star = \tilde{\boldsymbol\eta}_j\big(\{\mathbb E[\mathbf u_i]\}_{i \in \mathrm{pa}_j}\big) \\ &\quad + \textstyle\sum_{k \in \mathrm{ch}_j} \mathbf m_{k \to j} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: node j’s new factor depends only on its parents, children and co-parents. In natural-parameter form: the prior term, evaluated at the parents’ expected statistics, plus one message from each child. A message mk→j is the coefficient of uj(xj) in E[ln p(xk | pak)].
Say it“Eta-j-star is the prior part from the parents’ expectations plus the sum of the children’s messages.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \theta_j\!: \ \lambda &= \mathbb E[\kappa] + n_j \mathbb E[\tau] \\ \lambda\bar\theta &= \mathbb E[\kappa]\mathbb E[m] + \mathbb E[\tau] \textstyle\sum_i x_{ij} \\ \kappa\!: \ a &= a_0 + \tfrac J2 \\ b &= b_0 + \tfrac12 \textstyle\sum_j \mathbb E\big[(\theta_j - m)^2\big] \\ \tau\!: \ a &= a_0 + \tfrac{n}{2} \\ b &= b_0 + \tfrac12 \textstyle\sum_{ij} \mathbb E\big[(x_{ij} - \theta_j)^2\big] \\ m\!: \ \lambda &= \sigma_m^{-2} + J\,\mathbb E[\kappa] \\ \lambda\bar m &= \mathbb E[\kappa] \textstyle\sum_j \mathbb E[\theta_j] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the four kinds of update in the lab, with λ a precision and λθ̄ a precision times mean. A Gaussian node adds precisions and precision-weighted means from its messages; a Gamma node adds ½ to its shape and half an expected squared residual to its rate for every child. Priors: m ~ N(0, 10²), κ and τ ~ Gam(1, 1); n = 20 measurements in all.
Say it“Gaussians add precisions; Gammas add a half to the shape and half the expected squared error to the rate.”

PRML 10.7 · minimising KL(p‖q) by moment matching

Which way round? Moment matching and KL(p‖q)

Every method so far minimised KL(q‖p), the divergence that averages over the approximation q. That direction is cheap, because expectations under q are easy, and it is zero-forcing: q is punished heavily wherever it puts mass and p has none, so q shrinks into one mode and underestimates spread. The reverse, KL(p‖q), averages over the true distribution p. It is punished wherever p has mass and q has none, so q stretches to cover everything.

For an exponential-family q, minimising KL(p‖q) has a closed-form answer. Write q with natural parameter η; only the cross-entropy term depends on η, and setting its gradient to zero gives Eq[u] = Ep[u]. The best approximation matches the expected sufficient statistics: for a Gaussian q, the mean and the covariance of p. That is moment matching, and it is the single operation that expectation propagation repeats. Pull the two bumps apart and compare the two Gaussian fits: one covers both bumps and sits in the valley between them, the other commits to a bump.

\[ \begin{aligned} q(\mathbf z) &= h(\mathbf z)\, g(\boldsymbol\eta)\, e^{\boldsymbol\eta^{\top}\mathbf u(\mathbf z)} \\ \mathrm{KL}(p \,\Vert\, q) &= -\ln g(\boldsymbol\eta) \\ &\quad - \boldsymbol\eta^{\top}\mathbb E_p[\mathbf u(\mathbf z)] + \text{const} \\ -\nabla \ln g(\boldsymbol\eta) &= \mathbb E_p[\mathbf u(\mathbf z)] \\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbb E_q[\mathbf u] &= \mathbb E_p[\mathbf u] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: as a function of η, KL(p‖q) is the normaliser term minus a linear term in the expected statistics under p. In the exponential family the gradient of −ln g(η) is exactly Eq[u], so the optimum sets q’s expected statistics equal to p’s.
Say it“The best q in KL of p to q has the same expected sufficient statistics as p.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{EP:}\ \ \mu_q &= \mathbb E_p[z] \\ \sigma_q^2 &= \mathbb E_p[z^2] - \mu_q^2 \\ \text{VB:}\ \ q^\star &= \arg\min_q \int q(z) \ln \frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\, dz \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for a Gaussian q, moment matching copies p’s mean and variance, in closed form and with a single optimum. The reverse direction has no closed form for a general p and can have one local optimum per mode; the lab finds them by Newton’s method on (μ, ln σ) from three starting points, with the integral done numerically.
Say it“Moment matching copies the mean and variance; VB’s q-star minimises the integral of q log q over p.”

PRML 10.7, 10.7.1 · expectation propagation and the clutter problem

Expectation propagation: refine one factor at a time

Write the joint of data and parameters as a product of factors fi(θ): the prior and one per observation. The exact posterior is their product divided by p(D). Expectation propagation approximates each factor by a simple site f̃i(θ) from an exponential family, so that their product q(θ) is in the family too. Minimising KL(p‖q) for the whole posterior is impossible, because it needs the moments of the true posterior. EP minimises it one factor at a time.

To refine site j: divide it out of q to get the cavity q\j, the belief from every other factor; multiply in the true factor fj to get the tilted distribution, which is exact for factor j in the context of the others; match its moments to get the new q; and set the new site to new q over cavity, with the scale chosen so that its integral against the cavity equals the tilted normaliser Zj. Then move to another factor, and cycle until nothing changes. Nothing guarantees convergence, and a site can end with a negative variance, which is harmless as long as q and every cavity stay proper. In return, EP gives an estimate of the model evidence from the final sites.

The clutter problem. We want the mean θ of a Gaussian, but each observation is, with probability w, clutter from a broad background N(0, a). Each likelihood factor is a two-component mixture, so the exact posterior is a mixture of 2N Gaussians: with N = 15 that is 32,768 terms, still fine on a one-dimensional grid but hopeless in general. EP needs one Gaussian site per point. Step through the sites one at a time, then drag a point towards the edge and watch its site switch off as the point becomes believable clutter.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathcal D, \boldsymbol\theta) &= \textstyle\prod_i f_i(\boldsymbol\theta) \\ q(\boldsymbol\theta) &= \tfrac{1}{Z} \textstyle\prod_i \tilde f_i(\boldsymbol\theta) \\ q^{\setminus j}(\boldsymbol\theta) &= q(\boldsymbol\theta) / \tilde f_j(\boldsymbol\theta) \\ Z_j &= \textstyle\int f_j(\boldsymbol\theta)\, q^{\setminus j}(\boldsymbol\theta)\, d\boldsymbol\theta \\ q^{\text{new}} &= \text{moments of } f_j\, q^{\setminus j} / Z_j \\ \tilde f_j &= Z_j\, q^{\text{new}} / q^{\setminus j} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the posterior is a product of factors and q is a product of sites. To refine site j, remove it (cavity), put back the true factor (tilted), project the tilted distribution onto the family by matching moments, and divide the cavity back out to get the new site. In natural parameters, dividing and multiplying Gaussians is just subtracting and adding precisions and precision-means.
Say it“Cavity is q over the site; the new q matches the moments of the factor times the cavity; the new site is Z-j times new q over the cavity.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &p(x \mid \theta) = (1 - w)\, \mathcal N(x \mid \theta, 1) \\ &\quad + w\, \mathcal N(x \mid 0, a) \\ &Z_n = (1-w)\, \mathcal N(x_n \mid m^{\setminus n}, v^{\setminus n} + 1) \\ &\quad + w\, \mathcal N(x_n \mid 0, a) \\ &\rho_n = 1 - \frac{w}{Z_n}\, \mathcal N(x_n \mid 0, a) \\ &m = m^{\setminus n} + \rho_n \frac{v^{\setminus n}}{v^{\setminus n} + 1} (x_n - m^{\setminus n}) \\ &v = v^{\setminus n} - \rho_n \frac{(v^{\setminus n})^2}{v^{\setminus n} + 1} \\ &\quad + \rho_n (1 - \rho_n) \frac{(v^{\setminus n})^2 (x_n - m^{\setminus n})^2}{(v^{\setminus n} + 1)^2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the clutter likelihood, and the moments of its tilted distribution in one dimension. ρn is the probability, under the cavity, that xn is a genuine observation rather than clutter. The mean moves towards xn by ρn times a Kalman-style gain; the variance shrinks by ρn times the usual amount, but grows again by the last term, which is the uncertainty about whether the point is clutter. Here a = 10, the prior is N(0, 100).
Say it“Rho-n is one minus the clutter share of Z-n; the mean moves by rho-n times the gain; the variance adds a rho-one-minus-rho term.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &p(\mathcal D) \approx Z_{\text{EP}} = \textstyle\int \prod_i \tilde f_i(\boldsymbol\theta)\, d\boldsymbol\theta \\ &\ln Z_{\text{EP}} = A(\boldsymbol\eta_q) - A(\boldsymbol\eta_0) \\ &\quad + \textstyle\sum_{n} \Big[ \ln Z_n + A\big(\boldsymbol\eta^{\setminus n}\big) - A(\boldsymbol\eta_q) \Big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the evidence estimate is the integral of the product of the scaled sites (the prior is kept exactly as its own site). Written with the Gaussian log-normaliser A(η) = r²/(2λ) − ½ ln λ of natural parameters (λ, r) = (1/v, m/v), every term is something EP already computed: the final q, the prior, each Zn and each cavity at convergence.
Say it“Log Z-EP is A of q minus A of the prior, plus, for each point, log Z-n plus A of its cavity minus A of q.”

PRML 10.7 · assumed density filtering versus EP

One pass or many: why EP revisits its factors

Take EP and stop after the first pass. Starting from the prior, each factor is folded in once, moment-matched, and never looked at again. That is assumed density filtering (ADF), also called moment matching or online Bayesian learning, and it is what the original TrueSkill rating system does after every game. It is cheap and works in a stream, but the answer depends on the order of the data: the first factors are absorbed into a broad belief and the last into a narrow one, and an early approximation error is never corrected.

EP fixes that by keeping each factor’s site and, on later passes, taking it back out before putting the factor back in. At a fixed point every site is consistent with all the others, whatever order was used to get there. The lab is a four-player league with a TrueSkill-style factor per game: each skill s has a Gaussian belief, a player’s performance in a game is their skill plus noise of spread β, and the winner is the one who performed better. That makes each factor a probit Φ((swin − slose)/(√2β)) on the skill difference, whose tilted moments have a closed form. The beliefs stay fully factorised, one Gaussian per player, which makes this EP on a factor graph, the subject of the next section. Shuffle the order of the games with one pass, then add passes: the spread of answers across all 40,320 orders shrinks towards zero.

\[ \begin{aligned} f_g(s_i, s_j) &= \Phi\Big(\frac{s_i - s_j}{\sqrt2\, \beta}\Big) \\ c^2 &= 2\beta^2 + \sigma_i^2 + \sigma_j^2 \\ t &= (\mu_i - \mu_j) / c \\ \mu_i &\leftarrow \mu_i + \frac{\sigma_i^2}{c}\, \mathsf v(t) \\ \sigma_i^2 &\leftarrow \sigma_i^2 \Big(1 - \frac{\sigma_i^2}{c^2}\, \mathsf w(t)\Big) \\ \mathsf v(t) &= \phi(t) / \Phi(t) \\ \mathsf w(t) &= \mathsf v(t)\big(\mathsf v(t) + t\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the factor for one game in which i beat j, and the moments of its tilted distribution. The winner’s mean rises by an amount that is large when the win was a surprise (t negative, so v(t) is large) and small when it was expected; both variances shrink by a factor between 0 and 1. The loser’s update is the same with the sign of the mean shift reversed. Here μ0 = 25, σ0 = 25/3, β = 25/6, the original TrueSkill scale.
Say it“Mu-i moves by sigma-i-squared over c times v of t; sigma-i-squared shrinks by the factor one minus sigma-i-squared over c-squared times w of t.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{ADF:}\ \ q_k \propto \operatorname{proj}\big[f_k\, q_{k-1}\big] \\ &\quad k = 1, \dots, K, \text{ once} \\ &\text{EP:}\ \ q^{\setminus k} = q / \tilde f_k \\ &q \leftarrow \operatorname{proj}\big[f_k\, q^{\setminus k}\big] \\ &\tilde f_k \leftarrow q / q^{\setminus k} \\ &\quad \text{repeat over } k \text{ until stable} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ADF projects (moment-matches) after each new factor and moves on. EP does the same arithmetic, but on the cavity, the belief with this factor’s old site removed, and stores the site. EP’s first pass from flat sites is exactly ADF; the later passes are what make the answer order-free.
Say it“ADF projects factor times previous belief, once each; EP projects factor times cavity, and keeps going.”

PRML 10.7.2 · expectation propagation on graphs

EP on a factor graph is belief propagation

So far θ was one block. Now let the model be a factor graph over many variables, p(x) ∝ ∏j fj(xj), where xj is the set of variables factor j touches, and choose a fully factorised approximation, one factor qi(xi) per variable. Each site must then factorise as well: f̃j(xj) = ∏k f̃jk(xk), one small piece per variable it touches. Moment matching a fully factorised q to the tilted distribution only needs that distribution’s single-variable marginals.

Work the EP update through and something familiar appears. The cavity for factor j, restricted to variable k, is the product of all the other factors’ pieces on xk: the sum-product message from variable k to factor j. The new piece f̃jl(xl) is the tilted marginal of xl divided by its cavity, and that is the sum of fj times the incoming variable messages over every variable except xl: the sum-product message from factor j to variable l. So EP with a fully factorised approximation is belief propagation, with the sites as stored factor-to-variable messages. On a tree it reaches the exact marginals once messages have travelled both ways along every edge (here, within two sweeps); on a graph with loops it is loopy belief propagation, with all its convergence questions. Choosing a richer, partly factorised q gives structured versions between the two extremes. Step through the factors, then close the loop and compare the marginals with the exact ones.

\[ \begin{aligned} q(\mathbf x) &= \textstyle\prod_i q_i(x_i) \\ \tilde f_j(\mathbf x_j) &= \textstyle\prod_{k \in \mathrm{ne}(j)} \tilde f_{jk}(x_k) \\ q_k^{\setminus j}(x_k) &= q_k(x_k) / \tilde f_{jk}(x_k) \\ &= \textstyle\prod_{j' \in \mathrm{ne}(k) \setminus j} \tilde f_{j'k}(x_k) \\ \tilde f_{jl}(x_l) &\propto \textstyle\sum_{\mathbf x_j \setminus x_l} f_j(\mathbf x_j) \\ &\quad \times \textstyle\prod_{k \in \mathrm{ne}(j) \setminus l} q_k^{\setminus j}(x_k) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with a factorised q every site splits into one piece per variable. The cavity on xk is the product of the other factors’ pieces, which is the variable-to-factor message; the updated piece sums the factor against the cavities of its other variables, which is the factor-to-variable message. The tilted marginal of xl is cavity times new piece, so matching it is automatic.
Say it“f-tilde-j-l of x-l is the sum over the other variables of f-j times their cavities: the sum-product message.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm D_\alpha(p \,\Vert\, q) &= \frac{4}{1 - \alpha^2} \big(1 - I_\alpha\big) \\ I_\alpha &= \int p(x)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\, q(x)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}\, dx \\ \alpha \to 1 &: \ \mathrm{KL}(p \,\Vert\, q) \ \ (\text{EP, BP}) \\ \alpha \to -1 &: \ \mathrm{KL}(q \,\Vert\, p) \ \ (\text{VB}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the alpha family of divergences runs from the inclusive KL(p‖q) to the exclusive KL(q‖p). Local versions of other members give power EP and fractional belief propagation; tree-reweighted belief propagation is a close relative that always yields an upper bound on the log normaliser. Message passing on graphs is one framework with a dial, not a set of unrelated tricks.
Say it“D-alpha is four over one minus alpha-squared, times one minus the integral of p to the one-plus-alpha-over-two times q to the one-minus-alpha-over-two.”

Deep Learning 19.5, 19.5.2 · learned approximate inference

Learned inference: amortise the optimisation into a network

Every method so far solves an optimisation problem for each new data point: iterate the updates until q stops changing. That is fine for one posterior, but a deep generative model needs a posterior for every example in every minibatch. The answer is to notice that the optimisation is a function: data point v in, the parameters of the best q out. So learn that function. An inference network f(v; φ) outputs q’s parameters directly, and is trained by maximising the same lower bound, averaged over the data, by gradient ascent on φ. Inference becomes a single forward pass. The cost of the optimisation has been amortised over all the data the network was trained on.

Two things now separate the network’s q from the truth. The approximation gap is the KL from the best member of the family to the true posterior: here q is Gaussian, so a two-peaked posterior will always cost something. The amortisation gap is the extra loss because the network, with finite capacity and finite training, does not output the best member for every v. The lab has one latent h ~ N(0, 1) and two observations: v1 ~ N(h, 1) and v2 ~ N(h² − 1, 0.3²). The second only sees h², so with v1 near zero the posterior has two peaks. The bound has a closed form in this model, so the network is trained on the exact bound. Train a small network, then a wider one, and click around the data to find where each gap is large.

\[ \begin{aligned} q(h \mid \mathbf v) &= \mathcal N\big(h \mid \mu_\phi(\mathbf v), \sigma_\phi(\mathbf v)^2\big) \\ \mathcal L(\mathbf v, q) &= \mathbb E_{q}\big[\ln p(h) + \ln p(\mathbf v \mid h)\big] \\ &\quad + \tfrac12 \ln\big(2\pi e\, \sigma^2\big) \\ \max_\phi &\ \tfrac{1}{M} \textstyle\sum_{m} \mathcal L\big(\mathbf v^{(m)}, q_\phi\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a network maps each data point to a Gaussian over the latent. The bound for one point is the expected log joint plus q’s entropy; training maximises its average over the training set. Here E[h²] = μ² + σ² and E[h4] = μ4 + 6μ²σ² + 3σ4 make the bound exact; in a variational autoencoder it is estimated with sampled h instead.
Say it“Pick phi to maximise the average bound, where the network supplies mu and sigma for each data point.”
\[ \begin{aligned} & \ln p(\mathbf v) - \mathcal L(\mathbf v, q_\phi) \\ &= \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi \,\Vert\, p(h \mid \mathbf v)\big) \\ &= \underbrace{\ln p(\mathbf v) - \max_{q \in \mathcal Q} \mathcal L(\mathbf v, q)}_{\text{approximation gap}} \\ &\quad + \underbrace{\max_{q \in \mathcal Q} \mathcal L(\mathbf v, q) - \mathcal L(\mathbf v, q_\phi)}_{\text{amortisation gap}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the bound is short of the log evidence by the KL to the true posterior. Split it at the best Gaussian for this particular v: the first part is the family’s fault, the second the network’s. In the lab, ln p(v) is integrated on a fine grid in h and the best Gaussian is found by Newton’s method from three starting points.

Other forms of learned inference

The same idea appears all over deep learning, with different training signals. In predictive sparse decomposition (Kavukcuoglu, Ranzato and LeCun), an encoder is trained to predict the sparse code that an expensive optimisation would find, and the sparse-coding model is trained alongside it, so that the codes become easy to predict. Salakhutdinov and Larochelle trained a network to output a good starting point for the mean-field fixed point of a deep Boltzmann machine in one pass. Gregor and LeCun unrolled a fixed number of iterations of a sparse-coding solver into a network (LISTA) and trained its weights, so that a few learned steps do the work of many generic ones. And the variational autoencoder (Kingma and Welling; Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra) trains the inference network and the generative model jointly, on one bound, with sampled latents and the reparameterisation trick; there the encoder is simply the learned inference network. Learned inference also changes learning: a generative model trained with an inference network is pushed towards models whose posteriors the network can represent.

Deep Learning 19.5.1 · the wake-sleep algorithm

Wake-sleep: train inference on the model’s own dreams

The oldest learned-inference scheme predates the reparameterisation trick by two decades. The Helmholtz machine of Hinton, Dayan, Frey and Neal has a generative network, here binary hidden causes h with independent priors and visible pixels v ~ Bernoulli(σ(Wh + c)), and a recognition network q(h | v) that runs the other way. Training alternates two phases. In the wake phase a real image v is shown, the recognition network samples h ~ q(h | v), and the generative weights take a step towards making (h, v) more likely: a local delta rule, because h is now known. In the sleep phase the generative network dreams: it samples h from its prior and v from p(v | h). Now the true causes of the dream are known, so the recognition network takes a delta-rule step towards predicting h from v.

The sleep phase is supervised learning on fantasy data. Its weakness is that it minimises KL(p(h | v) ‖ q(h | v)) on the model’s dreams, not the KL(q ‖ p) of the lower bound on the real data, so no single objective is being climbed, and early on the dreams look nothing like the data. In practice it works surprisingly well, and it is the ancestor of reweighted wake-sleep and of today’s inference networks. The lab trains it on the bars problem: 4 × 4 images in which each of the 4 horizontal and 4 vertical bars is on independently with probability 0.2. Eight hidden units could model the data exactly, one unit per bar, and with only 256 hidden states the exact log-likelihood can be tracked against the best possible value, the data’s own entropy. The pixel biases start at the data’s average log-odds of a pixel being on, ln(0.36/0.64); every weight starts small and random. Train, and see how many weights turn into bars.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf h) &= \textstyle\prod_k \operatorname{Bern}\big(h_k \mid \sigma(b_k)\big) \\ p(\mathbf v \mid \mathbf h) &= \textstyle\prod_i \operatorname{Bern}(v_i \mid p_i) \\ \mathbf p &= \sigma(\mathbf W \mathbf h + \mathbf c) \\ q(\mathbf h \mid \mathbf v) &= \textstyle\prod_k \operatorname{Bern}(h_k \mid q_k) \\ \mathbf q &= \sigma(\mathbf R \mathbf v + \mathbf d) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a one-layer sigmoid belief net with factorised top-level priors generates the image; a separate factorised network guesses the causes from the image. With 8 hidden units the marginal p(v) is a sum of 256 terms, small enough to compute exactly for monitoring, though the algorithm never uses it.
Say it“Each pixel is on with probability sigma of W h plus c; each cause is guessed with probability sigma of R v plus d.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{wake: } \mathbf v \sim \text{data},\ \mathbf h \sim q(\cdot \mid \mathbf v) \\ &\Delta \mathbf W = \varepsilon\, \big(\mathbf v - \sigma(\mathbf W\mathbf h + \mathbf c)\big)\, \mathbf h^{\top} \\ &\Delta \mathbf b = \varepsilon\, \big(\mathbf h - \sigma(\mathbf b)\big) \\ &\text{sleep: } \mathbf h \sim p(\mathbf h),\ \mathbf v \sim p(\cdot \mid \mathbf h) \\ &\Delta \mathbf R = \varepsilon\, \big(\mathbf h - \sigma(\mathbf R\mathbf v + \mathbf d)\big)\, \mathbf v^{\top} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: both phases are gradient steps on a log-probability of a fully observed pair: ln p(v, h) on (real v, inferred h) for the generative weights, ln q(h | v) on (dreamed h, dreamed v) for the recognition weights. The biases c and d follow the same rule with the input fixed at 1.

Real-world project

Project: rating 701 chess players by expectation propagation

Lichess, the free and open-source chess server, releases its game records under the Creative Commons CC0 licence (public-domain dedication). This page embeds every game of three consecutive Hourly Bullet Arena tournaments played on 19 November 2026 (1 minute per player, no increment), retrieved through the public Lichess API on the same day: 2,423 games, of which 42 never started and are dropped, leaving 2,381 games between 701 players: 1,209 white wins, 1,119 black wins and 53 draws. Player names are replaced by numbers in order of first appearance. For each game the file also keeps both players’ Lichess ratings before the game: Glicko-2 ratings built from each player’s whole Lichess history, which we use only as a yardstick.

The model is TrueSkill without time dynamics. Every player has a fixed skill si with prior N(1500, 350²); in each game the two performances are skill plus Gaussian noise of spread β; white wins if white’s performance beats black’s by more than a draw margin ε, black wins in the mirror case, and otherwise the game is drawn. Each game is one factor on a pair of skills, the approximation is one Gaussian per player, and EP sweeps over the 2,381 factors until no mean moves by more than 10−7. Only the ratio σ0/β is identifiable from results, so σ0 is fixed and β and ε are chosen by maximising EP’s evidence estimate. To test predictions, every fifth game is held out (476 games, 459 of them decisive), the model is fitted to the other 1,905, and we score the probability it gave to the actual winner of each decisive held-out game.

\[ \begin{aligned} f_g(s_w, s_b) &= P(\text{result}_g \mid s_w, s_b) \\ d &= s_w - s_b \\ P(\text{w}) &= \Phi\Big(\frac{d - \varepsilon}{\sqrt2\,\beta}\Big) \\ P(\text{b}) &= \Phi\Big(\frac{-d - \varepsilon}{\sqrt2\,\beta}\Big) \\ P(\text{draw}) &= 1 - P(\text{w}) - P(\text{b}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: P(w) and P(b) are the probabilities that white or black wins. Each result is a window on the skill difference, blurred by performance noise. A win says the difference is probably above ε, a draw that it is probably within ±ε. The tilted moments for all three cases come from the same truncated-Gaussian formulas as in the four-player lab, with a one- or two-sided window.
Say it“White wins with probability Phi of d minus epsilon over root-two beta.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{log-loss} = -\tfrac{1}{|T|} \textstyle\sum_{g \in T} \ln \hat p_g(\text{winner}) \\ &\hat p_g(\text{white}) = \frac{P(\text{w})}{P(\text{w}) + P(\text{b})} \\ &c^2 = 2\beta^2 + \sigma_w^2 + \sigma_b^2 \\ &\hat p^{\,\text{Lichess}}_g = \big(1 + 10^{-(r_w - r_b)/400}\big)^{-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: on decisive held-out games, the average surprise at the real winner (a coin scores ln 2 = 0.693). EP’s prediction integrates over both skill beliefs, which replaces √2β by c in the formulas on the left. The baseline feeds the two pre-game Lichess ratings into the usual logistic expected-score formula.

The same computation in Python

An independent implementation, using Python’s own math.erfc for the normal distribution and SciPy only for the rank correlation: the same EP updates and evidence formula, the evidence grid, and the scoreboard. The page’s numbers come from its own JavaScript, run live; the two agree to every printed digit.

import json, sys, math
from scipy.stats import spearmanr
S = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "./"
D = json.load(open(S + "x116-chess.json")); G, first, NP = D["g"], D["first"], D["meta"]["nPlayers"]
train = [g for k, g in enumerate(G) if k % 5 != 4]; test = [g for k, g in enumerate(G) if k % 5 == 4 and g[2] != 1]
print(f"{len(G)} games, {NP} players; train {len(train)}, decisive test {len(test)}")
Phi = lambda x: 0.5 * math.erfc(-x / math.sqrt(2)); phi = lambda x: math.exp(-x * x / 2) / math.sqrt(2 * math.pi)
def trunc(mu, c, res, eps):                    # moments of d ~ N(mu, c^2) cut to the result's window
    lo, hi = {2: (eps, math.inf), 1: (-eps, eps), 0: (-math.inf, -eps)}[res]
    a, b = (lo - mu) / c, (hi - mu) / c
    Z = (Phi(b) if b < math.inf else 1.0) - (Phi(a) if a > -math.inf else 0.0)
    pa, apa = (phi(a), a * phi(a)) if a > -math.inf else (0.0, 0.0)
    pb, bpb = (phi(b), b * phi(b)) if b < math.inf else (0.0, 0.0)
    m = (pa - pb) / Z
    return Z, m, m * m - (apa - bpb) / Z
A = lambda lam, r: r * r / (2 * lam) - 0.5 * math.log(lam)
def fit(games, mu0, s0, beta, eps, alg="ep"):  # mu0: a number, or None for each player's first Lichess rating
    l0 = 1 / s0**2; lam = [l0] * NP; r = [(mu0 if mu0 is not None else first[i]) * l0 for i in range(NP)]; r0 = r[:]
    K = len(games); sl = [[0.0, 0.0] for _ in range(K)]; sr = [[0.0, 0.0] for _ in range(K)]
    order = list(range(K))[::-1] if alg == "rev" else list(range(K)); sweeps = 0; lzadf = 0.0
    while True:
        sweeps += 1; dm = 0
        for k in order:
            i, j, res = games[k][:3]
            lci, rci, lcj, rcj = lam[i] - sl[k][0], r[i] - sr[k][0], lam[j] - sl[k][1], r[j] - sr[k][1]
            vi, vj = 1 / lci, 1 / lcj; mi, mj = rci * vi, rcj * vj; c = math.sqrt(2 * beta**2 + vi + vj)
            Z, m, w = trunc(mi - mj, c, res, eps); lzadf += math.log(Z) if sweeps == 1 else 0.0
            ni, nj = mi + vi * m / c, mj - vj * m / c; wi, wj = vi - vi * vi * w / c**2, vj - vj * vj * w / c**2
            dm = max(dm, abs(ni - r[i] / lam[i]), abs(nj - r[j] / lam[j]))
            lam[i], r[i], lam[j], r[j] = 1 / wi, ni / wi, 1 / wj, nj / wj
            sl[k] = [lam[i] - lci, lam[j] - lcj]; sr[k] = [r[i] - rci, r[j] - rcj]
        if alg != "ep" or dm < 1e-7 or sweeps == 300: break
    lz = sum(A(lam[i], r[i]) - A(l0, r0[i]) for i in range(NP))
    for k, g in enumerate(games):              # EP evidence: A(q) - A(prior) + sum_g [ln Z_g + A(cavity_g) - A(q)]
        i, j, res = g[:3]; lci, rci, lcj, rcj = lam[i] - sl[k][0], r[i] - sr[k][0], lam[j] - sl[k][1], r[j] - sr[k][1]
        c = math.sqrt(2 * beta**2 + 1 / lci + 1 / lcj)
        lz += math.log(trunc(rci / lci - rcj / lcj, c, res, eps)[0]) + A(lci, rci) + A(lcj, rcj) - A(lam[i], r[i]) - A(lam[j], r[j])
    return [r[i] / lam[i] for i in range(NP)], [lam[i] ** -0.5 for i in range(NP)], (lz if alg == "ep" else lzadf), sweeps
def score(p):                                  # p(game) = predicted P(white wins | decisive)
    ll = acc = 0.0
    for g in test:
        q = min(max(p(g), 1e-12), 1 - 1e-12); y = g[2] == 2
        ll -= math.log(q if y else 1 - q); acc += 0.5 if q == 0.5 else float((q > 0.5) == y)
    return ll / len(test), acc / len(test)
def pwhite(mu, sd, beta, eps):
    def p(g):
        i, j = g[0], g[1]; c = math.sqrt(2 * beta**2 + sd[i]**2 + sd[j]**2); d = mu[i] - mu[j]
        a, b = Phi((d - eps) / c), Phi((-d - eps) / c); return a / (a + b)
    return p

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • In a conjugate-exponential model the mean-field updates are mechanical: latents use the expected natural parameters, parameters add expected sufficient statistics to a conjugate prior. On a graph this becomes variational message passing, where each node adds its children’s messages to its prior and the bound never falls.
  • KL(q‖p) makes q hide inside one mode; KL(p‖q) makes it cover everything, and for an exponential-family q its minimiser simply matches expected sufficient statistics: moment matching.
  • Expectation propagation keeps one site per factor and refines each in turn: remove it (cavity), restore the true factor (tilted), match moments, divide back. It is not guaranteed to converge, sites may have negative variance, and it gives an evidence estimate; on the lab’s clutter data it lands closer to the exact mean, spread and evidence than VB or Laplace.
  • One EP pass from flat sites is assumed density filtering, which depends on the order of the data; more passes remove that dependence. With a fully factorised q on a factor graph, EP is belief propagation: exact on trees, loopy BP on graphs with cycles.
  • Learned inference trains a network to output q in one pass, paying an amortisation gap on top of the family’s approximation gap; wake-sleep trains it instead on the generative model’s own dreams. On real chess results, EP rates 701 players from one morning of games and, given Lichess ratings as a prior, beats those ratings at predicting held-out games.

Check your understanding

In variational message passing, what does a parent send its child, and what does a child send its parent?

A parent sends the expectations of its own sufficient statistics under its current q (for a Gaussian, E[x] and E[x²]). A child sends each parent the coefficients of that parent’s sufficient statistics in the child’s expected log conditional, with the child’s own value and the co-parents averaged out. The parent adds these vectors to its prior’s natural parameters.

Why does a Gaussian fitted by KL(q‖p) to a two-mode distribution pick one mode, while the moment-matched Gaussian sits between them?

KL(q‖p) averages ln(q/p) under q, so q pays heavily for putting mass where p is nearly zero, such as the valley between the modes, and it retreats into one mode. KL(p‖q) averages under p, so q pays heavily for missing any region where p has mass; its optimum matches p’s mean and variance, which places it over the valley with a large spread.

An EP site ends with a negative variance. Is the algorithm broken?

Not necessarily. A site is not a distribution, only a factor in a product, and a negative-precision site simply widens the approximation (in the clutter problem, a point that is probably clutter pushes the belief to be less certain than the cavity). What must stay positive is the precision of q and of every cavity; if a cavity becomes improper, that update is skipped or damped.

Why does a one-pass rating system like the original TrueSkill give different ratings if the same games are processed in a different order, and how does EP fix it?

ADF folds each game into the current belief once and never revisits it, so early games are absorbed when beliefs are broad and later ones when they are narrow, and the moment-matching error of each step is frozen in. EP stores each game’s site, and on later passes removes it, recomputes it against the cavity built from all the other games, and puts it back. At a fixed point no site depends on the order, so the ratings do not either.

The wake phase and the sleep phase both use delta rules. Which objective does each push, and why is that a weakness?

The wake phase increases ln p(v, h) on real images with h sampled from q, which is a stochastic step on the lower bound for the generative weights. The sleep phase increases ln q(h | v) on dreamed pairs, which minimises KL(p(h | v) ‖ q(h | v)) averaged over the model’s own images, not the bound’s KL(q ‖ p) on the data. The two phases climb different objectives, so there is no single quantity guaranteed to improve, and early dreams can mislead the recognition network.
