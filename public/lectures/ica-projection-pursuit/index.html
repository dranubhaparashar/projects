ICA, Projection Pursuit & Slow Features

Pulling independent sources out of their mixtures

Two signals play at once and two sensors each record a blend of them. Independent component analysis gets the original signals back from the blends alone. It knows nothing about the signals or the room. It assumes only that the sources are independent and that they are not Gaussian. On the right, the two arrows are the mixing directions: how loudly each source reaches each sensor. Drag either arrowhead. The sensor recordings change, and ICA still pulls out a sine and a sawtooth.

The problem

The cocktail party

Two people talk at the same time in a room with two microphones. Each microphone picks up both voices, louder for the nearer speaker. Sound adds up in the air, so each recording is a weighted sum of the two voices. The weights depend on where everyone stands, and nobody tells us what they are. Recovering the voices from the recordings is called blind source separation: blind because we know neither the voices nor the mixing.

This looks impossible: there are more unknowns than equations. ICA makes it solvable with three assumptions. The sources are statistically independent. At most one of them is Gaussian. And there are at least as many sensors as sources, so the mixing can be undone. Here the two “voices” are synthetic, a little tune and a whispered rhythm, so you can hear the answer. Move the leak sliders, then press the play buttons to hear the microphones and what ICA separates from them.

\[ \begin{aligned} x_1(t) &= a_{11}s_1(t) + a_{12}s_2(t) \\ x_2(t) &= a_{21}s_1(t) + a_{22}s_2(t) \\ \mathbf{x} &= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: at every instant t, microphone 1 records a11 parts of voice 1 plus a12 parts of voice 2, and the same for microphone 2. The mixing matrix A stays fixed for the whole recording; only the sources change over time.
Say it“x equals A times s: the recordings are a fixed linear blend of the sources.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{y} &= \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} \\ \mathbf{W}\mathbf{A} &= \mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Lambda} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ICA learns an unmixing matrix W. It has succeeded when WA is a permutation P times a diagonal scaling Λ: each output then holds exactly one source. The order, the sign and the volume of the outputs cannot be recovered. A source twice as loud with a mixing weight half as large gives the same recordings.
Say it“y equals W x, and W times A should be a permutation times a diagonal.”

Second-order statistics

Whitening gets you halfway, then leaves a rotation

Almost every ICA algorithm starts the same way: centre the data and whiten (or sphere) it with PCA. Rotate onto the principal axes and divide each axis by its standard deviation. The new variables z are uncorrelated with unit variance. A slanted, stretched cloud becomes a round one, or a square one, as you will see.

Whitening is genuinely useful. After it, the remaining mixing matrix is orthogonal, a pure rotation, so the search shrinks from n² numbers to n(n−1)/2 angles: one angle for two sources. But whitening cannot find that angle. Rotate white data and it is still white: correlation is blind to rotation. This is the same rotation ambiguity that factor analysis has (chapter 1.13a). Bishop puts it as a limitation of Gaussian latent variables: their joint density is round, so rotating the latent space leaves the model unchanged. Turn the rotation dial, then switch the sources to Gaussian.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{z} &= \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{E}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}) \\ \operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{z}) &= \mathbf{I} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: E holds the eigenvectors of the covariance of x and D its eigenvalues. E⊤ rotates onto the principal axes; D−1/2 shrinks each axis to unit variance. Call the whole matrix K = D−1/2E⊤.
Say it“z is D to the minus a half, E transpose, times x minus its mean; the covariance of z is the identity.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{z} &= (\mathbf{K}\mathbf{A})\,\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{s} \\ \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\top} &= \mathbf{I} \\ \operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{z}) &= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{I}\,\mathbf{R}^{\top} = \mathbf{I} \\ &\text{for every rotation } \mathbf{R} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with unit-variance sources, the leftover mixing Q = KA must be orthogonal, because Cov(z) = QQ⊤ = I. So the answer is a rotation of z. Every rotation of z is equally white, though, so second-order statistics cannot say which one.
Say it“z is an orthogonal Q times s; and R z stays white for every rotation R.”

The key idea

Why non-Gaussianity identifies the sources

The central limit theorem says that a sum of independent variables looks more Gaussian than the variables themselves. Every recording is such a sum. So as you move from a blend towards a single source, the signal becomes less Gaussian. Turn that around and you have an algorithm: look for directions w in which the projection w⊤z is as non-Gaussian as possible. Each of those directions picks out one source.

It also explains the one thing ICA can never do. Rotate two independent Gaussian variables and they are still two independent Gaussian variables, so no direction is special. That is why at most one source may be Gaussian. Add sources to the sum and watch the histogram turn into a bell.

\[ \begin{aligned} \kappa(y) &= \mathbb{E}[y^4] - 3 \\ &\big(\mathbb{E}y = 0,\ \mathbb{E}y^2 = 1\big) \\ \kappa\Big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{k} s_i\Big) &= \dfrac{\kappa(s)}{k} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: excess kurtosis measures tails and peakedness against a Gaussian, which scores 0. A flat, box-like (sub-Gaussian) density is negative: uniform is −1.2. A spiky, heavy-tailed (super-Gaussian) one is positive: Laplace is +3. Add k independent copies, rescale to unit variance, and the kurtosis shrinks by a factor of k.
Say it“Kappa of y is the expected fourth power minus three; for a standardised sum of k sources it is kappa of one source over k.”

Measuring non-Gaussianity: negentropy

Kurtosis is easy to compute, but a single outlier can swing it wildly. Information theory offers a better yardstick. Of all densities with a given variance, the Gaussian has the largest entropy. Negentropy J is how far below that maximum a variable sits: zero for a Gaussian, positive for anything else. It cannot be computed exactly from samples, but a smooth contrast function G gives a robust approximation (Hyvärinen’s). Below, the whitened data from the previous section is projected onto a direction at angle θ. Sweep the direction and watch both measures peak where the projection hits a source.

\[ \begin{aligned} J(y) &= H(\nu) - H(y) \ \ge 0 \\ J(y) &\approx \big[\mathbb{E}\,G(y) - \mathbb{E}\,G(\nu)\big]^2 \\ G(u) &= \log\cosh u \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: H is differential entropy and ν is a standard Gaussian. The approximation compares the average of G under y with its average under a Gaussian, which is 0.3746 for log cosh. A larger difference, either way, means less Gaussian. Log cosh grows like |u| in the tails, so outliers barely move it, unlike u4.
Say it“Negentropy is the Gaussian’s entropy minus y’s; roughly, the squared gap between the mean of log cosh y and its Gaussian value.”
\[ \begin{aligned} I(\mathbf{y}) &= \textstyle\sum_j H(y_j) - H(\mathbf{z}) \\ &\quad - \log\lvert\det \mathbf{W}\rvert \\ &= \text{const} - \textstyle\sum_j J(y_j) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: mutual information I is zero exactly when the outputs y = Wz are independent. For whitened z and orthogonal W, log|det W| = 0 and every yj has unit variance. Then minimising dependence is the same as maximising the total negentropy. “Most independent” and “least Gaussian” are the same goal.
Say it“The mutual information is the sum of the marginal entropies minus the joint entropy minus log det W, which is a constant minus the total negentropy.”

A probabilistic model

ICA by maximum likelihood

Bishop and Goodfellow et al. present ICA as a latent-variable model, a cousin of factor analysis with two changes. The latent sources get a factorised, non-Gaussian prior p(s) = ∏j pj(sj). And the decoder x = As is deterministic, with no noise term. Then W = A−1 maps each x back to exactly one s, and the change-of-variables rule gives the exact likelihood. We can simply climb it.

The price is that you must choose the source prior. Bishop’s choice, p(s) = 1/(π cosh s), has heavy tails, which suits speech. If the true sources are flat instead (sub-Gaussian), the same prior steers the model to the wrong answer. Extended infomax (Lee, Girolami & Sejnowski, 1999) fixes this by switching to a two-bump prior for such sources. Choose sources and a prior, then press Run: match them, then mismatch them.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{x}) &= \lvert\det\mathbf{W}\rvert \textstyle\prod_{j} p_j(\mathbf{w}_j^{\top}\mathbf{x}) \\ \ell(\mathbf{W}) &= \log\lvert\det\mathbf{W}\rvert \\ &\quad + \tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_{n}\sum_j \log p_j(\mathbf{w}_j^{\top}\mathbf{x}_n) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: wj⊤ is row j of W, so wj⊤x is the j-th recovered source. The density of x is the prior evaluated at the recovered sources, times |det W|. That factor accounts for how W stretches volume. Without it, the likelihood could be pushed up just by shrinking every y towards the peak of the prior.
Say it“p of x is the absolute determinant of W times the product of the source densities at w-j transpose x. The log-likelihood is log det W plus the average log prior.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{W} &\leftarrow \mathbf{W} + \eta\,\mathbf{M}\,\mathbf{W} \\ \mathbf{M} &= \mathbf{I} - \tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_n \varphi(\mathbf{y}_n)\mathbf{y}_n^{\top} \\ \varphi(y) &= -\tfrac{d}{dy}\log p(y) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: this is the natural gradient of ℓ (Amari et al., 1996): the ordinary gradient times W⊤W, which needs no matrix inverse. For the 1/cosh prior, φ(y) = tanh y. For the two-bump prior ½[N(−1, 1) + N(1, 1)], φ(y) = y − tanh y. Learning stops when E[φ(y)y⊤] = I.
Say it“W moves by eta times identity minus the average of phi of y times y transpose, all times W.”

Goodfellow et al. also point out how this family grows. Nonlinear ICA replaces x = As with a learned invertible network; the NICE flow model of chapter 2 is one. Independent subspace analysis lets features be dependent inside small groups but independent between groups. Topographic ICA lays the features on a grid and lets neighbours be similar. Trained on patches of natural images, all of these learn Gabor-like edge detectors, much like the receptive fields in the visual cortex.

The workhorse

FastICA: a fixed point instead of a step size

Gradient ascent needs a learning rate and many small steps. Hyvärinen and Oja (1997) derived a Newton-style update for maximising non-Gaussianity on whitened data that needs neither. Start from any unit vector w and apply the update. Then rescale w to length 1, and repeat until w stops turning. With the kurtosis contrast, on data that follow the model exactly, it converges cubically: the number of correct digits roughly triples at every step. On a finite sample it is a little slower, but each step still shrinks the change roughly a hundredfold. For more than one source you find directions one at a time and remove earlier ones (deflation), or update them all together and re-orthogonalise (symmetric, the scikit-learn default). Press Step and watch the arrow jump; then try another contrast or a fresh start.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{w}^{+} &= \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{z}\, g(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{z})\big] - \mathbb{E}\big[g'(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{z})\big]\,\mathbf{w} \\ \mathbf{w} &\leftarrow \mathbf{w}^{+}/\lVert\mathbf{w}^{+}\rVert \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: g is the derivative of the contrast G: tanh u for log cosh, u³ for kurtosis, u·e−u²/2 for the Gaussian contrast. It is an approximate Newton step for E G(w⊤z) on the unit sphere. Whitening makes the Hessian nearly diagonal, which is why it collapses to this short formula.
Say it“w-plus is the average of z times g of w-transpose-z, minus the average of g-prime times w; then normalise.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{deflation:} \\ &\mathbf{w}_p \leftarrow \mathbf{w}_p - \textstyle\sum_{j \lt p} (\mathbf{w}_p^{\top}\mathbf{w}_j)\,\mathbf{w}_j \\ &\text{symmetric:} \\ &\mathbf{W} \leftarrow (\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{\top})^{-1/2}\,\mathbf{W} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the separating directions of whitened data are orthogonal. Deflation subtracts from a new direction its projections on those already found (Gram–Schmidt), so it cannot find the same source twice. The symmetric version treats all rows alike, so errors in early components do not pile up in later ones.
Say it“Subtract from w-p its projections on the earlier w-j; or replace W by W W-transpose to the minus a half, times W.”

Older than ICA

Exploratory projection pursuit

Long before ICA, Friedman and Tukey (1974) asked how to look at data with many variables. We can only see one or two dimensions at a time, so choose the projection that shows the most. Diaconis and Freedman (1984) later proved that most low-dimensional projections of high-dimensional data look nearly Gaussian. So “interesting” means non-Gaussian: clusters, holes, skew, sharp edges. Projection pursuit optimises a projection index that scores this. ESL’s point is that ICA with a negentropy index is projection pursuit, done for all directions at once. The same idea, with a supervised index, gives projection pursuit regression (chapter 11 of ESL).

Sphere the data first (Friedman’s advice). Otherwise the index just finds the direction with the largest variance, which is what PCA does, and variance is not structure. The cloud below has 6 variables and 600 points. Hidden in it is a plane where the points form three groups. Four noise directions have more variance than that plane, so PCA looks straight past it. Switch the views, then press Pursue.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{w}^{\star} &= \arg\max_{\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert=1} \ \mathcal{I}(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{z}) \\ \mathcal{I}(y) &= \big[\mathbb{E}\log\cosh y - 0.3746\big]^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: among all unit directions in the sphered 6-D space, find the one whose 1-D shadow scores highest on the index; here the index is the negentropy estimate from above. The second direction is pursued orthogonally to the first. Friedman and Tukey’s original index multiplied a robust spread by a local density; Huber (1985) tied the family together and showed most good indices are measures of non-Gaussianity.
Say it“w-star is the unit vector that maximises the projection index of w-transpose-z.”

Modelling the densities

A direct approach: product-density ICA

FastICA and infomax both commit to a contrast or a prior before seeing the data. Hastie and Tibshirani’s product-density ICA (ESL 14.7.4) estimates each source’s density from the data instead, and maximises the resulting likelihood directly. Each density is a Gaussian times a smooth correction, a tilted Gaussian. Fitting alternates between two easy steps: fit the tilts gj for the current directions, then update the directions with a FastICA-style step in which the fitted log-density takes the place of G.

Why bother? Because any fixed contrast has blind spots. The sources below are lopsided: 79% of each is one bump and 21% a smaller, distant bump. The two weights are chosen so the kurtosis is exactly zero, and log cosh is almost as blind, yet the density is plainly not Gaussian: it is skewed. Press Run, and compare the three criteria in the lower plot.

\[ \begin{aligned} f_j(s) &= \phi(s)\, e^{g_j(s)} \\ \ell_j &= \tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_i \big[\log\phi(s_{ij}) + g_j(s_{ij})\big] \\ &\quad - \textstyle\int \phi\, e^{g_j} - \lambda_j \textstyle\int \big(g_j'''\big)^2 \\ &\max_{\mathbf{A},\,g}\ \textstyle\sum_j \ell_j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: φ is the standard Gaussian density and sij = aj⊤zi. The tilt gj bends the Gaussian into the source’s shape. The −∫φeg term makes the fitted density integrate to 1 at the optimum, without an explicit constraint. The roughness penalty keeps g smooth, and penalising the third derivative leaves quadratic tilts free, so a Gaussian of any width costs nothing. On the page, g lives on 60 bins and is fitted as a penalised Poisson regression, with third differences standing in for g′′′.
Say it“Each source density is phi times e to the g-j; maximise the average log-density, minus the integral, minus lambda times the roughness of g.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{a}_j &\leftarrow \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{z}\, g_j'(\mathbf{a}_j^{\top}\mathbf{z})\big] \\ &\quad - \mathbb{E}\big[g_j''(\mathbf{a}_j^{\top}\mathbf{z})\big]\,\mathbf{a}_j \\ \mathbf{A} &\leftarrow (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{-1/2}\mathbf{A} \\ d(\mathbf{P}) &= \tfrac{1}{2n(n-1)}\Big[\textstyle\sum_i \big(\tfrac{\sum_j |p_{ij}|}{\max_j |p_{ij}|} - 1\big) \\ &\quad + \textstyle\sum_j \big(\tfrac{\sum_i |p_{ij}|}{\max_i |p_{ij}|} - 1\big)\Big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the direction update is the FastICA fixed point with G = gj, the fitted log-tilt. On whitened data E log φ(a⊤z) is the same for every unit a, so only g matters. Then the rows are re-orthogonalised. The Amari error d of P = W·(true mixing) is 0 exactly when P is a scaled permutation, whatever the order and signs.
Say it“a-j becomes the average of z times g-j-prime, minus the average of g-j-double-prime times a-j; then orthogonalise. The Amari error measures how far P is from a scaled permutation.”

Using time

Slow feature analysis

ICA throws away the order of the samples: shuffle the recording and it gives the same answer. But the world changes slowly while sensors flicker. A pixel jumps from bright to dark when an edge slides past it, though the object itself moves smoothly. Wiskott and Sejnowski’s slow feature analysis (2002) turns this slowness principle into a learning rule: find features of the input that change as slowly as possible over time, while still carrying some signal. Goodfellow et al. present it as a linear factor model that uses time instead of non-Gaussianity. So it can separate Gaussian sources that ICA cannot touch, as long as they move at different speeds.

The constraints are what make it sensible. Without them, a constant feature would be the slowest of all. Sweep the direction below, then press Solve.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{f_i}\ \mathbb{E}_t\big[(f_i(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}) - f_i(\mathbf{x}_t))^2\big] \\ &\text{s.t. } \mathbb{E}_t f_i = 0,\ \ \mathbb{E}_t f_i^2 = 1 \\ &\phantom{\text{s.t. }} \mathbb{E}_t f_i f_j = 0\ \ (j \lt i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the slowness Δ of a feature is its mean squared change from one time step to the next. Zero mean and unit variance rule out the trivial constant answer. Decorrelation from the earlier features forces each new feature to say something new.
Say it“Minimise the expected squared change of f-i between consecutive times, keeping it zero-mean, unit-variance and uncorrelated with the slower features.”
\[ \begin{aligned} f(\mathbf{x}) &= \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{z},\quad \lVert \mathbf{w}\rVert = 1 \\ \Delta\mathbf{z}_t &= \mathbf{z}_{t+1}-\mathbf{z}_t \\ \dot{\mathbf{C}} &= \mathbb{E}_t\big[\Delta\mathbf{z}_t\,\Delta\mathbf{z}_t^{\top}\big] \\ \Delta(\mathbf{w}) &= \mathbf{w}^{\top}\dot{\mathbf{C}}\,\mathbf{w} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for linear features, first whiten the input to z; then every unit w already meets the variance constraint. The slowness is a quadratic form in w, so the slowest feature is the eigenvector of the difference covariance Ċ with the smallest eigenvalue, the next slowest the next eigenvector, and so on. The method is closed-form, with no iterations and no local optima.
Say it“Delta of w is w-transpose C-dot w, where C-dot is the covariance of the time differences; take its smallest eigenvectors.”

Nonlinear slow features: expand, then solve

Linear SFA can only rotate. To find nonlinear features, expand the input with fixed nonlinear functions, then run linear SFA on the expansion. The quadratic expansion is the classic choice, and stacking such layers gives deep SFA. Below is Wiskott and Sejnowski’s own toy. The inputs are x1(t) = sin t + cos²(11t) and x2(t) = cos(11t). Both race along at the fast frequency, yet the slow sin t is hiding in them as x1 − x2². Switch on the quadratic expansion.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x}) &= \big(x_1,\ x_2,\ x_1^2,\ x_1x_2,\ x_2^2\big) \\ f(\mathbf{x}) &= \mathbf{w}^{\top}\,\mathrm{whiten}\big(\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x})\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the five expanded signals are whitened and fed to linear SFA. Any feature that is a quadratic polynomial of the inputs, such as x1 − x2², is now within reach. Goodfellow et al. note the cost: the expansion grows fast with input size. They also note a rare strength of SFA: when the environment’s dynamics are known, the optimal features can be predicted in advance, by the calculus of variations.
Say it“Expand x into x-one, x-two, their squares and their product; whiten; then do linear SFA.”

Real-world project

Project: removing eye blinks from real EEG

Electroencephalography (EEG) records microvolt voltages from electrodes on the scalp. Every blink drags a much larger voltage across the front of the head. The eyeball is electrically charged and the eyelid slides over it. A blink can be ten times bigger than the brain activity you want to study. Throwing away every second with a blink wastes data. Makeig, Jung and colleagues (1996, 2000) showed that ICA does better. A blink is an independent source with a fixed scalp pattern. Unmix the recording, delete that one component, and mix the rest back.

The data is real: the first minute of subject 1, eyes open and at rest, from the PhysioNet EEG Motor Movement/Imagery database (Schalk et al., 2004; ODC-By licence). It has 64 electrodes at 160 samples per second; we keep the 21 standard 10–20 sites, 9,760 samples each. The page removes slow drift by subtracting a 1-second moving average. It then whitens the data and runs symmetric FastICA with log cosh, from the same starting matrix as scikit-learn, all in your browser. Press Unmix.

The components, most non-Gaussian first

Each card is one independent component. Its scalp map is its column of the mixing matrix A = W−1: how strongly it reaches each electrode (red positive, blue negative; nose at the top). Next to it are its time course and its kurtosis. Blinks are rare, huge spikes, so the blink component should be the most non-Gaussian, and its map should sit over the eyes. The same project in scikit-learn

import numpy as np
from sklearn.decomposition import FastICA
from scipy.stats import kurtosis

def read_edf(path):                     # EDF: 256-byte header, 256 bytes per signal, then int16 records
    b = open(path, "rb").read(); ns = int(b[252:256]); nrec = int(b[236:244])
    lab = [b[256 + 16*i: 272 + 16*i].decode().strip(" .") for i in range(ns)]
    nsr = [int(b[256 + ns*216 + 8*i: 256 + ns*216 + 8*i + 8]) for i in range(ns)]
    raw = np.frombuffer(b[int(b[184:192]):], "<i2")[: nrec*sum(nsr)].reshape(nrec, -1)
    cut = np.cumsum([0] + nsr)
    return {lab[i]: raw[:, cut[i]:cut[i+1]].ravel().astype(float) for i in range(ns)}   # 1 unit = 1 µV here

CH = ["Fp1","Fpz","Fp2","F7","F3","Fz","F4","F8","T7","C3","Cz","C4","T8",
      "P7","P3","Pz","P4","P8","O1","Oz","O2"]
sig = read_edf("S001R01.edf")                           # PhysioNet EEGMMIDB, subject 1, eyes open, 160 Hz
X = np.array([sig[c] for c in CH])                      # 21 channels x 9760 samples (61 s)
pad = np.pad(X, ((0, 0), (80, 80)), mode="edge")        # high-pass: subtract a centred 1-second moving average
X = X - np.array([np.convolve(p, np.ones(161) / 161, "valid") for p in pad])
X = X - X.mean(1, keepdims=True)

lam, E = np.linalg.eigh(X @ X.T / X.shape[1])           # whitening by PCA
lam, E = lam[::-1], E[:, ::-1]
E *= np.sign(E[np.abs(E).argmax(0), range(21)])         # sign rule: biggest entry of each eigenvector positive
K = (E / np.sqrt(lam)).T
Z = K @ X                                               # whitened: covariance = identity

W0 = np.round(np.random.RandomState(0).randn(21, 21), 4)
ica = FastICA(whiten=False, fun="logcosh", w_init=W0, tol=1e-4, max_iter=1000).fit(Z.T)
W = ica.components_; S = W @ Z; A = E * np.sqrt(lam) @ W.T  # sources and mixing matrix (X = A S)
ku = kurtosis(S, axis=1)
fp = (X[0] + X[2]) / 2
r = [abs(np.corrcoef(s, fp)[0, 1]) for s in S]
b = int(np.argmax(r))
print(f"FastICA iterations: {ica.n_iter_}")
print(f"variance in the top 5 principal components: {lam[:5].sum() / lam.sum():.4f}")
print(f"blink component: #{b}, kurtosis {ku[b]:.3f}, |corr| with (Fp1+Fp2)/2 = {r[b]:.4f}")
print("its scalp map (largest five):", ", ".join(f"{CH[i]} {A[i, b]:+.2f}" for i in np.argsort(-np.abs(A[:, b]))[:5]))
Xc = X - np.outer(A[:, b], S[b])                        # remove the blink component
for c in ["Fp1", "Fp2", "Fz", "Cz", "O1"]:
    i = CH.index(c)
    print(f"{c:4s} variance removed: {1 - Xc[i].var() / X[i].var():7.2%}")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • ICA models recordings as x = As with independent, non-Gaussian sources, and recovers them up to order, sign and scale.
  • Whitening fixes everything except a rotation; correlation is blind to rotation, and Gaussian sources leave it undecidable.
  • Mixtures are more Gaussian than their parts, so the least Gaussian directions (by kurtosis or, more robustly, negentropy) are the sources; that is also exploratory projection pursuit.
  • Maximum likelihood needs a source prior on the right side of Gaussian; FastICA’s fixed point needs no step size; product-density ICA fits the densities themselves.
  • Slow feature analysis uses time instead: the slowest unit-variance features of the input, from one eigen-decomposition (after a nonlinear expansion if needed).

Check your understanding

Why can’t PCA alone separate two mixed voices?

PCA uses only the covariance. After whitening, every rotation of the data has the same identity covariance, so second-order statistics cannot single out the rotation that undoes the mixing. ICA needs higher-order information (non-Gaussianity) or time structure (as in SFA) to pick the angle.

Two sources are both Gaussian. What does ICA return, and why?

An arbitrary rotation of the whitened data. The joint density of two independent unit Gaussians is perfectly round, so every rotation gives two independent Gaussians again; the likelihood and every non-Gaussianity measure are flat in the angle.

You run infomax with the 1/cosh prior on uniformly distributed sources. What happens?

The prior is super-Gaussian but the sources are sub-Gaussian, so the true solution is a minimum, not a maximum, of the likelihood in the rotation angle. The algorithm settles about 45° away, on blends of the sources. Switching to a sub-Gaussian prior (as extended infomax does) fixes it.

Why does projection pursuit sphere the data first?

Without sphering, an index that depends on scale would mostly reward large-variance directions, which is just PCA. After sphering every direction has variance 1, so the index can only respond to shape: clusters, skew, heavy tails or holes.

An EEG component has a scalp map concentrated on Fp1 and Fp2 and a kurtosis of 22. What is it, and what does removing it do?

An eye-blink artefact: blinks are rare, large spikes (high kurtosis) whose voltage is strongest over the eyes. Removing it subtracts its column of A times its time course from every channel. Most of the frontal variance goes; posterior channels such as O1 barely change.
