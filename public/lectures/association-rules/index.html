Association Rules & Market-Basket Analysis

Which things turn up together, and is it more than chance?

A shop keeps every receipt. Nobody labelled them, and there is nothing to predict, yet the pile hides useful facts: customers who buy the green Regency teacup usually buy the pink one too. Association rules turn those patterns into statements of the form “if a basket holds A, it probably holds B”, with three numbers attached: how common, how reliable and how surprising. On the right are the strongest pairings in a year of real orders from a British online gift shop: 19,773 baskets, 829 popular products. Touch a product to see the rule it is part of.

The idea

Looking for what goes together

So far every chapter had an answer column y to predict. This part of the book drops it. In unsupervised learning we only have N observations of a vector X = (X1, …, Xp), and the goal is to say something about their joint distribution Pr(X) directly: where it piles up, which variables move together. There is no test error to keep us honest, so the methods lean on sensible summaries rather than a single score.

The bluntest summary is the mode: the single value of X that occurs most often. For a shop, that would be “the most common exact basket”. It sounds useful, but count how many baskets are possible. Each product is in or out, so p products give 2p possible baskets, far more than there are receipts. Almost every exact basket is unique, and the mode tells us nothing. Slide the number of products and watch the mode collapse.

\[ v^{*} = \arg\max_{v}\ \Pr(X = v) \]
Read it as: the mode is the value of the whole vector X that has the highest probability. With p yes/no products there are 2p candidate values v, and we estimate each probability by the fraction of baskets that equal v exactly.
Say it“v-star is the v that maximises the probability that X equals v.”
\[ \Pr\Big[\bigcap_{j \in \mathcal{J}} (X_j \in s_j)\Big] \ \text{ is large} \]
Read it as: instead of pinning down every variable, look at a few of them (the index set 𝒥) and ask that each lands in an allowed set of values sj. A statement of that shape, “X1 is in s1 and X2 is in s2 …”, is a conjunctive rule, and we hunt for ones that hold often.
Say it“The probability that X-j lies in s-j for every j in script J is large.”

So we give up on the full joint distribution and look for regions of high probability that are simple to describe: a few variables, each restricted to a few values. That is bump hunting in its most readable form, and association rules are its version for data with thousands of yes/no variables.

Market-basket analysis

Baskets, items and support

Turn every possible value into a yes/no column. For a shop that is natural: one column per product, Zk = 1 if the basket holds product k. In general a variable with several values becomes several dummy columns, one per value. A collection 𝒦 of columns is an item set, and its support is the fraction of baskets that contain every item in it. The task of market-basket analysis is to list every item set whose support clears a floor t. Below is a toy corner shop with six products and twelve receipts. Click product names to build an item set, click any cell to edit a receipt, and move the floor.

\[ T(\mathcal{K}) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ \prod_{k \in \mathcal{K}} z_{ik} \]
Read it as: the product of 0/1 values is 1 only when basket i holds every item in 𝒦, so the sum counts those baskets and dividing by N turns the count into a fraction. It estimates the probability that all the items turn up together. We want every 𝒦 with T(𝒦) > t.
Say it“T of K is one over N times the sum over baskets of the product of z-i-k over the items in K.”

The algorithm

Apriori: prune the lattice

Six products give 63 item sets; 829 products give more than 10249. Counting them all is out of the question. Agrawal and Srikant's Apriori algorithm (1994) rests on one observation: adding an item can only shrink the set of baskets that contain everything, so support never goes up as an item set grows. If {Bread, Tea} is rare, every set containing both is rare too, and need never be counted. Apriori therefore works upwards one size at a time. Pass m builds candidates of size m only from frequent sets of size m−1, throws out any candidate with a rare subset, then makes one pass through the receipts to count the survivors. Press Step to run it pass by pass on the corner shop.

\[ \mathcal{L} \subseteq \mathcal{K} \ \Longrightarrow\ T(\mathcal{L}) \ \ge\ T(\mathcal{K}) \]
Read it as: every basket that holds all of 𝒦 also holds all of the smaller set ℒ, so ℒ is at least as common. Turned round: if ℒ is below the floor, so is every larger set containing it.
Say it“If L is a subset of K, then the support of L is at least the support of K.”
passes over the data
item sets counted
frequent found

Apriori needs one pass through the data per item-set size, so a handful of passes in practice, and it scales to millions of baskets because the floor keeps the candidate lists short. The price is the floor itself. A rule such as {vodka} ⇒ {caviar} may be very reliable, but if caviar is in one basket in a thousand the item set never clears a sensible floor and the rule is never seen. Lower the floor to catch it and the number of frequent sets explodes. Later algorithms (FP-growth, for instance) avoid generating candidates at all, but they find exactly the same item sets.

From item sets to rules

Rules: support, confidence and lift

Each frequent item set 𝒦 can be cut into two disjoint parts A and B, giving a rule A ⇒ B: “baskets with A tend to hold B”. A is the antecedent and B the consequent. Three numbers describe a rule. Support says how often the whole thing happens. Confidence says how often B appears when A does. Lift compares that confidence with how often B appears anyway: a lift of 1 means A tells you nothing about B. Apriori's output is the list of rules with support above t and confidence above a second threshold c. Pick a rule from the list to see its three numbers as areas.

\[ \begin{aligned} T(A \Rightarrow B) &= T(A \cup B) \\ C(A \Rightarrow B) &= \frac{T(A \cup B)}{T(A)} \\ L(A \Rightarrow B) &= \frac{C(A \Rightarrow B)}{T(B)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: support is the share of baskets holding everything in A and B. Confidence estimates Pr(B | A), the share of A-baskets that also hold B. Lift divides by T(B), the confidence you would expect if A and B were unrelated, so lift above 1 means A makes B more likely, and below 1 less likely. Lift is symmetric: L(A ⇒ B) = L(B ⇒ A) = T(A ∪ B)/(T(A)T(B)).
Say it“Support of A implies B is T of A union B; confidence is that over T of A; lift is the confidence over T of B.”

A worked example

Survey answers as items

Market-basket analysis is not only for shops. ESL's worked example takes a household survey from the San Francisco Bay Area, about 9,400 questionnaires and 14 demographic questions (income, age, education, occupation, type of home and so on), and turns each answer into items. An ordered answer is cut at its median into two items, “below the median” and “at or above it”. An unordered answer with several categories becomes one item per category. Apriori, run with support 10% and confidence 60%, then returns thousands of rules about who lives where and how, each one a plain sentence.

We do the same with the gift shop's invoices. Each one has a time of day, a weekday, a month, a country, whether the customer was registered or checked out as a guest, how many different products it holds and its value. That is seven questions and 22 items. Move the two floors and click a dot to read its rule.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{ordered: } \{X_j < m_j\},\ \{X_j \ge m_j\} \\ &\text{categorical: } \{X_j = v\},\ v \in \mathcal{S}_j \\ &K = \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{p} (\text{items from } X_j) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: mj is the median of variable j, so each ordered variable gives two items that split the data roughly in half. A categorical variable with value set 𝒮j gives |𝒮j| items. Every invoice switches on exactly one item per variable. K counts the items: here 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 22.
Say it“An ordered variable becomes two items, below and above its median; a categorical one becomes one item per value; K is the total.”

A change of view

Unsupervised as supervised learning

Here is a neat trick that turns density hunting into a problem we already know how to solve. Call the unknown density of the data g(x). Pick a reference density g0(x) that we know and can sample from, for example uniform over the range of the data. Draw a sample from g0 of the same size N, label the real data y = 1 and the reference sample y = 0, and pool them. Now fit any classifier. The probability it estimates, μ(x) = Pr(y = 1 | x), is a known function of g/g0, so a classifier is secretly a density estimator. Everything from earlier chapters (logistic regression, trees, smoothers) becomes a tool for unsupervised learning.

Below are the gift shop's invoices again, by time of day and by number of products (log scale). The classifier is a Gaussian kernel smoother: at each place it compares the nearby weight of real invoices with the nearby weight of reference points. Click the plot to probe a point, switch the reference, and change the smoother's width.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu(x) &= \Pr(y = 1 \mid x) \\ &= \frac{g(x)}{g(x) + g_0(x)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with equal numbers of real and reference points, a point found at x is real with probability equal to the real density's share of the total density there. Where data piles up, μ is near 1; where only the reference reaches, it is near 0.
Say it“Mu of x is g of x over g of x plus g-nought of x.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat g(x) &= g_0(x)\,\frac{\hat\mu(x)}{1 - \hat\mu(x)} \\ f(x) &= \log\frac{g(x)}{g_0(x)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: solve the first formula for g. The classifier's odds μ/(1−μ) estimate the density ratio g/g0, and its log-odds f(x) is the log of that ratio, which is exactly what logistic regression models. With N0 reference points instead of N, multiply the odds by N0/N.
Say it“g-hat is g-nought times mu-hat over one minus mu-hat; the log-odds is log of g over g-nought.”

Beyond single values

Generalised association rules

Back to rules. Market-basket analysis has two blind spots. First, every item is a single value, so “Thursday between 6 and 8 pm” can only be found if someone happened to code exactly that as an item. A generalised item set lets each variable take a whole subset sj of its values. Second, support rewards whatever is common. In the invoice data, “UK ⇒ registered” has huge support and confidence simply because both items are common on their own; it says nothing about how they relate.

The cure for the second is the reference trick with a smarter g0: the product of the marginals. A sample from it keeps every variable's own distribution exactly but breaks every link between variables; you can draw one by shuffling each column of the data independently (or resampling each column on its own, which is what this page does). A region where the data is denser than this reference is a genuine association, not just a popular value. Click the grid to choose weekdays and hours, or try the presets.

\[ g_0(x) = \prod_{j=1}^{p} g_j(x_j) \]
Read it as: gj is the distribution of variable j on its own. Multiplying them gives the joint distribution the data would have if the variables were independent: right totals for every row and column, no associations.
Say it“g-nought of x is the product over j of g-j of x-j.”
\[ R = \frac{\hat{\Pr}\big[\bigcap_{j} (X_j \in s_j)\big]}{\prod_{j} \hat{\Pr}(X_j \in s_j)} \]
Read it as: the top is the support of the generalised item set in the data. The bottom is its probability under g0. R is the data-to-reference density ratio over the region, the generalisation of lift: R = 1 is independence, R = 2 means twice as common as chance.
Say it“R is the estimated probability that every X-j lies in s-j, over the product of the separate probabilities.”

Choosing the learner

Trees or PRIM?

Once the problem is “data (y = 1) against reference (y = 0)”, the regions we want are those where the average of y is high. Any classifier would do for estimating the ratio, but we want the answer as rules, so the natural tools are ones whose output is a set of boxes: each box is a conjunction “X1 in this range and X2 in that range”. Both were met earlier in the book. A decision tree (CART, chapter 1.7) splits the space greedily to make its leaves as pure as possible, and each leaf with a high share of data is a rule. PRIM, the patient rule induction method of chapter 1.7a, starts with a box around everything and peels a thin slice off whichever face raises the mean of y the most, until the box holds only a small share of the points. It aims straight at high-mean boxes, peels gently, and so tends to find tighter bumps than a tree.

Both share one weakness here, which you can see below. The reference has the same marginals as the data, so a single cut on a single variable leaves both sides with about as much data as reference: the first move is close to blind, and only later moves see the associations. Switch between the two and move the sliders.

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar y_{B} &= \frac{1}{N_B} \sum_{x_i \in B} y_i \\ R_B &= \frac{\bar y_{B}}{1 - \bar y_{B}} \cdot \frac{N_0}{N} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: NB is the number of pooled points (data and reference) inside box B, and ȳB is the share of them that are real. The odds of that share, scaled by the sample sizes, estimate the density ratio over the box, the R of the previous section. Here N0 = N, so RB is just the odds.
Say it“Y-bar over B is the average of y inside B; R-B is y-bar over one minus y-bar, times N-nought over N.”

import numpy as np, pandas as pd, itertools
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
# x037_lib: decodes the page's own data string, so Python and the browser see identical inputs;
# mulberry32 is the page's random-number generator, ported to Python
from x037_lib import load, baskets, invoices, resampled

D = load(); B = baskets(D); N, K = len(B), D["K"]          # 19,773 invoices, 829 products
Z = np.zeros((N, K), dtype=np.int32)
for i, b in enumerate(B): Z[i, b] = 1
pairs = Z.T @ Z                                             # brute-force count of every pair

def apriori(t):
    need = int(np.ceil(t * N)); F = {}
    L = [(k,) for k in range(K) if Z[:, k].sum() >= need]
    F.update({s: int(Z[:, s[0]].sum()) for s in L}); passes = [(K, len(L))]
    while L:
        Ls = set(L)
        C = [a + (b[-1],) for a, b in itertools.combinations(L, 2) if a[:-1] == b[:-1]]
        C = [c for c in C if all(tuple(x for x in c if x != y) in Ls for y in c)]
        L = [c for c in C if Z[:, list(c)].all(1).sum() >= need]
        F.update({c: int(Z[:, list(c)].all(1).sum()) for c in L}); passes.append((len(C), len(L)))
    return F, passes

for t in [0.03, 0.02, 0.015, 0.01]:
    F, passes = apriori(t)
    print(f"t = {t:.3f}: {len(F):5d} frequent sets, (candidates, frequent) per pass = {passes}")
ok = all(pairs[s[0], s[1]] == n for s, n in F.items() if len(s) == 2)
print("pair counts agree with the brute-force Z'Z:", ok)

R = []
for s, n in F.items():
    for r in range(1, len(s)):
        for A in itertools.combinations(s, r):
            Bs = tuple(x for x in s if x not in A); conf = n / F[A]
            R.append((A, Bs, n / N, conf, conf / (F[Bs] / N)))
print("rules at t = 1%:", len(R), "| confidence >= 0.5:", sum(r[3] >= 0.5 for r in R))
name = lambda s: " + ".join(D["names"][i] for i in s)
for A, Bs, sup, conf, lift in sorted([r for r in R if r[3] >= 0.5], key=lambda r: -r[4])[:3] + \
        sorted([r for r in R if len(r[0]) == 1 and len(r[1]) == 1], key=lambda r: -r[3])[:2]:
    print(f"  {name(A)} => {name(Bs)}: support {sup:.4f}, confidence {conf:.3f}, lift {lift:.2f}")

X = invoices(D)[:, :6]                         # hour, weekday, month, country, registered, lines
X0 = resampled(X, seed=37)                     # every column resampled on its own: product of marginals

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Association rules hunt for simple regions of high probability: conjunctions of a few variables, each limited to a few values, because the exact mode of a high-dimensional X is hopeless to estimate.
  • Support T(𝒦) is the share of baskets holding every item in 𝒦. It can only fall as 𝒦 grows, so Apriori counts sizes in order and never counts a set with a rare subset.
  • A rule A ⇒ B has support T(A ∪ B), confidence T(A ∪ B)/T(A) and lift confidence/T(B); lift near 1 means the rule only restates that B is common.
  • Pool the data (y = 1) with a sample from a reference density g0 (y = 0): any classifier's odds then estimate g/g0, so supervised tools do unsupervised work.
  • With g0 the product of the marginals, high-ratio boxes found by a tree or PRIM are generalised association rules: ranges and subsets that are more common than chance, even at low support. On a year of gift-shop invoices, 829 products gave 2,007 frequent sets at 1% support.

Check your understanding

The support floor is 2%. {tea, scones} has support 1.5%. Does Apriori ever count {tea, scones, jam}?

No. {tea, scones} is below the floor, so it is not a frequent set of size 2, and any size-3 candidate containing it is removed before counting: its support can be at most 1.5%.

A rule has confidence 0.92 and lift 0.99. Is it useful?

Hardly. Confidence 0.92 sounds strong, but lift 0.99 says B is in 93% of all baskets anyway, so knowing A changes nothing. “UK ⇒ registered” in the invoice data is exactly this kind of rule.

You pool N data points with 3N reference points and a classifier gives μ̂(x) = 0.5. What is ĝ(x)/g0(x)?

Then μ = Ng/(Ng + 3Ng0), so g/g0 = 3μ/(1 − μ) = 3. Equal odds with three times as many reference points means the data is three times as dense as the reference there.

Why does a uniform reference find the same regions as ordinary support, but the product of the marginals finds different ones?

With a uniform g0 the ratio g/g0 is proportional to g itself, so high-ratio regions are simply high-density regions, popular values included. The product of the marginals already contains every variable's popularity, so only regions where variables occur together more than chance stand out.

Why can't a tree's first split separate data from a product-of-marginals reference?

A split on one variable compares the data's marginal with the reference's marginal, and they are the same (exactly, after shuffling; up to sampling noise after resampling). Both children stay close to 50:50, so the first split is close to arbitrary. Only splits inside it, which look at two or more variables together, can find associations.
