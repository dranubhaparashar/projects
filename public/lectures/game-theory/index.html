Game Theory for Multi-Agent Systems

When the best move depends on what everyone else does

A penalty taker has two good options and one problem: the goalkeeper. Kick to the same side every time and an attentive keeper will be waiting there. Dive the same way every time and the kicker will punish it. Neither player has a best action, only a best way of being unpredictable. This chapter is about decisions like that, made by agents whose outcomes depend on one another: robots sharing a corridor, drivers sharing roads, poker players hiding cards, and an AI assistant that is unsure what its human wants. Take some penalties by clicking either half of the goal, then switch the keeper from reading your habits to playing the equilibrium.

One decision maker, or many?

Until now most agents in this book have had the world to themselves. Other things moved, but they moved like the weather: the agent could treat them as part of the environment, perhaps a random part. A multiagent environment is one in which some of those other things are agents too, with goals of their own and the ability to choose. How much that matters depends on who makes the decisions.

With one decision maker there may be many bodies but only one mind. A single agent with several effectors working at once, two arms or a voice and a hand, faces multieffector planning: it has to schedule actions that run concurrently. When the effectors are physically separate, such as a fleet of warehouse robots run by one controller, the same problem is called multibody planning. The controller picks a joint action, one action per body, at every step. Nothing strategic happens, because the robots have no preferences of their own and cannot disagree.

With multiple decision makers each agent chooses for itself. If they share one utility function they form a team, and the difficulty is coordination: each has to predict what the others will do. If their utilities differ, possibly in direct opposition, each must also reason about what the others want, and the analysis becomes game theory. The same corridor can pose either problem. Below, two robots must swap ends of a corridor that has one passing bay. Switch between one planner for both robots and two separate planners, then press Play.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf a &= (a_1, \dots, a_n) \in A_1 \times \cdots \times A_n \\ |A| &= \textstyle\prod_{i=1}^{n} |A_i| \\ s' &\sim P(s' \mid s, a_1, \dots, a_n) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a joint action lists one action for every agent, so the number of joint actions is the product of the individual counts. The next state depends on all of the actions together. A single planner for n robots that each have five moves (stay, or one of four directions) must consider 5n joint actions at every step.
Say it“The joint action a is the tuple a-one to a-n; the number of joint actions is the product of each agent’s action count; the next state is drawn given the state and all n actions.”
\[ \begin{aligned} U_i(\pi_i, \pi_{-i}) &= \mathbb E\Big[\textstyle\sum_t \gamma^t R_i(s_t, \mathbf a_t) \\ &\qquad \Big|\ \pi_i,\ \pi_{-i}\Big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: agent i’s expected return depends on its own policy πi and on everyone else’s, written π−i. With one decision maker there is a single objective to maximise over all the policies at once. With many, each agent controls only its own πi, and its best choice can change whenever π−i does. Equilibrium concepts are the way out of that circle.

Joint plans, conventions and flocks

Multiagent planning extends single-agent planning in two ways. First, a plan becomes a joint plan: actions for each agent, some of which run at the same time. Take doubles tennis. You and your partner must return the ball. One joint plan has you move to the net while your partner stays at the baseline and hits the ball; another swaps the roles. Second, concurrency matters. Some actions must happen together (two robots lifting a table from either end) and some must not (both partners swinging at the same ball). A planner records this with concurrent action constraints attached to its action schemas: this action requires, or forbids, a particular action by another agent at the same moment.

Even when every agent shares the goal, two equally good joint plans create a coordination problem. If you both go to the net, the ball sails past you. Agents settle on one plan in a few ways: a convention fixed in advance (drive on the left; whoever is nearer the net takes it), a social law that rules out conflicts for a whole population, communication (“mine!”), or plan recognition, inferring a partner’s plan from its first moves and fitting in around it. Coordinated behaviour can also emerge from local rules with nobody in charge. The classic example is a flock: in 1987 Craig Reynolds showed that simulated birds, which he called boids, form convincing flocks when each one obeys three local rules, separation, alignment and cohesion. Turn the rules up and down and watch order appear and vanish.

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta\mathbf v_i &= w_s \textstyle\sum_{j \in S_i} \frac{\mathbf x_i - \mathbf x_j}{\Vert \mathbf x_i - \mathbf x_j\Vert^2} \\ &\quad + w_a\big(\bar{\mathbf v}_{N_i} - \mathbf v_i\big) \\ &\quad + w_c\big(\bar{\mathbf x}_{N_i} - \mathbf x_i\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: boid i sees only its neighbours Ni, those within a fixed radius, and Si, the few that are very close. Separation pushes it away from the very close ones, harder the closer they are. Alignment turns its velocity towards the neighbours’ average velocity. Cohesion steers it towards their average position. Here the velocity changes by Δvi times a small time step, plus a little random jitter, and speeds are kept within a range. No boid knows the flock exists.
Say it“The change in boid i’s velocity is w-s times the push away from its close neighbours, plus w-a times the gap between their mean velocity and its own, plus w-c times the gap between their centre and its position.”
\[ \phi = \frac{1}{N}\,\Big\Vert \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{\mathbf v_i}{\Vert\mathbf v_i\Vert}\Big\Vert \]
Read it as: the polarisation φ averages every boid’s heading as a unit arrow and measures the length of the result. If all fly the same way, φ = 1. If headings are random, the arrows cancel and φ is small, typically about 1/√N, which is 0.12 for the 70 boids here. It is a one-number summary of how coordinated the group is.
Say it“Phi is one over N times the length of the sum of every boid’s unit heading.”

Russell & Norvig 18.2 · 18.2.1

Normal-form games: dominance and Nash equilibrium

Non-cooperative game theory models agents that each pursue their own utility and cannot make binding agreements. The simplest model is a game with a single move, made by all players at once. Its normal form (or strategic form) has three parts: the players; for each player a set of actions, here called pure strategies; and a payoff function giving every player’s utility for each combination of choices, called a strategy profile. For two players it fits in a table. The row player picks a row, the column player a column, and each cell holds the pair (row’s payoff, column’s payoff).

The best-known example is the prisoner’s dilemma. Two suspects, Asha and Ben, are questioned separately. Each can stay silent, which protects the other, or testify against them. Payoffs here are points, and more is better: both silent earns 3 each, both testifying earns 1 each, and a lone testifier earns 5 while the silent partner gets 0. Whatever Ben does, Asha does better by testifying, since 5 > 3 and 1 > 0. A strategy that does better than another against every possible choice of the others strictly dominates it, and a rational player never uses a strictly dominated strategy. So both testify and get 1 each, although both would prefer the 3 each they could have had. An outcome is Pareto optimal if no other outcome makes someone better off without making someone worse off. (Testify, testify) is Pareto dominated by (silent, silent), and that gap between what is rational for each and what is good for both is the dilemma.

Most games have no dominant strategies. The central idea is then the Nash equilibrium: a profile in which every player’s strategy is a best response to the others’, so that no one can gain by changing strategy alone. In the table, the row player’s best reply to each column is underlined in teal and the column player’s best reply to each row in amber; a cell marked in both colours is a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium. Dominance still helps. Deleting strictly dominated strategies one at a time, called iterated elimination, never removes a Nash equilibrium, and sometimes leaves just one cell. Pick a game, edit any payoff, and step through the eliminations.

\[ \begin{aligned} &s_i \text{ strictly dominates } s_i' \iff \\ &u_i(s_i, s_{-i}) > u_i(s_i', s_{-i}) \\ &\text{for all } s_{-i} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: s−i stands for the choices of everyone except player i. Strategy si strictly dominates si′ if it gives i a higher payoff whatever the others do. Weak dominance asks only for ≥ everywhere and > somewhere. A strategy that dominates every other is a dominant strategy, and if every player has one, the profile is a dominant-strategy equilibrium.
Say it“s-i strictly dominates s-i-prime if u-i of s-i and s-minus-i is greater than u-i of s-i-prime and s-minus-i, for every s-minus-i.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &s^* = (s_1^*, \dots, s_n^*) \text{ is a} \\ &\text{Nash equilibrium} \iff \\ &u_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \ge u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*) \\ &\text{for all } i \text{ and all } s_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: hold everyone else at s*−i; then no alternative si earns player i more than si*. Each strategy is a best response to the rest, so the profile is stable against one player changing at a time. Every dominant-strategy equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium, but not the other way round, and a game can have several Nash equilibria or, in pure strategies, none.
Say it“s-star is a Nash equilibrium if, for every player i and every alternative s-i, u-i of s-i-star and s-minus-i-star is at least u-i of s-i and s-minus-i-star.”

Mixed strategies, minimax and the penalty kick

Some games have no pure equilibrium at all. In the penalty kick, if the keeper always dives to the kicker’s natural side the kicker should go the other way; if the kicker always goes the other way, so should the keeper; and so on round the cycle. The way out is to randomise. A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over pure strategies: kick to your natural side with probability p. In 1950 John Nash proved that every game with finitely many players and pure strategies has at least one equilibrium once mixed strategies are allowed.

How do you find one? A player who is willing to randomise between two actions must be indifferent between them, because if one paid more she would play it every time. So in a mixed equilibrium each player chooses her mix to make the other player indifferent. That has a surprising consequence: your equilibrium mix depends only on your opponent’s payoffs. For two-player zero-sum games, in which one player’s gain is the other’s loss, there is more. John von Neumann’s minimax theorem (1928) says that the payoff the row player can guarantee, maximising over her mix the worst case over the column player’s, equals the cap the column player can enforce by minimising the best case. That common number is the value of the game, and a linear program finds it. Two-finger Morra is the textbook example: each player shows one or two fingers; if the total is even, Even wins that many points from Odd, and if it is odd, Odd wins them from Even. Its value is −1/12, so the game slightly favours Odd. Drag the point around the square and follow the arrows to the equilibrium.

\[ \begin{aligned} u_1(p,q) &= pq\,u_{11} + p(1-q)\,u_{12} \\ &\quad + (1-p)q\,u_{21} \\ &\quad + (1-p)(1-q)\,u_{22} \\ q^* &= \frac{u_{22} - u_{12}}{u_{11} - u_{12} - u_{21} + u_{22}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: p is the row player’s probability of her first row, q the column player’s probability of his first column, and uab the row player’s payoff in cell (a, b). Her expected payoff averages the four cells. Setting her two rows’ expected payoffs equal and solving for q gives q*, the column mix that leaves her indifferent. Her own mix p* comes from the same calculation with the column player’s payoffs.
Say it“q-star is u-two-two minus u-one-two, over u-one-one minus u-one-two minus u-two-one plus u-two-two.”
\[ \begin{aligned} v &= \max_{\mathbf p} \min_{\mathbf q}\ \mathbf p^\top U \mathbf q \\ &= \min_{\mathbf q} \max_{\mathbf p}\ \mathbf p^\top U \mathbf q \\[4pt] &\text{LP: maximise } v \text{ subject to} \\ &\textstyle\sum_a p_a U_{ab} \ge v \ \text{ for every } b, \\ &\textstyle\sum_a p_a = 1, \ \ p_a \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: in a zero-sum game U holds the row player’s payoffs and the column player receives −U. Against a fixed mix p the column player’s best reply is always one of his pure strategies, so the row player’s guaranteed payoff is the smallest entry of p⊤U: the lower envelope in the right-hand plot. Maximising it is a linear program with one constraint per column. A game whose payoffs always add to the same constant, like goal and save probabilities, behaves exactly like a zero-sum game.
Say it“The value v is the max over p of the min over q of p-transpose U q, which equals the min over q of the max over p.”

Russell & Norvig 18.2.2

Social welfare: good for whom?

An equilibrium describes what self-interested agents will do. Social welfare asks how good the result is for the group, which matters whenever we design the agents, or the rules they play by. There are several standards. Pareto optimality is the weakest: no one can be made better off without someone being made worse off. Most games have many Pareto optimal outcomes, which together form the Pareto frontier. Utilitarian social welfare adds everyone’s utility and prefers the largest total, however unequally it is shared. Egalitarian social welfare looks at the worst-off agent and prefers the outcome in which that agent does best. Inequality itself can be measured, for example by the Gini coefficient, which is 0 when everyone gets the same.

The prisoner’s dilemma shows that equilibrium and welfare can disagree completely: its one equilibrium is the only outcome that is not Pareto optimal. A standard measure of the damage is the price of anarchy: the best achievable welfare divided by the welfare of the worst equilibrium (for costs, the worst equilibrium cost divided by the best). The plane below shows every pure outcome as a point (u1, u2). Randomising jointly over outcomes, say by tossing a coin both players can see, reaches anything in the shaded region between them. Pick a game and a welfare standard, then compare the star with the equilibria.

\[ \begin{aligned} W_{\text{util}}(o) &= \textstyle\sum_i u_i(o) \\ W_{\text{egal}}(o) &= \min_i u_i(o) \\ \text{PoA} &= \frac{\max_{o} W(o)}{\min_{o \in \text{NE}} W(o)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: o is an outcome. Utilitarian welfare is the total utility; egalitarian welfare is the utility of the worst-off player. The price of anarchy compares the best outcome anyone could arrange with the worst outcome that self-interest can lock in. It equals 1 when every equilibrium is socially optimal, and it is only meaningful when welfare is positive.
Say it“Utilitarian welfare is the sum of the utilities; egalitarian welfare is the smallest; the price of anarchy is the best welfare over the worst equilibrium’s welfare.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &o \succ_P o' \iff \\ &u_i(o) \ge u_i(o') \ \forall i, \\ &\text{with} > \text{for some } i \\ &G = \frac{\sum_{i}\sum_{j} |u_i - u_j|}{2 n^2 \bar u} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: o Pareto dominates o′ if nobody is worse off under o and somebody is better off. An outcome that nothing Pareto dominates is Pareto optimal. The Gini coefficient G averages the gaps between every pair of the n players’ utilities, scaled by the mean ū; for positive utilities it runs from 0 (equal shares) towards 1 (one player has everything).
Say it“o Pareto dominates o-prime if every u-i of o is at least u-i of o-prime and one is strictly greater; G is the sum of all pairwise gaps over two n-squared u-bar.”

Selfish routing: the price of anarchy can grow without limit

The same idea applies to traffic. In Pigou’s example, a unit of traffic (think of many small drivers) goes from A to B by one of two roads. The first always takes one hour. The second takes xd hours when a share x of the traffic uses it, so it is quick when empty and never slower than the first. Every driver does best on the second road, so in equilibrium everyone takes it and every journey takes an hour. A planner could send some drivers the slow way and make the average journey shorter. With d = 1 the best split halves the traffic, the average falls to 45 minutes, and the price of anarchy is 4/3. Make the second road’s congestion steeper with d and see how much selfishness costs.

\[ \begin{aligned} C(x) &= x \cdot x^{d} + (1-x)\cdot 1 \\ x_{\text{opt}} &= (d+1)^{-1/d} \\ \text{PoA} &= \frac{C(1)}{C(x_{\text{opt}})} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: C(x) is the average journey time when a share x drives on the second road: that share takes xd hours each, the rest take an hour. Setting the derivative (d+1)xd − 1 to zero gives the planner’s best share xopt. The equilibrium has x = 1 and C(1) = 1, so the price of anarchy is 1 / C(xopt), which grows without bound as d increases.
Say it“C of x is x times x-to-the-d plus one minus x; x-opt is d-plus-one to the minus one-over-d; the price of anarchy is C of one over C of x-opt.”

Russell & Norvig 18.2.3

Repeated games: the prisoner’s dilemma, again and again

In a repeated game the same stage game is played round after round, and each player sees the history so far. A strategy is now a complete plan: what to do after every possible history. That changes the prisoner’s dilemma, because defecting today can be punished tomorrow. Write the stage payoffs as T (temptation: defect against a cooperator), R (reward for mutual cooperation), P (punishment for mutual defection) and S (sucker’s payoff), with T > R > P > S; this lab uses R = 3, P = 1, S = 0 and lets you set T.

Repetition does not help if the end is known. Suppose both players know there will be exactly 100 rounds. Round 100 is a one-shot dilemma, so both defect. Then nothing that happens in round 99 can be rewarded or punished later, so they defect there too, and the argument unravels back to round 1: backward induction predicts defection throughout. Cooperation becomes possible when the game might go on. Let δ be the probability that another round follows this one, so the expected number of rounds is 1/(1 − δ). Against grim trigger, which cooperates until the other player defects and then defects for ever, a defection gains T − R once and loses R − P in every round after. It pays only if δ is small. More generally the folk theorem says that with patient enough players almost any outcome giving each player more than the payoff she can guarantee herself can be held in place by the threat of punishment.

Which strategies do well in practice? Around 1980 Robert Axelrod ran computer tournaments in which submitted programs played the repeated dilemma against each other. The winner was one of the simplest entries, Anatol Rapoport’s tit for tat: cooperate first, then copy the opponent’s previous move. It is nice (never the first to defect), retaliatory, forgiving and easy to read. It never outscores the opponent in front of it, yet it does well on average. Its weakness is noise: one accidental defection between two tit-for-tat players echoes back and forth. Run the tournament, then add noise and watch the ranking change.

\[ \bar u_i = (1-\delta)\textstyle\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \delta^t\, u_i(\mathbf a_t) \]
Read it as: if the game continues after each round with probability δ, round t is reached with probability δt. The sum is player i’s expected total payoff, and multiplying by (1 − δ), one over the expected number of rounds, turns it into an average per round, on the same scale as the stage payoffs. The same formula describes a player who discounts future rounds by δ.
Say it“u-bar-i is one minus delta times the sum over t of delta to the t times u-i of the round-t actions.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{R}{1-\delta} &\ \ge\ T + \frac{\delta P}{1-\delta} \\ \iff \delta &\ \ge\ \delta^* = \frac{T - R}{T - P} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: on the left, the expected total for cooperating for ever with a grim-trigger partner. On the right, the total for defecting now: T once, then P in every later round, because the partner never cooperates again. Cooperation is a best response, and (grim, grim) a Nash equilibrium, whenever δ is at least δ*. With T = 5, R = 3, P = 1 that threshold is 0.5: a 50% chance of meeting again is enough.

Russell & Norvig 18.2.4 · perfect information

Sequential games: game trees and backward induction

Many interactions happen in turns. The extensive form describes them with a game tree, like the trees of adversarial search but more general: any number of players, a payoff for every player at each leaf rather than one score, and chance nodes where nature moves with known probabilities, such as a dealt card or a dice roll. When every player sees every earlier move, the game has perfect information, and it can be solved from the leaves up by backward induction: at each node the player to move picks the child that is best for her, given how the rest of the game will be played.

The result is a subgame perfect equilibrium: a strategy profile that is a Nash equilibrium of every subgame, not just of the whole game. The refinement matters because a plain Nash equilibrium can rest on threats that would never be carried out. In the entry game an incumbent firm threatens a price war if a rival enters its market. If the rival believes the threat, (stay out, fight) is a Nash equilibrium. But once the rival has entered, fighting hurts the incumbent too, so the threat is not credible, and backward induction throws it away. The centipede game shows how far the logic reaches. Two players take turns either to take the larger share of a growing pot or to pass it on. Backward induction says the first player takes at once, even though passing to the end would leave both better off. Any extensive-form game can be turned into a normal-form table whose pure strategies say what to do at every one of a player’s nodes, but that table grows exponentially with the tree. Step through backward induction, then lengthen the centipede.

\[ \begin{aligned} V(h) &= u(h) \ \ \text{if } h \text{ is a leaf} \\ V(h) &= V(ha^*) \ \ \text{at decisions, with} \\ a^* &= \arg\max_a V_{P(h)}(ha) \\ V(h) &= \textstyle\sum_a \Pr(a \mid h)\, V(ha) \\ &\qquad \text{at chance nodes} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: h is a history, a path from the root; ha is h followed by action a. V(h) is the vector of payoffs, one per player, that results from h when everyone plays by backward induction. At a decision node the player to move, P(h), chooses the child whose payoff to her is largest. At a chance node the children’s payoff vectors are averaged with nature’s probabilities.
Say it“V of h is the leaf payoff at a leaf; otherwise V of h-a-star, where a-star maximises the mover’s own entry of V of h-a; at chance nodes, the probability-weighted average.”
\[ |S_i| = \textstyle\prod_{h \in H_i} |A(h)| \]
Read it as: a pure strategy for player i picks one action at every node Hi where i moves, even nodes her own earlier choices make unreachable. So the number of pure strategies is a product over her nodes, exponential in their number, and the normal-form table has |S1| × |S2| cells. Backward induction visits each node once.
Say it“The number of player i’s pure strategies is the product, over the nodes where i moves, of the number of actions there.”

Russell & Norvig 18.2.4 · imperfect information

Hidden cards: information sets and Kuhn poker

In card games players do not see everything. The extensive form handles this with information sets: groups of nodes that the player to move cannot tell apart, because they differ only in things she has not seen. A strategy must choose the same action, or the same mix, at every node of an information set. Kuhn poker, which Harold Kuhn analysed in 1950, is the smallest interesting example. The deck has three cards: Jack, Queen and King. Each player puts one chip in the pot (the ante) and is dealt one card face down. Player 1 may check or bet one more chip. After a check, player 2 may check, which leads to a showdown, or bet. A player facing a bet may call, matching it for a showdown over the bigger pot, or fold and lose the ante. In a showdown the higher card wins. The game has 12 information sets: a player’s own card combined with the betting so far.

Even this game has 26 = 64 pure strategies per player in normal form. Larger imperfect-information games are solved in other ways. The sequence form describes a strategy by the probabilities of sequences of a player’s own actions, which keeps the problem linear in the size of the tree, so one linear program solves any two-player zero-sum game exactly. The method behind the programs that beat professional players at heads-up no-limit Texas hold’em, Libratus and DeepStack, builds on counterfactual regret minimisation (CFR). The program plays against itself over and over. At each information set it records how much better each action would have done than its current mix, weighting by the chance that the deal and the opponent’s play lead there, and then plays each action in proportion to its positive accumulated regret. Its average strategy converges to a Nash equilibrium. Run CFR and watch it discover bluffing.

\[ \begin{aligned} r^t(I,a) &= \pi_{-i}^{\sigma^t}(I) \\ &\quad\times \big[v_i^{\sigma^t}(I,a) - v_i^{\sigma^t}(I)\big] \\ R^T(I,a) &= \textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T} r^t(I,a) \\ \sigma^{T+1}(I,a) &= \frac{\max(R^T(I,a),\,0)}{\sum_b \max(R^T(I,b),\,0)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: I is an information set of player i, σt the strategies at iteration t. π−i(I) is the probability that the deal and the opponent’s moves lead to I. vi(I, a) is i’s expected payoff if she takes a at I, and vi(I) the payoff of her current mix there. Regrets accumulate over iterations, and regret matching plays actions in proportion to their positive regret (uniformly if none is positive). The average of the σt, weighted by i’s own chance of reaching I, converges to an equilibrium.
Say it“The regret of a at I is the opponent’s reach probability times the value of a minus the value of the current mix; the next strategy plays each action in proportion to its positive total regret.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\Pr(\text{bet} \mid J) = \alpha, \ \ \alpha \in [0, \tfrac13] \\ &\Pr(\text{bet} \mid K) = 3\alpha \\ &\Pr(\text{call a bet} \mid Q) = \alpha + \tfrac13 \\ &v_1 = -\tfrac{1}{18} \approx -0.0556 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Kuhn found a one-parameter family of equilibrium strategies for player 1. She bluffs with the Jack at some rate α, bets the King three times as often, never bets the Queen, and with the Queen calls a bet (after checking) with probability α + 1/3. Player 2’s equilibrium strategy is unique: with the King always bet and call, with the Queen call a bet a third of the time, with the Jack bet a third of the time after a check and never call. Every equilibrium gives player 1 −1/18 chip per hand: acting first is a disadvantage.
Say it“Player 1 bets the Jack with probability alpha and the King with three alpha, calls with the Queen with alpha plus a third, and expects to lose one eighteenth of a chip a hand.”

Uncertain payoffs and assistance games

So far every player has known the payoffs. A case that matters for AI is a player who does not know its own objective, because that objective is someone else’s. Suppose a robot, Robbie, exists to help a human, Harriet. Robbie’s payoff is Harriet’s utility, but only Harriet knows what her utility is. This is an assistance game: a two-player game of partial information in which both players share one payoff function and only the human knows its parameters. The game is cooperative, yet equilibrium reasoning still applies, because each player’s best action depends on how the other will interpret it.

Two consequences follow. First, Harriet’s actions become teaching. In equilibrium she may act differently from how she would act alone, because Robbie is watching to learn what she wants. Second, Robbie has a positive incentive to let Harriet switch it off. In the off-switch game, Robbie can take an action whose value U to Harriet is uncertain, switch itself off (value 0), or wait and let Harriet decide. A rational Harriet lets it go ahead only when U > 0, so waiting is worth E[max(U, 0)], which is never less than acting or switching off. The incentive shrinks as Robbie becomes more certain, or as Harriet’s decisions become less reliable, and a robot that believes it knows the objective exactly has no reason to defer at all. Change Robbie’s uncertainty and Harriet’s reliability, and see when waiting wins.

\[ \begin{aligned} V_{\text{act}} &= \mathbb E[U] = \mu, \quad V_{\text{off}} = 0 \\ V_{\text{wait}} &= (1-\varepsilon)\,\mathbb E[U^+] \\ &\quad + \varepsilon\,\mathbb E[U^-] \\ \mathbb E[U^+] &= \mu\,\Phi(\mu/\sigma) + \sigma\,\varphi(\mu/\sigma) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Robbie believes U is normal with mean μ and standard deviation σ. U+ = max(U, 0) and U− = min(U, 0). If Robbie waits, Harriet decides, making the wrong call with probability ε: she blocks a good action or allows a bad one. Φ and φ are the standard normal distribution and density. At ε = 0, Vwait = E[max(U, 0)] ≥ max(E[U], 0), because an average of maxima is never smaller than the maximum of averages.
Say it“Acting is worth mu, switching off is worth zero, and waiting is worth one minus epsilon times the expected positive part of U plus epsilon times the expected negative part.”
\[ \begin{aligned} u_H &= \theta \cdot \#\text{clips} \\ &\quad + (1-\theta) \cdot \#\text{staples} \\ \text{2 clips} &\ \text{ if } 92\,\theta > 51 \\ \text{2 staples} &\ \text{ if } 92\,(1-\theta) > 51 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Harriet values a paperclip at θ and a staple at 1 − θ. She makes two items, 2 clips, 2 staples, or one of each, and Robbie, who starts believing θ is uniform on [0, 1], watches and then makes 90 clips, 90 staples or 50 of each. If Robbie answers 2 clips with 90 clips, one of each with 50 of each, and 2 staples with 90 staples, then making 2 clips is worth 92θ to her, one of each is worth 51, and 2 staples 92(1 − θ). So she makes one of each whenever 41/92 < θ < 51/92, about 0.446 to 0.554.
Say it“Harriet’s utility is theta per clip plus one minus theta per staple; she makes two clips if ninety-two theta beats fifty-one, two staples if ninety-two times one minus theta does, and one of each otherwise.”

Real-world project

Project: do professional penalty takers play minimax?

A penalty kick is about as close to a textbook game as real life gets. There are two players and few actions; the moves are effectively simultaneous, because the ball arrives too quickly for a keeper to wait and see where it goes; the outcome is recorded; and the stakes are high enough that players think hard about it. Economists have used penalties to test minimax theory on real data (Chiappori, Levitt & Groseclose, 2002; Palacios-Huerta, 2003). This project repeats their two main tests, and then fits a game, on a free data set.

The data are the Wyscout match events published by Pappalardo and colleagues (2019) under a CC BY 4.0 licence: the recorded match events (passes, shots, duels and so on) of every match in the 2017/18 seasons of the top divisions in England, France, Germany, Italy and Spain, plus the 2016 European Championship and the 2018 World Cup. They contain 658 penalty kicks. One, tagged as struck with the head, is dropped, which leaves 657 kicks from 512 matches by 316 kickers, 76 of them in shoot-outs. For each kick the data record the foot used, where the ball went and whether it was scored. Wyscout tags the goal mouth with zones such as “goal low left” or “out high right”, and the left/right labels are from the kicker’s point of view. (For the 68 World Cup kicks this was checked against a second, independently collected event feed: no kick was mirrored, and the two feeds disagree only on nine kicks near the middle, which one calls central and the other does not.) Following the literature, directions are recoded relative to the kicking foot: a right-footer’s natural side is his left, the side that is easier to reach with the inside of the right foot, and a left-footer’s is his right. A kick on target that was not scored counts as saved; one wide, high or off the post counts as missed.

Two predictions of a mixed equilibrium hold whatever the exact payoffs are. (1) Every direction a kicker uses must score equally often; otherwise he should shift towards the better one. (2) A kicker’s choices must be unpredictable, so his direction should not depend on his previous penalty. A third check needs the payoffs themselves, so part 3 fits a 2 × 2 game to the side kicks and compares its equilibrium with what the players actually did.

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= \begin{pmatrix} a_N(1-s) & a_N \\ a_O & a_O(1-s) \end{pmatrix} \\ k^* &= \frac{a_O}{a_N + a_O} \\ g^* &= \frac{a_N - a_O + a_O s}{s\,(a_N + a_O)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: A holds goal probabilities. Its rows are the kicker’s choices, natural side N or opposite side O; its columns are the keeper’s dive, N or O. aN and aO are the shares of kicks to each side that are on target. A kick on target beats a keeper who dives the wrong way; a keeper who guesses right saves it with probability s. Dives are not in the data, so s is a slider. Its default is the value at which the observed saves fit a keeper who always dives one way or the other: s = SN + SO, the saved shares of on-target kicks to each side. k* is the kicker’s equilibrium chance of choosing N, which does not depend on s; g* is the keeper’s chance of diving N.
Say it“k-star is a-O over a-N plus a-O; g-star is a-N minus a-O plus a-O s, all over s times a-N plus a-O.”

The same computation in Python

import json, numpy as np
from scipy.stats import chi2_contingency, norm
J = json.load(open("x102-pen.json")); d = np.array(J["d"])
comp, so, foot, side, rel, out, kid = d.T          # rel: 0 natural side, 1 centre, 2 opposite; out: 0 goal, 1 saved, 2 missed
print(f"{len(d)} penalties from {J['matches']} matches, {kid.max() + 1} kickers, {so.sum()} in shootouts, right-footed share {foot.mean():.4f}")
def table(mask):                                   # rows natural / centre / opposite; columns goal / saved / missed
    return np.array([[np.sum(mask & (rel == r) & (out == o)) for o in range(3)] for r in range(3)])

# Test 1, indifference: in a mixed equilibrium every direction the kicker uses must score equally often
for name, mask in [("all", so >= 0), ("in play", so == 0), ("shootouts", so == 1)]:
    T = table(mask); n = T.sum(1)
    chi2, p, dof, _ = chi2_contingency(np.c_[T[:, 0], T[:, 1:].sum(1)], correction=False)
    print(f"{name:9s} n={T.sum():3d}  mix N/C/O {np.round(n / n.sum(), 4)}  goal rate N/C/O {np.round(T[:, 0] / n, 4)}  "
          f"overall {T[:, 0].sum() / T.sum():.4f}  chi2 = {chi2:.4f}, dof {dof}, p = {p:.4f}")
print("counts (goal, saved, missed), rows natural / centre / opposite:", table(so >= 0).tolist())

# Test 2, no pattern: a kicker's direction should not depend on his previous penalty's direction
prev, tr = {}, np.zeros((3, 3), int)
for r, k in zip(rel, kid):                          # rows are already in date order
    if k in prev: tr[prev[k], r] += 1
    prev[k] = r
chi2, p, dof, E = chi2_contingency(tr, correction=False)
print(f"transitions previous -> next: {tr.tolist()}  pairs {tr.sum()}")
print(f"same direction as last time {np.trace(tr) / tr.sum():.4f}, expected if independent {np.trace(E) / tr.sum():.4f}; chi2 = {chi2:.4f}, dof {dof}, p = {p:.4f}")

# A 2x2 game fitted to the side kicks: kick natural (N) or opposite (O); keeper dives N or O
T = table(so >= 0); nN, nO = T[0].sum(), T[2].sum()
aN, aO = T[0, :2].sum() / nN, T[2, :2].sum() / nO   # on-target rate of each side
SN, SO = T[0, 1] / T[0, :2].sum(), T[2, 1] / T[2, :2].sum()   # share of on-target kicks that were saved
def game(s):
    A = np.array([[aN * (1 - s), aN], [aO, aO * (1 - s)]])   # goal probability; rows kick N/O, columns dive N/O
    den = A[0, 0] - A[0, 1] - A[1, 0] + A[1, 1]
    k, g = (A[1, 1] - A[1, 0]) / den, (A[1, 1] - A[0, 1]) / den   # each mixes to make the OTHER indifferent
    return A, k, g, g * A[0, 0] + (1 - g) * A[0, 1]
s0 = SN + SO                                         # save rate when the keeper guesses right, if he always dives
print(f"on target N {aN:.4f}, O {aO:.4f}; saved share N {SN:.4f}, O {SO:.4f}; calibrated s = {s0:.4f}")
kobs, gobs = nN / (nN + nO), SN / (SN + SO)
for s in (s0, 0.6):
    A, k, g, v = game(s)
    print(f"s = {s:.4f}: A = {np.round(A, 4).tolist()}")
    print(f"   kicker P(N): equilibrium {k:.4f}, observed {kobs:.4f};  keeper P(N): equilibrium {g:.4f}, observed {gobs:.4f};  "
          f"value {v:.4f} vs observed goal rate N {T[0, 0] / nN:.4f}, O {T[2, 0] / nO:.4f}")
A, k, g, v = game(s0); z = (kobs - k) / np.sqrt(k * (1 - k) / (nN + nO))
print(f"kicker mix test: z = {z:.3f}, two-sided p = {2 * norm.sf(abs(z)):.5f} on {nN + nO} side kicks")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • With one decision maker, many bodies are just a bigger planning problem over joint actions; with several decision makers, each one’s best plan depends on the others’, and coordination needs conventions, social laws, communication or plan recognition.
  • A normal-form game lists players, strategies and payoffs. Strictly dominated strategies can be deleted; a Nash equilibrium is a profile where everyone is already best-responding, and with mixed strategies every finite game has one.
  • In a mixed equilibrium each player randomises to make the other indifferent; in two-player zero-sum games maximin equals minimax, and a linear program finds the value.
  • Equilibria need not be good for anyone: Pareto optimality, utilitarian and egalitarian welfare judge outcomes, and the price of anarchy measures what selfishness costs. Repetition with an uncertain end lets threats sustain cooperation, as in tit for tat and the folk theorem.
  • Game trees are solved by backward induction (subgame perfection removes empty threats); hidden information needs information sets, the sequence form or CFR. When a robot is unsure of its human’s payoffs, the assistance game rewards teaching by the human and deference by the robot.

Check your understanding

A warehouse controller drives 20 robots. Is that a game?

No. There is one decision maker choosing joint actions to maximise one objective, which is multibody planning. It becomes a game, or at least a coordination problem, only when the robots choose separately: then each must predict the others, and if their objectives differ, reason about what the others want.

In the prisoner’s dilemma, why is (testify, testify) a Nash equilibrium even though both players prefer (silent, silent)?

At (testify, testify) a player who switches alone to silent drops from 1 to 0, so neither wants to change alone. (Silent, silent) is better for both, but each could gain by switching alone to testifying (3 to 5), so it is not an equilibrium. Nash equilibrium is about unilateral changes, not joint ones.

A kicker becomes more accurate on his natural side. In the equilibrium of the fitted penalty game, should he kick there more or less often?

Less. His mix is set to make the keeper indifferent, so it depends on the keeper’s payoffs: k* = aO / (aN + aO) falls as aN rises. What changes in his favour is the keeper’s mix, which shifts towards the natural side, and the value of the game, which rises.

Why does backward induction predict defection in every round of a 100-round prisoner’s dilemma, and what breaks the argument?

The last round is a one-shot game, so both defect; given that, nothing in round 99 affects the future, so both defect there too, and so on back to round 1. The argument needs a known last round. If another round follows with probability δ and δ ≥ (T − R)/(T − P), grim trigger or tit for tat can sustain cooperation as an equilibrium.

In the off-switch game, why does a rational Harriet give Robbie an incentive to stay switchable, and when does that incentive disappear?

Waiting lets Harriet filter out the bad cases, so waiting is worth E[max(U, 0)], which is at least max(E[U], 0). The gain vanishes when Robbie is certain (σ → 0), because then there is nothing to learn, and it can turn negative when Harriet’s decisions are unreliable enough.
