Fine-tuning & LoRA

Teaching a trained model something new, without rebuilding it

Pretraining is the slow, expensive part. Fine-tuning is the cheap part that turns a general model into a specialist. The idea

Why fine-tune? A head start

Suppose you want a model of Shakespeare but have only a little of his text. Training from scratch means learning everything from nothing: which letters follow which, where spaces go, what a word is. A model pretrained on Austen already knows most of that, because both write English. It only has to learn what is different. This is transfer, the idea behind ULMFiT (Howard & Ruder, 2018) and every modern LLM: pretrain once, on a lot of general text, then adapt cheaply. Below, two identical networks get the same 400 training steps on the same Shakespeare. One starts from random weights, the other from the Austen model. Pick how much Shakespeare they get and compare.

\[ \begin{aligned} L(\theta) &= -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log_2 p_\theta\big(x_t \mid c_t\big) \\ c_t &= x_{t-12},\dots,x_{t-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the model sees the previous 12 characters, ct, and gives a probability to each of the 37 possible next ones. L is the average surprise, in bits per character, at the character that really came next, on text it never trained on. Zero would be perfect foresight; guessing uniformly among 37 symbols scores log₂37 = 5.21 bits. Lower is better.
Say it“L of theta is minus the average, over the text, of log base two of the probability the model gave the true next character.”

Supervised fine-tuning

Keep training, on new text

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) sounds grander than it is. It is the same next-character loss and the same optimiser, started from the pretrained weights instead of random ones, and fed with the new data. All 23,777 weights are free to move. We watch two held-out scores at once: Shakespeare, which we are teaching, and Austen, which we are not. Train in bursts of 200 steps, read the samples, and try a larger learning rate.

\[ \begin{aligned} \theta_0 &= \theta_{\text{pre}} \\ \theta_{k+1} &= \theta_k - \eta\, \nabla_\theta L_{\text{new}}(\theta_k) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: start where pretraining stopped, then step downhill on the loss of the new data only. η is the learning rate. (Each step here is an Adam step on a batch of 32 windows of Shakespeare; Adam rescales the gradient per weight but the idea is the same.) Notice what is missing: nothing in this objective mentions Austen, so nothing protects what the model knew about her.
Say it“Theta-nought is the pretrained weights; each step subtracts eta times the gradient of the new data’s loss.”

Instruction tuning

Instruction tuning: the format is the lesson

A pretrained model continues text. Ask it a question and it may answer, or it may write three more questions, because both happen on the web. Instruction tuning is SFT on pairs of (instruction, response), each written into a fixed template, so the model learns the shape of a conversation: the prompt ends, an answer begins, the answer stops. It needs surprisingly little data. FLAN (Wei et al., 2022) instruction-tuned a 137-billion-parameter model on more than 60 NLP tasks phrased as instructions; it beat zero-shot GPT-3 (175B) on 20 of 25 tasks. LIMA (Zhou et al., 2023) fine-tuned a 65B LLaMa on just 1,000 carefully curated examples.

One detail matters: the loss is usually counted only on the response. The model is meant to learn to answer, not to write the user’s questions. (This is a choice, not a law: Shi et al., 2024, found that also training on the instruction can help when instructions are long and answers short.) Below, the Austen base model scores each character of an example; our model’s tokens are single lower-case characters, but the mask works the same way for subword tokens. Switch the mask and see which characters would teach the model.

\[ \begin{aligned} L &= -\frac{\sum_t m_t \log_2 p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{\lt t})}{\sum_t m_t} \\ m_t &= \begin{cases} 1 & x_t \in \text{response} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same next-token surprise as before, but each position carries a weight mt. Prompt and template positions get 0, so their surprise adds nothing to the loss and sends no gradient. The average is taken over the counted positions only. Real chat templates also end the response with a special end-of-turn token; it is part of the response, so it is trained, and that is how a model learns to stop.
Say it“L is the masked sum of surprises over the number of positions that count; m-t is one on the response and zero elsewhere.”

LoRA

LoRA: freeze W, learn a thin update

Full fine-tuning stores a complete new copy of every weight. For a 175-billion-parameter model that is 350 GB per task. Hu et al. (2021) bet that the change a fine-tune makes does not need all those degrees of freedom: that the update ΔW to each weight matrix has a low “intrinsic rank”. So they write the change as a product of two thin matrices, ΔW = BA, freeze the pretrained W0, and train only A and B. This is Low-Rank Adaptation, LoRA.

How low-rank is a real update? Below is the actual change that full fine-tuning (2,000 steps, done offline) made to our model’s first layer, a 128 × 144 matrix. The page computes its singular value decomposition and keeps only the strongest r directions. Slide the rank and watch both the picture and the loss.

\[ \begin{aligned} h &= W_0\,x + \tfrac{\alpha}{r}\,B A\,x \\ B &\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\ \ A \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times k} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the frozen layer W0 (d outputs, k inputs) still does its job; a side path squeezes x down to r numbers with A and expands them back with B. Training touches only A and B: r(d + k) numbers instead of dk. The factor α/r keeps the size of the update steady when you change r (the paper fixes α and does not tune it; we use α = 4).
Say it“h is W-nought x plus alpha over r times B A x, with B tall and thin and A short and wide.”
\[ \frac{\lVert \Delta W - \Delta W_r \rVert_F^2}{\lVert \Delta W \rVert_F^2} = \frac{\sum_{i>r} \sigma_i^2}{\sum_{i} \sigma_i^2} \]
Read it as: write ΔW = Σ σi uivi⊤ (its SVD). Keeping the top r terms gives ΔWr, the best rank-r approximation there is (Eckart & Young, 1936), and the share of the update it misses is the share of squared singular values left out.
Say it“The relative squared error of the rank-r approximation is the sum of the left-out sigma squareds over the sum of all of them.”

Free at inference

Start at zero, merge at the end

Two small design choices make LoRA practical. First, B starts at zero (A is random). Then BA = 0, so before the first step the adapted model is the pretrained model, exactly, and training begins from everything it knows. Second, matrix products are associative: once training ends you can compute W = W0 + (α/r)BA once and discard A and B. The merged model has the same shape and speed as the original, which adapters that add new layers cannot claim. Or keep one copy of W0 and swap small adapters per task: for GPT-3 the paper reports a checkpoint of about 35 MB instead of 350 GB, so 100 adapted models fit in about 354 GB rather than 35 TB. Type a context and compare the three ways of computing the next character.

\[ \begin{aligned} \underbrace{\big(W_0 + s\,BA\big)\,x}_{\text{merged}} &= \underbrace{W_0\,x + s\,B\,(A\,x)}_{\text{side path}} \\ s &= t\cdot\frac{\alpha}{r} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: merging is just doing the multiplication in a different order, so both routes give the same answer up to rounding. The side path costs r(d + k) extra multiply-adds per token for each adapted matrix; the merged route costs nothing extra. t is the same strength dial as in the hero: t = 1 is the adapter as trained, t = 0 switches it off.
Say it“W-nought plus s B A, times x, equals W-nought x plus s B times A x.”

The family

Adapters, prefixes and prompts: other ways to train less

LoRA belongs to a family of parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods. Adapters (Houlsby et al., 2019) insert two small bottleneck networks into every transformer layer, d → m → d; on GLUE they came within 0.4% of full fine-tuning while adding 3.6% parameters per task. Prefix tuning (Li & Liang, 2021) learns p “virtual tokens” of keys and values in every layer, about 0.1% of the parameters. Prompt tuning (Lester et al., 2021) learns only p input embeddings, and becomes competitive with full tuning once models pass billions of parameters. QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) trains LoRA adapters on top of a frozen 4-bit copy of the model, enough to fine-tune a 65B model on one 48 GB GPU. Memory is the other half of the story: training with Adam in mixed precision keeps about 16 bytes per trainable parameter (Rajbhandari et al., 2020), while a frozen weight needs only its 2 bytes. Pick a model and compare.

\[ \begin{aligned} N_{\text{LoRA}} &= L \cdot n_W \cdot r\,(d + d) \\ N_{\text{adapter}} &= L \cdot 2\,(2dm + d + m) \\ N_{\text{prefix}} &= L \cdot 2\,p\,d \\ N_{\text{prompt}} &= p\,d \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: L layers of width d. LoRA adapts nW of the d × d attention matrices per layer. An adapter is a down-projection d×m and an up-projection m×d with biases, two per layer. A prefix is p key vectors and p value vectors per layer; a soft prompt is p vectors at the input only.
Say it“LoRA trains layers times matrices times r times two d; adapters, layers times two times two d m plus d plus m.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{memory} \approx{} &16\ \text{B} \times N_{\text{trainable}} \\ {}+{} &2\ \text{B} \times N_{\text{frozen}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each trainable weight carries a 16-bit copy, a 16-bit gradient, a 32-bit master copy and two 32-bit Adam moments: 2 + 2 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 16 bytes. A frozen weight is just its 16-bit value. Activations come on top and depend on batch and sequence length, so this is a floor, not a forecast.
Say it“Memory is about sixteen bytes per trainable weight plus two bytes per frozen one.”

The price

Catastrophic forgetting

McCloskey & Cohen (1989) noticed that a network trained on a second task can lose the first one almost completely; they called it catastrophic interference, now usually catastrophic forgetting. You saw it above: the Austen score rose while the Shakespeare score fell. There are three classic remedies. Replay: keep mixing in a little of the old data (Robins, 1995). Anchoring: penalise distance from the pretrained weights, as in L2-SP (Li et al., 2018) or EWC, which weights each parameter by how much the old task needs it (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017). Restricting the change: LoRA can only move the weights along a few directions, and Biderman et al. (2024) found that it “learns less and forgets less” than full fine-tuning. Each run below draws its path in the plane of new skill against old skill. Run each remedy and see where it ends.

\[ F = L_{\text{old}}(\theta_T) - L_{\text{old}}(\theta_0) \]
Read it as: forgetting is how much worse the model has become on held-out text of the old domain, from before fine-tuning (θ0) to after T steps (θT). Measured in bits per character, like the losses themselves.
Say it“Forgetting is the old-domain loss after fine-tuning minus the old-domain loss before.”
\[ \begin{aligned} L(\theta) = {}&(1-\rho)\,L_{\text{new}}(\theta) + \rho\,L_{\text{old}}(\theta) \\ &+ \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\,\lVert \theta - \theta_0 \rVert^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: replay sets ρ, the share of each batch drawn from the old data (in expectation); anchoring sets λ, the pull back toward the pretrained weights θ0. Plain fine-tuning is ρ = 0, λ = 0.
Say it“The loss is one-minus-rho times the new loss, plus rho times the old loss, plus lambda over two times the squared distance from the start.”

Real-world project

Project: teaching an Austen model Shakespeare

Two real public-domain texts. Pride and Prejudice (Austen, 1813; Project Gutenberg eBook #1342): 682,057 characters after cleaning. Tiny Shakespeare (Karpathy, 2015, from his char-rnn repository): 1,115,394 characters of dialogue from the plays. Both are lower-cased and reduced to 37 symbols: letters, space, line break and nine punctuation marks. The model reads 12 characters, embeds each in 12 numbers, passes the 144 through a tanh layer of 128 units and scores 37 next characters: 23,777 weights. It was pretrained offline in numpy on the first 90% of the novel (30,000 Adam steps). In your browser it is now fine-tuned on 100,000 characters of Shakespeare (ten 10,000-character slices of the first 90% of the plays), and scored on 2,000 held-out characters of each text, taken from the last 10%. Every run sees the same 500 batches of 32 windows, so the only difference is what is allowed to change: all the weights, or LoRA adapters of rank r on both weight matrices.

      "  shakespeare %.6f  austen %.6f" % evf(m0))

    m = base()
    if r: m.add_lora(r, alpha=4, seed=100 + r)         # A ~ N(0, 1/k), B = 0
    finetune(m, SH, steps=500, lr=3e-3 if r else 1e-3, batch=32, seed=11, mode="lora" if r else "full")
    n = r * (144 + 128) + r * (128 + 37) if r else 23777

h = hero(); L = h.lora; s = L["s"]                     # the rank-16 adapter from the hero
X, Y = ES[0][:64], ES[1][:64]
e = h.E[X].reshape(len(X), -1)
merged = e @ (h.W1 + s * L["B1"] @ L["A1"]).T
side = e @ h.W1.T + s * (e @ L["A1"].T) @ L["B1"].T
print("merged vs side path: max |diff| = %.1e" % np.abs(merged - side).max())

loss, g = h.grads(X, Y)                                # g["W1"] = dL/dW_merged
dA1, dB1 = s * L["B1"].T @ g["W1"], s * g["W1"] @ L["A1"].T
for name, M, i, j, an in (("A1", L["A1"], 0, 0, dA1[0, 0]), ("B1", L["B1"], 0, 0, dB1[0, 0])):

The page and numpy run the same algorithm on the same numbers: the same rounded weights, the same text, the same mulberry32 random stream for batches and for the initial A, and the same Adam. Both work in 64-bit floating point; they differ only in the order of additions, so the losses agree to about 10−12 bits. This is a scaled-down model: real LoRA adapts transformer attention matrices with d in the thousands, where the ratio r(d + k)/dk is far smaller than in our 128 × 144 layer. And 500 steps is a short fine-tune; longer training lowers Shakespeare further and raises the forgetting.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Fine-tuning is just more training: the same loss and optimiser, started from pretrained weights, on the data you care about. The head start is worth far more than the new data alone.
  • Instruction tuning is SFT on (instruction, response) pairs in a fixed template; the loss is usually masked to the response, so the model learns to answer rather than to ask.
  • LoRA freezes W0 and learns ΔW = BA with r(d + k) numbers instead of dk; B = 0 at the start, so training begins from the pretrained model exactly.
  • After training, W0 + (α/r)BA can be merged into one matrix: no extra latency. Or keep one base model and swap megabyte-sized adapters per task.
  • Training on new data alone makes a model forget the old; replay, anchoring to θ0 and low-rank updates all trade a little new skill for a lot less forgetting.

Check your understanding

A LoRA layer has d = 4096, k = 4096 and r = 8. How many trainable numbers does it add, and what fraction of the full matrix is that?

r(d + k) = 8 × 8,192 = 65,536, against dk = 16,777,216: about 0.39%. The fraction is r(d+k)/dk = r(1/k + 1/d), so it shrinks as the layers get wider.

Why is B initialised to zero rather than, like A, at random?

So that BA = 0 at the start and the adapted model is exactly the pretrained one. If both were random, the very first forward pass would add a random perturbation to every adapted layer, damaging what the model knows before any learning happened. (One of them must be non-zero, or the gradients of both would be zero and nothing would train.)

You merge a LoRA adapter into W and deploy. Then a user wants the base model back. What do you do?

Subtract the same (α/r)BA from the merged W (up to rounding), or simply keep an unmerged copy of W0. Merging is a cheap, reversible addition, which is also how one base model can serve many adapters.

In instruction tuning, what goes wrong if the response is followed by no end-of-turn token in training?

The model is never taught where an answer stops, so at generation time it tends to keep going: inventing the next user turn or rambling. The end-of-turn token belongs to the response and must be inside the loss mask.

After fine-tuning, the new-domain loss is excellent but the model has become worse at everything else. Name two fixes and what each costs.

Replay some general data (costs some of the new-domain gain and needs access to old data), anchor the weights to θ0 with an L2 or EWC penalty (costs gain, needs a tuned λ), or use a low-rank adapter (learns less, forgets less, and is cheaper to store).
