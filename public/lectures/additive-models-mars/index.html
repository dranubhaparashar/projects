Generalised Additive Models, PRIM & MARS

Flexible models you can still read, one curve at a time

A surface over many inputs is hard to estimate and harder to explain. Additive models make one bargain: let every input have its own curve, of any shape, and simply add the curves up. The surface on the right is exactly that, a curve for x1 (drawn along the bottom) plus a curve for x2 (up the side). MARS builds the same kind of model from bent straight lines and lets two inputs multiply where the data demand it; PRIM ignores the surface and just hunts for the box where the response is highest. Move your pointer or finger over the figure to see the height split into its two curves, then switch on the interaction.

The idea

Many inputs, one curve each

Chapters 1.4 and 1.6 fitted smooth curves to one input. With many inputs, the obvious move is to smooth in all of them at once, for example by averaging the k nearest neighbours. That fails quickly: in many dimensions every neighbourhood that holds a few points is so wide it stops being local. This is the curse of dimensionality. An additive model refuses to smooth in many dimensions at all. It assumes the regression function is a sum of one-input curves, so each curve is estimated from all N points, as easily as in one dimension.

Below, the truth depends on x1 and x2 only, and it really is additive. The other inputs are noise. Raise the number of inputs p and compare the k-nearest-neighbour fit with the additive fit, both trained on the same 200 points.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathrm{E}(Y \mid X_1,\dots,X_p) \\ &\quad = \alpha + f_1(X_1) + \cdots + f_p(X_p) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the expected response is a constant plus one function per input. Each fj may be any smooth shape; it is not a straight line as in linear regression. What is ruled out is inputs working together: the effect of X1 is the same whatever X2 is.
Say it“The expected value of Y given the X's is alpha plus f-one of X-one plus f-two of X-two, and so on up to f-p of X-p.”
\[ e_p(r) = r^{1/p} \]
Read it as: with inputs spread evenly over the unit cube, a sub-cube that holds a fraction r of the data needs edges of length r1/p. In one dimension, 5% of the data sits in 5% of the range; in ten dimensions the same 5% needs 74% of the range of every input.
Say it“The edge length e-p of r is r to the power one over p.”

Fitting

Backfitting: one curve at a time

How do you fit p curves at once? You don't. Hold every curve but one fixed, and look at what is left over: the partial residuals yi − α − Σk≠j fk(xik). They are exactly what fj still has to explain, and they depend on xj alone, so any one-input smoother can fit them. Fit, move on to the next input, and cycle until nothing changes. That is backfitting (Friedman & Stuetzle, 1981; Hastie & Tibshirani, 1990). The smoother here is a penalised cubic spline, a close cousin of the smoothing spline of chapter 1.4b. Press “Update next curve” a few times, then make the inputs strongly correlated and watch it take more cycles.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{PRSS} = &\sum_{i=1}^N \Big(y_i - \alpha - \sum_{j=1}^p f_j(x_{ij})\Big)^2 \\ &+ \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j \int f_j''(t)^2\,dt \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the penalised residual sum of squares. The first part rewards fitting the data; the second charges each curve for its total curvature, at a price λj. Large λ forces near-straight lines; λ = 0 lets curves chase the noise. Each minimising fj is a cubic smoothing spline. Our page uses 10 cubic B-splines per input and penalises second differences of their coefficients, which plays the same role.
Say it“P-R-S-S is the sum of squared residuals, plus lambda-j times the integral of f-j double-prime squared, summed over the inputs.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \hat f_j &\leftarrow \mathcal{S}_j\Big[\big\{y_i - \hat\alpha - \textstyle\sum_{k\ne j}\hat f_k(x_{ik})\big\}_{1}^{N}\Big] \\ \hat f_j &\leftarrow \hat f_j - \tfrac1N \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^N \hat f_j(x_{ij}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: set α̂ to the mean of y. Then, for j = 1, 2, …, p, 1, 2, …: smooth the partial residuals against xj with the smoother Sj, and shift the new curve so it averages zero over the data (otherwise the constant could slide between α and the curves). For linear smoothers this is the Gauss–Seidel method for a big linear system, and it converges to the minimiser of PRSS.
Say it“f-j-hat becomes S-j applied to the partial residuals, then f-j-hat minus its own average.”

Beyond least squares

Additive logistic regression

For a yes/no response, model the log-odds additively, exactly as logistic regression (chapter 1.3) models them linearly. That is one member of the family of generalised additive models: pick a link g, and set g(μ) = α + Σ fj(Xj). The identity link gives the Gaussian model above, the logit link gives this one, and the log link gives additive Poisson models for counts. The fitting trick, local scoring, is the IRLS you met for logistic regression with a smoother in place of the weighted least-squares fit: turn the current fit into a working response z and weights w, run weighted backfitting on z, repeat. Step through the iterations and watch the working responses settle.

\[ \log\frac{\mu(X)}{1-\mu(X)} = \alpha + f_1(X_1) + \cdots + f_p(X_p) \]
Read it as: μ(X) = Pr(Y = 1 | X). Its log-odds is a sum of curves. One curve per input still means one readable picture per input, now on the log-odds scale: a curve rising by 1 multiplies the odds by e ≈ 2.7.
Say it“The log of mu over one minus mu is alpha plus the sum of f-j of X-j.”
\[ \begin{aligned} z_i &= \hat\eta_i + \frac{y_i - \hat p_i}{\hat p_i(1-\hat p_i)} \\ w_i &= \hat p_i\,(1-\hat p_i) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: η̂i is the current log-odds and p̂i its probability. The working response nudges η̂i towards the observed yi, by an amount that is huge when the model is confident and wrong; the weight wi is small exactly then, so those points barely count. Backfit z with weights w, recompute, repeat. Each round is a Newton step for the penalised likelihood.
Say it“z-i is eta-hat-i plus y-i minus p-hat-i, over p-hat-i times one minus p-hat-i; w-i is p-hat-i times one minus p-hat-i.”

Honest limits

What additive models miss

Any function of two inputs can be split into a constant, a main effect for each input, and an interaction: whatever is left once the main effects are taken out. An additive model keeps the main effects and throws the interaction away. When the truth is additive that is free; when the effect of x1 depends on x2, the leftover shows up as structure in the residuals. Switch the truth to one with an interaction and look at what the additive fit leaves behind.

\[ \begin{aligned} f(X) = \alpha &+ \sum_j f_j(X_j) \\ &+ \sum_{j \lt k} f_{jk}(X_j, X_k) + \cdots \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: an ANOVA decomposition: main effects, then two-way interactions, then higher. An additive model stops after the first sum. You can add chosen two-way terms (smoothing them in two dimensions) or let a method such as MARS discover which interactions are worth having.
Say it“f of X is alpha plus the sum of main effects f-j, plus the sum of pairwise interactions f-j-k, and so on.”
signal captured
interaction share of the signal

Two more limits are practical. Every input gets a curve, so with hundreds of inputs you need selection as well as smoothing: sparse additive models put a lasso-style penalty on whole curves (chapter 1.2b), and boosting with one-input trees (chapter 1.8a) is another way in. And a large data set makes the many backfitting passes slow, which is why modern packages often solve the penalised problem directly.

Bump hunting

PRIM: peeling towards the high-response box

Sometimes you don't want the whole surface, just the region where the response is highest: the customers most likely to buy, the emails most likely to be spam. Friedman & Fisher's Patient Rule Induction Method (PRIM, 1999) finds a box. Start with a box around all the data, then peel: shave a thin slice (a share α, typically 5–10% of the points) off whichever face raises the box mean most, and repeat until the box gets too small. Then paste: grow any face back if that raises the mean. A tree's first split cuts the data roughly in half in one greedy move; PRIM's small steps are why it is called patient. Peel one step at a time and read the four candidates in the table.

\[ \begin{aligned} B &= \{x : a_j < x_j < b_j,\ j = 1,\dots,p\} \\ \bar y_B &= \frac{1}{n_B}\sum_{x_i \in B} y_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a box is one interval per input; its mean is the average response of the nB training points inside. Its support is nB/N, the share of the data it covers. PRIM trades support for mean.
Say it“B is the set of x with each x-j between a-j and b-j; y-bar-B is the average y inside B.”
\[ b^\ast = \arg\max_{b}\ \frac{\bar y_{B\setminus b} - \bar y_B}{n_B - n_{B\setminus b}} \]
Read it as: among the 2p candidate slices b (the bottom or top α of each input), pick the one whose removal raises the mean most per point removed. When every slice removes the same number of points, as here, this is simply the slice that leaves the highest mean, the rule in ESL. Dividing by the size matters with tied values, where a slice can't be thin (see the spam project).
Say it“b-star is the slice that maximises the gain in box mean per point removed.”

MARS, the pieces

Hinges and reflected pairs

Friedman's Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines (MARS, 1991) build a model from one kind of brick: the hinge (x − t)+, zero up to the knot t and a straight line after it. Hinges come in reflected pairs, one bending up to the right of t, one to the left. Add a pair to a model and the fit can change slope at t. The candidate knots are the observed values of each input, so there is a finite list to search. Drag the knot along the x axis and watch the error of a one-pair model; then press “Add the best pair” to grow the model the way MARS does.

\[ \begin{aligned} (x - t)_+ &= \max(0,\ x - t) \\ (t - x)_+ &= \max(0,\ t - x) \\ \mathcal{C} &= \{(X_j - t)_+,\ (t - X_j)_+ \} \\ &\quad t \in \{x_{1j}, \dots, x_{Nj}\},\ j = 1,\dots,p \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each pair is two piecewise-linear functions meeting at the knot t. The collection C has a pair at every observed value of every input: up to 2Np functions. With N = 100 points and one input that is 200 candidates.
Say it“x minus t, plus, is the max of zero and x minus t; C holds both hinges at every data value of every input.”
\[ f(X) = \beta_0 + \sum_{m=1}^{M} \beta_m\, h_m(X) \]
Read it as: the model is linear in its basis functions hm, which are hinges from C or products of them. Once the hm are chosen, the β's come from ordinary least squares. All the cleverness is in choosing the hm.
Say it“f of X is beta-nought plus the sum of beta-m times h-m of X.”

MARS, the procedure

Growing and pruning: forward, backward, GCV

The forward pass is greedy. The model starts as the constant. At each step MARS considers every term hℓ already in the model as a parent, multiplies it by every candidate reflected pair, and adds the pair of products that lowers the residual sum of squares most. Multiplying by a parent is how interactions arise: a hinge in x2 times a hinge in x1 is a ramp that only exists in one corner. Usually an input may appear at most once in a product, and the degree (how many hinges may multiply) is capped. Then the backward pass deletes terms one at a time, always the one whose loss hurts least, and generalised cross-validation picks the size. The data below follow the first simulated example in ESL §9.4.2. Run the forward pass, then prune; then add 18 pure-noise inputs and see whether MARS is fooled.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\hat\beta_{M+1}\, h_\ell(X)\,(X_j - t)_+ \\ &\quad +\ \hat\beta_{M+2}\, h_\ell(X)\,(t - X_j)_+ \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the new pair added at a forward step: a parent hℓ already in the model (the constant 1 is a parent too), times a reflected pair in input Xj at knot t. The search is over all parents ℓ, inputs j and knots t; all coefficients are then refitted by least squares.
Say it“beta M-plus-one times h-ell of X times X-j minus t plus, plus beta M-plus-two times h-ell of X times t minus X-j plus.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{GCV}(\lambda) &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N \big(y_i - \hat f_\lambda(x_i)\big)^2}{\big(1 - M(\lambda)/N\big)^2} \\ M(\lambda) &= r + cK \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: λ indexes the pruned models. RSS is divided by a factor that grows with the model's effective number of parameters M(λ): r terms, plus c for each of the K knots, because choosing a knot costs more than fitting one coefficient. ESL uses c = 3 (2 for additive models). The smallest GCV picks the size.
Say it“GCV is the residual sum of squares over one minus M of lambda over N, squared; M is r plus c times K.”

Relatives

MARS versus trees

MARS was designed as a fix for regression trees (chapter 1.7), and the link is exact. Make two changes to the MARS forward pass: use step functions instead of hinges, and when a term is multiplied by a candidate pair, replace it by the two products so it can't be used again. The result is CART: each term is a leaf, each step splits one leaf in two. Trees are therefore piecewise constant and every split cuts the data under it in two; MARS keeps the parent, so a new hinge can act across all the data and the fit stays continuous. That is why MARS fits smooth or additive structure with far fewer pieces. Switch between the three versions at the same number of forward steps.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{pieces: } I(X_j - t > 0),\ I(X_j - t \le 0) \\ &\text{CART: } h_\ell \to h_\ell\, I(X_j > t) \\ &\qquad\quad \text{and } h_\ell\, I(X_j \le t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: I(·) is 1 when its condition holds and 0 otherwise. Under the CART rule, the parent hℓ leaves the model when it is split, so the terms always partition the data into boxes: the leaves of a tree. Keep the parent instead, and the steps can overlap.
Say it“The pieces are the indicator of X-j above t and of X-j at or below t; under the CART rule, h-ell is replaced by its two products.”

Two more practical points. For classification with two classes, code the response 0/1 and fit MARS as a regression, as the spam project does; with K classes fit K 0/1 responses at once, or use PolyMARS, which grows a multiple-logistic model. And a categorical input enters through splits of its categories into two groups, just as in trees.

Computation

What it costs

Scoring every candidate knot by refitting from scratch would make MARS hopeless: there is a knot at every data value. The trick is that, for a fixed parent and input, the knots can be visited in order. Sort the points by xj, sweep from the largest down, and keep running sums; each knot's improvement in RSS then costs a handful of operations per term in the model, instead of a pass over the data. Race the two methods on one forward step and push N up: the naive one grows like N², the sweep like N. Both find the same knot.

\[ \sum_{i} r_i\, b_i\,(x_i - t)_+ \;=\; \sum_{x_i > t} r_i b_i x_i \;-\; t \sum_{x_i > t} r_i b_i \]
Read it as: bi is the parent's value at point i and ri the current residual. The inner product of the residual with a hinge splits into two running sums over the points above the knot, so moving t down to the next data value only adds that point's contribution. The same split works for the hinge's length and its overlap with every term already in the model.
Say it“The sum of r b times x minus t, plus, equals the sum over x above t of r b x, minus t times the sum over x above t of r b.”

Real-world project

Project: spam, word by word

In 1999 George Forman and colleagues at Hewlett-Packard Labs collected 4,601 emails, 1,813 of them spam, and described each by 57 numbers: how often each of 48 words (“remove”, “free”, “george”, “hp”…) and 6 characters (“!”, “$”…) appear, as a percentage of the email, and three measures of runs of CAPITAL letters. This is the UCI Spambase data (CC BY 4.0), the running example of ESL chapter 9. As there, every input is transformed to log(x + 0.1), because the counts are very skewed. We shuffle once, train on 3,065 emails and test on the other 1,536 (ESL's split sizes). Press “Fit every model”: your browser fits a logistic GAM by local scoring, MARS by the fast forward pass, PRIM boxes, and a linear logistic regression for comparison.

Each word's effect curve

This is what an additive model buys you. The log-odds of spam is a sum of 57 curves, so each word can be read on its own: how the odds change as the word becomes more frequent, all other words held fixed. The penalty is λ‖D2β‖² per curve, plus a small first-difference penalty (weight 1) that stops a word seen only in spam from getting an infinitely steep slope.

\[ \begin{aligned} \min_{\alpha,\,\beta}\; &-\ell(\alpha, f_1,\dots,f_{57}) \\ &+ \tfrac12\sum_{j=1}^{57}\Big(\lambda\,\lVert D_2\beta_j\rVert^2 + \lVert D_1\beta_j\rVert^2\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ℓ is the logistic log-likelihood; fj = Bjβj uses 10 cubic B-splines over the range of log(xj + 0.1); D2 and D1 take second and first differences of neighbouring coefficients. Local scoring with backfitting converges to the minimiser; the Python reference reaches the same point by Newton's method on all 514 coefficients at once.
Say it“Minimise minus the log-likelihood plus one half the sum over words of lambda times the squared second differences plus the squared first differences.”

import numpy as np, zipfile
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
z = zipfile.ZipFile("spambase.zip")                      # UCI Spambase, CC BY 4.0
D = np.loadtxt(z.open("spambase.data"), delimiter=",")[np.random.RandomState(2026).permutation(4601)]
names = [l.split(":")[0].split("_")[-1] for l in z.read("spambase.names").decode().splitlines() if "continuous." in l]
X, y = np.log(D[:, :57] + 0.1), D[:, 57]                  # log(x + 0.1), as in ESL
Xtr, ytr, Xte, yte = X[:3065], y[:3065], X[3065:], y[3065:]
lin = LogisticRegression(C=1.0, max_iter=10000, tol=1e-10).fit(Xtr, ytr)
print(f"linear logistic        test error {1 - lin.score(Xte, yte):.4f}")

# ---- GAM: 10 cubic B-splines per input; penalty lam*|D2 b|^2 + |D1 b|^2; Newton steps ----
NB, lo, hi = 10, Xtr.min(0), Xtr.max(0)
def bs(x, j):                                         # uniform cubic B-splines on [lo_j, hi_j]
    u = (np.clip(x, lo[j], hi[j]) - lo[j]) / (hi[j] - lo[j]) * (NB - 3)
    i = np.minimum(u.astype(int), NB - 4); t = u - i; B = np.zeros((len(x), NB)); r = np.arange(len(x))
    for k, w in enumerate([(1-t)**3, 3*t**3 - 6*t**2 + 4, -3*t**3 + 3*t**2 + 3*t + 1, t**3]): B[r, i+k] += w / 6
    return B
mu = [bs(Xtr[:, j], j).mean(0) for j in range(57)]     # centre each curve, drop one column
cols = lambda x, j: (bs(x, j) - mu[j])[:, :-1]
des = lambda X: np.hstack([np.ones((len(X), 1))] + [cols(X[:, j], j) for j in range(57)])
A, At = des(Xtr), des(Xte)
D2, D1 = np.diff(np.eye(NB), 2, axis=0), np.diff(np.eye(NB), 1, axis=0)
def gam(lam):
    P = np.zeros((A.shape[1],) * 2); pj = (lam * D2.T @ D2 + D1.T @ D1)[:-1, :-1]
    for j in range(57): P[1+9*j:10+9*j, 1+9*j:10+9*j] = pj
    th = np.zeros(A.shape[1])
    for it in range(100):
        p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-A @ th)); H = (A * (p * (1 - p))[:, None]).T @ A + P
        step = np.linalg.solve(H, A.T @ (ytr - p) - P @ th); th += step
        if abs(step).max() < 1e-10: break
    df = np.trace(np.linalg.solve(H, H - P)) - 1      # effective degrees of freedom of the 57 curves
    dev = -2 * np.sum(ytr * np.log(p) + (1 - ytr) * np.log(1 - p))
    return th, df, dev, np.mean(((At @ th) > 0) != yte)
for lam in [0.3, 1, 3, 10, 30, 100, 1000]:
    th, df, dev, err = gam(lam)
    print(f"GAM lambda {lam:<5g} df {df:5.1f}  deviance {dev:7.2f}  test error {err:.4f}")
th = gam(3)[0]
for w in ["remove", "free", "hp", "george", "!"]:
    j = names.index(w); f = lambda v: (cols(np.log(np.array([v]) + 0.1), j) @ th[1+9*j:10+9*j])[0]
    print(f"  lambda 3: f_{w}(0) = {f(0):+.4f}  f_{w}(1) = {f(1):+.4f}")

# ---- MARS: reflected pairs (x-t)+ and (t-x)+, forward with the fast knot scan, backward by GCV ----
def ortho(Q, v):
    for _ in range(2): v = v - Q @ (Q.T @ v)
    return v
def mars(X, y, max_terms=41, max_deg=2, minspan=5, c=3.0):
    N, p = X.shape; B = [np.ones(N)]; T = [(-1, -1, 0., 0, (), -1)]   # parent, var, knot, sign, vars, pair
    Q = np.ones((N, 1)) / np.sqrt(N); r = y - y.mean(); npair = 0
    while len(B) < max_terms:
        best = (0, None)
        for m, (_, _, _, _, vs, _) in enumerate(T):
            if len(vs) >= max_deg: continue
            for j in [j for j in range(p) if j not in vs]:
                nz = np.nonzero(B[m])[0]; o = nz[np.argsort(-X[nz, j], kind="stable")]; x, b = X[o, j], B[m][o]
                u0 = B[m] * X[:, j]; u = ortho(Q, u0); uu = u @ u; Qa, g0, r2 = Q, 0., r
                if not np.any(u0): continue
                if uu >= 1e-10 * (u0 @ u0):                                # B*x is new: add it first
                    q = u / np.sqrt(uu); g0 = (r @ q) ** 2; r2 = r - (r @ q) * q; Qa = np.hstack([Q, q[:, None]])
                Qb = Qa[o] * b[:, None]                                    # running sums from the largest x down
                Sx, S0 = np.cumsum(Qb * x[:, None], 0), np.cumsum(Qb, 0)
                Rx, R0 = np.cumsum(r2[o] * b * x), np.cumsum(r2[o] * b)
                Hxx, Hx, H0 = np.cumsum(b * b * x * x), np.cumsum(b * b * x), np.cumsum(b * b)
                k = np.nonzero(x[:-1] > x[1:])[0]; k = k[k + 1 >= minspan]; t = x[k + 1]
                hh = Hxx[k] - 2 * t * Hx[k] + t * t * H0[k]; P = Sx[k] - t[:, None] * S0[k]
                den = hh - (P * P).sum(1); num = Rx[k] - t * R0[k]
                gain = np.where(den > 1e-10 * hh, g0 + num ** 2 / np.where(den > 0, den, 1), g0)
                for kk in range(len(k)):
                    if gain[kk] > best[0]: best = (gain[kk], (m, j, t[kk]))
        if best[1] is None: break
        m, j, t = best[1]
        for s in (1, -1):
            h = B[m] * np.maximum(s * (X[:, j] - t), 0)
            if np.any(h): B.append(h); T.append((m, j, t, s, T[m][4] + (j,), npair)); v = ortho(Q, h); Q = np.hstack([Q, (v / np.sqrt(v @ v))[:, None]])
        npair += 1; r = y - Q @ (Q.T @ y)
    Bm = np.array(B).T; S = list(range(len(B))); path = []
    def fit(S):
        coef = np.linalg.lstsq(Bm[:, S], y, rcond=None)[0]; R = np.sum((y - Bm[:, S] @ coef) ** 2)
        M = len(S) + c * len({T[i][5] for i in S if i})                 # M(lambda) = r + c K
        return R, R / (1 - M / N) ** 2, coef
    path.append((S, *fit(S)))
    while len(S) > 1:
        S = min(([i for i in S if i != k] for k in S[1:]), key=lambda S: fit(S)[0]); path.append((S, *fit(S)))
    return T, path
def mars_basis(X, T):
    B = []

    return np.array(B).T

# ---- PRIM: peel 10% at a time (gain in box mean per case removed), paste, then cover ----
def prim(X, y, alpha=0.1, minsup=0.1):
    N, p = X.shape; lo, hi = np.full(p, -np.inf), np.full(p, np.inf)
    inbox = lambda: np.all((X > lo) & (X < hi), 1)
    while True:
        b = inbox(); n = b.sum(); m0 = y[b].mean(); k = int(np.ceil(alpha * n)); best = (0, None)
        for j in range(p):
            v = np.sort(X[b, j])
            for side, t in ((0, v[k - 1]), (1, v[n - k])):
                keep = b & ((X[:, j] > t) if side == 0 else (X[:, j] < t)); nk = keep.sum()
                if minsup * N <= nk < n and (y[keep].mean() - m0) / (n - nk) > best[0]: best = ((y[keep].mean() - m0) / (n - nk), (j, side, t))
        if best[1] is None: break
        j, side, t = best[1]
        if side == 0: lo[j] = t
        else: hi[j] = t
    while True:                                                           # pasting
        b = inbox(); n = b.sum(); m0 = y[b].mean(); k = int(np.ceil(alpha * n)); best = (m0, None)
        for j in range(p):
            others = np.all(((X > lo) & (X < hi))[:, np.arange(p) != j], 1)
            for side in (0, 1):
                c = np.sort(X[others & ((X[:, j] <= lo[j]) if side == 0 else (X[:, j] >= hi[j])), j])
                if len(c) == 0: continue
                t = (-np.inf if k >= len(c) else c[::-1][k]) if side == 0 else (np.inf if k >= len(c) else c[k])
                L, H = lo.copy(), hi.copy(); (L if side == 0 else H)[j] = t
                bb = np.all((X > L) & (X < H), 1)
                if y[bb].mean() > best[0]: best = (y[bb].mean(), (L, H))
        if best[1] is None: break
        lo, hi = best[1]
    return lo, hi
left_tr, left_te = np.ones(len(ytr), bool), np.ones(len(yte), bool)

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • An additive model writes g(E[Y|X]) = α + Σ fj(Xj): every input gets its own smooth curve, each estimated from all the data, so it escapes the curse of dimensionality and can be read one picture at a time.
  • Backfitting fits the curves by smoothing partial residuals one input at a time, centring each curve, until nothing changes; for a binary response, local scoring wraps it in IRLS with working responses z and weights w.
  • Additive models drop every interaction. PRIM ignores the surface altogether and patiently peels a box of high response, giving rules a person can read.
  • MARS adds reflected pairs of hinges, greedily, multiplying by terms already in the model to make interactions; the backward pass and GCV with M(λ) = r + cK decide the size. Steps instead of hinges, with parents replaced, turn MARS into CART.
  • On Spambase a logistic GAM gets about 5% test error, slightly better than a linear logistic model, and its 57 curves explain every decision; MARS needs a fast sorted sweep over the knots to be practical at all.

Check your understanding

Why does backfitting centre each curve after smoothing?

Adding a constant to fj and subtracting it from α leaves every prediction unchanged, so the model is only identified up to such shifts. Centring each curve to average zero over the data fixes them, and puts the whole level into α, which then equals the mean of y.

Backfitting takes many more cycles when x1 and x2 are strongly correlated. Why?

Each curve is fitted to the partial residuals given the other. With correlated inputs, part of what f1 should explain looks like a function of x2, so the two curves hand the same signal back and forth, each correction only partly undone by the next. It is the same slow zig-zag as Gauss–Seidel (or coordinate descent) on an ill-conditioned problem.

In local scoring, a point with p̂ = 0.999 and y = 0 gets a huge working response. Why doesn't it wreck the fit?

Its working response is η̂ − 1/(1 − p̂), far below the curve, but its weight p̂(1 − p̂) ≈ 0.001 is tiny. Weight times squared deviation is what the backfitting step minimises, and the product stays moderate: exactly the Newton step for the log-likelihood.

What would MARS have to change to become a regression tree?

Use step functions I(x > t) and I(x ≤ t) instead of hinges, and when a term is multiplied by a candidate pair, remove that parent from the model so it can't be multiplied again. Then the terms always partition the input space into boxes: the leaves.

Why does the GCV for MARS charge c ≈ 3 per knot rather than 1?

A knot is not a fixed column: it was chosen from hundreds of candidates to fit this very training set, so it uses up more of the data's freedom than one least-squares coefficient. Simulations suggest about three degrees of freedom per knot (two in additive models), so ignoring that would make GCV keep too many terms.
