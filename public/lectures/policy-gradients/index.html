Policy Gradients & Actor–Critic

Learn what to do, not just what it is worth

Q-learning and DQN learn the value of every action, then act greedily. Policy-gradient methods skip the middle step. The policy itself is a function with dials θ, and learning turns the dials so that actions followed by high returns become more likely. On the right, a policy of just five numbers, learnt by PPO in this chapter’s project, balances a pole on a cart. The idea

Why learn the policy itself?

A value-based agent learns Q(s, a) and then picks the best action, every time. Its policy is deterministic, with a little ε-greedy noise on top. Usually that is fine. But sometimes the best thing to do is to be random on purpose. Sutton and Barto’s short corridor shows it. Every step costs −1 and the goal is on the right. In the second cell the controls are reversed, and the agent sees nothing that tells the cells apart, so it must use one rule everywhere: go right with probability p. Always right bounces between the start and the reversed cell for ever; always left never leaves the start. Slide p and find the best mix, then walk an episode.

\[ J(p) = v_\pi(S_0) = -\,\frac{2\,(2-p)}{p\,(1-p)} \]
Read it as: J is the expected return from the start, which here is minus the expected number of steps to the goal. The formula comes from solving the three Bellman equations of chapter 3.1, one per cell, for a policy that goes right with probability p. It blows up at p = 0 and p = 1 and is best at p = 2 − √2 ≈ 0.59, where J ≈ −11.66.
Say it“J of p equals minus two times two minus p, over p times one minus p.”

Three more reasons to learn the policy directly. With continuous actions (a steering angle, a motor torque) there is no list of actions to take the max over, but a policy can simply output a number. A policy is often simpler than the values: “push towards the side the pole is falling” is easier to write down than the expected future reward of every state. And small changes to θ give small, smooth changes in behaviour, while a tiny change in Q can flip an argmax.

Parametrising a policy

A policy with dials: the softmax

For a handful of discrete actions the standard choice gives each action a preference h, any real number, and turns the preferences into probabilities with the softmax. The preferences can be a table, a linear function of features, or the last layer of a neural network; θ is whatever sets them. Learning needs one more ingredient: the score ∇ log π(a), the direction in θ-space that makes action a more likely. Pick the action that was taken (click its bar), say how good the outcome was with G, and apply one update.

\[ \pi(a \mid s;\theta) = \frac{e^{h(s,a;\,\theta)}}{\sum_{b} e^{h(s,b;\,\theta)}} \]
Read it as: exponentiate each preference so it is positive, then divide by the total so the probabilities add up to 1. Only differences between preferences matter: add 5 to all of them and nothing changes.
Say it“Pi of a given s is e to the h of a, over the sum of e to the h of every action.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial \log \pi(a)}{\partial h_b} = \mathbb{1}[b = a] - \pi(b) \\ &h \leftarrow h + \alpha\, G\, \nabla_h \log \pi(a) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the score raises the taken action’s preference by 1 − π(a) and lowers every other preference by its own probability. Scaled by a positive G it makes a more likely; by a negative G, less likely. Rare actions get the biggest push, because 1 − π(a) is large.
Say it“The derivative of log pi of a with respect to h-b is one if b is a, minus pi of b. Then h moves by alpha times G times the score.”

The key identity

The policy gradient: a slope you can sample

We want the slope of J(θ), the expected return, with respect to θ. The trouble is that θ changes which actions get sampled, and we cannot differentiate through a coin flip. The log-derivative trick gets around it. It rewrites the slope as an average of reward × score over the actions the policy actually takes, so every sampled action gives a noisy but unbiased estimate. Below, a two-lever bandit: the left lever pays 1 with probability 0.4, the right one with probability 0.7, and π(right) = σ(θ). Each faint line is the slope estimated from one batch of N pulls. Draw some estimates, then change N.

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta J &= \textstyle\sum_a r(a)\,\nabla_\theta\, \pi_\theta(a) \\ &= \textstyle\sum_a \pi_\theta(a)\, r(a)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a) \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{A\sim\pi_\theta}\!\big[\,R\;\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A)\,\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: J = Σ π(a) r(a) is an average reward, so its slope is Σ r(a) ∇π(a). Multiply and divide by π(a), using ∇π = π ∇log π, and the sum becomes an expectation under the policy itself. That expectation can be estimated by sampling: pull, observe R, multiply by the score, average.
Say it“The gradient of J is the expected value of the reward times the gradient of log pi of the action taken.”

The same trick works for whole episodes. The probability of a trajectory is a product of policy terms π(At|St) and environment terms p(St+1|St, At). Take the log and it becomes a sum. The environment terms do not depend on θ, so their gradients vanish. That is the policy gradient theorem (Sutton et al., 2000): we can improve the policy without a model of the world.

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\Big[&\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} G_t \\ &\times \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t \mid S_t)\Big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each action is credited with Gt, the return from that step on. Rewards that came before the action could not have been caused by it, and on average they add nothing to the estimate except noise. With discounting, the exact gradient carries an extra factor γt; most code leaves it out.
Say it“The gradient of J is the expected sum over time of the return from t on, times the gradient of log pi of the action taken at t.”
The first algorithm

REINFORCE: credit every action with its return

Ronald Williams (1992) turned the estimate into an algorithm: play a whole episode, then nudge θ along Gt ∇log π(At|St) for every step. Back to the short corridor, with p = σ(θ) and the start at p = 0.05, the ε-greedy “mostly left” policy. Every return is negative, so every action is pushed down. The trick is that the actions in long episodes are pushed down hardest, and on balance that pushes p towards the best value. Press Run and watch the dot climb the curve.

\[ \theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} G_t\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t \mid S_t) \]
Read it as: after each episode, add up return × score over its steps and take a small step that way. Here p = σ(θ), so the score is 1 − p after choosing right and −p after choosing left. Gt = −(steps still to go). No value function, no bootstrapping: a pure Monte-Carlo method.
Say it“Theta moves by alpha times the sum over the episode of the return from t times the gradient of log pi of the action at t.”

Variance

Baselines: the same direction, far less noise

REINFORCE is unbiased but noisy. In the corridor every return is negative, so every action is pushed down, and the useful signal is only the difference between pushes. Subtract a baseline b from the return and the expected gradient does not change, because the score averages to zero. But the noise can shrink a lot. A natural baseline is the value of the state, V(s). Then G − V(s) says whether this action did better or worse than usual: an estimate of the advantage A(s, a) = Q(s, a) − V(s). Slide b on the two-lever bandit and watch the spread change while the mean stays put.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\big[\,b\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A)\big] &= b \sum_a \nabla_\theta\, \pi_\theta(a) \\ &= b\,\nabla_\theta 1 = 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the probabilities always add up to 1, so their gradients add up to zero. Any baseline that does not depend on the action adds nothing on average.
Say it“The expected value of b times the score is b times the gradient of one, which is zero.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}\big[&(G_t - b(S_t)) \\ &\times \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t \mid S_t)\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: credit each action with how much better than b it did. The best constant b is close to the average return, weighted towards the samples with large scores.
Say it“The gradient is the expected value of G minus the baseline, times the score.”

Bootstrapping

Actor–critic: a critic grades every step

REINFORCE waits for the end of the episode. An actor–critic learns a value function Vw(s), the critic, and grades each action as soon as it lands, with the TD error δ from chapter 3.1. A positive δ means things went better than the critic expected, so the actor makes that action more likely. A negative δ means worse, so less likely. Bootstrapping on V makes the grades less noisy but slightly biased, because the critic can be wrong. Barto, Sutton and Anderson used exactly this split in 1983, for the same pole-balancing task as our project. Below, a gridworld: reach the goal (+1), avoid the pits (−1), γ = 0.9. Step through a few moves, then run 50 episodes.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\delta_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma\, V_w(S_{t+1}) - V_w(S_t) \\ &\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha\,\delta_t\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t \mid S_t) \\ &w \leftarrow w + \beta\,\delta_t\,\nabla_w V_w(S_t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: δ compares what happened (the reward plus the critic’s value of where we landed) with what the critic expected. On average it equals the advantage of the action. The actor moves along δ × score; the critic moves its estimate towards the target. In this table-based version, ∇V is simply 1 for the current cell and 0 for every other cell.
Say it“Delta is the reward plus gamma times V of the next state minus V of this state. The actor moves by alpha delta times the score; the critic by beta delta times the gradient of V.”

Stability

PPO: improve, but not too far

Collecting experience is expensive, so we would like to squeeze several gradient steps out of every batch. But the batch was collected by the old policy. Step too far and the new policy visits states the batch knows nothing about, and performance can collapse in one update. TRPO (Schulman et al., 2015) limited each step with a constraint on the KL divergence. Proximal Policy Optimization (Schulman et al., 2017) does it with a simple clip: once the probability ratio ρ between the new and old policy leaves [1 − ε, 1 + ε] in the helpful direction, the objective goes flat and that sample stops pulling. Move the ratio and flip the sign of the advantage.

\[ \rho_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(A_t \mid S_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(A_t \mid S_t)} \]
Read it as: how much more (ρ > 1) or less (ρ < 1) likely the new policy makes the action that was actually taken. Since ∇ρ = ρ ∇log π, at ρ = 1 the surrogate ρÂ has the ordinary policy gradient.
Say it“Rho is pi-theta of the action over pi-old of the action.”
\[ \begin{aligned} L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) &= \mathbb{E}_t\big[\min(\rho_t \hat A_t,\ c_t \hat A_t)\big] \\ c_t &= \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: take the smaller of the plain surrogate and its clipped version. For a good action (Â > 0) the gain stops growing once ρ passes 1 + ε. For a bad one (Â < 0) the gain stops once ρ drops below 1 − ε. Moves that make things worse are never clipped, so mistakes can still be undone.
Say it“L-clip is the average of the minimum of rho times A-hat and rho clipped to one plus or minus epsilon, times A-hat.”

Many epochs on one batch

Here is why the clip matters. A three-armed bandit (true mean rewards 1.0, 0.0 and 0.6, plus noise) gives one batch of 24 pulls from a uniform policy, and we take K gradient steps on that same batch. The plain surrogate keeps moving the policy for as long as you let it, further and further from the policy that collected the data, on the strength of 24 noisy pulls. The clipped one stops near the band: once a sample’s ratio has left it in the helpful direction, that sample stops pulling. Draw a few new batches and raise K.

Continuous actions

Continuous actions: a Gaussian policy

A robot joint does not choose from a menu; it outputs a torque. A policy-gradient method handles that with no extra machinery. Let the policy output the mean μ and spread σ of a normal distribution, sample the action from it, and use the same score-function update. Here the task is a single throw at a target on a line: reward = −(a − target)². Each update uses a batch of 16 throws, with the batch’s mean reward as the baseline. Press Run, then click the axis to move the target.

\[ \pi(a \mid \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\; e^{-(a-\mu)^2 / 2\sigma^2} \]
Read it as: a bell curve centred on μ with width σ. In a real controller μ and log σ are outputs of a network that reads the state.
Say it“Pi of a is the normal density with mean mu and standard deviation sigma.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \log\pi}{\partial \mu} &= \frac{a-\mu}{\sigma^2} \\ \frac{\partial \log\pi}{\partial \log\sigma} &= \frac{(a-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2} - 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a throw that beat the baseline drags μ towards itself. A good throw far out in the tails (|a − μ| > σ) widens σ; a good throw near the centre narrows it.
Say it“The score for mu is a minus mu over sigma squared; for log sigma it is a minus mu squared over sigma squared, minus one.”

Real-world project

Project: CartPole, three ways

CartPole is the pole-balancing task of Barto, Sutton and Anderson (1983), and the “hello world” of reinforcement learning. A cart on a 4.8 m track carries a hinged pole. Every 0.02 s the agent pushes the cart left or right with 10 N, and earns +1 for every step the pole stays within 12° of upright and the cart stays on the track, up to 500 steps. This page runs the equations of Gymnasium’s CartPole-v1 line for line; the Python check below steps Gymnasium 1.4.0’s own environment alongside it, and the states agree to within 5 × 10−15.

All three learners use the same five-number policy: a logistic function of the four state variables (each divided by a fixed scale) plus a bias. All three also train the same small critic, a network with 16 tanh units: REINFORCE uses it as a baseline, actor–critic and PPO to bootstrap. Each learner gets 100,000 steps of experience, five times, with seeds 1 to 5. Press Train: the 15 runs happen in your browser now.

\[ \begin{aligned} \pi(\text{right} \mid s) &= \sigma(z) \\ z &= w_1 \tfrac{x}{2.4} + w_2 \tfrac{\dot x}{3} \\ &\quad + w_3 \tfrac{\vartheta}{0.21} + w_4 \tfrac{\dot\vartheta}{3} + b \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: x is the cart’s position, ẋ its velocity, ϑ the pole’s angle from vertical (positive leaning right) and ϑ̇ how fast it is turning. A positive weight on ϑ means “push right when the pole leans right”, which slides the cart back under it. Training only ever adjusts these five numbers. The critic is used only to grade them.
Say it“The probability of pushing right is the sigmoid of a weighted sum of the four scaled state variables, plus a bias.”

PPO’s clip at work

PPO collects 1,024 steps, estimates advantages with GAE (λ = 0.95), then takes 8 passes over the batch in minibatches of 256. Below are the ratios ρ of every sample in the final pass of one update. Most stay close to 1. The ones that leave the band in the helpful direction contribute no gradient: the clip is doing its job.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat A_t &= \sum_{k \ge 0} (\gamma\lambda)^k\, \delta_{t+k} \\ \delta_t &= R_{t+1} + \gamma V(S_{t+1}) - V(S_t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: generalised advantage estimation (Schulman et al., 2016) blends TD errors over the following steps. λ = 0 gives the one-step actor–critic’s δ. λ = 1 gives Gt − V(St), REINFORCE with a baseline. In between it trades bias against variance. The sum stops at the end of an episode.
Say it“A-hat at t is the sum over k of gamma lambda to the k, times the TD error k steps later.”

Agreement to the last bit takes care. CartPole amplifies tiny differences: an early version that used each language’s built-in sin, cos and exp drifted apart after a few hundred episodes, because the two maths libraries round a few results differently in the last bit. So the page and the reference compute exp, tanh, sin and cos with the same short series in plain arithmetic, accurate to about 10−15 (that is the only difference from Gymnasium’s states). The hyperparameters (step sizes, network size, batch sizes) were tuned briefly by hand, with similar effort for each learner, so treat the ranking as one honest experiment, not a law. With bigger networks, Adam and longer training, all three methods can solve CartPole reliably.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Policy-gradient methods learn the policy πθ(a|s) directly and climb J(θ), the expected return. Stochastic policies and continuous actions come free.
  • The log-derivative trick, ∇J = E[Gt ∇θ log πθ(At|St)], turns the gradient into an average over your own experience. The environment’s dynamics never need to be known.
  • REINFORCE is that estimate, one episode at a time: unbiased but noisy. Subtracting a baseline such as V(s) keeps it unbiased and cuts the noise.
  • Actor–critic replaces the return with the critic’s TD error δ: it learns every step, with less variance and a little bias. GAE’s λ slides between the two.
  • PPO reuses each batch for several epochs but clips the ratio πnew/πold to [1 − ε, 1 + ε], so no single update moves the policy too far. That stability made it the workhorse of deep RL and of RLHF.

Check your understanding

Why can’t a greedy policy derived from Q values solve the short corridor well, even with perfect Q values?

The agent cannot tell the cells apart, so it needs one rule for all of them. Any greedy rule always goes the same way and gets stuck: always right loops in the reversed cell, always left never leaves the start. The best rule is random (right with probability about 0.59), and an argmax can never be random on purpose. ε-greedy adds only a fixed, small amount of randomness.

Every return in the corridor is negative, so REINFORCE pushes down every action it takes. How can it still learn?

The pushes differ in size. Actions in long, bad episodes have large negative returns and are pushed down hard; actions in short episodes are pushed down gently. Also, in expectation the score sums to zero over actions, so pushing every action down by the same amount changes nothing. Only the differences between returns move the policy, which is exactly why a baseline helps.

Why doesn’t subtracting a baseline b(s) bias the gradient, and why must b not depend on the action?

E[b(s) ∇log π(A|s)] = b(s) Σa ∇π(a|s) = b(s) ∇1 = 0. That step pulls b out of the sum over actions. If b depended on a it could not be pulled out, and the term would no longer vanish.

A critic is trained badly and its values are all far too high. What happens to a one-step actor–critic?

δ = R + γV(s′) − V(s) is biased: the actor gets graded against wrong expectations, so it may favour actions that lead to states the critic overrates. The gradient is no longer unbiased; that is the price of bootstrapping. REINFORCE with a baseline stays unbiased however bad the baseline is; a bad baseline only adds noise.

In PPO a sample has Â < 0 and ratio ρ = 1.5. Is it clipped? What does the objective do?

No. For a bad action the clip only applies when ρ < 1 − ε. At ρ = 1.5 the new policy has made a bad action more likely. The minimum picks the unclipped term ρÂ, whose gradient pushes ρ back down. PPO always lets you undo a move in the wrong direction.
