Models & Dyna: Planning While Learning

Every real step teaches twice: once directly, and again in imagination

The learners of the last few chapters used each experience once and threw it away. A model-based learner also keeps a note of what happened, “from here, going left led there”, and replays those notes between real steps. That replay is planning, and it can make learning many times faster. On the right, a Dyna agent is finding its way from office 269 to the staircase on one floor of a real university building, mapped by volunteers in OpenStreetMap. The shaded cells are what it has learned; the line is its current route. Tap a corridor cell to close it and watch the agent bump into the change, repair its model and re-plan.

The idea

Models and planning

A model of the environment is anything the agent can ask “if I am in s and do a, what happens next?”. It comes in two flavours. A distribution model answers with every possible outcome and its probability, p(s′, r | s, a): the full table that dynamic programming needs. A sample model answers with one outcome, drawn with the right probability, like a simulator or a game engine. A sample model is usually far easier to build: writing down every probability of a dice game is tedious, rolling the dice is not.

Planning means turning a model into better values or a better policy without touching the real world. In this chapter it always has the same shape: the model produces simulated experience, and the very same updates we use on real experience (Q-learning, value iteration) are applied to it. The simplest planner, random-sample one-step tabular Q-planning, picks a state and action at random, asks the sample model what happens, and applies one Q-learning update. Repeat it often enough and it converges to the optimal values of the model, just as Q-learning converges on real experience.

The maze below is slippery: an intended move succeeds with probability 1 − p and otherwise slides to one of the two sideways neighbours. Click a cell, pick an action, draw samples from the model, then let the planner run.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{distribution:}\;\; & p(s', r \mid s, a) \\ \text{sample:}\;\; & (S', R) \sim p(\cdot \mid s, a) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the distribution model lists the probability of each next state s′ and reward r after action a in state s. The sample model hands back one (S′, R) pair, drawn from that same distribution. Count enough samples and the frequencies approach the probabilities.
Say it“A distribution model gives p of s-prime and r given s and a; a sample model draws S-prime and R from it.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &Q(S,A) \leftarrow Q(S,A) + \alpha\,\delta \\ &\delta = R + \gamma \max_{a} Q(S',a) - Q(S,A) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: pick a state S and action A at random, ask the sample model for (S′, R), and nudge Q(S, A) a fraction α of the way towards the reward plus the discounted value of the best action in S′; δ, the TD error, is the size of that gap. It is exactly Q-learning, fed by the model instead of the world. Here α = 0.1, γ = 0.95, and reaching the goal G pays 1.
Say it“Q of S, A moves alpha of the way towards R plus gamma times the max over a of Q of S-prime, a.”

Learn the model, then solve it

Adaptive dynamic programming

Dynamic programming needs p(s′ | s, a); a learner doesn’t have it. The most direct fix is to estimate the model from counts and solve it. A passive agent follows a fixed policy π and wants its utilities Uπ. It counts how often each action led to each next state, turns the counts into probabilities, and after every trial solves the Bellman equations of the estimated model, a small linear system here, exactly as policy evaluation would with the true model. Russell and Norvig call this adaptive dynamic programming (ADP). It squeezes everything out of each transition, because every count feeds a full solve, and its estimate is the best the data allow: the maximum-likelihood model, solved exactly.

The world is the classic 4 × 3 grid: moves go the intended way with probability 0.8 and sideways with 0.1 each, every move costs 0.04, and the two terminal squares pay +1 and −1. The fixed policy is drawn as arrows. Run trials and watch the estimates settle on the true utilities; click a square to see its learned transition probabilities.

\[ \hat P(s' \mid s,a) = \frac{N(s,a,s')}{N(s,a)} \]
Read it as: N(s, a) counts the times action a was taken in s; N(s, a, s′) counts the times it led to s′. Their ratio is the maximum-likelihood estimate of the transition probability. A state the policy never visits has no counts, so its row of the model, and its utility, stay unknown.
Say it“P-hat of s-prime given s, a is the count of s, a, s-prime over the count of s, a.”
\[ \begin{aligned} U^{\pi}(s) = \sum_{s'} \hat P\big(s' \mid s,\pi(s)\big) \\ \times \big[R(s,\pi(s),s') + \gamma\, U^{\pi}(s')\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the utility of s under π is the expected reward of the next move plus the discounted utility of where it lands, averaged with the estimated probabilities. One such equation per state (terminals have U = 0) gives a linear system; with γ = 1 and 9 unknowns it is solved exactly after every trial.
Say it“U-pi of s is the sum over s-prime of P-hat times the reward plus gamma U-pi of s-prime.”

Planning, acting and learning together

Dyna-Q: n imagined steps after every real one

ADP re-solves the whole model after each trial, which is affordable for 11 states and hopeless for millions. Sutton’s Dyna architecture (1990) interleaves the three jobs a little at a time. Real experience is used twice: for direct reinforcement learning (an ordinary Q-learning update) and for model learning (remember what happened). Then the model is used for planning: n times, pick a state the agent has seen and an action it has tried there, ask the model what happens, and apply the same Q-learning update to the imagined transition. Acting, learning and planning all write to one table of Q-values.

In a deterministic maze the model can be a lookup table: Model(S, A) = (R, S′), the last outcome seen. During the first episode nothing useful happens, because every reward is 0 until the goal is found. But the moment it is, planning spreads that one reward backwards through everything the agent has already walked, so by the second episode most of the maze points the right way. Run the same maze with n = 0, 5 and 50 and step through the second episode.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{1. act: } Q(S,A) \leftarrow Q(S,A) + \alpha\,\delta \\ &\delta = R + \gamma \max_a Q(S',a) - Q(S,A) \\ &\text{2. store: } \text{Model}(S,A) \leftarrow (R,\, S') \\ &\text{3. } n \text{ times: pick a seen } \tilde S, \tilde A \\ &\;\; (\tilde R, \tilde S') \leftarrow \text{Model}(\tilde S, \tilde A) \\ &\;\; \text{update } Q(\tilde S, \tilde A) \text{ as in step 1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: step 1 is Q-learning on the real transition (S, A, R, S′); δ is its TD error. Step 2 stores it in the model. Step 3 repeats the same update n times on transitions recalled from memory: the states and actions are chosen uniformly at random among those already experienced. With n = 0 this is plain Q-learning. Here α = 0.1, γ = 0.95, ε = 0.1, and the goal pays 1.
Say it“Act and update Q by alpha delta; store the outcome in the model; then n times, recall a seen pair from the model and update Q again.”

When the model is wrong

Blocked paths, hidden shortcuts and Dyna-Q+

A learned model can be wrong: it was built from too little experience, or the world has changed since. Errors come in two kinds, and they heal very differently. If the model is too optimistic, promising a path that is now blocked, the agent will try that path, find the wall, and the model corrects itself. If it is too pessimistic, unaware that a better path has opened, nothing ever sends the agent to look, and the error can last forever.

Dyna-Q+ (Sutton, 1990) adds curiosity. For every state–action pair it records τ, the number of time steps since that pair was last tried for real, and during planning it pays a bonus of κ√τ on top of the remembered reward. Untried actions are allowed in planning too, modelled as “stay put, reward 0”. Long-neglected corners slowly start to look attractive, until the agent goes and checks. In both mazes the wall changes after a while: in the blocking maze the short way is closed and a long way opens; in the shortcut maze a short way opens and the long one stays. Because these mazes are deterministic, one observation is the truth, so here every learner uses α = 1 (and n = 10, ε = 0.1, γ = 0.95). Switch mazes and move κ.

\[ \tilde R \;=\; R + \kappa \sqrt{\tau(S,A)} \]
Read it as: in a planning update for the pair (S, A), the reward used is the remembered reward plus a bonus that grows with the square root of τ, the time since (S, A) was last tried in the real world. κ sets how curious the agent is: too small and stale knowledge is never questioned, too large and the agent spends its time re-checking corners instead of reaching the goal.

Planning where it matters

Prioritised sweeping

Dyna-Q plans blindly. In the first episode every reward is 0, so thousands of planning updates move nothing; and once the goal is found, only the few pairs next to it can change, yet planning keeps picking pairs uniformly from everywhere. Far better to work backwards from where something changed. When the value of a state moves, the pairs that lead into it (its predecessors in the model) are the ones whose values are now out of date, and the bigger the change, the more urgent the update.

Prioritised sweeping (Moore & Atkeson, 1993; Peng & Williams, 1993) keeps a priority queue of state–action pairs ordered by how much their value would change. Each real step inserts its own pair if the change is above a small threshold θ; planning then pops the most urgent pair, updates it, and pushes each predecessor with its new priority. Updates ripple backwards from the goal in a wave, and when nothing has changed, the queue is empty and no effort is wasted. Run one episode with each method, then race them on mazes of growing size.

\[ \begin{aligned} P &= |R + \gamma \max_a Q(S',a) - Q(S,A)| \\ & \text{insert } (S,A) \text{ if } P > \theta \\ \bar P &= |\bar R + \gamma \max_a Q(S,a) - Q(\bar S,\bar A)| \\ & \text{for each pair } (\bar S,\bar A) \text{ leading to } S \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: P is the size of the update (S, A) would receive: its priority. After Q(S, A) is updated, every pair (S̄, Ā) that the model says leads to S, with reward R̄, gets its own priority P̄ and joins the queue if it exceeds θ. Planning always takes the pair with the largest priority first, up to n per real step. Here n = 5, θ = 10−7, α = 1, γ = 0.95.
Say it“The priority is the absolute value of R plus gamma max Q of S-prime minus Q of S, A; after each update, push its predecessors with their own priorities.”

How to spend one unit of computation

Expected versus sample updates

Every one-step update met so far is one of eight possibilities: it updates a state value or an action value, for a fixed policy or for the optimal one, and it is either expected (a weighted sum over every possible next state, which needs a distribution model) or sample (one next state, which needs only a sample model or real experience). Seven of the eight have names:

Value estimatedExpected update (distribution model)Sample update (one transition)
vπ(s)iterative policy evaluationTD(0)
v*(s)value iteration(no common name: a max over actions needs every action’s outcome)
qπ(s, a)Q-policy evaluationSarsa
q*(s, a)Q-value iterationQ-learning

Which is better? An expected update has no sampling error, but it costs about b times as much, where the branching factor b is the number of possible next states. With the same effort you could do b sample updates on b different pairs. Suppose all b successors are equally likely and their values are already right, with unit spread. The expected update is exact after b computations. Sample updates with step size 1/t, started from an estimate in error by 1, average the successors they happen to draw, and their error falls as below. Raise b: for large b a small fraction of b samples already removes most of the error, which is why sampling wins in big stochastic problems.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{expected:} \\ &\; Q(s,a) \leftarrow \sum_{s',r} p(s',r\mid s,a) \\ &\qquad\quad \times \big[r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a')\big] \\ &\text{sample:} \\ &\; Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\,\delta \\ &\; \delta = R + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(S',a') - Q(s,a) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the expected update averages over every outcome with its probability, so it touches all b successors. The sample update looks at the one successor S′ that the model or the world produced, and moves part of the way towards it. Each “maxa′ Q(s′, a′)” is one unit of computation.
Say it“Expected: Q of s, a becomes the probability-weighted sum of r plus gamma max Q. Sample: Q moves alpha times delta, towards one sampled target.”
\[ \text{RMS error}(t) = \sqrt{\frac{b-1}{b\,t}} \]
Read it as: the error left after t sample updates. With step size α = 1/t, t sample updates leave the average of t draws from the b successor values. Its error comes only from which successors were drawn: their spread is (b − 1)/b on average for unit-variance values, divided by t. After the first sample the error is already √((b − 1)/b), just under 1; the expected update reaches 0 after b computations.
Say it“The RMS error after t samples is the square root of b minus one over b t.”

Where to spend the updates

Trajectory sampling

Dynamic programming sweeps through the whole state space, giving every state the same attention. But in most problems the vast majority of states are irrelevant: a good chess player never reaches them. The alternative is to update states in the order an agent would actually meet them, by simulating trajectories from the start state with the current policy and updating the pairs along the way. This on-policy distribution of updates costs nothing extra to generate, since the simulation produces it for free, and it concentrates effort where the policy lives.

Is it better? The experiment below is a version of the one in Sutton & Barto’s section 8.6, run in your browser. Each random task has N states with two actions each; every action leads to one of b equally likely next states (chosen at random when the task is made) with a fixed random reward drawn from a standard normal distribution, and every transition ends the episode with probability 0.1. Both methods use expected updates. Uniform sweeps through all state–action pairs in turn; on-policy follows ε-greedy episodes (ε = 0.1) from state 0 and updates each pair it visits. After every batch of updates the page evaluates the current greedy policy (iterative policy evaluation, to within 10−6) and reports what it is worth from state 0. Change N and b and run it.

\[ \begin{aligned} Q(s,a) \leftarrow\;& \frac{0.9}{b} \sum_{j=1}^{b} \Big[\, r_{s,a,j} \\ & + \max_{a'} Q(s_j, a') \Big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the expected update for this task: with probability 0.1 the episode ends (worth 0); otherwise one of the b successors sj follows, each with probability 1/b, paying its reward rs,a,j plus the value of the best action there. There is no discount (γ = 1); the 0.1 chance of ending keeps values finite. The two methods use this same update and differ only in which (s, a) they apply it to.

Dynamic programming, on the road

Real-time dynamic programming

Real-time dynamic programming (RTDP; Barto, Bradtke & Singh, 1995) is trajectory sampling applied to value iteration. It keeps a table of state values and the full distribution model, but instead of sweeping, it drives: from a start state it acts greedily with respect to the current values, and at each state it actually visits it performs one expected value-iteration update. It is asynchronous DP, with the order of updates chosen by where the greedy policy goes.

That focus can be exact, not just a heuristic. For an episodic task with strictly negative rewards, a goal reachable from every start, and initial values no lower than the optimal ones (0 works here, since every step costs −1), RTDP is guaranteed to find a policy that is optimal on all the states that matter, the ones reachable from the start under optimal play, even if it leaves other states untouched. The test bed is a small racetrack designed for this chapter: the car’s state is its cell and its velocity (each component 0 to 4, moving right and up), an action nudges each velocity component by −1, 0 or +1, and with probability 0.1 the nudge fails. Leaving the track sends the car back to a random cell on the start line. Run RTDP episode by episode, then compare with full DP sweeps.

\[ \begin{aligned} v(S_t) &\leftarrow \max_a \sum_{s',r} p(s',r \mid S_t,a) \\ &\qquad \times \big[r + v(s')\big] \\ A_t &= \text{the maximising } a \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: only the state the car is actually in, St, is updated, by an ordinary value-iteration update (every step pays r = −1, no discount), and the car then takes the action that update found best, with ties broken at random. Here each action has two outcomes: the nudge works (probability 0.9) or it fails and the velocity stays (0.1). Crossing the finish line ends the episode with value 0.
Say it“v of S-t becomes the max over actions of the expected reward plus value of the next state; then act on that max.”

import json, math, collections

def mulberry32(a):                                # the page's random-number generator, bit for bit
    s = [a & 0xFFFFFFFF]
    def r():
        s[0] = (s[0] + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF; a = s[0]
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return r

F = json.load(open("x072-maze.json"))            # level 2 of the Informatikbau, rasterised at 1.5 m
W, cells, bits = F["W"], F["cells"], F["bits"]; n = len(cells)
idx = {c: i for i, c in enumerate(cells)}
at = lambda r, c: idx[r * W + c]
def maze(blocked=()):                             # next cell for N, E, S, W; walls and blocked cells: stay put
    return [[idx[cells[i] + d] if bits[i] & b and idx[cells[i] + d] not in blocked else i
             for d, b in ((-W, 1), (1, 2), (W, 4), (-1, 8))] for i in range(n)]
start = at(3, 10)                                 # room 269, far end of the north wing
goal = {i for i in range(n) if F["spaces"][F["sp"][i]]["ref"] == "TR052"}   # the staircase
CLOSED = {at(35, 7), at(35, 8)}                   # simulated closure of one corridor branch
open_map, closed_map = maze(), maze(CLOSED)

def shortest(nb):
    D = {start: 0}; q = collections.deque([start])
    while q:
        u = q.popleft()
        if u in goal: return D[u]
        for v in nb[u]:
            if v not in D: D[v] = D[u] + 1; q.append(v)

def dyna(maps, change, steps=None, episodes=None, n_plan=0, alpha=0.1, gamma=0.95, eps=0.1, kappa=0.0, seed=1):
    """Dyna-Q (kappa = 0) or Dyna-Q+; the map switches from maps[0] to maps[1] at time step `change`."""
    rng = mulberry32(seed); Q = [[0.0] * 4 for _ in range(n)]
    model, last, acts, obs = {}, {}, {}, []
    s, t, cur, cum, lengths, marks = start, 0, 0, 0, [], {}
    while (steps is None or t < steps) and (episodes is None or len(lengths) < episodes):
        nb = maps[0] if t < change else maps[1]
        if t == change: marks["at change"] = cum
        if rng() < eps: a = int(rng() * 4)
        else:
            m = max(Q[s]); best = [i for i in range(4) if Q[s][i] == m]
            a = best[int(rng() * len(best))] if len(best) > 1 else best[0]
        s2 = nb[s][a]; r = 1.0 if s2 in goal else 0.0
        Q[s][a] += alpha * (r + (0.0 if s2 in goal else gamma * max(Q[s2])) - Q[s][a])
        if s not in acts: acts[s] = []; obs.append(s)
        if a not in acts[s]: acts[s].append(a)
        model[s, a] = (r, s2); t += 1; last[s, a] = t
        for _ in range(n_plan):                   # planning: replay remembered transitions
            ps = obs[int(rng() * len(obs))]
            if kappa > 0:                         # Dyna-Q+: any action; untried ones "stay put, reward 0"
                pa = int(rng() * 4); pr, ps2 = model.get((ps, pa), (0.0, ps))
                pr = pr + kappa * math.sqrt(t - last.get((ps, pa), 0))
            else:
                L = acts[ps]; pa = L[int(rng() * len(L))]; pr, ps2 = model[ps, pa]
            Q[ps][pa] += alpha * (pr + (0.0 if ps2 in goal else gamma * max(Q[ps2])) - Q[ps][pa])
        cur += 1; cum += r
        if s2 in goal: lengths.append(cur); cur = 0; s = start
        else: s = s2
    marks["at end"] = cum
    return lengths, marks

print(f"{n} reachable cells of 1.5 m, {len(F['doors'])} doors, start room 269, goal staircase TR052 ({len(goal)} cells)")
print(f"shortest route: {shortest(open_map)} steps; with the corridor branch closed: {shortest(closed_map)} steps")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A model answers “what happens if I do a in s?”, as a full distribution or as one sample; planning turns it into values by applying the usual updates to simulated experience.
  • Adaptive dynamic programming estimates the model from counts and solves it; Dyna does the same thing a little at a time, n planning updates after every real step, so learning, acting and planning share one value table.
  • A model that is too optimistic is corrected by experience; one that is too pessimistic is not, which is why Dyna-Q+ pays a small κ√τ bonus for checking what it hasn’t seen lately.
  • Spend planning where values are changing: prioritised sweeping works backwards from surprises, and trajectory sampling and RTDP update the states the policy actually visits.
  • Expected updates are exact but cost b times more; when the branching factor is large, many cheap sample updates usually beat a few expensive exact ones.

Check your understanding

In the first episode of Dyna-Q in a maze with reward only at the goal, what do the planning updates achieve?

Nothing to the values: every remembered reward is 0 and every Q-value is 0, so each planning update leaves Q at 0. Planning only becomes useful once the goal has been reached and a non-zero value exists to spread backwards. This is exactly the waste that prioritised sweeping avoids: its queue stays empty until something changes.

Why does an optimistic model error correct itself while a pessimistic one may not?

An optimistic error (a path that looks good but is blocked) attracts the agent, so it goes there, experiences the truth and fixes the model. A pessimistic error (a good path the model doesn’t know about) gives the agent no reason to go there, so only random exploration, or an explicit bonus like Dyna-Q+’s, will ever reveal it.

A state has 1,000 equally likely successors. Roughly how much error remains after 100 sample updates, and what would an expected update have cost?

√((1000 − 1)/(1000 × 100)) ≈ 0.1, so about 90% of the initial error is gone after one tenth of the computation. The expected update would cost 1,000 computations to reach zero error.

How does adaptive dynamic programming differ from Dyna-Q in how it uses its model?

ADP re-solves the Bellman equations of the whole estimated model (here exactly, by a linear solve) whenever the model changes. Dyna-Q spends a fixed budget of n sample updates per real step on randomly recalled transitions. ADP extracts more from each experience; Dyna scales to models far too big to solve.

Why can RTDP be optimal without ever updating some states?

Its guarantee only concerns the states reachable from the start under an optimal policy. Optimistic initial values pull the greedy car towards any promising option it hasn’t tried, and each visit corrects that value downwards, so every alternative that might be better gets checked. A state that can only be reached through states already known to be poor is never worth visiting, so its value is never needed and may stay at its initial guess.
