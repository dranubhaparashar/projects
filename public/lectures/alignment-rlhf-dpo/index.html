Alignment: RLHF & DPO

Turning “I prefer this one” into a better model

A model fine-tuned on examples imitates them, flaws and all. It never learns which of two plausible answers people actually prefer. Alignment adds that signal. Show people two replies, ask which is better, fit a reward model to their choices, then push the language model towards high reward, but on a leash, so it cannot wander far from the model you started with. DPO, the newer route, does both steps at once. On the right, a reward model trained on real human votes scores six replies to one question. Loosen the leash β and watch the probability flow from the reference model’s habits to the replies people favour.

The signal

Judging is easier than writing

A pretrained language model continues text the way the internet would. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on written demonstrations turns it into an assistant, but demonstrations are slow to write and teach one answer per question. People are far better judges than authors. Most of us cannot write the perfect reply, but we can usually say which of two replies is better. That is the signal behind RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022).

Why ask “which is better?” rather than “rate this from 1 to 7”? Because people use rating scales differently. One rater gives everything a 6, another rarely goes above 3. Below, 20 simulated raters judge 12 replies of known quality. Each makes three judgements: three ratings, or three comparisons. Make the raters disagree about the scale and see which method still recovers the true order.

\[ \begin{aligned} u_{ij} &= a_j\,q_i + b_j + \varepsilon_{ij} \\ \text{rating}_{ij} &= \operatorname{round}(4 + 1.3\,u_{ij}) \\ A \succ_j B &\iff a_j(q_A - q_B) \\ &\qquad + \varepsilon_{Aj} - \varepsilon_{Bj} > 0 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: rater j’s impression u of reply i is its true quality qi, stretched by the rater’s personal scale aj, shifted by their personal offset bj, plus noise ε. A rating reports u on a 1–7 scale (rounded, and capped at 1 and 7), offset and all. A comparison only asks which impression is bigger, so the offset bj cancels and a positive scale aj cannot change the answer. Only the noise is left to cause mistakes.
Say it“The impression is a times q plus b plus noise. A rating reports it; a comparison only asks which of two is bigger, and the offset b drops out.”

From votes to scores

Bradley–Terry: a score from a contest

Comparisons give us wins and losses. We want a number per reply. The Bradley–Terry model (1952), the same maths as chess’s Elo ratings, says that every reply has a hidden score r, and the chance that A beats B depends only on the gap rA − rB, through the logistic sigmoid. A gap of 0 is a coin flip; a gap of 2 wins 88% of the time. Fitting the model means finding the scores that make the observed wins most likely. Drag the four replies along the reward line, then simulate comparisons and let maximum likelihood recover the scores.

\[ \begin{aligned} P(A \succ B) &= \sigma(r_A - r_B) \\ &= \frac{e^{r_A}}{e^{r_A} + e^{r_B}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: σ(z) = 1/(1 + e−z) turns a score gap into a probability. Only gaps matter: add the same constant to every score and nothing changes, so the scores have no natural zero. That will matter later.
Say it“The probability that A beats B is the sigmoid of r-A minus r-B.”
\[ \hat r = \arg\max_r \sum_{(w,\,l)} \log \sigma(r_w - r_l) \]
Read it as: sum over every recorded comparison, with w the winner and l the loser. Each term rewards scores that make the observed winner likely. It is logistic regression on pairs, so the same solvers fit it. Because of the free constant we pin the mean score to the truth’s before comparing.
Say it“r-hat maximises the sum of log-sigmoid of winner’s score minus loser’s score.”

Generalising the votes

Training a reward model

Bradley–Terry scores only the replies that were compared. A reward model scores any reply, so the policy can be graded on replies nobody has seen. In practice it is the language model itself with its word-predicting head swapped for one output number, rφ(x, y), for prompt x and reply y. Training is Bradley–Terry again, with r computed from the reply instead of looked up. Below, each reply is boiled down to two made-up features (how detailed it is and how much it hedges), and the reward is a weighted sum w·φ(y). Each grey line joins the loser of a pair to its winner. Step gradient descent, or drag the arrow w yourself, and watch the loss.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(w) &= -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \sigma(m_i) \\ m_i &= r_w(y_w^{(i)}) - r_w(y_l^{(i)}) \\ \nabla \mathcal{L} &= -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \sigma(-m_i)\,\Delta\phi_i \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: mi = rw(yw) − rw(yl) is the margin by which the model prefers pair i’s winner, and Δφi = φ(yw) − φ(yl) is the difference of the pair’s features. The loss is small when every margin is large and positive. In the gradient each pair pushes w along its feature difference, weighted by σ(−mi), the probability the model currently gives to the wrong answer. Pairs it already gets confidently right hardly pull at all.
Say it“The loss is minus the average log-sigmoid of the reward margin between winner and loser.”

The objective

Reward, on a leash

Now improve the language model, called the policy π, using the reward model. Pure reward-maximising would put all probability on the single highest-scoring reply: no variety, and a model that exploits every flaw in the reward model. So RLHF subtracts a penalty for drifting from the reference model πref (the SFT model), measured by the KL divergence. The strength β is the length of the leash. This objective has an exact solution: reweight the reference by er/β. Below, 12 possible replies are ordered by how detailed they are; the reference prefers short ones. Pick a reward and slide β.

\[ \begin{aligned} \max_{\pi}\ J(\pi) = {} & \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\big[r(y)\big] \\ & - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi \,\Vert\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: KL(π ‖ πref) = Σ π(y) log(π(y)/πref(y)) is zero when the two agree and grows as π moves probability to replies the reference finds unlikely. (In a real LLM, y is a whole reply and the sum runs over every possible one, for every prompt x.) Large β: stay put. Small β: chase reward.
Say it“Maximise the expected reward minus beta times the KL divergence from the reference.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \pi^*(y) &= \frac{1}{Z}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\, e^{\,r(y)/\beta} \\ J(\pi^*) &= \beta \log Z \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Z = Σ πref(y) er(y)/β just makes the probabilities sum to 1. Every reply keeps its reference probability, multiplied by er/β. A reply the reference never says (πref = 0) stays at 0, however high its reward. That is the leash at work.
Say it“Pi-star is the reference times e to the reward over beta, normalised by Z.”

Getting there by sampling

RLHF with PPO

For a real language model you cannot write π* down: Z sums over every possible reply. So RLHF climbs towards it with reinforcement learning. Sample replies from the policy, score each one with the reward model minus β times its log-ratio to the reference, and take PPO steps, exactly the clipped update of chapter 3.3. The KL penalty becomes part of the reward. Each sampled reply pays for how much more likely the policy makes it than the reference did. InstructGPT did this with a 6-billion-parameter reward model and a per-token penalty (Ouyang et al., 2022). Here the policy is a softmax over the 12 replies of the section above, with the same reward. Run it, then switch the penalty off.

\[ \begin{aligned} R(y) &= r_\phi(y) - \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} \\ \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[R(y)\big] &= \mathbb{E}\big[r_\phi\big] \\ &\quad - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta \,\Vert\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the shaped reward R of one sampled reply is its reward-model score, minus a charge for every nat of extra probability the policy gives it. Averaged over the policy’s own samples, R is exactly the objective J of the section above, so ordinary policy-gradient RL on R maximises J. The fixed point is where R is the same for every reply the policy uses, which is π*.
Say it“Big R is the reward minus beta times log of pi-theta over pi-ref; its average is the KL-regularised objective.”

Goodhart’s law

Over-optimising the reward model

The reward model is a stand-in for people, and a flawed one. “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” Push hard against a learned reward and the policy finds what the model gets wrong. Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2023) measured this: as the KL from the reference grows, the reward model’s score keeps rising, but the score of a better “gold” judge rises, peaks and falls. Below, 400 replies were sampled from a base model. People (the gold reward) like detail, up to a point; rambling past about 150 words is penalised. The reward model learned from comparisons between typical replies, so it believes longer is always better. Tighten and loosen the leash, and change what the reward model saw.

\[ \begin{aligned} \pi_\beta(y) &\propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\, e^{\,\hat r(y)/\beta} \\ d &= \sqrt{\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\beta \,\Vert\, \pi_{\text{ref}})} \\ \text{gold gain}(d) &\approx d\,(a - b\,d) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: optimise the proxy r̂ with leash β and measure how far the policy moved by d, the square root of its KL. The proxy’s own score always rises with d. The gold score of the same policy rises at first, while the proxy and the truth agree, then falls once the policy is exploiting the gap. For best-of-n sampling, Gao et al. found this rise-and-fall shape, with coefficients a and b that change smoothly with the reward model’s size. Here the reference is uniform over the 400 samples, so πβ is a soft best-of-400.
Say it“Gold gain is roughly d times a minus b d, where d is the square root of the KL.”

The shortcut

DPO: the policy is the reward model

RLHF trains a reward model, then runs a fiddly RL loop with four networks in memory (policy, reference, reward model and value critic). Rafailov et al. (2023) noticed that the leash’s exact solution can be turned around. If π* is the reference reweighted by er/β, then the reward can be read off any policy as β times its log-ratio to the reference. Put that implicit reward into Bradley–Terry and the awkward Z cancels, because both replies answer the same prompt. What is left is a plain classification loss on the preference pairs, applied to the policy directly. That is Direct Preference Optimization. Below is one pair. The axes are the policy’s log-ratios h for the preferred reply yw and the rejected one yl. Take gradient steps, then drag the point into the bottom-left corner.

\[ \begin{aligned} h^*(y) &= \log\frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} \\ r(x,y) &= \beta\, h^*(y) + \beta \log Z(x) \\ P(y_w \succ y_l) &= \sigma\big(r(y_w) - r(y_l)\big) \\ &= \sigma\big(\beta h^*(y_w) - \beta h^*(y_l)\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: h* is the optimal policy’s log-ratio to the reference. Line two is π* = πref er/β/Z solved for r. In line three (with x left implicit) only reward gaps matter (the free constant of Bradley–Terry), so β log Z(x), the same for both replies to prompt x, drops out in line four. Human preferences can therefore be written entirely in terms of the optimal policy. No reward model is needed.
Say it“The reward is beta log pi-star over pi-ref plus a term that depends only on the prompt, and that term cancels in a comparison.”
\[ \begin{aligned} h_\theta(y) &= \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \\ \hat r_\theta(y) &= \beta\, h_\theta(y) \\ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} &= -\mathbb{E}\big[\log\sigma(\hat r_w - \hat r_l)\big] \\ \nabla\mathcal{L} &= -\beta\,\mathbb{E}\big[\sigma(\hat r_l - \hat r_w) \\ &\quad \times \big(\nabla h_\theta(y_w) - \nabla h_\theta(y_l)\big)\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: replace π* by the trainable policy πθ and maximise the likelihood of the human choices. r̂ = βhθ is the implicit reward, and r̂w, r̂l are its values for the two replies. The gradient raises the preferred reply’s log-probability and lowers the rejected one’s, weighted by σ(r̂l − r̂w), how badly the implicit reward currently ranks the pair. Same weighting as the reward model’s gradient, because it is a reward model’s loss.
Say it“The DPO loss is minus the expected log-sigmoid of beta times the winner’s log-ratio minus beta times the loser’s.”

A known weakness

When DPO slips the leash

DPO’s derivation assumes the true preference probabilities. Training sees a finite sample. If a reply never wins in the data (the usual case, since most datasets compare each prompt’s two replies just once), the best DPO can do is make its margins infinite: the loss keeps falling as its probability is pushed towards zero, and β no longer limits anything (Azar et al., 2024). An unregularised reward model fitted to the same counts would blow up too. In practice reward models are regularised, for example by early stopping, and that is part of why RLHF keeps its leash. Azar et al.’s fix, IPO, swaps the log-sigmoid for a squared error with a finite target margin. Below, four replies with known true rewards, and a small preference dataset sampled from them. Train for longer and shrink the dataset.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\text{IPO}}(\theta) &= \mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(m_\theta - \frac{1}{2\beta}\Big)^2\Big] \\ m_\theta &= h_\theta(y_w) - h_\theta(y_l) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: instead of rewarding ever-larger margins, IPO pulls each pair’s log-ratio gap towards a fixed target 1/(2β). A pair that A won 7 times out of 10 settles at a gap of (2·0.7 − 1)/(2β). A pair A won every time aims at 1/(2β): finite, however long you train. (When replies appear in several pairs, the shared log-ratios settle on a least-squares compromise.) (Azar et al. call the strength τ; it plays the part of β.)
Say it“The IPO loss is the expected square of the winner’s log-ratio minus the loser’s, minus one over two beta.”

Real-world project

Project: aligning to real human votes

Anthropic’s HH-RLHF dataset (Bai et al., 2022; MIT licence) is the preference data behind one of the first RLHF assistants. Crowdworkers chatted with a 52-billion-parameter model and, at each turn, picked the more helpful of two replies. We use the helpful-base split: 43,646 training and 2,343 test comparisons (we drop the 200 of 46,189 pairs whose two conversations differ before the last reply, or whose two replies are identical). A browser cannot run a 52B model, so this project is an honest miniature. Each reply is reduced to 11 counted features (its length, whether it apologises, asks questions, posts a link, and so on). The page embeds only those counts, for a random 3,000 training pairs and all 2,343 test pairs, plus the text of nine test pairs.

4 · Align a policy three ways

The policy answers each test prompt with one of its two replies. The reference is a fair coin, since both replies were sampled from the same base model. The policy tilts the coin: πθ(y | x) ∝ πref(y | x) eθ·φ(y). There are three ways to set θ. The exact optimum of the leash objective is θ = w/β. RLHF gets there by sampling, with the PPO loop of the section above, using the reward model as the reward. DPO never looks at the reward model and fits θ to the 3,000 training pairs directly. Then we ask the people: on the 2,343 test prompts, how often does the policy pick the reply the crowdworker preferred? Choose β and run both.

\[ \begin{aligned} \pi_\theta(y_1 \mid x) &= \sigma(\theta\cdot\Delta\phi) \\ \Delta\phi &= \phi(y_1) - \phi(y_2) \\ \text{DPO: }\ \hat r_\theta(y) &= \beta\,\theta\cdot\phi(y) + \text{const}(x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: with a fair-coin reference, the tilted policy is a logistic function of the feature difference. DPO’s implicit reward is βθ·φ. So the DPO loss is the reward-model loss with w = βθ, and its minimiser is θ = w/β, the exact RLHF optimum. For a policy this simple the two routes meet exactly. For a real LLM they need not: PPO samples fresh replies from the current policy, DPO only ever sees the fixed dataset, and neither optimiser reaches its optimum.
Say it“The policy picks reply one with the sigmoid of theta dot the feature difference; DPO’s implicit reward is beta theta dot phi.”

The same project in Python

The features come from al-prep.py, which reads the official helpful-base files and draws the 3,000 training pairs with NumPy seed 2022. The reference fit uses scikit-learn’s logistic regression on the differences, with each pair entered in both orders and no penalty. It solves the closed form with NumPy and fits DPO with SciPy:

import json, numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from scipy.optimize import minimize
from scipy.special import expit, log_expit

d = json.load(open("al-data.json"))            # 11 counted features per reply, from HH-RLHF helpful-base
def phi(F):
    F = np.array(F, float); F[..., 0] = np.log1p(F[..., 0]); return F   # words -> log(1 + words)
TR, TE = phi(d["tr"]), phi(d["te"])            # [pair, chosen/rejected, feature]
D_tr, D_te = TR[:, 0] - TR[:, 1], TE[:, 0] - TE[:, 1]   # phi(chosen) - phi(rejected)
n = len(D_tr)

# 1. Bradley-Terry reward model = logistic regression on differences, no intercept, no penalty
X = np.vstack([D_tr, -D_tr]); y = np.r_[np.ones(n), np.zeros(n)]
w = LogisticRegression(C=np.inf, fit_intercept=False, tol=1e-12, max_iter=10000).fit(X, y).coef_[0]
acc = lambda D, w: np.mean((D @ w > 0) + 0.5 * (D @ w == 0))
words = np.array(d["te"])[:, :, 0]
print("reward weights w:", np.round(w, 4))
print(f"BT loss {-log_expit(D_tr @ w).mean():.4f}   train acc {acc(D_tr, w):.4f}   test acc {acc(D_te, w):.4f}")
print(f"'longer reply wins' on test: {np.mean((words[:, 0] > words[:, 1]) + 0.5 * (words[:, 0] == words[:, 1])):.4f}")

# 2. The KL-regularised optimum on the 2,343 test prompts: pi_ref = 1/2 for each reply
print(" beta   P(policy picks the human's choice)   KL(pi||ref)   mean words")
for beta in [2, 1, 0.5, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05]:
    z = D_te @ w / beta; p = expit(z)          # pi*(chosen|x) = pi_ref e^{r/beta} / Z
    kl = np.mean(p * log_expit(z) + (1 - p) * log_expit(-z) + np.log(2))
    print(f"{beta:5}   {p.mean():.4f}   {kl:.4f}   {np.mean(p * words[:, 0] + (1 - p) * words[:, 1]):.2f}")

# 3. DPO with a log-linear policy pi_theta(y|x) ~ pi_ref(y|x) exp(theta . phi(y)), beta = 0.1
beta = 0.1
dpo = lambda t: -log_expit(beta * D_tr @ t).mean()
grad = lambda t: -(beta * expit(-beta * D_tr @ t))[:, None].__mul__(D_tr).mean(0)
theta = minimize(dpo, np.zeros(11), jac=grad, method="BFGS", options={"gtol": 1e-12}).x
print(f"DPO loss {dpo(theta):.4f}   max |beta*theta - w| = {np.abs(beta * theta - w).max():.2e}")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • People judge more reliably than they write or rate. Pairwise comparisons cancel each rater’s personal offset and scale.
  • A reward model is Bradley–Terry with a network inside: minimise −log σ(r(yw) − r(yl)). Only reward differences are learned.
  • RLHF maximises E[r] − β KL(π ‖ πref). Its exact solution is πrefer/β/Z, and PPO reaches it by sampling with the KL charged inside the reward.
  • A learned reward is a proxy. Optimise it too hard and the true quality peaks, then falls (Goodhart). The KL leash and early stopping are the defences.
  • DPO reads the reward off the policy, β log(πθ/πref), and fits it to the pairs directly. Same optimum, no RL loop, but a reply that never wins in the data can pull it off the leash. IPO bounds the margin.

Check your understanding

Your reward model gives every reply a score 5 higher than yesterday’s version. Does the RLHF policy change?

No. Adding a constant to every reward multiplies every er/β by the same factor, which Z divides out. Bradley–Terry cannot even see the shift, and PPO’s advantages subtract a baseline. Only reward differences matter. (In practice reward models are often normalised to mean zero for numerical comfort.)

What happens to π* as β → 0, and as β → ∞?

As β → ∞, er/β → 1 and π* = πref: no change. As β → 0, all probability goes to the highest-reward reply among those the reference can produce. That reply is the one most likely to exploit the reward model’s errors.

Why does the log Z(x) term disappear from the DPO loss?

It depends only on the prompt x, and both replies in a pair answer the same prompt. Bradley–Terry uses only the reward difference r(x, yw) − r(x, yl), so the two copies of β log Z(x) cancel. That is what makes DPO tractable: Z sums over every possible reply.

After RLHF, a model’s answers are 40% longer, but people do not prefer them more. What probably happened?

Reward hacking through a length bias. In the training comparisons longer replies tended to win, so the reward model learned “longer is better”, and the policy exploited it beyond the range where it was true. Remedies: penalise length or control for it in the reward model, use a larger β or stop earlier, collect comparisons that include long replies, ensemble reward models.

DPO’s training loss is nearly zero, yet the policy’s answers got worse. How can that be?

A low loss only says the implicit reward gap h(yw) − h(yl) is large. That can be achieved by making the rejected replies vanishingly unlikely, or by lowering both replies’ probabilities, the loser’s faster. When a rejected reply never wins anywhere in the data, as with one comparison per prompt, the gap can grow without limit and the policy drifts far from the reference. Remedies: early stopping, a larger β, more data per pair, or a bounded loss such as IPO.
