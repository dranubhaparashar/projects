Off‑policy Traces

Q(λ), Tree-Backup(λ) and the stable variants: carrying credit backwards when the actions were chosen by a different policy

An eligibility trace is a multi-step return written backwards: every TD error is handed back to the states and actions that led to it, decaying by γλ per step. The previous two chapters gave you traces (on-policy) and off-policy learning (one step at a time). Put them together and the importance-sampling ratios move inside the trace, where they multiply. The figure follows one episode of a tiny corridor task and plots the trace of its first action under four rules. Drag λ up and watch the ratio-based trace spike while the others stay tame.

The problem · 12.9

Why traces and importance ratios clash

Two ideas from the last two chapters are about to meet. From chapter 3.2c: an eligibility trace zt is a vector with one entry per weight that remembers which features were active recently, fading by γλ each step, and every TD error δt is applied to all weights in proportion to their trace. That is TD(λ) and Sarsa(λ), and it was on-policy: the actions in the data were drawn from the policy being learnt about. From chapter 3.2b: when the behaviour policy b that chose the actions differs from the target policy π we want to learn about, each update is re-weighted by the importance-sampling ratio ρt = π(At|St) / b(At|St). One step, one ratio.

A multi-step return has many steps, and every action between the state being updated and the reward needs its own ratio. The ratios multiply. Since a trace is nothing but a multi-step return written backwards, the ratios must go inside the trace: the trace component of a state–action pair visited k steps ago is (γλ)k times a product of k ratios. Two things follow, and both are visible in the figure. If any intermediate action is one the target would never take (π = 0), the whole product is zero: the credit is cut. If the target likes an action more than the behaviour does (ρ > 1), a run of such actions multiplies up, and the trace can grow instead of fading. Each ratio has mean exactly 1 under the behaviour policy, so the product has mean 1 too; but its second moment is Σa π(a|s)²/b(a|s) per step, which exceeds 1 whenever π ≠ b, so the variance of the product grows geometrically with the number of steps. That geometric growth, tamed or avoided, is the whole subject of this chapter.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E_b[\rho_t] &= \sum_a b(a\mid s)\,\frac{\pi(a\mid s)}{b(a\mid s)} = 1, \qquad \mathbb E_b[\rho_t^2] = \sum_a \frac{\pi(a\mid s)^2}{b(a\mid s)} \;\ge\; 1 \\ \text{trace of a pair } n \text{ steps back: } \; (\gamma\lambda)^n \prod_{i=1}^{n}\rho_i, \quad &\text{mean } (\gamma\lambda)^n, \quad \text{s.d. } (\gamma\lambda)^n\sqrt{\big(\mathbb E_b[\rho^2]\big)^n - 1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: averaged over the behaviour’s choices, a ratio is worth exactly one, so re-weighting is unbiased. Its square averages to more than one by Jensen’s inequality (equality only when π = b). For a pair visited n steps ago the trace coefficient is the decayed product of the n ratios in between; the mean is the on-policy value (γλ)n, but the standard deviation carries the factor (E[ρ²])n/2, which doubles every few steps. The formula assumes the ratios at successive steps are independent with the same distribution; in the figure they are, because the policies do not depend on the cell.
Say it“A ratio averages to one, its square to more than one; so a product of n ratios averages to one with a standard deviation that grows like the n-th power.”

One remedy from the previous chapter does not carry over directly. Weighted importance sampling divides a sum of ratio-weighted returns by the sum of the ratios, which needs all the episodes of a batch at once; a trace is updated one transition at a time and has no denominator to divide by. Mahmood, van Hasselt and Sutton (2014) built a weighted-IS version of least-squares TD, and the importance-weight-aware steps of section 11.9 still apply, but the within-trace fixes of this chapter are different in kind: they do not re-normalise the ratios, they replace them, by zero (the cut), by π(A|S) (Tree-Backup) or by min(1, ρ) (Retrace).

The symbols this chapter leans on

  • ρt = π(At|St) / b(At|St)The importance-sampling ratio of the action taken at time t: how much more likely the target was to choose it than the behaviour. Zero if the target never takes it; Eb[ρt] = 1.
  • ctThe trace coefficient: the number the old trace is multiplied by (together with γλ) when action At is taken. ρt for importance sampling, π(At|St) for Tree-Backup, min(1, ρt) for Retrace, 1 for no correction.
  • ztThe eligibility trace, a vector with one entry per weight. Accumulating form: zt = γλctzt−1 + ∇q̂(St, At). In a lookup table ∇q̂ is a one-hot vector and the trace is a table of the same shape.
  • δts, δtaThe state-value TD error Rt+1 + γv̂(St+1) − v̂(St) and the action-value (Expected-Sarsa) error Rt+1 + γV̄t(St+1) − q̂(St, At), with V̄t(s) = Σa π(a|s) q̂(s, a).
  • Gtλs, GtλaThe off-policy λ-returns for states and for actions, the forward-view targets whose backward views are the traces above. Approximately equal to the current estimate plus the trace-weighted sum of later TD errors.
  • Ft, MtEmphatic-TD’s follow-on trace and emphasis: scalars that say how much the current update should count, given how the target policy would have arrived here.
  • AλThe matrix of the expected update, w ← w + α(bλ − Aλw). Positive definite on the feature span means the expected iteration contracts; the stable variants are stable because they change this matrix.

The maths · 12.9

The off-policy λ-return, with control variates

Start forward, with state values, and build the return one step at a time. The on-policy λ-return of chapter 3.2c mixes the n-step returns with weights (1−λ)λn−1; written recursively it is Gtλ = Rt+1 + γ[(1−λ) v̂(St+1) + λ Gt+1λ]: take one step, then either bootstrap (weight 1−λ) or keep going (weight λ). The off-policy version of Sutton and Barto’s section 12.9 multiplies that whole step by the ratio of the action that produced it, ρt, and then adds a second term, (1 − ρt) v̂(St). That second term is a control variate. Its expectation under the behaviour policy is zero (because Eb[ρt] = 1), so it changes nothing on average; but it is negatively correlated with the first term, so it cancels much of the noise. Read it as a correction for over- or under-counting: if the ratio was large, the first term over-counts and the control variate subtracts some of the current estimate; if the ratio was zero, the target collapses to v̂(St) itself, which says “an action the target would never take teaches nothing”, not “the value is zero”.

State values: the off-policy λ-return with a control variate
\[ G_t^{\lambda s} \;\doteq\; \rho_t\Big( R_{t+1} + \gamma\big[(1-\lambda)\,\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf w_t) + \lambda\, G_{t+1}^{\lambda s}\big]\Big) \;+\; (1-\rho_t)\,\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w_t) \]
Read it as: the first bracket is the ordinary recursive λ-return, re-weighted by this step’s ratio (per-decision importance sampling: later rewards pick up later ratios through the recursion, not this one). The added term has mean zero and is the control variate. At a terminal state G = 0. With λ = 0 and ρ = 1 this is the TD(0) target; with λ = 1 it is a per-decision importance-sampled Monte Carlo return with control variates at every step. The textbook writes it with state-dependent γt and λt; constants are used here.
Say it“Rho times the one-step continuation, plus one minus rho times the current estimate.”

Now unroll it. Subtract v̂(St) from both sides and collect terms, and the return becomes the current estimate plus a sum of TD errors, each multiplied by the ratios and decays accumulated since time t (the equality is approximate because the weights move between steps). A forward view that is a sum of decayed TD errors always has a backward view: a trace that collects the decays, applied to each TD error as it arrives. The only novelty is that the ratio of each action enters the trace once, when the action is taken, and is then carried forward to every later error.

The same return as a sum of TD errors, and its trace
\[ \begin{aligned} G_t^{\lambda s} &\approx \hat v(S_t,\mathbf w_t) + \rho_t \sum_{k=t}^{\infty} \delta_k^{s} \prod_{i=t+1}^{k} \gamma\lambda\rho_i, \qquad \delta_t^{s} = R_{t+1} + \gamma\,\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf w_t) - \hat v(S_t,\mathbf w_t) \\ \mathbf z_t &= \rho_t\big(\gamma\lambda\,\mathbf z_{t-1} + \nabla\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w_t)\big), \qquad \mathbf w_{t+1} = \mathbf w_t + \alpha\,\delta_t^{s}\,\mathbf z_t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the error at time k reaches back to time t through the product of the ratios and decays of every step in between, and the ratio of time t itself multiplies everything because, for a state value, the action at t must also be corrected. The trace does this incrementally: multiply yesterday’s trace by γλ, add today’s gradient, then scale the whole thing by today’s ratio. This is off-policy TD(λ) with importance sampling and control variates; it is semi-gradient, and it is the algorithm Baird’s counterexample will test in section 07.
Say it“z is rho times gamma-lambda z-previous plus the gradient; w moves by alpha delta z.”

For action values the bookkeeping shifts by one step. The pair q̂(St, At) is about an action that was actually taken, so its own ratio is not needed; it is the next action, At+1, whose ratio first appears. The TD error becomes the Expected-Sarsa error, which bootstraps on the target policy’s average of the next state’s action values, V̄t(s) = Σa π(a|s) q̂(s, a, wt), and the control variate appears as a correction to how much the actual continuation differed from that average.

Action values: λ-return, TD error and trace
\[ \begin{aligned} G_t^{\lambda a} &\doteq R_{t+1} + \gamma\Big( \bar V_t(S_{t+1}) + \lambda\,\rho_{t+1}\big[G_{t+1}^{\lambda a} - \hat q(S_{t+1},A_{t+1},\mathbf w_t)\big] \Big), \qquad \bar V_t(s) \doteq \sum_a \pi(a\mid s)\,\hat q(s,a,\mathbf w_t) \\ \delta_t^{a} &= R_{t+1} + \gamma\,\bar V_t(S_{t+1}) - \hat q(S_t,A_t,\mathbf w_t), \qquad G_t^{\lambda a} \approx \hat q(S_t,A_t,\mathbf w_t) + \sum_{k=t}^{\infty}\delta_k^{a}\prod_{i=t+1}^{k}\gamma\lambda\rho_i \\ \mathbf z_t &= \gamma\lambda\,\rho_t\,\mathbf z_{t-1} + \nabla\hat q(S_t,A_t,\mathbf w_t), \qquad \mathbf w_{t+1} = \mathbf w_t + \alpha\,\delta_t^{a}\,\mathbf z_t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: one step of reward, then the target policy’s expected value of the next state, plus λρt+1 times the surprise in the actual continuation. In the trace the ratio now multiplies only the old trace, never the new gradient: the pair just visited gets a full trace of 1 and is corrected only by the ratios of the actions that come after it. Every method in sections 03 to 05 is this trace with something else in place of ρt, and the stable GQ(λ) of section 08 uses it as written.
Say it“z is gamma-lambda rho z-previous plus the gradient of q-hat; the ratio corrects the past, not the present.”

Two steps by hand. Take the corridor with b(right) = 0.5, so a right move has ρ = 1.8, and use the exact vπ as the estimate: vπ(3) = 0.860 and vπ(4) = 0.977 for the target π(right) = 0.9 (vπ(4) is below 1 because the target itself goes left one time in ten). Episode: from cell 3, right to cell 4, right to the goal, reward +1, with λ = 0.8. Work backwards. At cell 4 the return is G1 = 1.8 × 1 + (1 − 1.8) × 0.977 = 1.018 with the control variate and 1.800 without. At cell 3, G0 = 1.8 × 0.9 × [0.2 × 0.977 + 0.8 × G1] + (1 − 1.8) × 0.860 = 0.948 with, against 2.649 without. Both are samples of the same mean, vπ(3) = 0.860; this episode was a lucky one (two target-favoured moves), and the control variate has pulled its target back from 1.79 above the truth to 0.09 above. Now an unlucky one: left to cell 2 (ρ = 0.2), then right, right, right to the goal. Without the control variate the return is 0.562, because the 0.2 kills nearly everything; with it, 0.845, because the (1 − 0.2) × v̂(3) term puts most of the current estimate back. Across many episodes the two sets of targets average to the same place; the control-variate set is simply much tighter, as the figure shows.

  • λ = 0The λ-return collapses to ρt(Rt+1 + γv̂(St+1)) + (1−ρt)v̂(St) = v̂(St) + ρtδt: the previous chapter’s one-step off-policy TD(0), whose update is αρtδt∇v̂. The trace is just ρt∇v̂(St).
  • λ = 1No bootstrapping at all: a per-decision importance-sampled Monte Carlo return with a control variate at every step. Unbiased for vπ, and the variance of section 01 in full.
  • ρ ≡ 1On-policy: the control variate vanishes, the trace is γλzt−1 + ∇v̂, and the method is TD(λ) of chapter 3.2c. Everything new on this page is what ρ ≠ 1 does.
  • ρt = 0An action the target would never take. The target becomes v̂(St): the pair teaches nothing, and the trace is wiped, so nothing before it learns from anything after it. This is the cut of section 03, derived rather than imposed.

How much does the control variate buy? The figure introduces the corridor, the tiny task used through the middle of the chapter: five cells in a row, actions left and right, walking off the right end pays +1 and ends the episode, walking off the left end pays 0 and ends it, every other step pays 0, γ = 0.9. Control · 12.10

Watkins’s Q(λ) and Peng’s Q(λ)

Chris Watkins met this problem in 1989, in the thesis that introduced Q-learning, a decade before the ratio machinery above existed. Q-learning’s target policy is greedy, so π(a|s) is 1 for the greedy action and 0 for every other: the ratio is 0 for an exploratory action and 1/b(a|s) for the greedy one. Watkins’s Q(λ) keeps the trace decaying by γλ as long as the behaviour keeps choosing greedy actions and cuts it to zero the moment it takes an exploratory one; the pair that took the exploratory action starts a fresh trace of its own. Its λ-return is the usual mixture of n-step returns up to the first exploratory action, with all the remaining weight placed on the return that stops there and bootstraps with maxa q̂. The cut is the honest part: after the behaviour has left the greedy path, nothing that follows says anything about the greedy policy’s value. Dropping the factor 1/b(a|s) is the convenient part, and section 04 will show it was justified all along.

Watkins’s Q(λ), trace form
\[ \begin{aligned} \delta_t &= R_{t+1} + \gamma\,\max_a \hat q(S_{t+1},a,\mathbf w_t) - \hat q(S_t,A_t,\mathbf w_t) \\ \mathbf z_t &= \mathbb 1\big[A_t \in \arg\max_a \hat q(S_t,a,\mathbf w_t)\big]\;\gamma\lambda\,\mathbf z_{t-1} + \nabla\hat q(S_t,A_t,\mathbf w_t), \qquad \mathbf w_{t+1} = \mathbf w_t + \alpha\,\delta_t\,\mathbf z_t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the one-step Q-learning error, applied through a trace that decays normally when the action just taken was greedy and is wiped when it was not. The indicator plays the role of ρt in the action-value trace of section 02, with the ratio’s 1/b replaced by 1. Ties for the maximum count as greedy. In a lookup table this is the familiar “decay e, or zero it on an exploratory action” rule.
Say it“Decay the trace by gamma-lambda if the action was greedy, otherwise start over; then add the gradient and apply the Q-learning error.”
Watkins’s λ-return, forward view
\[ G_t^{\lambda} = (1-\lambda)\sum_{n=1}^{\tau-t-1}\lambda^{\,n-1}\,G_{t:t+n} \;+\; \lambda^{\,\tau-t-1}\,G_{t:\tau}, \qquad G_{t:t+n} \doteq R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \dots + \gamma^{\,n-1}R_{t+n} + \gamma^{\,n}\max_a \hat q(S_{t+n},a) \]
Read it as: τ is the first time after t at which the behaviour takes a non-greedy action (or the end of the episode). Up to τ the n-step returns are the ordinary Q-learning n-step returns, bootstrapped with max, mixed with the usual weights (1−λ)λn−1; all the weight that would have gone to longer returns is given to the return truncated at τ. If the very next action is exploratory (τ = t+1), the sum is empty and the target is the one-step Q-learning target. The trace form of the previous box is the backward view of exactly this return.
Say it“Mix the n-step Q-learning returns up to the first exploratory action; give the rest of the weight to the return that stops there.”

The cost is obvious once you count. With an ε-greedy behaviour, an exploratory action arrives every 1/ε steps on average, so at ε = 0.1 a trace rarely survives ten steps; and early in learning, when the greedy action changes from update to update, many actions that were greedy when chosen look exploratory a moment later. Watkins’s Q(λ) slides towards one-step Q-learning exactly when long traces would help most. Jing Peng and Ronald Williams (1994, 1996) proposed to never cut. In Peng’s Q(λ) the pair just visited receives the ordinary Q-learning error, while every earlier pair receives a different error through the trace: δ′t = Rt+1 + γ maxa q̂(St+1, a) − maxa q̂(St, a), a “max-to-max” error that compares the greedy value of the state left with the greedy value of the state entered. Its λ-return follows the behaviour’s path for n steps and then bootstraps greedily, so it is a hybrid of Sarsa(λ) and Q-learning: for a fixed behaviour its fixed point lies somewhere between qb and q*, and it reaches q* only if the behaviour itself becomes greedy in the limit. A third rule, often implemented by accident, is the naive one: Sarsa(λ)’s uncut trace with Q-learning’s max target. Its fixed point is still q*, because the Q-learning error has mean zero at q* whatever happened before, but the iteration is guaranteed to contract only for small λ (Harutyunyan et al., 2016, give λ < (1−γ)/(2γ), which is 0.056 here).

Peng’s Q(λ): two errors, no cut
\[ \begin{aligned} \delta_t &= R_{t+1} + \gamma \max_a \hat q(S_{t+1},a) - \hat q(S_t,A_t), \qquad \delta'_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma \max_a \hat q(S_{t+1},a) - \max_a \hat q(S_t,a) \\ \mathbf z_t &\leftarrow \gamma\lambda\,\mathbf z_{t-1}, \quad \mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w + \alpha\,\delta'_t\,\mathbf z_t \;\;(\text{earlier pairs}), \quad \mathbf z_t \leftarrow \mathbf z_t + \nabla\hat q(S_t,A_t), \quad \mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w + \alpha\,\delta_t\,\nabla\hat q(S_t,A_t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the λ-return behind it is (1−λ)Σnλn−1[Rt+1 + … + γn−1Rt+n + γn maxa q̂(St+n, a)], whose successive differences are exactly the δ′ terms; so the current pair gets δt and every earlier pair gets δ′t through its trace. At q* the max-to-max error is not zero for a non-greedy action (compare γv*(St+1) with v*(St) after a bad move), which is why the fixed point is biased.
Say it“Earlier pairs get the max-to-max error through an uncut trace; the current pair gets the ordinary Q-learning error.”

The figure learns q* for the corridor from a fixed behaviour policy that goes right with probability p, so all three learners see the same stream of experience (same seed, same actions) and differ only in their traces. The exact q* is known (q*(s, right) = 0.94−s), so the error can be measured honestly. Lower p and watch Watkins’s learner slow down as its traces get cut more often, while Peng’s settles at a bias that grows.

Watkins’s Q(λ), tabular, in full
initialise Q(s, a) = 0 for all s, a
for each episode:
    z(s, a) = 0 for all s, a;  S = start
    repeat until S is terminal:
        A = behaviour's action at S                    # e.g. epsilon-greedy on Q
        take A, observe R, S'
        A* = argmax_a Q(S, a)                           # the greedy action *before* the update; ties count as greedy
        delta = R + gamma * max_a Q(S', a) - Q(S, A)    # max_a Q(terminal, a) = 0
        if Q(S, A) == Q(S, A*):  z = gamma * lam * z    # greedy: decay
        else:                    z = 0                  # exploratory: cut
        z(S, A) += 1                                    # accumulating trace (or z(S, A) = 1 for a replacing one)
        Q += alpha * delta * z
        S = S'

Which to use? With an ε-greedy behaviour whose ε is small, Watkins’s Q(λ) is the safe default: correct fixed point, traces that survive about 1/ε steps, and nothing to tune beyond λ. With a very exploratory behaviour it degenerates to one-step Q-learning, and Peng’s is faster but lands in the wrong place; the Tree-Backup view of the next section explains why there is no free lunch here, and the Retrace coefficient of section 05 is the modern compromise. What one should not do is use Sarsa(λ)’s uncut trace with the max target and hope: it happens to work on the corridor, and that is not a property of the method.

Expectation instead of ratio · 12.10

Tree-Backup(λ): no ratios at all

Recall the n-step tree backup from chapter 3.2a. Walk down the sampled path St, At, Rt+1, St+1, …. At each state along the way, the actions that were not taken become leaves of a tree, each contributing its current estimate q̂(s, a) weighted by the target policy’s probability π(a|s); the action that was taken is the branch along which the path continues, and it contributes whatever comes next weighted by its own probability π(A|S). Nothing is divided by b. The expectation under π is formed explicitly over the untaken actions, and the taken action is simply given the probability π would have given it. The one-step tree backup is Expected Sarsa; Tree-Backup(λ), due to Precup, Sutton and Singh (2000), is the λ-mixture of the n-step tree-backup returns.

The n-step tree-backup return, recursively
\[ G_{t:t+n} \doteq R_{t+1} + \gamma\sum_{a\neq A_{t+1}}\pi(a\mid S_{t+1})\,\hat q(S_{t+1},a,\mathbf w) + \gamma\,\pi(A_{t+1}\mid S_{t+1})\,G_{t+1:t+n}, \qquad G_{t+n-1:t+n} = R_{t+n} + \gamma\,\bar V(S_{t+n}) \]
Read it as: one reward, then the target’s probability-weighted estimates of all the actions not taken, then the probability of the action that was taken times the rest of the return along the sampled path. The recursion bottoms out after n steps with the expected value at the last state. The one-step case is Expected Sarsa. No probability of the behaviour policy appears anywhere.
Say it“Reward, plus the leaves weighted by pi, plus pi of the taken action times the rest.”
The Tree-Backup λ-return and its trace
\[ \begin{aligned} G_t^{\lambda a} &\doteq R_{t+1} + \gamma\Big( (1-\lambda)\bar V_t(S_{t+1}) + \lambda\Big[\sum_{a\neq A_{t+1}}\pi(a\mid S_{t+1})\,\hat q(S_{t+1},a,\mathbf w_t) + \pi(A_{t+1}\mid S_{t+1})\,G_{t+1}^{\lambda a}\Big]\Big) \\ &= R_{t+1} + \gamma\,\bar V_t(S_{t+1}) + \gamma\lambda\,\pi(A_{t+1}\mid S_{t+1})\big[G_{t+1}^{\lambda a} - \hat q(S_{t+1},A_{t+1},\mathbf w_t)\big] \\ &\approx \hat q(S_t,A_t,\mathbf w_t) + \sum_{k=t}^{\infty}\delta_k^{a}\prod_{i=t+1}^{k}\gamma\lambda\,\pi(A_i\mid S_i) \\ \mathbf z_t &= \gamma\lambda\,\pi(A_t\mid S_t)\,\mathbf z_{t-1} + \nabla\hat q(S_t,A_t,\mathbf w_t), \qquad \mathbf w_{t+1} = \mathbf w_t + \alpha\,\delta_t^{a}\,\mathbf z_t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: after the reward, either bootstrap on the expected value (weight 1−λ) or go one level deeper (weight λ), where the untaken actions are leaves and the taken one continues with weight π(At+1|St+1). The second line regroups: the untaken leaves plus the taken action’s own estimate add up to V̄t(St+1), leaving the surprise of the continuation weighted by γλπ. Compare with section 02: ρt+1 has become π(At+1|St+1), the numerator alone. The TD error δa is the same Expected-Sarsa error as before.
Say it“z is gamma-lambda times pi of the action taken, times z-previous, plus the gradient.”

Why is it legitimate to drop the denominator? Because the TD error already contains the expectation over π at the next state. Importance sampling tries to re-weight the sampled path so that it looks as if π had chosen the actions; Tree-Backup does not try. It accepts that the path was sampled by b, weights each continuation by the probability π would have assigned, and lets the leaves carry the rest of the expectation. In expectation the update is a mixture of n-step tree-backup updates whose weights depend on the behaviour, but every leaf and every continuation is weighted by π, so the fixed point is qπ for any behaviour that explores. The price is paid in trace length: for a stochastic target the trace is multiplied by π(A|S) < 1 at every step, even when the behaviour did exactly what the target would have done, so Tree-Backup’s effective λ is λ·π(A|S) and its credit fades faster than it needs to.

Now set the target policy greedy. Then π(A|S) is 1 when the behaviour takes the greedy action and 0 when it does not; the trace decays by γλ or is wiped, and V̄ is the max. That is Watkins’s Q(λ), exactly. Watkins’s omission of 1/b was not a shortcut: Tree-Backup is the derivation he did not have. A unifying view

One recursion, four traces

Everything so far fits one line. The action-value trace is zt = γλ ct zt−1 + ∇q̂(St, At), the update is w ← w + αδta zt with the Expected-Sarsa error, and the methods differ only in the trace coefficient ct, the number by which the past is multiplied when action At is taken. Importance sampling uses ct = ρt: unbiased re-weighting of the sampled path, with the variance of section 01. Tree-Backup uses ct = π(At|St): no ratio, no blow-up, but credit fades even when the behaviour agrees with the target. Retrace(λ) (Munos, Stepleton, Harutyunyan and Bellemare, 2016) uses ct = min(1, ρt): the ratio when it is below one, and one otherwise, so the trace never grows and is not shrunk when the behaviour is at least as fond of the action as the target. And the naive rule uses ct = 1, no correction at all; Harutyunyan et al. (2016) call it Qπ(λ) and show it converges to qπ as long as λγε ≤ 1 − γ, where ε is the largest total-variation distance between π(·|s) and b(·|s) over the states.

Two facts organise the comparison. First, every one of these rules has qπ as a fixed point, because the Expected-Sarsa error has mean zero at qπ given the pair (St, At), whatever the trace that multiplies it (the trace depends only on the past). What differs is whether the expected iteration contracts towards that fixed point for any behaviour (importance sampling, Tree-Backup and Retrace do; the naive rule only under the condition above) and how much variance the sampled updates carry. Second, the trace coefficient also decides how far credit travels: the expected number of steps a unit of credit survives is governed by γλEb[ct], which is γλ for importance sampling and the naive rule, smaller for Tree-Backup, and in between for Retrace.

The choice need not be all-or-nothing within an episode. The n-step Q(σ) of chapter 3.2a lets each step decide, with a parameter σt ∈ [0, 1], whether to sample (Sarsa-style, which off-policy means a ratio) or to expect (tree-backup style, which means π); its λ-trace has coefficient ct = σtρt + (1 − σt)π(At|St), which interpolates between the first and the second rule above. The ABQ(ζ) idea mentioned in section 09 goes one step further and chooses λt itself per step so that the product with the ratio stays bounded. The table of five is really a continuum, and the two ends of it, re-weight the sample or form the expectation, are the two ways there have ever been to learn off-policy.

Four questions to ask of a trace coefficient

  • Fixed pointIs qπ (or q*) a fixed point of the expected update? For the action-value trace with the Expected-Sarsa (or max) error the answer is yes for every ct, because the error has conditional mean zero at the fixed point and the trace depends only on the past. Peng’s Q(λ) fails this test, because its max-to-max error does not.
  • ContractionDoes the expected iteration move towards the fixed point for every behaviour that explores? Yes for ρ, π(A|S) and min(1, ρ), whose products never exceed the corresponding target-policy products in expectation; for ct = 1 only when λγε ≤ 1 − γ. This is a tabular statement; with function approximation it becomes the positive-definiteness question of sections 07 and 08.
  • VarianceHow spread out are the sampled updates? Governed by Eb[ct2]: Σaπ(a|s)2/b(a|s) ≥ 1 for ρ (can be enormous), Σa b(a|s)π(a|s)2 ≤ 1 for Tree-Backup, at most 1 for Retrace, exactly 1 for the naive rule.
  • ReachHow far back does credit travel? The expected coefficient is Eb[ct]: 1 for ρ and for the naive rule (credit travels as far as on-policy), Σa b(a|s)π(a|s) < 1 for Tree-Backup (shorter, even when b = π), in between for Retrace. Retrace is the only one that scores well on all four, which is why it became the default in deep off-policy actor–critics.

Stability · 12.11

Traces meet the deadly triad

Ratios inside traces are a variance problem. With function approximation they also inherit the stability problem of chapter 3.2b: bootstrapping, off-policy data and function approximation together can diverge, and a trace does not remove any leg of that triad. The test case is again Baird’s counterexample: seven states, eight weights, every reward zero so the true value is zero everywhere and representable (w = 0). The behaviour policy makes the next state uniform over all seven (dashed action with probability 6/7 to a random upper state, solid with 1/7 to the lower one); the target always takes the solid action; γ = 0.99. So ρ = 7 on a solid step and 0 on a dashed one, and the importance-sampling trace of section 02 is zt = ρt(γλ zt−1 + xt): wiped on every dashed step, multiplied by 7 on every solid one.

Does λ rescue plain off-policy TD(λ)? In expectation the update is w ← w − αAλw, and the iteration is stable for small α exactly when the symmetric part of Aλ is positive definite on the span of the features. The matrix has a clean form: the behaviour’s state distribution Db, the expected trace (I − γλPπ)−1, and the TD operator (I − γPπ). At λ = 1 the last two cancel and A1 = XTDbX, the Monte Carlo matrix, which is always positive definite: no bootstrapping, no triad. At λ = 0 it is the matrix of the previous chapter, which is not. In between, the page computes the smallest eigenvalue live: drag λ and watch it stay negative until λ is about 0.99 (the sign changes at λ = 0.988: a bisection on the live matrix finds −0.09 at 0.985 and +0.08 at 0.99). In other words, on this problem the trace has to be almost pure Monte Carlo before the behaviour’s weighting stops mattering. And even where the expected update is stable, the sampled runs are wild, because the sampled trace multiplies 7s.

  • Function approximationv̂(i) = 2wi + w8 for the six upper states, v̂(7) = w7 + 2w8 for the lower one: eight weights for seven states, so the representation is not merely adequate but redundant, and w = 0 is one of many exact solutions.
  • BootstrappingEvery TD error is γv̂(St+1) − v̂(St) with γ = 0.99: the target is almost entirely the estimate at the next state. A trace with λ < 1 still bootstraps, on a mixture of later states; only λ = 1 stops.
  • Off-policy dataThe behaviour visits all seven states equally; the target, if it ran, would sit in state 7 for ever. The updates are weighted by the former while the errors follow the latter. This mismatch is the leg that traces cannot remove: neither the ratios nor the trace change which states get updated how often.
Expected off-policy TD(λ) and its stability matrix
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E_b\big[\delta_t\,\mathbf z_t\big] &= \mathbf b_\lambda - A_\lambda\,\mathbf w, \qquad A_\lambda = X^{\top} D_b\,(I - \gamma\lambda P_\pi)^{-1}(I - \gamma P_\pi)\,X, \qquad \mathbf b_\lambda = X^{\top} D_b\,(I - \gamma\lambda P_\pi)^{-1}\,\mathbf r_\pi \\ \text{stable for small } \alpha &\iff \mathbf y^{\top}A_\lambda\,\mathbf y > 0 \;\text{ for every } \mathbf y \neq 0 \text{ in the span of the features} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: X stacks the feature vectors, Db is diagonal with the behaviour’s state frequencies (uniform 1/7 here), Pπ is the target’s transition matrix (every row puts all its mass on state 7), and rπ = 0. The factor (I − γλPπ)−1 = Σk(γλPπ)k is the expected trace: how much each state’s feature vector is still present when the error at a later state arrives. The trouble is Db: the states are weighted as the behaviour visits them, while the trace and the TD operator follow the target. At λ = 1 the two bracketed factors cancel and only Db remains, which is harmless. The eigenvalue readout is of the symmetric part (A + AT)/2 restricted to the seven-dimensional span of the features; the eighth direction is seen by no state and is left out.

The fixes · 12.11

GTD(λ), GQ(λ) and Emphatic-TD(λ)

The previous chapter’s two repairs both extend to traces. GTD(λ) (Maei, 2011; also called TDC(λ)) does stochastic gradient descent on the λ-weighted projected Bellman error. It keeps the importance-sampling trace of section 02 and, as TDC did, a second weight vector v that tracks the least-squares fit of the TD errors to the features; the only new ingredient is that the correction term is scaled by γ(1−λ), because the part of the bootstrapped target that the correction has to account for is the (1−λ) share that bootstraps immediately. At λ = 1 the correction vanishes and the method is importance-sampled Monte Carlo, which needs no help.

GTD(λ), state values
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf z_t &= \rho_t\big(\gamma\lambda\,\mathbf z_{t-1} + \mathbf x_t\big), \qquad \delta_t^{s} = R_{t+1} + \gamma\,\mathbf w_t^{\top}\mathbf x_{t+1} - \mathbf w_t^{\top}\mathbf x_t \\ \mathbf w_{t+1} &= \mathbf w_t + \alpha\,\delta_t^{s}\,\mathbf z_t - \alpha\,\gamma(1-\lambda)\,(\mathbf z_t^{\top}\mathbf v_t)\,\mathbf x_{t+1} \\ \mathbf v_{t+1} &= \mathbf v_t + \beta\,\delta_t^{s}\,\mathbf z_t - \beta\,(\mathbf v_t^{\top}\mathbf x_t)\,\mathbf x_t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the first part of the w update is plain off-policy TD(λ); the second subtracts, along the next feature vector, the component of the error that bootstrapping would otherwise feed back on itself. v is updated by least-mean-squares towards E[δz] / E[xxT] and must learn faster than w (β larger than α). In expectation the update is −AλTC−1(Aλw − bλ), a true gradient of a quadratic, so the matrix governing it is AλTC−1Aλ, positive semi-definite for every λ however bad Aλ itself is.
Say it“TD(λ) with ratios, minus alpha gamma one-minus-lambda, z-dot-v, along the next feature vector; v does LMS on the TD errors.”

GQ(λ) (Maei and Sutton, 2010) is the action-value version. It uses the Expected-Sarsa error with the target policy’s average feature vector at the next state, x̄t+1 = Σa π(a|St+1) x(St+1, a), the action-value trace zt = γλρtzt−1 + xt from section 02, and the same correction, now along x̄t+1. With a greedy target the average feature vector is the feature vector of the greedy action and the method is Greedy-GQ(λ) (Maei, Szepesvári, Bhatnagar and Sutton, 2010), the stable cousin of Watkins’s Q(λ); the project runs it. Note what it does not fix: its trace still carries the full ratios, so when the behaviour strays far from the target the trace’s growth factor γλ/b(greedy) can exceed one and the variance problem of section 01 returns in full. Stability in expectation and sanity of the samples are different properties.

GQ(λ), action values
\[ \begin{aligned} \bar{\mathbf x}_t &\doteq \sum_a \pi(a\mid S_t)\,\mathbf x(S_t,a), \qquad \delta_t^{a} = R_{t+1} + \gamma\,\mathbf w_t^{\top}\bar{\mathbf x}_{t+1} - \mathbf w_t^{\top}\mathbf x_t, \qquad \mathbf z_t = \gamma\lambda\,\rho_t\,\mathbf z_{t-1} + \mathbf x_t \\ \mathbf w_{t+1} &= \mathbf w_t + \alpha\,\delta_t^{a}\,\mathbf z_t - \alpha\,\gamma(1-\lambda)\,(\mathbf z_t^{\top}\mathbf v_t)\,\bar{\mathbf x}_{t+1}, \qquad \mathbf v_{t+1} = \mathbf v_t + \beta\,\delta_t^{a}\,\mathbf z_t - \beta\,(\mathbf v_t^{\top}\mathbf x_t)\,\mathbf x_t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: xt is the feature vector of the pair (St, At); in a lookup table it is a one-hot vector and w is the Q table. The correction is applied to the pair the target would move to on average (the greedy pair, for a greedy target). Everything else is GTD(λ) with the action-value bookkeeping of section 02.
Say it“Expected-Sarsa error, ratio trace, and the gradient correction along the target’s average next feature.”

Emphatic-TD(λ) (Sutton, Mahmood and White, 2016) takes the other road: keep the TD update, change how much each one counts. A scalar follow-on trace Ft accumulates, discounted and importance-weighted, how much the states that led here were cared about; the emphasis Mt mixes the immediate interest It (weight λ) with the follow-on (weight 1−λ); and the eligibility trace is built from Mtxt instead of xt. At λ = 0 this is the Emphatic-TD of the previous chapter; at λ = 1 the emphasis is just the interest and the method is importance-sampled Monte Carlo. The reason it is stable is the same at every λ: in expectation the emphasis turns the behaviour’s state weighting Db into the emphatic weighting Mλ, and with that weighting in front, the matrix of the previous section becomes positive definite for every λ, every π and every b (Sutton, Mahmood and White prove the expected-update result; Huizhen Yu, 2015, proved that the sampled algorithm converges with probability one, despite the unbounded variance of F).

Emphatic-TD(λ) and why its matrix is positive definite
\[ \begin{aligned} F_t &= \gamma\,\rho_{t-1}\,F_{t-1} + I_t, \qquad M_t = \lambda\,I_t + (1-\lambda)\,F_t \\ \mathbf z_t &= \rho_t\big(\gamma\lambda\,\mathbf z_{t-1} + M_t\,\mathbf x_t\big), \qquad \mathbf w_{t+1} = \mathbf w_t + \alpha\,\delta_t^{s}\,\mathbf z_t \\ A^{\text{ETD}}_\lambda &= X^{\top} M_\lambda\,(I - \gamma\lambda P_\pi)^{-1}(I - \gamma P_\pi)\,X, \qquad \mathbf m_\lambda^{\top} = \lambda\,\mathbf d_b^{\top} + (1-\lambda)\,\mathbf d_b^{\top}(I - \gamma P_\pi)^{-1} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: F starts at the interest (1 everywhere here) and grows along runs of target-favoured actions, by a factor γρ = 6.93 per solid step in Baird’s problem. Mλ is diagonal with the emphatic weighting mλ: at λ = 0 the behaviour’s distribution pushed forward through the target’s dynamics (the lower state collects a weight of 99 against 1/7 for each upper state), at λ = 1 the behaviour’s distribution itself, and a mixture in between. Multiplying out, mλ makes the row sums of the bracketed operator behave as they do on-policy, which is the property that made on-policy TD(λ) stable in the first place.
Say it“F is gamma rho-previous F-previous plus the interest; M mixes interest and follow-on; the trace is rho times gamma-lambda z plus M x.”

Reach for GTD(λ) / GQ(λ) when…

  • you want an objective: the λ-weighted projected Bellman error, and a guarantee that holds for any λ and any step-size pair with β faster than α;
  • you can afford a second weight vector and a second step size, and you will tune β;
  • the ratios are tame, because the trace still carries them in full (or you switch its coefficient to Retrace’s);
  • you learn action values for control: Greedy-GQ(λ) is the stable counterpart of Watkins’s Q(λ).

Reach for Emphatic-TD(λ) when…

  • you want to keep the plain TD update and a single weight vector, with one extra scalar per step;
  • the states you care about are not the states the behaviour visits most: the interest It lets you say so, and the emphasis passes it on to the states the target would reach;
  • you can live with a follow-on trace of unbounded variance, by capping it, averaging iterates or shrinking α on spikes;
  • you want the on-policy flavour of convergence: the TD fixed point under the emphatic weighting, with the usual TD error bound.

In practice · 12.12

Implementation issues

Cost. A trace has one entry per weight, and TD(λ) touches every entry on every step: decay all of them, then update all of them. For a lookup table over a big state space, or a linear approximator with many features, that turns an O(number of active features) method into an O(number of weights) one. With sparse, binary features, such as tile coding, the way out is to notice that only recently active components are non-negligible. Keep a list of the components whose trace exceeds a threshold θ, drop a component when it decays below θ, and the work per step is proportional to the length of that list. A component that was active k steps ago has trace (γλ)k (on-policy, accumulating), so it stays on the list for about lnθ / ln(γλ) steps, and with m features active per step the list holds about m·lnθ/ln(γλ) entries. The figure simulates exactly this with eight tilings over a one-dimensional random walk. Push λ towards 1 and watch the list grow towards the full weight vector; push θ up and watch it shrink, together with the share of the trace mass you throw away.

Read it as: m is the number of features active at each step (one per tiling), θ the cut-off, and the ratio of logarithms the number of steps a fresh component needs to decay below θ. Which trace. The accumulating trace adds the gradient to the decayed trace (every formula on this page). The replacing trace sets a revisited binary feature’s component to 1 instead of adding; it was a popular fix for the accumulating trace’s tendency to over-credit states visited many times, but it has no counterpart for general features and no clean forward view. The dutch trace of chapter 3.2c, zt = γλzt−1 + (1 − αγλzt−1Txt)xt, is the one that makes the backward view exactly equal to the online λ-return with linear features; van Hasselt, Mahmood and Sutton (2014) extended that equivalence to the off-policy case, with ratios in the trace and a correction term of their own, and Mahmood, Yu and Sutton (2017) built ABQ(ζ), an action-dependent bootstrapping scheme in which the trace coefficient is chosen per step so that the product λtρt never exceeds one, a Retrace-like idea derived from the λ-return side.

Episodes and resets. Traces are reset to zero at the start of every episode; at a terminal state the next value is zero and nothing is bootstrapped. In continuing tasks they are never reset, and off-policy traces with ratios then need the attention the figures above suggest: watch the trace norm, log the largest ratio, and prefer Retrace or Tree-Backup coefficients. Emphatic-TD’s follow-on trace Ft is the extreme case: its expectation is finite but its variance is not, so practical implementations cap it, average the iterates, or shrink the step size when it spikes.

Step size. With traces, the effective step size of one update is αδztTxt, and ztTxt can be well above one when a state is revisited or a ratio multiplies up. Importance-weight-aware steps and normalised step sizes (the previous chapter’s remedies) carry over directly: choose α so that α·zTx stays below one, or scale by it.

Choosing λ off-policy. On-policy, λ trades bias for variance and a value around 0.8 to 0.9 is a common sweet spot. Off-policy the effective decay is γλEb[ct], so the same λ means a shorter trace under Tree-Backup (Eb[π(A|S)] < 1) and the same trace length but far more variance under ratios. A reasonable procedure: pick the trace length you want in steps, 1/(1 − γλEb[c]), solve for λ given the coefficient rule, and then check the growth factor of the coefficient along runs of target-favoured actions; if it exceeds one, lower λ or clip.

Deep networks and minibatches. For a neural network the trace is as large as the parameter vector and must be updated after every step, in order, which is incompatible with sampling minibatches from a replay buffer and with running many actors in parallel. Modern deep RL therefore uses the forward view: compute a truncated λ-return, or a Retrace target, from a stored sequence and regress on it. IMPALA’s V-trace (Espeholt et al., 2018) is exactly section 02’s per-decision importance-sampled return for state values with the ratios clipped twice, once for the TD error and once for the trace coefficient; ACER and Reactor use Retrace for action values. The backward view of this chapter is the cheapest way to learn online from a single stream; the forward view is the practical way to learn from stored, parallel, off-policy data. They compute the same thing.

Where off-policy traces live in modern systems

per-decision ratios, clipped twice

V-trace (IMPALA)

Hundreds of actors generate trajectories under slightly stale policies; the learner corrects them with the state-value λ-return of section 02, truncated to n steps, with the ratio in the TD error clipped at ρ̄ and the trace coefficient clipped at c̄ (usually both 1). Forward view, computed on a GPU over a batch of sequences.

min(1, ρ) in the trace

Retrace (ACER, Reactor, Agent57)

Action-value targets computed from replayed sequences with the Retrace coefficient: unbiased fixed point, bounded variance, and credit that travels as far as the behaviour agreed with the target. The forward-view twin of the backward-view race of section 05.

c = 1, deliberately, with short n

n-step returns in replay (Ape-X, R2D2)

Uncorrected n-step returns from a replay buffer are the naive rule with a hard truncation at n = 3 or 5. Small n keeps λγε small in the sense of section 05, and in practice the bias is accepted for the sake of speed. Knowing it is the naive rule tells you when it will bite: when the stored policy is far from the current one.

The sparse trace list, for binary features
live = {}                                   # feature index -> trace value, only entries above theta
for each step:
    decay = gamma * lam * c_t               # c_t: the rule's coefficient for the action just taken
    for i in list(live):                    # O(|live|), not O(number of weights)
        live[i] *= decay
        if abs(live[i]) < theta: del live[i]
    for i in active_features(S_t, A_t):     # m of them, one per tiling
        live[i] = live.get(i, 0.0) + 1.0
    for i, z_i in live.items():
        w[i] += alpha * delta_t * z_i       # the update touches only the live entries

With a general (non-binary, non-sparse) approximator there is no list to keep short: the trace is dense, one entry per parameter, and the step costs a full pass over it. That is the cost one pays for a neural network, and the reason the forward view wins there.

Project

Project: cliff walking, three off-policy learners (and a fourth for contrast)

Sutton and Barto’s cliff walking gridworld, 4 rows by 12 columns. The agent starts bottom-left and must reach the goal bottom-right; every step costs −1, and the ten cells between start and goal along the bottom row are a cliff: stepping on one costs −100 and sends the agent back to the start without ending the episode. The optimal policy hugs the cliff edge and reaches the goal in 13 steps, return −13; a safer policy along the top row needs 17. The behaviour policy is ε-greedy with respect to the learner’s own current Q, so it explores and occasionally falls; the target is the greedy policy, so this is off-policy control, and the question is which trace rule learns the greedy policy best from ε-soft experience.

Four learners share the environment and, run by run, the same random seed. Watkins’s Q(λ): cut on exploration, max target. Tree-Backup(λ) for a target that is almost greedy, επ-soft with επ = 0.1 by default: it learns a policy that keeps a little exploration, and the greedy policy it induces prefers the safer path, so its curve settles at −15 rather than −13 (set επ to 0 and it becomes Watkins’s Q(λ) exactly, curve for curve). Greedy-GQ(λ): the stable gradient method of section 08 with full ratios in its trace and β = α. Peng’s Q(λ), the uncut hybrid, for contrast. The discount is γ = 0.95 rather than the textbook’s 1: with γ = 1 a policy that walks into a wall for ever has no finite value, the projected Bellman error has no minimiser, and Greedy-GQ, which descends it, cannot be expected to work; the three Q(λ) variants do not care. Experiments to run

  1. Push the behaviour away from the target. Set ε = 0.3 and λ = 0.9. GQ’s growth factor becomes 0.95 × 0.9 / 0.775 = 1.10: every greedy step now multiplies its trace, and within a few episodes its largest trace component is in the thousands and its greedy policy never reaches the goal. Watkins and Tree-Backup, whose coefficients never exceed one, are unaffected. (The Python output’s last line is this experiment.)
  2. Make Tree-Backup greedy. Set επ = 0. The Tree-Backup curve now lies exactly on Watkins’s, episode for episode, because with a greedy target π(A|S) is the indicator of the greedy action and V̄ is the max: the two algorithms are the same algorithm.
  3. Turn traces off. Set λ = 0. All four learners become one-step methods (Q-learning, Expected Sarsa with a soft target, one-step Greedy-GQ, Q-learning again), the differences between the trace rules vanish, and learning slows: credit for reaching the goal now travels one cell per episode.
  4. Take bigger steps. Set α = 0.5. The accumulating trace’s revisits (walking into a wall adds 1 to the same entry every step) make the effective step αzTx exceed one and Q oscillates; GQ, whose correction is also scaled by α, suffers most. This is the step-size remark of section 09 in action.
  5. Explore less. Set ε = 0.05. Watkins’s traces now survive about 20 steps and its early curve improves; but the behaviour rarely tries the cliff-edge cells, so finding the shortest path takes longer for everyone. Off-policy learning needs the behaviour to cover the target’s choices, not just to be close to them.

Reading the curves. Every curve starts at −100 (clipped): the greedy policy of an all-zero Q goes up, bumps the wall and never arrives. The first thing each learner must discover is that the goal exists, which only the behaviour’s random walk can do; from then on, how quickly the +1-free, −1-per-step signal propagates back depends on the trace. Watkins’s and Tree-Backup’s curves rise together and part at the end, one to −13 (or −14 on average, since an occasional run is still a step short at episode 300) and one to −15, because they are answering different questions. Peng’s curve rises early and then wanders, the signature of a fixed point that is not q*. GQ’s rises last: the gradient correction is scaled by (1−λ) and the ratio trace is noisier, so it learns the same thing more slowly, and it is the only one that can be broken by moving ε.

Extending the project

  1. Add function approximation. Replace the lookup table by tile coding over the (row, column) position with, say, four 2×2 tilings, so that neighbouring cells share weights. Watkins’s Q(λ) is then semi-gradient and no longer guaranteed; Greedy-GQ(λ) is. Baird’s problem says the difference can matter; this gridworld will tell you whether it does here.
  2. Swap GQ’s coefficient. Keep the gradient correction but use min(1, ρ) in the trace. The fixed point is unchanged, the growth factor can no longer exceed one, and the ε = 0.3 experiment should stop failing. Measure what it costs in learning speed at ε = 0.2.
  3. Learn two targets from one behaviour. Run Tree-Backup for the greedy target and for the 0.1-soft target on the same trajectories (same seed, one behaviour, two Q tables). This is what off-policy learning is for: several questions answered from one stream of experience.
  4. Try Retrace with replay. Store the episodes and recompute Retrace targets forwards from the stored sequences, as a deep-RL implementation would, then check that the forward and backward views agree on the fixed point.

import numpy as np

# ---------- the page's random-number generator, bit for bit ----------
def mulberry32(a):
    a &= 0xFFFFFFFF
    def rng():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = a
        t = ((t ^ (t >> 15)) * (1 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = (t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) ^ t
        t &= 0xFFFFFFFF
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return rng

# ---------- cliff walking: 4 x 12, start bottom-left, goal bottom-right, the cliff in between ----------
H, W, NA = 4, 12, 4
NS, START, GOAL = H * W, 3 * W, 3 * W + 11
DR, DC = [-1, 0, 1, 0], [0, 1, 0, -1]            # up, right, down, left

def step(s, a):
    r, c = divmod(s, W)
    r = min(H - 1, max(0, r + DR[a])); c = min(W - 1, max(0, c + DC[a]))
    s2 = r * W + c
    if r == 3 and 1 <= c <= 10: return START, -100, False   # fell off: back to the start, episode goes on
    if s2 == GOAL: return s2, -1, True
    return s2, -1, False

def greedy(Q, s):                                 # first maximum, so ties go to the lowest index
    return int(np.argmax(Q[s * NA:(s + 1) * NA]))

def eval_greedy(Q):                               # exact rollout of the greedy policy, at most 100 steps
    s, ret = START, 0
    for t in range(100):
        s2, r, done = step(s, greedy(Q, s)); ret += r
        if done: return ret, True
        s = s2
    return ret, False

def run(learner, seed, eps=0.2, eps_pi=0.1, lam=0.8, alpha=0.1, beta=0.1, gamma=0.95, episodes=300):
    rng = mulberry32(seed)
    Q, v, z = np.zeros(NS * NA), np.zeros(NS * NA), np.zeros(NS * NA)
    rets, oks, maxz, cuts, steps = [], [], 0.0, 0, 0
    for e in range(episodes):
        z[:] = 0; s = START
        for t in range(3000):
            gs = greedy(Q, s)
            a = int(rng() * NA) if rng() < eps else gs      # epsilon-greedy behaviour on the learner's own Q
            s2, rew, done = step(s, a)
            is_g = a == gs; b = eps / NA + ((1 - eps) if is_g else 0.0); i = s * NA + a; steps += 1
            if learner == 'peng':
                mx = 0.0 if done else Q[s2 * NA + greedy(Q, s2)]; mxs = Q[s * NA + gs]
                dp = rew + gamma * mx - mxs; d = rew + gamma * mx - Q[i]
                z *= gamma * lam; Q += alpha * dp * z            # earlier pairs: max-to-max error, trace never cut
                z[i] += 1; Q[i] += alpha * d                      # the current pair: ordinary Q-learning error
                maxz = max(maxz, z[i])
            else:
                if learner == 'watkins':
                    c = 1.0 if is_g else 0.0; cuts += 0 if is_g else 1
                    target = 0.0 if done else Q[s2 * NA + greedy(Q, s2)]
                elif learner == 'tb':
                    c = eps_pi / NA + ((1 - eps_pi) if is_g else 0.0)        # pi(A_t | S_t) for the eps_pi-soft target
                    if done: target = 0.0
                    else:
                        g2 = greedy(Q, s2); target = 0.0
                        for a2 in range(NA): target += (eps_pi / NA + ((1 - eps_pi) if a2 == g2 else 0.0)) * Q[s2 * NA + a2]
                else:                                             # gq: greedy target, ratio inside the trace
                    c = (1 / b) if is_g else 0.0
                    target = 0.0 if done else Q[s2 * NA + greedy(Q, s2)]
                d = rew + gamma * target - Q[i]
                z *= gamma * lam * c; z[i] += 1
                if learner == 'gq':
                    zv = 0.0
                    for k in np.flatnonzero(z): zv += z[k] * v[k]
                    vx = v[i]; j2 = -1 if done else s2 * NA + greedy(Q, s2)
                    Q += alpha * d * z; v += beta * d * z
                    if j2 >= 0: Q[j2] -= alpha * gamma * (1 - lam) * zv
                    v[i] -= beta * vx
                    maxz = max(maxz, float(np.abs(z).max()))
                else:
                    Q += alpha * d * z; maxz = max(maxz, float(z.max()))
            if done: break
            s = s2
        ret, ok = eval_greedy(Q); rets.append(ret); oks.append(ok)
    return np.array(rets), np.array(oks), Q, maxz, cuts / steps

Wrap-up · 12.13

Five lines to remember

  • A trace is a multi-step return written backwards, so off-policy the importance-sampling ratios go inside it and multiply. Each ratio averages to 1, but a product of n of them has a standard deviation that grows like (E[ρ²])n/2: zero most of the time, huge once in a while.
  • The off-policy λ-return with control variates is the clean forward view: per-decision ratios plus a zero-mean term (1−ρ)v̂ that cancels most of the noise. Its backward view is z = ρ(γλz + ∇v̂) for states and z = γλρz + ∇q̂ for actions, where the ratio corrects the past and never the pair just visited.
  • Every action-value method here is zt = γλctzt−1 + ∇q̂ with the Expected-Sarsa error; only ct changes: ρ (importance sampling), π(A|S) (Tree-Backup), min(1,ρ) (Retrace), 1 (naive). Watkins’s Q(λ) is Tree-Backup with a greedy target: cut on exploration, decay otherwise. Peng’s never cuts and converges to the wrong thing unless the behaviour turns greedy.
  • Traces do not remove the deadly triad: off-policy TD(λ) with ratio traces diverges on Baird’s counterexample for every λ below 0.988. GTD(λ)/GQ(λ) descend the projected Bellman error with a second vector and a correction scaled by γ(1−λ); Emphatic-TD(λ) re-weights by a follow-on trace so that its matrix is positive definite at every λ.
  • Stable in expectation is not the same as sane in samples. Watch the trace’s growth factor γλ·c along runs of target-favoured actions; prefer clipped or π-weighted coefficients; keep only trace components above a threshold; and in deep RL compute the same returns forwards, from stored sequences, as Retrace and V-trace do.

Six mistakes this chapter should stop you making

  • Ratio on the wrong termFor action values the ratio of At multiplies the old trace, never the gradient of the pair just visited; put it on the whole trace and you re-weight a pair for an action that was given, which biases nothing but doubles the variance for no reason. For state values it does multiply the whole trace, because the action at St is part of what v(St) averages over.
  • Cutting on the wrong testWatkins’s cut asks whether the action taken was greedy with respect to the current Q, ties included, not whether the behaviour’s random-number draw happened to be the exploratory branch. An “exploratory” draw that lands on the greedy action is a greedy action.
  • Sarsa’s trace with Q-learning’s targetThe naive rule. Its fixed point is right and its guarantee is for λ below (1−γ)/(2γ). It often works, which is exactly what makes it dangerous: it fails quietly when the behaviour drifts far from greedy.
  • Traces that survive the episode boundaryReset z at the start of every episode and treat the terminal state’s value as zero. A trace that leaks across episodes credits the end of one episode to the start of the next.
  • Judging stability from expected updates alonePositive definiteness is about the mean step. Emphatic-TD(λ) is the cautionary example: provably convergent, and a single sampled run can look like divergence for a thousand steps. Always look at the sampled runs, several seeds, and the largest trace component.
  • Undiscounted tasks with gradient methodsWith γ = 1, any policy that can loop for ever has no finite value and the projected Bellman error has no minimiser; GQ(λ) and GTD(λ) descend an objective that is not there. Discount, or make sure every policy terminates.

Check your understanding

Why does the action-value trace multiply the old trace by ρt (zt = γλρtzt−1 + xt) while the state-value trace multiplies the whole thing (zt = ρt(γλzt−1 + xt))?

A state value v(St) averages over the action at St under π, but the data contain b’s choice, so the error at time t must itself be re-weighted by ρt, and so must everything that error is credited to. An action value q(St, At) is about the action actually taken: no correction is needed for the current pair, and the Expected-Sarsa error already averages over the next action under π. The ratio of At is still needed by the earlier pairs, whose returns pass through At, which is why it multiplies the old trace.

In the by-hand episode the exploratory pair (3, left) received credit under every rule, even Watkins’s. Is that a mistake?

No. The cut removes credit from pairs before an exploratory action, because their greedy continuation was abandoned. The exploratory pair itself is the start of a new trace, and the actions after it were all greedy, so its return is a legitimate sample of what following the greedy policy from (3, left) yields. Its value should and does learn from the +1.

Tree-Backup never divides by b. Why is its fixed point still qπ, and what does it pay for the privilege?

Every bootstrapped leaf is weighted by π(a|s) and the path continues with weight π(A|S), so each backup is an expectation under the target policy over all actions at every depth; the behaviour only decides which n-step tree backups appear in the mixture, and all of them have qπ as fixed point. The price is that the continuation weight is π(A|S) < 1 even when the behaviour took exactly the action the target would have, so the trace fades faster than γλ and credit does not travel as far.

Why is off-policy TD(λ) with the importance-sampling trace stable on Baird’s problem at λ = 1 but not at λ = 0.9?

Its expected update matrix is XTDb(I−γλPπ)−1(I−γPπ)X. At λ = 1 the two bracketed factors cancel, leaving XTDbX, a weighted Gram matrix, positive definite whenever the features are linearly independent: that is Monte Carlo, with no bootstrapping and therefore no triad. For λ < 1 the operator still bootstraps, and the mismatch between Db (behaviour’s weighting) and Pπ (target’s dynamics) makes the symmetric part indefinite until λ is within about 0.01 of 1.

Emphatic-TD(λ) is stable at every λ in expectation, yet its sampled runs on Baird’s problem look terrible. Reconcile.

The follow-on trace multiplies by γρ = 6.93 on each solid step, so a run of k solid steps gives F about 6.93k; its expectation is finite but its variance is infinite, and a single long run produces one enormous update. Positive definiteness guarantees that the expected step points towards the fixed point; it says nothing about the spread of the sampled steps. In practice the emphasis is capped, the step size shrunk, or the iterates averaged.

In the project, GQ(λ) works at ε = 0.2 and collapses at ε = 0.3 with λ = 0.9. What number changed sign, so to speak?

The trace’s growth factor along a run of greedy actions, γλρgreedy = γλ/(1 − ε + ε/4): 0.95·0.8/0.85 = 0.894 at the defaults, 0.95·0.9/0.775 = 1.10 in the second case. Below one the trace of a pair fades as the greedy run continues; above one it grows with every greedy step, so a long greedy run produces trace components in the hundreds or thousands and the update overshoots. Watkins’s and Tree-Backup’s coefficients never exceed one, so they have no such factor.

Why does Tree-Backup(λ) with an επ-soft target settle at a greedy return of −15 on the cliff, while Watkins’s reaches −13?

Tree-Backup learns q for the soft target, a policy that falls off the cliff with probability about επ/4 on every edge step; for that policy the row above the cliff is genuinely worth more than the edge, so the greedy policy of its q takes the longer, safer path. Watkins’s learns q*, for which the edge is best. Same environment, same data, different question. This is the Sarsa-versus-Q-learning lesson of the tabular chapters, re-enacted by two trace rules.

A colleague implements GQ(λ) and finds it unstable on a problem where plain Q(λ) works. Name two things to check before blaming the theory.

First, the trace’s growth factor γλρ along runs of target-favoured actions: if it exceeds one, the problem is variance, not the expected update, and clipping the coefficient (Retrace) or lowering λ fixes it. Second, the discount and the step sizes: with γ = 1 and absorbing loops the projected Bellman error has no minimiser, and with β not faster than α the second vector lags and the correction uses stale estimates. Only after those are ruled out is the deadly triad a candidate, and then Baird’s problem is the test to run.
