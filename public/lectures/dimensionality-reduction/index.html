Dimensionality Reduction

Fewer numbers, the same story

A photo is a thousand numbers, a customer a hundred, a wine thirteen. Yet the numbers rarely vary independently: most of what they say can be told with far fewer. Dimensionality reduction finds those few directions and keeps them. On the right is a cloud of 380 points in three dimensions with a picture hidden inside. The idea

Many columns, few ideas

A row with 13 columns is a point in 13-dimensional space; an 8×8 image is a point in 64 dimensions. But columns move together. Wines rich in phenols are rich in flavanoids; neighbouring pixels in a photo are nearly the same grey. When two features are strongly correlated, one number along their shared direction says almost everything both said. Reducing dimensions pays four ways: we can see the data (we can only look at two or three axes), store less, remove noise that lives in the minor directions, and speed up later models while easing the curse of dimensionality from chapter 1.6.

Start with two standardised features (mean 0, standard deviation 1). Turn the correlation up and down, then try two real pairs from the UCI wine data.

\[ \begin{aligned} \Sigma &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & r \\ r & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda_{1,2} = 1 \pm |r| \\ \text{share}_1 &= \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2} = \frac{1+|r|}{2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: after standardising, the only thing two features can still tell us about their joint spread is their correlation r. The diagonal direction (1, ±1)/√2 carries variance 1 + |r| and the other diagonal 1 − |r|. At r = 0 no direction is special and each keeps half; at |r| = 1 the cloud is a line and one number says everything.
Say it“The two variances are one plus and one minus the absolute correlation; the main axis keeps one plus |r|, over two.”

Projection

Every view is a shadow

Squeezing two numbers into one means choosing a direction u and keeping each point’s shadow on it, z = u·(x − x̄). Whatever sticks out sideways, the residual r, is thrown away. Because the shadow and the residual are at right angles, Pythagoras splits every point’s squared distance from the centre into shadow² + residual². Add up over the points and the total variance splits into kept + lost, whatever the direction. So the direction that keeps the most is also the one that loses the least. Turn the direction and watch the two trade; you can drag the points too.

\[ \begin{aligned} z_i &= \mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{x}_i-\bar{\mathbf{x}}) \\ \mathbf{r}_i &= \mathbf{x}_i-\bar{\mathbf{x}}-z_i\,\mathbf{u} \\ \text{kept} &= \tfrac{1}{n-1}\textstyle\sum_i z_i^2 \\ \text{lost} &= \tfrac{1}{n-1}\textstyle\sum_i \lVert\mathbf{r}_i\rVert^2 \\ \text{kept} + \text{lost} &=\tfrac{1}{n-1}\textstyle\sum_i \lVert\mathbf{x}_i-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\rVert^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: u is a unit vector (length 1). zi is how far point i lies along u from the centre; ri is the part of it that u cannot describe. The right-hand side of the last line is the total variance of the data, fixed before we choose anything. Every direction divides that same total between kept and lost.
Say it“z-i is u dotted with x-i minus the mean; kept is the variance of the z’s, lost is the mean squared residual, and they add up to the total variance.”

The algorithm

PCA: the covariance’s own axes

Trying every angle works in two dimensions. With 1,024 pixels there are too many directions to try, so we need the answer in closed form. It comes from the covariance matrix Σ. The variance kept along any unit direction u is u⊤Σu, and the u that makes it largest is the top eigenvector of Σ. The eigenvectors are the principal components, their eigenvalues are the variances along them, and they are mutually perpendicular: PCA (Pearson 1901, Hotelling 1933) simply rotates the axes onto the cloud’s own axes. Drag the points and the ellipse follows. Then press Multiply by Σ and watch power iteration, the simplest eigenvector algorithm, swing a random arrow onto PC1.

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i &= \mathbf{x}_i-\bar{\mathbf{x}} \\ \Sigma &= \tfrac{1}{n-1}\textstyle\sum_i \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i^{\top} \\ \text{kept}(\mathbf{u}) &= \mathbf{u}^{\top}\Sigma\,\mathbf{u} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: x̃i is point i measured from the centre. Σ holds every variance (on the diagonal) and every covariance (off it). Squeeze the data onto a unit direction u and the variance of the shadows is u⊤Σu, so Σ alone answers “how much is kept?” for every direction at once.
Say it“Sigma is the average outer product of the centred points; the variance kept along u is u-transpose sigma u.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\Sigma\,\mathbf{u}_j = \lambda_j\,\mathbf{u}_j, \qquad \lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge 0 \\ &\textstyle\sum_j \lambda_j = \text{total variance} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: multiplying an eigenvector by Σ only stretches it, by λj. u₁ maximises u⊤Σu, and the maximum is λ₁; u₂ is the best direction at right angles to u₁, and so on. The eigenvalues share out the total variance exactly.
Say it“Sigma times u-j equals lambda-j times u-j; the lambdas, largest first, add up to the total variance.”
\[ \mathbf{v}_{t+1} = \frac{\Sigma\,\mathbf{v}_t}{\lVert\Sigma\,\mathbf{v}_t\rVert} \]
Read it as: write v as a mix of u₁ and u₂. Multiplying by Σ scales the u₁ part by λ₁ and the u₂ part by λ₂, so after rescaling to length 1 the u₂ part shrinks by the factor λ₂/λ₁ every step. The smaller that ratio, the faster v lands on PC1.
Say it“The next v is sigma times v, divided by its length.”

How many?

Explained variance: how many to keep?

With p features PCA returns p components, largest variance first. Keeping the first k gives the best k-dimensional linear summary there is: no other k directions keep more variance or leave a smaller squared reconstruction error (the Eckart–Young theorem). Choosing k is a judgement. Common rules: keep enough for 90% or 95% of the variance; cut at the “elbow” where the bars flatten; or let a downstream model choose by cross-validation. One warning first: PCA chases variance, and variance depends on units. Compare raw and standardised wine, then look at 64-pixel digits.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{EVR}_j &= \frac{\lambda_j}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\dots+\lambda_p} \\ \text{kept}(k) &= \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\text{EVR}_j \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the explained variance ratio of component j is its share of the total variance. The cumulative share kept(k) climbs to 1 at k = p. A steep start means a few directions tell most of the story; a slow, straight climb means the features really are independent and there is little to compress.
Say it“The explained variance ratio of component j is lambda-j over the sum of all the lambdas; kept of k adds up the first k.”

PCA as a codec

Compress, rebuild, spot the odd one

PCA is a compressor with a matching decompressor. Encode an image into k numbers, its coordinates on the first k components; decode by adding k “eigen-digits” to the average digit. The components below come from all 1,797 handwritten digits (8×8 pixels, ink 0–16). Real digits are built from a few common strokes, so a dozen components rebuild them well. A shape that is not a digit rebuilds badly, so the rebuild error doubles as an anomaly score. Draw on the pad (or load a real digit), then slide k.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{z} &= U_k^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}) \\ \hat{\mathbf{x}} &= \boldsymbol{\mu} + U_k\,\mathbf{z} \\ &= \boldsymbol{\mu} + \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{k} z_j\,\mathbf{u}_j \\ \lVert\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}\rVert^2 &= \textstyle\sum_{j>k} z_j^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Uk holds the first k components as columns and μ is the average digit. The code z is k numbers instead of 64. The rebuild is the average plus each component times its coordinate. Because the 64 components form a complete set of perpendicular axes, the squared error is exactly the energy in the coordinates we dropped.
Say it“z is U-k transpose times x minus mu; x-hat is mu plus U-k z; the error is the sum of the squared coordinates beyond k.”

The limit

When straight lines fail

PCA can only rotate and drop axes, so it flattens data the way a shadow flattens a hand: along straight lines. If the points lie on a curved sheet, like the rolled-up carpet called the Swiss roll, the shadow piles the layers on top of each other: points from opposite ends of the carpet land side by side. The variance kept can look respectable while the neighbourhoods are wrecked. So we need a second yardstick that asks what a map is for: does each point keep its neighbours? Pick a dataset and turn it in 3-D, then compare with PCA’s map.

\[ \text{kept}_k = \frac{1}{nk}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \big|\, N_k(i) \cap \hat N_k(i) \,\big| \]
Read it as: Nk(i) is the set of point i’s k nearest neighbours in the original space; N̂k(i) its k nearest on the map. Count how many survive, average over the points. 1 means every neighbourhood is intact; a random map scores about k/n. We use k = 10. The project uses trustworthiness (Venna & Kaski 2001), a refinement that also weighs how far away each intruder really was.
Say it“Kept-k is the average, over points, of how many of the k true neighbours are still among the k nearest on the map, divided by k.”

Non-linear maps

t-SNE: keep the neighbours close

t-SNE (van der Maaten & Hinton 2008) gives up on straight projections. It turns distances into neighbour probabilities, in the original space with a Gaussian around each point and on the map with a heavier-tailed Student-t curve, then moves the map points until the two sets of probabilities agree. Its one important dial, the perplexity, is roughly how many neighbours each point pays attention to. The page runs exact t-SNE with scikit-learn’s schedule (PCA start, 250 steps of early exaggeration, 1,000 in all). Press Run, then try another perplexity; click any point to see whom it calls a neighbour.

\[ \begin{aligned} p_{j|i} &= \frac{\exp(-d_{ij}^2/2\sigma_i^2)}{\sum_{k\ne i}\exp(-d_{ik}^2/2\sigma_i^2)} \\ 2^{H(P_i)} &= \text{perplexity} \\ p_{ij} & = \frac{p_{j|i}+p_{i|j}}{2n} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: dij is the distance between points i and j in the original space. Point i spreads its attention over the others with a Gaussian. Its width σi is found by binary search so that the effective number of neighbours, 2 to the power of the entropy H (in bits), equals the perplexity: points in dense regions get a narrow Gaussian, isolated points a wide one. pij averages the two directions into one symmetric probability.
Say it“p of j given i is a Gaussian of their distance, normalised over all k; sigma-i is tuned so two to the entropy is the perplexity.”
\[ \begin{aligned} t_{ij} &= \big(1+\lVert\mathbf{y}_i-\mathbf{y}_j\rVert^2\big)^{-1} \\ q_{ij} &= \frac{t_{ij}}{\sum_{k\ne l} t_{kl}} \\ \text{KL}(P\,\Vert\,Q) &= \textstyle\sum_{i\ne j}\, p_{ij}\log\dfrac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: yi is point i’s position on the map. There, similarity tij is a Student-t curve whose heavy tails let moderately distant points sit far apart, which cures the “crowding problem” of squeezing many dimensions into two. KL is large when true neighbours (big p) end up far apart (small q), and cheap when strangers end up close: t-SNE protects local structure, not global distances.
Say it“q-i-j is one over one plus the squared map distance, normalised; t-SNE minimises the KL divergence of P from Q.”
\[ \frac{\partial\,\text{KL}}{\partial \mathbf{y}_i} = 4\sum_{j}(p_{ij}-q_{ij})\,t_{ij}(\mathbf{y}_i-\mathbf{y}_j) \]
Read it as: every pair is joined by a spring. When p > q the pair should be closer and the spring pulls; when q > p it pushes. Gradient descent with momentum follows the springs. For the first 250 steps P is multiplied by 12 (“early exaggeration”), so clusters clump together before they settle.
Say it“The gradient for point i is four times the sum over j of p minus q, times t-i-j, times y-i minus y-j.”

The modern default

UMAP: a graph, then a layout

UMAP (McInnes, Healy & Melville 2018) works in two stages. First it builds a weighted k-nearest-neighbour graph, in which each point measures distance from its own nearest neighbour and in its own units σi, so dense and sparse regions get equally well-connected. Then it lays the graph out in 2-D: edges pull their ends together and randomly chosen non-neighbours push apart (“negative sampling”). It is much faster than t-SNE on large data, tends to keep more of the global arrangement, and can place new points on an existing map. The page runs a compact re-implementation of umap-learn’s algorithm: exact neighbours, its curve fitting, its sampling schedule, a PCA start. Change the number of neighbours and min_dist; switch the graph on to see what the layout is pulled by.

\[ \begin{aligned} w_{j|i} &= \exp\!\Big(-\frac{d_{ij}-\rho_i}{\sigma_i}\Big) \\ \textstyle\sum_{j} w_{j|i} &= \log_2 k \\ w_{ij} &=w_{j|i}+w_{i|j}-w_{j|i}\,w_{i|j} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: dij is the original distance, and kNN(i) the k nearest points to i, counting i itself as umap-learn does, so i has k − 1 weighted neighbours. ρi is the distance from i to its nearest neighbour, so that neighbour always gets weight 1 and no point is ever isolated. σi is found by binary search so i’s weights add up to log₂k. The two directed weights are merged as a fuzzy “or”: the chance that at least one of the two edges exists.
Say it“w of j given i is e to the minus distance-minus-rho-i over sigma-i; the weights sum to log-two of k; then combine the two directions as a plus b minus a b.”
\[ \begin{aligned} v_{ij} &= \frac{1}{1+a\,\lVert\mathbf{y}_i-\mathbf{y}_j\rVert^{2b}} \\ \text{CE} &= \textstyle\sum_{i\ne j}\, w_{ij}\log\dfrac{w_{ij}}{v_{ij}} \\ &\quad + (1-w_{ij})\log\dfrac{1-w_{ij}}{1-v_{ij}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a and b are fitted so that v stays near 1 out to the distance min_dist and then decays; min_dist sets how tightly points may pack on the map. The first term pulls graph neighbours together, the second pushes non-neighbours apart. UMAP never computes all n² terms: it samples edges in proportion to their weight, and a few random points per edge for the push.
Say it“v-i-j is one over one plus a times the map distance to the two b; UMAP minimises the fuzzy cross-entropy between w and v.”

Real-world project

Project: eigenfaces and a map of handwriting

The Olivetti faces were photographed at AT&T Laboratories Cambridge between 1992 and 1994: 400 grey-scale photos, ten each of 40 people, with varied lighting, expressions and glasses. scikit-learn ships them at 64×64 pixels; we average 2×2 blocks down to 32×32, so each face is 1,024 numbers. Each person’s tenth photo is held back: PCA learns from 360 faces and never sees the other 40. This is the method of Turk & Pentland’s Eigenfaces for recognition (1991), run in your browser.

The same project in scikit-learn

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import fetch_olivetti_faces, load_digits
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.manifold import TSNE, trustworthiness
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, LeaveOneOut
import umap                                                           # umap-learn 0.5

f = fetch_olivetti_faces(shuffle=False)
u8 = np.round(f.images.reshape(400, 32, 2, 32, 2).mean(axis=(2, 4)) * 255)   # 2x2 blocks, 8-bit
faces = u8.reshape(400, -1) / 255.0
person = np.repeat(np.arange(40), 10)
test = np.arange(400) % 10 == 9                                       # each person's 10th photo
pca = PCA(svd_solver="full").fit(faces[~test])
evr = pca.explained_variance_ratio_
print("faces: train", (~test).sum(), "test", test.sum())
print("explained variance, PCs 1-5:", np.round(evr[:5], 4))
cum = np.cumsum(evr)
print("components for 80/90/95% of the variance:", [int(np.searchsorted(cum, t) + 1) for t in (0.8, 0.9, 0.95)])
for k in (1, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 359):
    Z = pca.transform(faces)[:, :k]
    nn = KNeighborsClassifier(1).fit(Z[~test], person[~test])
    print(f"1-NN face recognition with k = {k:3d} components: {nn.score(Z[test], person[test]):.3f}")
raw = KNeighborsClassifier(1).fit(faces[~test], person[~test]).score(faces[test], person[test])
print(f"1-NN on raw pixels (1024 numbers):        {raw:.3f}")
x = faces[test][0]
for k in (10, 50, 150):
    z = (x - pca.mean_) @ pca.components_[:k].T
    xh = pca.mean_ + z @ pca.components_[:k]
    print(f"unseen face of person 1, k = {k:3d}: RMSE = {np.sqrt(np.mean((x - xh) ** 2)) * 255:.2f} grey levels")

d = load_digits()
X, y = d.data[:1000], d.target[:1000]
ts = TSNE(perplexity=30, init="pca", method="exact", random_state=0)
E = ts.fit_transform(X)
print(f"t-SNE (exact, 1000 digits): KL = {ts.kl_divergence_:.4f} after {ts.n_iter_ + 1} iterations")
P2 = PCA(2).fit_transform(X)
U = umap.UMAP(n_neighbors=15, min_dist=0.1, init="pca", random_state=0).fit_transform(X)
loo = lambda Z: cross_val_score(KNeighborsClassifier(1), Z, y, cv=LeaveOneOut()).mean()
for name, Z in (("PCA", P2), ("t-SNE", E), ("UMAP", U)):
    print(f"{name:6s} map: trustworthiness(10) = {trustworthiness(X, Z, n_neighbors=10):.4f}   1-NN accuracy in 2-D = {loo(Z):.3f}")

PCA is deterministic, and the page’s eigen-decomposition reproduces scikit-learn’s components to about 10−13, signs included (scikit-learn makes each component’s largest entry positive; so does the page). Every explained-variance ratio, recognition accuracy and rebuild error above therefore matches. t-SNE is a different story. The page builds exactly scikit-learn’s P (to 10−13), starts from the same PCA layout and follows the same update rule, and after five steps the two maps still agree to within 10−9. But the starting layouts differ in the seventh significant digit (float32 rounding), and t-SNE amplifies such differences: by step 20 the worst point is off by a quarter of the map’s spread, and by step 250 the two maps have gone their own ways. The end results are different but equally good maps, with the same KL divergence to within half a percent. UMAP here is a compact re-implementation, not umap-learn itself: its neighbour weights σi and ρi match umap-learn’s to float32 precision, and so do a and b, but its random numbers are its own, so its map is a different draw from the same recipe. That is why the table compares quality scores rather than coordinates.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Real features are redundant. When columns move together, a few directions carry most of the variance, and dropping the rest loses little.
  • PCA keeps the directions of largest variance: the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, ordered by their eigenvalues. The first k are the best k-dimensional linear summary, keeping the most variance and leaving the smallest squared rebuild error.
  • Explained variance says what you keep. Standardise first when features have different units, and choose k from the cumulative curve or from the task it serves.
  • PCA is a codec: z = Uk⊤(x − μ), x̂ = μ + Ukz. Twenty eigenface coordinates recognise people as well as all 1,024 pixels, and a large rebuild error flags something unusual.
  • For curved or clustered data, t-SNE and UMAP keep neighbours together where PCA cannot. Read their maps for which points are near which, never for cluster sizes or the distances between clusters.

Check your understanding

On raw wine data, PC1 “explains” 99.8% of the variance. Why is that not good news?

Proline is measured in units that run into the hundreds and thousands, so its variance dwarfs every other column and PC1 is essentially the proline axis. The 99.8% says “proline has the biggest numbers”, not “one direction describes wine”. Standardise, and PC1 drops to 36%, mixing many features.

PC1 explains 60% of the variance and PC2 25%. How much is lost if you keep two components, and why must PC2 be perpendicular to PC1?

15% is lost: the eigenvalues share out the total, so kept(2) = 85%. The eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix such as Σ are mutually perpendicular; PC2 is the best direction among those at right angles to PC1, so it carries no information PC1 already has (their scores are uncorrelated).

On a t-SNE map, cluster A looks twice as wide as cluster B and sits far from cluster C. What can you conclude?

Very little about sizes and gaps. t-SNE adapts each point’s σ to local density, so tight and loose clusters come out similar in size, and KL barely cares where unrelated clusters are placed. What you can trust is which points are neighbours of which.

Why can you send a new test example through PCA but not through the t-SNE on this page?

PCA is a fixed formula: z = Uk⊤(x − μ) works for any new x. t-SNE optimises a position for each training point directly; there is no function to apply to a new point, so you would have to re-run the whole map (parametric t-SNE and UMAP’s transform add one).

A machine’s sensor readings, projected on the top 10 components of normal operation, rebuild with three times the usual error. What might that mean?

The new reading has energy in directions that normal operation never uses: something unusual is happening. Rebuild error from PCA is a simple, widely used anomaly score; set its alarm threshold from the error distribution of known-normal data, as the digit pad does with real digits.
