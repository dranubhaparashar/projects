The applications chapter · 16

One recipe, eight systems

Chapter 16 of Sutton and Barto is the book’s gallery: a walk past systems that mattered, each built by people who had to make the ideas of Parts 1–3 survive contact with a real problem. Read as a group, the case studies are less varied than they look. Almost every one begins with a representation chosen by hand (features of a board, a queue, a user, a wind field), continues with a value function learned by temporal-difference updates, and gets its training data not from a labelled set but from interaction: self-play, a simulator, a live log. Where a model of the environment exists, search is layered on top of the learned values. Where it does not, the system learns on the job.

The eight studies below span sixty years. What made each one possible

  • Cheap interaction. Dice, a simulator, an emulator or a log replaced a labelled dataset; TD-Gammon played 1.5 million games, AlphaGo Zero 4.9 million, DQN 50 million frames per game.
  • Bootstrapping. Learning a prediction from the next prediction let credit travel without waiting for the end of a game, a round, a visit or a flight.
  • A value that turns choice into arithmetic. Once V or Q exists, choosing a move, a wager, a command or a bank angle is a maximisation, and search can be layered on top.
  • Function approximation that fits the problem. A sigmoid network for a board, a CMAC for a queue, a convolutional network for pixels, a 63-entry table for two binned cues.

What people still supplied

  • The representation. Which features, which bins, which frames to stack; the one decision no system in this chapter learned for itself.
  • The reward. Winning, clicks, bus cycles, metres climbed: simple to state, but each shaped what was learned (and the DRAM toy shows what a reward leaves out).
  • Stability engineering. Replay buffers, frozen targets, step sizes, exploration schedules, risk adjustments to Watson’s bets.
  • Verification. Off-policy evaluation before deployment; human champions as the test set; a real glider after the simulator.
Games · 16.1 and 16.2

TD-Gammon and Samuel’s checkers player

Backgammon has about 1020 positions and, because of the dice, a branching factor near 400: 21 distinct rolls, each with around 20 legal plays. Deep search is hopeless, so the quality of a program rests on its position evaluation. Gerald Tesauro’s TD-Gammon (1992–1995) learned that evaluation from nothing but self-play. A multilayer network took the raw board as input (198 units: for each of the 24 points, four units per colour encoding how many checkers stood there, plus units for the bar, the borne-off checkers and whose turn it was) and produced an estimate of the probability that White would win. After each move the network was nudged by TD(λ) toward its own estimate of the next position; at the end of a game it was nudged toward the actual result. The move chosen at every ply was simply the one whose afterstate the network liked best. No human games, no hand-written strategy.

Version 0.0, with 40 hidden units and 300,000 self-play games, was as good as Neurogammon, the best previous program, which had been trained on expert moves. Version 1.0 added Neurogammon’s hand-crafted features to the raw board; versions 2.0 and 2.1 added two-ply look-ahead; TD-Gammon 3.0 used 160 hidden units, three-ply search and 1.5 million games, and played at the level of the best humans, losing a 100-game exhibition to Malcolm Davis in 1998 by only 8 points. It changed how people play: its preferences in several opening rolls, once thought eccentric, became standard.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf z_t &= \gamma\lambda\,\mathbf z_{t-1} + \nabla_{\mathbf w}\hat v(S_t,\mathbf w_t), \qquad \mathbf z_{-1}=\mathbf 0 \\ \delta_t &= R_{t+1} + \gamma\,\hat v(S_{t+1},\mathbf w_t) - \hat v(S_t,\mathbf w_t) \\ \mathbf w_{t+1} &= \mathbf w_t + \alpha\,\delta_t\,\mathbf z_t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: zt is the eligibility trace, one entry per network weight: a decaying memory of how each weight influenced the recent predictions. δt is the TD error, the difference between consecutive predictions (or between the final prediction and the result, when RT is the 1 or 0 of a won or lost game). Every weight moves in proportion to its trace. TD-Gammon used γ = 1 (no discounting within a game), λ = 0.7 and α = 0.1; rewards are zero except at the end. For a sigmoid network ∇v̂ is obtained by back-propagation, so the trace of a weight in the first layer carries the whole chain of derivatives.
Say it“The trace decays by gamma-lambda and adds the gradient; the weights move by alpha times the TD error times the trace.”
  • TD-Gammon 0.0Raw board encoding only, 40 hidden units, 300,000 self-play games, no search. Roughly equal to Neurogammon, which had been trained on expert moves: the first evidence that self-play TD could reach a strong program’s level from scratch.
  • TD-Gammon 1.0Neurogammon’s hand-crafted features added to the raw board, 80 hidden units, 300,000 games, one-ply move selection. Lost by 13 points over 51 games to a group of world-class players (Robertie, Davis, Magriel): already a substantial expert.
  • TD-Gammon 2.0 / 2.1Two-ply search (consider the opponent’s 21 possible rolls and best replies), 40 then 80 hidden units, 800,000 then 1.5 million games. Version 2.1 lost by a single point over 40 games to Bill Robertie, then world champion.
  • TD-Gammon 3.0160 hidden units, three-ply search, 1.5 million games; won a 20-game match against Malcolm Davis by 6 points in 1998 and lost a longer 100-game exhibition to him by 8, i.e. within noise of the best humans. The figures follow Sutton and Barto’s Table 16.1.

Mini-Gammon: self-play TD(λ) you can watch

Real backgammon is too big for a page, so here is a race game with its two essential ingredients kept: dice and hitting. Each side has three checkers that start off the board and must travel the twelve points to bear off; one die is rolled per turn and the player chooses which checker to move. A lone checker (a blot) landed on by an opponent is sent back to the start; two or more checkers on a point make it, and the opponent cannot land there. The value network has 14 inputs (the sorted positions of all six checkers, the number of each side’s blots within range of an enemy, the pip lead, made points, checkers borne off and whose turn it is), a layer of sigmoid hidden units and one sigmoid output: the probability that White wins. Both sides pick the afterstate the network rates best for them, and the same network learns from both. Train it and watch the win rate against two fixed opponents: a random mover and a hand-written “safe” player that hits when it can, avoids leaving blots within range and otherwise advances its rear checker.

Samuel’s checkers player: the lineage

Thirty years before TD-Gammon, Arthur Samuel’s checkers program (1959, refined in 1967) on IBM’s first commercial computers already contained most of the recipe. It searched the game tree with minimax (later with alpha–beta-style pruning), scored the leaves with a linear evaluation polynomial over features such as piece advantage, mobility and centre control, and learned in two ways. Rote learning stored positions with their backed-up scores so that a later search could look them up and effectively search deeper. Learning by generalisation adjusted the polynomial’s weights so that the static score of a position moved toward the backed-up score obtained by searching below it. That second idea is temporal-difference learning in all but name: the deeper, later estimate is treated as the truth for the shallower, earlier one. Samuel also trained by self-play, with a learning copy (Alpha) playing a frozen copy (Beta) and replacing it whenever it won.

Two things were missing. The program received no reward for winning: its only anchor to reality was the piece-advantage term, whose weight Samuel fixed by hand, so that in principle the other weights could have drifted into a consistent but useless scoring scheme. And the backed-up target was a minimax value, not a sampled continuation, so the program could only learn what its own search could see. Move the two learnable weights in the toy below: the leaf scores and the backed-up root value both change, and the Samuel step pushes the static root score toward the backed-up one.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{score}(s) &= w_{\text{piece}}\,f_{\text{piece}}(s) + w_{\text{mob}}\,f_{\text{mob}}(s) + w_{\text{ctr}}\,f_{\text{ctr}}(s) \\ \Delta &= \underbrace{\text{backed-up}(s)}_{\text{minimax over the tree}} - \underbrace{\text{score}(s)}_{\text{static}} \\ w_i &\leftarrow w_i + \eta\,\Delta\, f_i(s) \quad (i \ne \text{piece}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the static score is linear in the features; the backed-up value is what minimax returns when the leaves two plies down are scored with the same polynomial. Samuel’s correction moved each adjustable weight so that the static score at the root agreed better with the backed-up score, holding the piece weight fixed. Written like this it is a one-step semi-gradient update toward a bootstrapped target: TD(0) with search supplying the next value.
Say it“Delta is the backed-up score minus the static score; each learnable weight moves by eta delta times its feature.”

To see exactly what the trace does, shrink the value function to something you can differentiate in your head: a linear estimate v̂(s, w) = w⊤x(s) with three features of a Mini-Gammon afterstate, always from White’s point of view: x1 = White’s pip lead divided by 10, x2 = White’s blots in danger minus Black’s, and x3 = 1 (a bias). The gradient of a linear function is just x(s), so the trace is a decayed sum of recent feature vectors. Start with w = (0.30, −0.20, 0.50), γ = 1, λ = 0.5 and α = 0.1. A short game: White takes a small lead, then exposes a blot while stretching the lead, gets hit and falls behind, and loses.

    Nothing else changes. For TD-Gammon’s sigmoid network the gradient of the output with respect to an output weight wj is y(1 − y)hj, and with respect to a first-layer weight wji it is y(1 − y)·wj·hj(1 − hj)·xi: the same quantities back-propagation computes for a supervised loss, but here there is no label, only δt. Each weight keeps its own trace, so a network with 198 inputs and 40 hidden units carries about 8,000 traces, one per weight, each decayed by γλ and incremented by its own partial derivative every ply. The Mini-Gammon demo above does exactly this (its net.trace and net.step functions are the two lines of the update), and the hand calculation is what those two lines do when the network collapses to a linear map.

    \( \mathbf w: (0.30,\,-0.20,\,0.50)\;\to\;(0.2955,\,-0.2236,\,0.4142) \)
    how exactly?
    The blot weight became more negative (−0.20 → −0.22): the exposed blot at S1 was followed by a hit and a loss, and its trace was still 0.5 at the terminal step, so part of the final error reached it. That is the point of λ: with λ = 0 the terminal error would have updated only x(S2), where the blot feature is 0, and the lesson about blots would have had to arrive one game-step at a time through the chain of δs.
    The bias fell (0.50 → 0.41): three states were predicted too optimistically in a lost game.
    The pip weight barely moved: the evidence about pips in this game was contradictory, and the trace arithmetic says so.
    Slide λ to 1 in the table: the traces stop decaying and the terminal update reaches S0 at full strength (Monte Carlo); slide it to 0 and each step learns only from the next.
Decisions under uncertainty · 16.3

Watson’s Daily-Double wagering

In February 2011 IBM’s Watson beat the two strongest human Jeopardy! champions, Ken Jennings and Brad Rutter, on live television. Most of the attention went to the question answering, but a quiz show is also a game, and Watson’s game strategy was built by Gerald Tesauro and colleagues with the tools of this part. The sharpest example is the Daily Double: a hidden clue on which the player who finds it must choose a wager before seeing the question, anything from a few dollars up to their whole score. Bet too little and a sure thing earns nothing; bet too much on a category you do not know and the game is gone.

Watson treated the wager as a one-step decision over a learned state-value function. The state was the game situation: the three scores, the money remaining on the board, how far the round had progressed. Its value V(s) was Watson’s probability of winning the game from s, learned by reinforcement learning from millions of simulated games against models of human players. The other ingredient was an in-category confidence p, the estimated probability that Watson would answer the clue correctly, taken from the question-answering system’s confidence on earlier clues in that category. The wager that maximises expected win probability is then a simple calculation, and the sliders let you do it: move Watson’s confidence from a shaky 50% to a confident 90% and watch the optimal bet jump; make Watson trail and watch it become willing to risk everything.

\[ \text{bet}^{*} = \arg\max_{b}\;\Big[\, p\,V\!\big(s^{+}_{b}\big) + (1-p)\,V\!\big(s^{-}_{b}\big) \Big], \qquad s^{\pm}_{b}: \text{Watson's score} \pm b \]
Read it as: for each legal bet b, imagine the two futures: right (score up by b) with probability p, wrong (score down by b) with probability 1 − p; weigh the learned value of each. The learned V makes the trade-off concrete: a dollar that lifts Watson from second place to a lock on first is worth far more than a dollar added to an already safe lead. Watson’s V was a nonlinear function approximator trained from simulation; the page uses a transparent stand-in described below. In practice Watson also tempered the maximising bet with a risk analysis, so its actual wagers were slightly more conservative than the raw maximiser.
Say it“Choose the bet whose confidence-weighted average of the two next-state values is largest.”
\[ V(\ell, n) \approx \Phi\!\left(\frac{\ell + 0.1\,c\,n}{c\sqrt{0.79\,n + 2}}\right), \qquad \ell = \text{Watson's score} - \text{best opponent's}, \quad c = \tfrac{\text{money left}}{n} \]
Read it as: the stand-in value function used on this page. The remaining n clues are each worth about c dollars; on each, Watson gains c with probability 0.45, the best opponent gains c with probability 0.35, nobody scores with probability 0.20, so the lead drifts by 0.1c per clue with variance 0.79c². Treat the final lead as Gaussian and add the equivalent of two more clues of noise for Final Jeopardy: the probability that the lead ends positive is a normal tail Φ. It has the two properties that matter here: it is steep when the game is close and flat when it is not. Watson’s real V, learned from simulation, encoded much more (three players, the lock-out thresholds before Final Jeopardy, typical human wagering).
Say it“The win probability is the normal tail of the expected final lead over its standard deviation.”

The rest of Watson’s game

The Daily Double is the cleanest of several strategic decisions Tesauro and colleagues built on the same value function, and together they show how a learned V(s) can be re-used across a game.

  • Which clue to pickWhen Watson controlled the board it chose squares, not only to score but to find the Daily Doubles, whose positions on the board follow known statistical patterns, and to deny them to opponents who were behind. The gain from each square was measured by the change in V.
  • Whether to buzzBuzzing on a clue with low confidence risks losing the clue’s value and handing it to an opponent. Watson buzzed when its confidence exceeded a threshold that itself depended on the game state through V: more cautious with a safe lead, more aggressive when behind and late in the game.
  • Final JeopardyAll three players wager simultaneously before the last clue. Watson’s wager came from a game-theoretic analysis against models of how humans in each score position tend to bet, again scored by the probability of ending ahead.
  • Risk adjustmentBecause V was learned from simulated opponents, the maximising Daily-Double bet could be over-confident. The deployed strategy shaded wagers toward the conservative side when the expected gain from the aggressive bet was small, a practical hedge against model error that the raw maximiser above does not include.
Systems · 16.4

Optimising memory control

A processor’s memory controller sits between the cores and the DRAM chips and decides, every clock cycle, which command to issue: activate a row into a bank’s row buffer, read or write a column of the open row, precharge to close it, or do nothing. Accesses to the open row are fast; anything else costs idle cycles while the bank is prepared. The standard policy, FR-FCFS (first-ready, first-come-first-served), always prefers a request that hits the open row and otherwise serves the oldest. İpek, Mutlu, Martínez and Caruana (2008) replaced the fixed policy with a learning one. The controller was a Sarsa agent implemented in hardware: its state was a handful of attributes of the transaction queue (how many reads and writes were waiting, how many of them hit open rows, the state of each bank), its actions were the legal DRAM commands, its reward was +1 for every read or write command (so that maximising return meant maximising data-bus utilisation), and its action values lived in a CMAC, the tile-coding approximator of chapter 3.1j, over the state attributes. In simulation it raised the throughput of a four-core chip by 19% on average over FR-FCFS, and learned a policy that no one had written: it would, for instance, keep the bus busy with writes it did not strictly need to do yet, so that reads did not later stall behind a write-queue drain.

The page cannot run a cycle-accurate DRAM, but it can run the decision that matters. One bank, eight rows, a queue of at most eight requests arriving with some row locality; a row hit takes 1 cycle, a miss needs 1 cycle to precharge and 2 to activate first. The scheduler chooses, at every decision, one of three meta-actions: serve the oldest request, serve the oldest row hit, or serve a request from the row with the most pending requests (opening that row sets up a run of hits). Its state is coarse: hits pending (0, 1, 2+), queue length (1–2, 3–5, 6–8) and the size of the largest same-row group (1, 2, 3+): 27 states. Train the scheduler, then race it against FCFS and FR-FCFS on the same arrival stream.

  • StateA handful of attributes of the transaction queue, read at the cycle of the decision: the numbers of pending reads and writes, how many pending requests hit currently open rows, whether the candidate command serves a read or a write, and the state of the banks. Continuous counts were tile-coded by the CMAC so that nearby queue states shared values.
  • Actions and rewardThe DRAM commands that are legal at that cycle under the timing constraints (precharge, activate, read, write, or wait), with +1 for a read or write and 0 otherwise, so that the return is the number of data-bus cycles used over the horizon set by γ.
  • Why on-policyThe controller acts and learns in the same loop, in hardware, with no simulator to rehearse in; Sarsa evaluates the policy actually being followed, which is what matters when exploration itself costs cycles.
  • ResultIn simulation of a four-core processor, 19% higher throughput on average than FR-FCFS across a set of parallel applications, and the learned policies adapted to each workload’s mix of reads, writes and row locality, which a fixed heuristic cannot.
\[ Q(S_t,A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t) + \alpha\Big[R_{t+1} + \gamma^{\,\tau_t}\,Q(S_{t+1},A_{t+1}) - Q(S_t,A_t)\Big], \qquad R_{t+1} = 1 - \kappa\,[\text{oldest request has waited} > 30], \quad \gamma = 0.9 \text{ per cycle} \]
Read it as: Sarsa over decisions, with one twist from the semi-Markov view of chapter 3.3a: τt is the number of clock cycles the decision occupied (1 for a row hit, up to 4 for a miss, plus any idle cycles before the next request arrived), and the future is discounted by γτ rather than γ. Every served request is worth 1, so a miss is not punished by a hand-set penalty but by time: it pushes the next unit of reward three cycles further into a discounted future, which at γ = 0.9 costs about a quarter of the value of a saturated bus. Maximising discounted return therefore maximises the rate of data cycles, which is bus utilisation. The optional κ term is reward shaping: it docks the scheduler when some request has been waiting more than 30 cycles, the only way this tiny state (which has no age attribute) can be nudged toward fairness. İpek et al. used the plain +1 reward with a richer state and a per-cycle formulation in which “wait” is itself an action.

Deep RL · 16.5

Human-level video game play

The Deep Q-Networks chapter built this system piece by piece: a network that scores every action from pixels, experience replay, a frozen target network, and the Double DQN fix for the optimism of the max. This section only places it in the gallery. What made Mnih and colleagues’ 2015 result a landmark was not any single game but the generality: one architecture, one set of hyper-parameters and one learning rule, applied unchanged to 49 Atari 2600 games, learned from the 210×160 screen and the score alone, with no game-specific knowledge. Each game is a different reinforcement-learning problem with a different reward scale, horizon and dynamics, and the same agent reached or exceeded the level of a professional human tester on 29 of them.

The engineering decisions were as important as the algorithm, and each is a choice from this part of the book. Frames were down-sampled to 84×84 greyscale and four consecutive frames stacked, so that velocities were observable and the state was approximately Markov. Rewards were clipped to {−1, 0, +1} so that a single step size worked across games whose scores differ by orders of magnitude. The agent acted every fourth frame. One million transitions were kept for replay; the target network was refreshed every 10,000 updates; ε was annealed from 1 to 0.1 over the first million frames; training ran for 50 million frames per game, about 38 days of play. Scores are reported as human-normalised percentages so that games can be compared: 0% is random play, 100% is the human tester. Drag the slider to see what a Breakout score is worth on that scale.

  • InputThe last four 84×84 greyscale frames, so the network sees motion. The 210×160 colour screen was converted to luminance, down-sampled, and the maximum of consecutive frames taken to remove flicker.
  • NetworkThree convolutional layers (32 filters of 8×8 with stride 4, 64 of 4×4 with stride 2, 64 of 3×3 with stride 1), a fully connected layer of 512 units, and one output per legal action (4 to 18 depending on the game): all action values in one forward pass.
  • LearningQ-learning with replay of the last one million transitions in minibatches of 32, a target network refreshed every 10,000 updates, RMSProp with learning rate 0.00025, discount 0.99, rewards clipped to {−1, 0, 1}.
  • Actingε-greedy with ε annealed from 1 to 0.1 over the first million frames; each action repeated for four frames; 50 million frames of training per game (about 38 days of play); evaluation with ε = 0.05 averaged over 30 episodes with random initial no-ops.

Search plus learning · 16.6

Mastering Go: networks inside the search

Go was the game that search alone could not crack: about 250 legal moves per position, games of 150 moves, and no simple evaluation of who is ahead. The Rollouts, MCTS & AlphaGo chapter covers the search in depth; here the point is how learned functions were placed inside it. AlphaGo (Silver et al., 2016) used two deep convolutional networks. A policy network p(a | s), first trained to imitate 30 million expert moves (57% accuracy) and then improved by policy-gradient self-play, proposed moves and gave the tree search its prior. A value network v(s), trained by regression on 30 million positions from self-play games of the improved policy, estimated who would win from a position without playing it out. Monte Carlo tree search then expanded the most promising lines, scoring each leaf half by the value network and half by a fast rollout, and chose the move with the most visits. It beat the European champion Fan Hui 5–0 in 2015 and Lee Sedol 4–1 in 2016.

AlphaGo Zero (Silver et al., 2017) removed the human data and the rollouts. One network with two heads, policy and value, started from random weights and learned entirely from self-play, with the search itself as the teacher: after each search, the visit counts at the root, sharper than the network’s prior, became the target for the policy head, and the game’s result became the target for the value head. Search improves the policy; the improved policy improves the search. After 72 hours on four TPUs it beat the version that had beaten Lee Sedol 100–0, and AlphaZero (2017) applied the same loop to chess and shogi. The toy below shows the mechanism on a game small enough to see whole: noughts and crosses, with a heuristic prior standing in for the policy network and either random rollouts or the exact minimax value standing in for the value network. Click the board to set up a position, run the search and compare the prior with the visit distribution it produces.

\[ a_t = \arg\max_a\Big[\,Q(s,a) + c\,P(s,a)\,\frac{\sqrt{N(s)}}{1+N(s,a)}\,\Big], \qquad \pi(a\mid s_0) \propto N(s_0,a)^{1/\tau} \]
Read it as: the PUCT rule used to descend the tree. Q(s, a) is the mean value of simulations through that edge; P(s, a) is the prior from the policy network; N counts visits. The second term favours moves the prior likes and moves tried rarely; as N(s, a) grows it fades and Q takes over. At the root, the move probabilities used for play and as the policy target are proportional to visit counts (temperature τ = 1 early in a game, τ → 0 later). Expanding a leaf asks the network once for (P, v); no rollout is needed when v is trusted.

What the toy leaves out. Try the heuristic prior with 20 simulations and then with 2,000 on the tricky position: with few simulations the search inherits the prior’s mistake (corners look good to the heuristic, but either corner loses for O), with many it overrides it, which is what makes the visit distribution a better teacher than the prior.

Decisions from logs · 16.7

Personalised web services and off-policy evaluation

A web page that chooses which offer, article or advertisement to show you is making a decision, and the usual way to tune it is a contextual bandit: predict the click-through rate of each option for a user like you and show the best. Theocharous, Thomas and Ghavamzadeh (2015), working on Adobe’s marketing systems, argued that this is myopic. A visitor is a sequence of visits, and an offer that maximises the click now can wear the visitor out and lose the clicks that would have come later. They framed the problem as an MDP whose reward is the click and whose return over a visitor’s lifetime is the life-time value (LTV), learned a policy with batch reinforcement learning (fitted Q iteration with random forests) from a bank’s logged data, and found that it promised more total clicks than the greedy policy.

Then the harder problem: you cannot A/B-test every candidate policy on live customers, so you must estimate how a new policy would do from data collected by the old one. That is off-policy evaluation, and this book met it in the stable off-policy learning chapter and its Open Bandit project. For sequences the importance ratios multiply along the visit history and their variance grows with the horizon, so Theocharous et al. paired the estimates with high-confidence lower bounds (Thomas et al., 2015): deploy the new policy only if, with probability at least 1 − δ, it is no worse than the old one. The simulation below has the whole loop: a visitor model with hidden fatigue, a family of policies from “always push” to “never push”, their true LTVs, and importance-sampling estimates of each from a log collected by a cautious behaviour policy. \[ \hat V_{\text{IS}} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_i\,G_i, \qquad \hat V_{\text{WIS}} = \frac{\sum_i \rho_i G_i}{\sum_i \rho_i}, \qquad \hat V_{\text{PDIS}} = \frac1n\sum_i\sum_{t} \gamma^t \rho_{i,0:t}\, R_{i,t+1}, \qquad \rho_{i,0:t} = \prod_{k=0}^{t}\frac{\pi(A_{i,k}\mid S_{i,k})}{b(A_{i,k}\mid S_{i,k})} \]
Read it as: Gi is the return of logged visitor i; ρi is the product of the per-visit ratios over that visitor’s whole history. Ordinary IS is unbiased but its variance explodes with the horizon; weighted IS normalises by the ratios and trades a little bias for a lot of variance; per-decision IS weights each reward only by the ratios up to that step, which is all it depends on. All three need the behaviour policy to have given every target action a positive probability at every visit.

What the real system did differently. Adobe’s visitors did not come with a fatigue counter: the state was a vector of features from the visit history (recency, frequency, which offers had been shown and clicked), and the policy was learned off-line by fitted Q iteration with random forests as the approximator, then evaluated off-policy against the deployed greedy policy before anyone saw it. The evaluation used the per-decision and weighted estimators above together with concentration inequalities that give a lower bound valid without a normality assumption, and the paper’s point was that such a bound can be tight enough to act on with a realistic amount of logged data. The simulation keeps the shape of the argument (myopic versus lifetime, estimate before deploy, variance versus horizon) and drops the state estimation.

Control in the physical world · 16.8

Thermal soaring

Birds and glider pilots climb without engines by finding thermals, columns of rising air, and circling inside them. The cues are local and noisy: a pilot feels the aircraft accelerate upward on entering lift and feels one wing lifted more than the other when the lift is stronger on that side. Reddy, Celani, Sejnowski and Vergassola (2016) asked whether a glider could learn to soar from such cues alone. They simulated a glider in a numerically generated turbulent convective flow and trained it with Sarsa. Its state was built from two sensed quantities, the vertical wind acceleration az and the lift difference across the wings τ (a roll-wise torque), each cut into three levels; its actions changed the bank angle (and, in the simulation, the angle of attack); its reward rewarded climbing. The learned policy was legible: bank toward the wing that is being lifted, and turn more tightly when the air is accelerating downward. In 2018 the same group put the learner into a two-metre glider and trained it in the sky above California, where it learned to gain hundreds of metres on real thermals despite the atmosphere’s turbulence.

  • Which cuesReddy et al. (2016) compared candidate sensorimotor cues (the local vertical wind speed, its acceleration az, and the roll-wise torque τ from the lift difference across the wings) and found az and τ the most useful pair: both are local differences a bird or a small glider can actually sense, and together they say whether the lift is improving and on which side it is stronger.
  • What was learnedThe policy that emerged resembled what pilots are taught: bank toward the wing that is being lifted, and tighten the turn when the air begins to accelerate downward, which signals leaving the core. A learned table, not a hand rule, but a legible one.
  • TurbulenceIn the simulated convective flow, performance fell as the turbulence intensity rose, because the cues are differences and gusts enter them directly. Coarser bins and persistent (slower-changing) actions helped; the trade-off between responsiveness and noise is the one the sliders below expose.
  • In the fieldReddy et al. (2018) trained the same kind of learner on a two-metre glider with on-board sensing of az and τ, over repeated flights of a few minutes each in real atmospheric thermals. The learned bank-angle policy gained altitude on real thermals and again looked like the pilots’ heuristics, with the vertical wind acceleration the more robust cue in strong turbulence.

This page’s world is a toy of that: a single Gaussian thermal, a glider at constant airspeed that can only change its bank, and gusts that corrupt what it senses. The project builds the thermal-soaring learner of Reddy et al. in miniature and runs it live. Everything above about the world holds: a Gaussian thermal of radius 60 m with a 3 m/s core, a glider at 10 m/s that sinks at 0.8 m/s wings-level and can only change its bank, gusts with a one-second correlation of 0.9, and sensor noise on the two cues. The same experiment in Python

The script below is a separate implementation of the same world and the same learner (plain Python with numpy for the table). It was run as pasted, with Python’s own random generator, so its numbers are not bit-for-bit those of the page; they come from the same model and tell the same story. Two seeds per turbulence level, 1,500 episodes each, α = 0.05, γ = 0.98, λ = 0.6, and ε decaying from 0.3 to 0.02 over the first 70% of training.

"""Thermal soaring with Sarsa(lambda): a glider learns to find and ride a simulated thermal.

The model mirrors the browser version in the lecture: a Gaussian updraft, a glider at
constant airspeed whose only control is its bank angle, two sensed quantities (the
vertical-wind acceleration a_z and the lift difference across the wingspan tau), each
cut into three bins, and a reward equal to the height gained in the step.
Needs only numpy.
"""
import math
import random
import numpy as np

# --- the world -------------------------------------------------------------
W0, R = 3.0, 60.0          # thermal core strength (m/s) and radius (m)
V, S0, G = 10.0, 0.8, 9.81  # airspeed (m/s), sink at wings level (m/s), gravity
HALF = 2.0                 # half wingspan (m)
T = 300                    # steps (seconds) per episode
TH_A, TH_T = 0.1, 0.04     # bin thresholds for a_z (m/s per s) and tau (m/s)
BANKS = [-45, -30, -15, 0, 15, 30, 45]
NB, NS, NA = len(BANKS), 9 * len(BANKS), 3
RHO, DF = 0.9, 0.1         # gust persistence and differential-gust fraction


def updraft(x, y):
    return W0 * math.exp(-(x * x + y * y) / (R * R))


def sink(bi):
    return S0 * (1.0 / math.cos(math.radians(BANKS[bi]))) ** 1.5


def bin3(v, th):
    return 0 if v < -th else (2 if v > th else 1)


class Glider:
    def __init__(self, rng):
        r = R * (0.5 + 1.5 * rng.random())
        th = rng.random() * 2 * math.pi
        self.x, self.y = r * math.cos(th), r * math.sin(th)
        self.psi = rng.random() * 2 * math.pi
        self.bi, self.z, self.eta, self.eta_d, self.w_prev = 3, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, None

    def step(self, a, rng, turb, sens):
        """a = 0: bank right, 1: hold, 2: bank left. Returns height gained (reward)."""
        self.bi = max(0, min(NB - 1, self.bi + (a - 1)))
        phi = math.radians(BANKS[self.bi])
        self.psi += G * math.tan(phi) / V
        self.x += V * math.cos(self.psi)
        self.y += V * math.sin(self.psi)
        self.eta = RHO * self.eta + turb * math.sqrt(1 - RHO * RHO) * rng.gauss(0, 1)
        self.eta_d = 0.7 * self.eta_d + DF * turb * math.sqrt(1 - 0.49) * rng.gauss(0, 1)
        wc = updraft(self.x, self.y) + self.eta
        lx, ly = -math.sin(self.psi) * HALF, math.cos(self.psi) * HALF
        wl, wr = updraft(self.x + lx, self.y + ly), updraft(self.x - lx, self.y - ly)
        vz = wc - sink(self.bi)
        self.z += vz
        az = (0.0 if self.w_prev is None else wc - self.w_prev) + sens * rng.gauss(0, 1)
        tau = (wl - wr) + self.eta_d + 0.4 * sens * rng.gauss(0, 1)
        self.w_prev = wc
        self.tau, self.az = tau, az
        self.s = (bin3(az, TH_A) * 3 + bin3(tau, TH_T)) * NB + self.bi
        return vz


# --- the learner -----------------------------------------------------------
def greedy(Q, s):
    return int(np.argmax(Q[s]))


def episode(Q, rng, alpha, gamma, lam, eps, turb, sens):
    E = np.zeros_like(Q)
    g = Glider(rng)
    g.step(1, rng, turb, sens)
    s = g.s
    a = rng.randrange(NA) if rng.random() < eps else greedy(Q, s)
    total, in_core = 0.0, 0
    for _ in range(T):
        r = g.step(a, rng, turb, sens)
        total += r
        in_core += math.hypot(g.x, g.y) < R
        s2 = g.s
        a2 = rng.randrange(NA) if rng.random() < eps else greedy(Q, s2)
        delta = r + gamma * Q[s2, a2] - Q[s, a]
        E[s, :] = 0.0
        E[s, a] = 1.0                      # replacing traces
        Q += alpha * delta * E
        E *= gamma * lam
        s, a = s2, a2
    return total, in_core / T


def baseline(kind, rng, turb, sens):
    g = Glider(rng)
    g.step(1, rng, turb, sens)
    total = 0.0
    for _ in range(T):
        a = 1
        if kind == "circle":
            a = 2 if g.bi < 5 else 1
        elif kind == "rule":          # bank towards the rising wing; tighten when sinking
            if g.tau > TH_T:
                a = 2
            elif g.tau < -TH_T:
                a = 0
            elif g.az < -TH_A:
                a = 2 if g.bi >= 3 else 0
        total += g.step(a, rng, turb, sens)
    return total


def train(turb, sens, n_episodes=1500, seed=3, alpha=0.05, gamma=0.98, lam=0.6):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    Q = np.zeros((NS, NA))
    gains = []
    for e in range(n_episodes):
        eps = max(0.02, 0.3 * (1 - e / (0.7 * n_episodes)))
        gains.append(episode(Q, rng, alpha, gamma, lam, eps, turb, sens)[0])
    rng2 = random.Random(seed + 1)
    greedy_gain = np.mean([episode(Q.copy(), rng2, 0.0, gamma, lam, 0.0, turb, sens)[0] for _ in range(100)])
    rng3 = random.Random(seed + 1)
    base = {k: np.mean([baseline(k, rng3, turb, sens) for _ in range(100)]) for k in ("straight", "circle", "rule")}
    return Q, np.array(gains), greedy_gain, base

Wrap-up · 16

Five lines to remember

  • The applications share one recipe: a representation chosen by people, a value function learned by temporal-difference updates, and training data manufactured by interaction (self-play, a simulator, a log) rather than labelled by hand. TD-Gammon is the clearest instance; Samuel’s 1959 program is its ancestor, with search supplying the bootstrapped target.
  • Eligibility traces are what let one game’s result teach the moves that caused it: the terminal error reaches every weight in proportion to its trace, and λ sets how far back the credit flows. Done by hand, three steps of TD(λ) already show a blot being punished for a hit that came one move later.
  • A learned value function turns a hard decision into a calculation. Watson’s Daily-Double wager maximises confidence-weighted win probability over two futures; a memory controller’s command choice maximises long-run bus utilisation; both were learned from simulation or on the job, not written down.
  • Scale changed what the recipe could do, not the recipe. DQN added replay and a frozen target so that the same network could learn 49 games from pixels; AlphaGo put policy and value networks inside Monte Carlo tree search, and AlphaGo Zero made the search the teacher: visit counts became the policy target and self-play results the value target.
  • In the world, the hard part is the signal. Personalising for lifetime value means evaluating a new policy from an old policy’s log, where importance ratios multiply along the horizon; a soaring glider must read a thermal from two noisy differences, and learning works until turbulence drowns them. Both systems were built to know when not to trust themselves.

Check your understanding

TD-Gammon never used an explicit exploration rule such as ε-greedy. Where did its exploration come from, and why would the same shortcut fail for a deterministic game?

The dice. Every position reached in self-play depends on random rolls, so the same greedy move choice is tried from a huge variety of positions, and no two games are alike. In a deterministic game, greedy self-play from the same opening would replay the same game over and over, so some explicit randomisation (Dirichlet noise on the prior and temperature sampling at the root in AlphaGo Zero, for instance) is needed.

Samuel’s program had no reward for winning and fixed the weight of piece advantage by hand. Why did it need that anchor, and what in TD-Gammon replaced it?

Without any ground truth, “make the static score agree with the backed-up score” is satisfied by any self-consistent scoring, including a useless one (all weights zero). Fixing the piece term tied the scale to something known to matter. In TD-Gammon the terminal reward (1 for a win, 0 for a loss) plays that role: every chain of predictions ends in a real outcome, so consistency with the future is consistency with winning.

In the Watson demo, lowering confidence p makes the best bet collapse toward the minimum, but when Watson trails badly a low-confidence large bet can still be optimal. Why?

Because the value function is not linear in the score. When Watson is far behind, V is flat around the current score (losing by a lot and losing by a little are both losing) and steep only well above it. A wager that is probably wrong still has a chance of reaching the steep region, while losing it costs almost nothing in win probability. The expectation is dominated by the upside, so the maximiser bets big even when p is low.

The learned DRAM scheduler beats FR-FCFS on utilisation but produces a much larger worst-case latency. Which part of the problem formulation is responsible, and how did the page let you fix it?

The reward counts data cycles and nothing else, and the state contains no information about how long a request has waited, so starvation is invisible to the agent and costless. The κ term is reward shaping that penalises decisions made while some request has waited more than 30 cycles; it trades a little utilisation for fairness. İpek et al.’s richer state and the systems context did this more gracefully.

Why does the visit distribution produced by PUCT make a good training target for the policy network in AlphaGo Zero?

Search is a policy-improvement operator: starting from the network’s prior, the simulations redistribute visits toward moves whose backed-up values are higher, so the visit distribution is a sharper, better-informed policy than the prior that produced it. Regressing the network onto it is a step of policy iteration, and repeating it with self-play results as the value target gives the whole loop.

In the web-services simulation, why does the ordinary importance-sampling estimate get worse as the horizon grows even though it stays unbiased?

The ratio for a visitor is the product of one ratio per visit; with a 50/50 behaviour policy each factor is 2 or 0 for a deterministic target, so after H visits a trajectory either has weight 2H or 0. The estimate is an average of a few enormous terms and many zeros: unbiased, but with variance growing exponentially in H. Weighted IS and per-decision IS damp this, and the high-confidence lower bound reports how little the data actually guarantee.

Samuel’s backed-up target comes from minimax search; TD-Gammon’s comes from the next position actually reached. What does each buy, and what does each cost?

A minimax target looks at every reply, so it is low-variance and already includes the opponent’s best play, but it can only be as good as the static evaluation at the leaves and the depth the search affords. A sampled next position is cheap and unbiased with respect to what actually happens under the current policy and the dice, but it is noisy: one roll, one reply. Modern systems combine them, AlphaGo Zero most explicitly, by using search to produce the training targets for the network that the search itself uses.

In the Mini-Gammon demo the same network is updated after both White’s and Black’s moves, yet it always predicts White’s win probability. Why does that work?

The side to move is one of the inputs, so the network represents two functions at once: the value of a position with White to move and with Black to move. Every afterstate is evaluated from White’s point of view, White maximises it and Black minimises it, and the TD errors along the game are differences between consecutive White-win probabilities regardless of who moved. TD-Gammon worked the same way; a single network, one perspective, both players.

The DRAM demo discounts by γτ with τ the cycles a decision took. What goes wrong if you discount by γ per decision instead, and how did the chapter on average reward frame the same issue?

Per-decision discounting treats a one-cycle hit and a four-cycle miss as equally long steps, so the agent is not charged for the time a miss wastes and has to be told about it by a hand-set penalty. Discounting by elapsed cycles makes the return the discounted count of data cycles, which is bus utilisation. The continuing-task chapter made the cleaner version of this point: for a rate problem the natural objective is the average reward per unit time, and differential Sarsa optimises it directly without any discount.

The glider’s learned policy at wings level is nearly “always bank the same way”. Is that a failure?

No: at wings level the glider is flying straight, which is almost never right inside or near a thermal, so the first thing to do from any cue is to start a turn. The interesting decisions live at the banked states (hold the circle, tighten it when the air accelerates down, reverse when the outer wing lifts), which is why the policy map has a bank-angle selector.
