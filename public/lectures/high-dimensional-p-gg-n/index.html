High-Dimensional Problems: When p ≫ N

Classifying with thousands of features and a few dozen samples

A gene-expression array measures thousands of genes at once, but a study may have only forty patients. Text, proteins, images and mass spectra raise the same problem: the number of features p is far larger than the number of samples N. Every model in earlier chapters then has more freedom than the data can pin down, and some of them stop working altogether. The way out is to regularise: shrink, simplify or select until the model asks no more of the data than it can give. On the right are twenty arrays of pure noise with random labels, and a support vector classifier trained on them. Drag across the figure to add noise genes. Once p reaches about N, the training arrays split perfectly while new arrays stay a coin toss.

The setting

Why p ≫ N changes everything

With N samples in p dimensions and p ≥ N, a linear model has enough coefficients to hit every training target exactly, whatever the targets are. The hero shows the classification version: N points in general position can be split in every one of the 2N possible ways once p ≥ N − 1. Zero training error then says nothing about the model. Least squares has no unique solution, the covariance matrix cannot be inverted, and logistic regression's coefficients run off to infinity.

So the job is no longer to fit the data; it is to decide how much to fit. Here is a simulation in the spirit of the one that opens the chapter in Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman. N = 100 samples, features correlated at 0.2, and every feature carries a little signal, with a signal-to-noise ratio of 2. We fit ridge regression for a range of penalties λ and measure the true test error. Switch between p = 20, 100 and 1000, and slide λ. Watch where the best fit sits, and how good it can be.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_\lambda &= (\mathbf X^{\top}\mathbf X+\lambda\mathbf I_p)^{-1}\mathbf X^{\top}\mathbf y \\ &= \mathbf X^{\top}(\mathbf X\mathbf X^{\top}+\lambda\mathbf I_N)^{-1}\mathbf y \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ridge regression in two equivalent forms. The first inverts a p×p matrix, the second an N×N one. When p ≫ N the second is far cheaper, and it shows that the fitted coefficients are a combination of the N training rows.
Say it“Beta-hat lambda is X-transpose X plus lambda I, inverse, times X-transpose y; or equally X-transpose times X X-transpose plus lambda I, inverse, times y.”
\[ \mathrm{df}(\lambda) = \sum_{j=1}^{\min(N,p)} \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda} \]
Read it as: dj are the singular values of X. Each direction in the data counts as one parameter when dj2 ≫ λ and as nothing when dj2 ≪ λ. However large p is, there are at most N directions, so df can never exceed N.
Say it“The effective degrees of freedom are the sum over j of d-j squared over d-j squared plus lambda.”

The simplest fix

Diagonal LDA: pretend the genes are independent

Linear discriminant analysis needs the inverse of the pooled within-class covariance matrix. With N samples and K classes that matrix has rank at most N − K, so as soon as p > N − K it is singular. You can force an answer with a pseudo-inverse, but the estimate is wild exactly where it has the least data.

The bluntest regularisation is often the best one: set every off-diagonal entry to zero. Each gene then needs only its own variance, which N samples estimate well. The rule that results is diagonal LDA: a nearest-centroid classifier in which each gene's distance is measured in units of its own standard deviation. Below, two classes of 20 samples differ in the first 10 genes only; every extra gene is noise. Slide p past 38 = N − K and watch full LDA fall apart while diagonal LDA degrades gently.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma} = \frac{1}{N-K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i:\,g_i=k} &(\mathbf x_i-\bar{\mathbf x}_k) \\ \times\, &(\mathbf x_i-\bar{\mathbf x}_k)^{\top} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the pooled covariance adds up N outer products, each centred on its own class mean. Centring on K means removes K dimensions, so its rank is at most N − K. A p×p matrix of rank N − K has p − (N − K) zero eigenvalues and no inverse.
Say it“Sigma-hat is one over N minus K times the sum over classes and samples of x-i minus its class mean, times its transpose.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \delta_k(\mathbf x^*) = &-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{(x_j^*-\bar x_{kj})^2}{s_j^2} \\ &+ 2\log\pi_k \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: score a new sample x* against class k by its squared distance to the class centroid, gene by gene, each scaled by that gene's pooled variance sj2; then add the log prior. The largest score wins. Only p means per class and p variances are estimated, never a covariance.
Say it“Delta-k of x-star is minus the sum over genes of x-star-j minus x-bar-k-j, squared, over s-j squared, plus two log pi-k.”

Selecting while classifying

Nearest shrunken centroids

Diagonal LDA uses every gene, and with thousands of genes most of them only add noise to the distance. Tibshirani, Hastie, Narasimhan & Chu (2002) shrink each class centroid towards the overall centroid, gene by gene, and let a gene drop out entirely once it has shrunk to the overall value in every class. The method, nearest shrunken centroids (NSC, sold as the software PAM), selects genes and classifies in one step, with a single dial Δ.

The shrinkage acts on a standardised difference dkj, which is essentially a t-statistic comparing class k with the overall mean on gene j. Two details matter. The constant s0, the median of the sj, stops genes with tiny variances from producing huge statistics by luck. And mk makes the denominator the standard error of the numerator. Below are four tumour classes, 8 training samples each, and 1000 genes; each class over-expresses its own 12 genes. Raise Δ: the noise genes drain away first, then the real ones, and the test error falls before it rises.

\[ \begin{aligned} d_{kj} &= \frac{\bar x_{kj}-\bar x_j}{m_k\,(s_j+s_0)} \\ m_k &= \sqrt{1/N_k-1/N} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: how far class k's mean on gene j sits from the overall mean, in units of its standard error. sj is the pooled within-class standard deviation of gene j and s0 is their median, added for stability.
Say it“d-k-j is x-bar-k-j minus x-bar-j, over m-k times s-j plus s-zero; m-k is the square root of one over N-k minus one over N.”
\[ \begin{aligned} d'_{kj} &= \operatorname{sign}(d_{kj})\,\big(|d_{kj}|-\Delta\big)_+ \\ \bar x'_{kj} &= \bar x_j + m_k\,(s_j+s_0)\,d'_{kj} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: soft-thresholding. Pull every dkj towards zero by Δ, and set it to zero if it would cross. Then rebuild the centroid from the shrunken difference. A gene whose d′kj is zero in every class has the same centroid value for all classes, so it no longer affects any decision.
Say it“d-prime is the sign of d times the positive part of the absolute value of d minus Delta; the shrunken centroid is x-bar-j plus m-k times s-j plus s-zero times d-prime.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \delta_k(\mathbf x^*) &= \sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{(x^*_j-\bar x'_{kj})^2}{(s_j+s_0)^2} - 2\log\pi_k \\ \hat p_k(\mathbf x^*) &= \frac{e^{-\delta_k(\mathbf x^*)/2}}{\sum_{\ell} e^{-\delta_\ell(\mathbf x^*)/2}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: diagonal LDA again, now with shrunken centroids and sj + s0 in place of sj. The class with the smallest δk wins (the sign convention is flipped from the previous section), and the same scores give class probabilities.
Say it“Delta-k of x-star is the sum over genes of x-star-j minus the shrunken centroid, squared, over s-j plus s-zero squared, minus two log pi-k; the probability of class k is e to the minus delta-k over two, normalised.”

Quadratic regularisation

RDA and ridge logistic regression

Diagonal LDA throws all the correlations away. Genes are not independent, though: genes in the same pathway rise and fall together. Regularised discriminant analysis (RDA) keeps a fraction γ of the estimated covariance and replaces the rest with its diagonal. At γ = 0 it is diagonal LDA; at γ = 1 it is LDA with its singular matrix. Anywhere below 1 the matrix is invertible.

Logistic regression needs the same treatment. With p > N the classes can be separated perfectly, so the likelihood keeps rising as the coefficients grow and there is no maximum. A ridge penalty on the coefficients fixes that. For K classes the book uses the symmetric multinomial form with one coefficient vector per class, each penalised, so no class is treated as the baseline. With two classes it reduces to the form below.

The data here is a practice array used for the next few sections: 2 × 20 samples, 200 genes in 20 co-regulated blocks of 10 (correlation 0.6 within a block). In blocks 1 and 2 every gene rises in class 2; in blocks 3 and 4 half the genes rise and half fall. Errors are exact. Slide γ, then switch to ridge logistic and slide λ.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}(\gamma) &= \gamma\,\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma} \\ &\quad + (1-\gamma)\operatorname{diag}(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}) \\ 0 &\le\gamma\le 1 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a blend of the full covariance and its diagonal. The diagonal part makes every eigenvalue at least (1 − γ) minj sj2, so the inverse exists. LDA is then run with Σ̂(γ) in place of Σ̂.
Say it“Sigma-hat of gamma is gamma Sigma-hat plus one minus gamma times the diagonal of Sigma-hat.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{\beta_0,\boldsymbol\beta}\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\log \Pr(g_i\mid\mathbf x_i) \\ &\qquad - \frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\boldsymbol\beta\rVert^2 \\ &\Pr(g{=}2\mid\mathbf x) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-(\beta_0+\mathbf x^{\top}\boldsymbol\beta)}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the usual log-likelihood minus a ridge penalty, with the intercept left unpenalised. The penalty caps how confident the model may become, so a finite best β exists even when the classes separate.
Say it“Maximise the sum of log-probabilities of the true classes minus lambda over two times the squared length of beta.”

Quadratic regularisation

The support vector classifier when everything separates

The support vector classifier of chapter 1.9 has the same shape as ridge logistic regression: a loss on the margins plus a quadratic penalty, only with the hinge loss in place of the log-likelihood. When p > N the training classes are separable, and that changes how the dial behaves. Once C is large enough for every training point to sit on or beyond its margin, raising C further changes nothing: the solution is the maximum-margin hyperplane. The dial only matters at the small end.

Rosset, Zhu & Hastie (2004) proved a second link. On separable data, as λ → 0 the direction of the ridge-logistic coefficients converges to the SVM's maximum-margin direction. In p ≫ N problems, the two methods are close relatives. Slide C and watch the error, the number of support vectors and the angle between the two directions.

\[ \begin{aligned} \min_{\beta_0,\boldsymbol\beta}\ &\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big[1-y_i(\beta_0+\mathbf x_i^{\top}\boldsymbol\beta)\big]_+ \\ &+ \frac{1}{2C}\lVert\boldsymbol\beta\rVert^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the hinge loss charges each point for falling short of margin 1 on its own side (labels y = ±1), and the penalty keeps β short. Large C means a weak penalty. Once every hinge term can be zero, the problem is to find the shortest β with every margin at least 1: the widest street.
Say it“Minimise the sum of the positive parts of one minus y-i f of x-i, plus one over two C times the squared length of beta.”
\[ \lim_{\lambda\to 0}\ \frac{\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\text{RL}}(\lambda)}{\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\text{RL}}(\lambda)\rVert} = \frac{\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\text{SVM}}}{\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\text{SVM}}\rVert} \]
Read it as: on separable data, ridge logistic regression (RL) with a vanishing penalty points the same way as the hard-margin SVM. Its coefficients grow without bound, but only their direction affects which class is predicted.
Say it“As lambda goes to zero, the unit vector of the ridge-logistic coefficients tends to the unit vector of the SVM coefficients.”

Fewer genes

Feature selection, and how to fool yourself with it

Quadratic penalties shrink coefficients but never set them to zero, so RDA, ridge logistic and the SVM all use every gene. Biologists usually want a short list. Two common recipes: filter the genes by a t-statistic and keep the top m, or use recursive feature elimination (RFE; Guyon, Weston, Barnhill & Vapnik 2002). RFE fits a linear SVM, drops the genes with the smallest squared weights, refits on what is left, and repeats.

Selection is part of the model, so any estimate of its error must repeat the selection inside every fold of cross-validation. If you pick genes on all the data and then cross-validate only the classifier, the held-out samples have already helped choose the genes, and the estimate is badly optimistic. Switch the labels to pure noise: the true error is 50%, yet selection before cross-validation still reports a classifier that seems to work.

\[ t_j = \frac{\bar x_{2j}-\bar x_{1j}}{s_j\sqrt{1/N_1+1/N_2}} \]
Read it as: the two-sample t-statistic of gene j: difference in class means over its standard error. The filter keeps the m genes with the largest |tj| and runs diagonal LDA on them.
Say it“t-j is x-bar-two-j minus x-bar-one-j, over s-j times the square root of one over N-one plus one over N-two.”
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathcal S_0 = \{1,\dots,p\} \\ &\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}^{(t)} = \text{SVM on the genes in } \mathcal S_t \\ &\mathcal S_{t+1} = \text{the } \lceil|\mathcal S_t|/2\rceil \text{ genes of } \mathcal S_t \\ &\qquad\text{with the largest } \big(\hat\beta^{(t)}_j\big)^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: in a linear SVM, βj2 measures how much gene j changes the margin. Halving from 1000 genes gives nested sets of 500, 250, 125, 63, 32, …, 1 genes; the classifier on each set is the SVM itself, and the t-filter is compared at the same sizes.
Say it“Fit the SVM on the current genes, keep the half with the largest squared coefficients, and refit, until m genes remain.”

Computation

The shortcut: fit in N dimensions, not p

Ridge regression, ridge logistic regression, RDA's linear algebra and the linear SVM all have a quadratic penalty, and that buys a computational gift. Write the thin singular value decomposition of the N×p data matrix as X = U D V⊤ = R V⊤, where R = U D is just N×N. Every row xi = V ri, and any part of β orthogonal to the columns of V leaves every fitted value unchanged while adding to the penalty. So the solution lies in the span of V, and the whole problem can be solved with the N-dimensional rows ri in place of the p-dimensional xi.

The saving is large: a Newton step for ridge logistic regression costs about p3 operations on the raw data and N3 on R, after a one-off N2p for the decomposition. It also works inside cross-validation, because any subset of rows of X is the same subset of rows of R times V⊤. Raise p, then solve the full problem as well and check that both routes give the same coefficients.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf X &= \mathbf U\mathbf D\mathbf V^{\top} = \mathbf R\mathbf V^{\top}, \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \mathbf V\hat{\boldsymbol\theta} \\ \hat{\boldsymbol\theta} &= \arg\min_{\beta_0,\boldsymbol\theta}\ \sum_{i=1}^{N} L\big(y_i,\ \beta_0+\mathbf r_i^{\top}\boldsymbol\theta\big) \\ &\qquad + \lambda\,\boldsymbol\theta^{\top}\boldsymbol\theta \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: fit the same model, with the same loss L and the same penalty, to the N-dimensional rows ri, then map the N coefficients back with V. Because V has orthonormal columns, θ⊤θ = β⊤β, so the penalty is unchanged too.
Say it“X is U D V-transpose, which is R V-transpose; beta-hat is V theta-hat, where theta-hat minimises the loss on the rows of R plus lambda theta-transpose theta.”

Sparsity

L1 classifiers and the fused lasso

Swap the ridge penalty for an L1 penalty and selection comes built in: coefficients hit exactly zero. Chapter 1.2b built the lasso and its paths; here is what changes when p ≫ N. The lasso logistic model can never use more than N genes, so with 40 arrays it picks at most 40 of 200, or of 20,000. And when genes are correlated it tends to pick one gene from each group and drop the rest. The elastic net mixes in some ridge penalty, and correlated genes then enter together. Slide λ down for the lasso, then switch to the elastic net.

\[ \begin{aligned} \min_{\beta_0,\boldsymbol\beta}\ &-\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\Pr(g_i\mid\mathbf x_i) \\ &+ \lambda\Big[\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\beta_j| + \frac{1-\alpha}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_j^2\Big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the average negative log-likelihood plus a mix of L1 and squared penalties. α = 1 is the lasso; α between 0 and 1 is the elastic net. Lowering λ lets more genes in.
Say it“Minimise minus the average log-likelihood plus lambda times alpha times the sum of absolute betas, plus one minus alpha over two times the sum of squared betas.”

Some features come in a natural order: positions along a chromosome, mass-to-charge values in a protein mass spectrum, wavelengths in a spectrum. Neighbouring coefficients should then be similar. Tibshirani and colleagues (2005) applied the lasso to mass spectrometry data, where the selected features are peaks, and added a second penalty on the differences between neighbouring coefficients. That is the fused lasso. In its simplest form the features are the positions themselves (X = I), and it smooths a noisy profile such as a copy-number (CGH) signal into flat segments, most of them exactly zero. Raise each penalty in turn on the simulated profile below.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ \sum_{i}\Big(y_i-\beta_0-\sum_j x_{ij}\beta_j\Big)^2 \\ &\quad + \lambda_1\sum_{j}|\beta_j| + \lambda_2\sum_{j}|\beta_{j+1}-\beta_j| \\[4pt] &\text{CGH } (\mathbf X=\mathbf I){:}\ \ \min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ \tfrac12\sum_i(y_i-\beta_i)^2 \\ &\quad + \lambda_1\sum_i|\beta_i| + \lambda_2\sum_i|\beta_{i+1}-\beta_i| \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: least squares plus a lasso penalty (most coefficients zero) plus a fusion penalty (neighbours equal). In the CGH case the solution can be computed exactly: smooth with the fusion penalty alone (1-D total variation), then soft-threshold every value by λ1.
Say it“Minimise the squared error plus lambda-one times the sum of absolute coefficients plus lambda-two times the sum of absolute differences between neighbours.”

No feature matrix

Classification when features are unavailable

Sometimes there is no natural feature vector at all: protein or DNA sequences of different lengths, documents, graphs. What you can often compute is a similarity K(x, z) or a distance between any two objects. For sequences, the spectrum kernel of Leslie, Eskin & Noble (2002) counts every substring of length k (a k-mer) in each sequence and takes the inner product of the two count vectors. The feature space has 4k dimensions for DNA and 20k for proteins, but each kernel value only needs the k-mers the two sequences actually contain.

The data is real: 106 DNA sequences of 57 bases from E. coli (UCI “Molecular Biology, Promoter Gene Sequences”), 53 of them promoters and 53 not. Pick two sequences and a k and see which k-mers they share.

\[ \begin{aligned} K_k(\mathbf x,\mathbf z) &= \sum_{a\,\in\,\mathcal A^k} c_a(\mathbf x)\,c_a(\mathbf z) \\ &= \langle\boldsymbol\phi_k(\mathbf x),\boldsymbol\phi_k(\mathbf z)\rangle \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ca(x) counts how often the k-letter word a appears in x. Sum the products of counts over every possible word: equivalently, the dot product of two huge, mostly-zero count vectors φk.
Say it“K-k of x and z is the sum over all k-letter words a of the count of a in x times the count of a in z.”

What can you do with nothing but the N×N kernel matrix? Quite a lot, because many methods only ever touch the data through inner products. Distances come for free: ‖φ(x) − φ(z)‖2 = K(x, x) + K(z, z) − 2K(x, z), so nearest neighbours work. So does the distance to a class centroid, even though the centroid itself is never built. Any linear model with a quadratic penalty can be written in terms of K, by the same argument as the shortcut above: the SVM, ridge and kernel logistic regression. So can principal components, after double-centring K.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\lVert\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)-\bar{\boldsymbol\phi}_k\rVert^2 \\ &\quad = K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x) - \frac{2}{N_k}\sum_{i\in k}K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_i) \\ &\qquad + \frac{1}{N_k^2}\sum_{i,i'\in k}K(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_{i'}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: expand the squared distance to the class mean in feature space (i ∈ k means sample i belongs to class k). Every term is a kernel value: the point with itself, the point with each class member, and the class members with each other. The nearest-centroid classifier needs nothing more.
Say it“The squared distance from phi of x to the class-k centroid is K of x and x, minus two over N-k times the sum of K of x and x-i over class k, plus one over N-k squared times the sum of K over all pairs in class k.”

The kernel view has limits. Without the features you cannot standardise them or put them on a common scale. You cannot ask which individual features matter, so there is no feature selection and no lasso. And you cannot fit models built on separate coordinates, such as diagonal LDA, shrunken centroids or additive models. The book's own example classifies scientific abstracts by author using nothing but a string similarity between documents, with nearest-centroid and nearest-neighbour rules; the promoter sequences above work exactly the same way.

Project

Project: telling two leukaemias apart with 15 genes

In 1999 Golub and colleagues at the Whitehead Institute showed that gene expression alone could tell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) from acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), two cancers that need different treatment. The data are public (OpenML dataset 1104): 72 Affymetrix arrays with 7,129 probes each. The first 38 form Golub's training set (27 ALL, 11 AML) and the last 34 his independent test set (20 ALL, 14 AML). We use the standard preprocessing of Dudoit, Fridlyand & Speed (2002): clip the values to [100, 16000], keep the genes that vary enough across the training arrays (max/min > 5 and max − min > 500), and take log10. That leaves 3,051 genes for 38 patients. On the page each value is kept to 0.01 on the log scale.

Nearest shrunken centroids is fitted below on the 38 training arrays, with Δ chosen by repeated 10-fold cross-validation: ten different random partitions, averaged. Slide Δ and watch the gene list shrink, or press the button to take the smallest list whose CV error stays at or below 5%.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.svm import SVC
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

# Golub et al. (1999) leukaemia arrays, OpenML dataset 1104: 72 arrays x 7129 probes, ALL or AML
rows = [l.strip().split(",") for l in open("leukemia.arff") if l.strip() and l[0] not in "%@"]
X = np.array([[float(v) for v in r[:-1]] for r in rows]); y = np.array([r[-1] == "AML" for r in rows], int)
X = np.clip(X, 100, 16000); A = X[:38]                       # the first 38 arrays are Golub's training set
keep = (A.max(0) / A.min(0) > 5) & (A.max(0) - A.min(0) > 500)  # Dudoit et al. filter, training arrays only
X = 2 + np.round((np.log10(X[:, keep]) - 2) * 100) / 100       # log10, kept to 0.01 as on the page
Xtr, ytr, Xte, yte = X[:38], y[:38], X[38:], y[38:]
print(f"{keep.sum()} genes; train {np.bincount(ytr)} (ALL, AML), test {np.bincount(yte)}")

def nsc_fit(X, y):                                             # nearest shrunken centroids (Tibshirani et al. 2002)
    N, cls = len(y), [y == 0, y == 1]
    cen, m = np.array([X[c].mean(0) for c in cls]), X.mean(0)
    s = np.sqrt(sum(((X[c] - cen[k]) ** 2).sum(0) for k, c in enumerate(cls)) / (N - 2)); s0 = np.median(s)
    mk = np.array([np.sqrt(1 / c.sum() - 1 / N) for c in cls])[:, None]
    return dict(m=m, s=s + s0, s0=s0, mk=mk, d=(cen - m) / (mk * (s + s0)), pri=np.array([c.mean() for c in cls]))
def nsc_scores(F, X, D):
    dp = np.sign(F["d"]) * np.maximum(np.abs(F["d"]) - D, 0)    # soft-threshold every standardised difference
    cen = F["m"] + F["mk"] * F["s"] * dp                       # shrunken centroids
    sc = np.array([(((X - c) / F["s"]) ** 2).sum(1) - 2 * np.log(p) for c, p in zip(cen, F["pri"])])
    return sc, (dp != 0).any(0)
def partition(rep):                                            # the ten stratified 10-fold partitions the page uses
    rng, f = np.random.RandomState(100 + rep), np.zeros(38, int)
    for c in (0, 1):
        idx = np.where(ytr == c)[0]; idx = idx[rng.permutation(len(idx))]; f[idx] = np.arange(len(idx)) % 10
    return f

F = nsc_fit(Xtr, ytr); grid = np.linspace(0, np.abs(F["d"]).max(), 61); cv = np.zeros(61)
for rep in range(10):                                          # repeated 10-fold cross-validation
    f = partition(rep)
    for k in range(10):
        Fk = nsc_fit(Xtr[f != k], ytr[f != k])
        cv += [(nsc_scores(Fk, Xtr[f == k], D)[0].argmin(0) != ytr[f == k]).sum() for D in grid]
cv /= 10
print(f"s0 = {F['s0']:.5f}, largest |d| = {grid[-1]:.5f}")
for i in list(range(0, 61, 6)):
    sc, act = nsc_scores(F, Xte, grid[i])
    print(f"Delta {grid[i]:6.3f}: {act.sum():4d} genes, CV errors {cv[i]:5.2f}/38, test errors {(sc.argmin(0) != yte).sum()}/34")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • When p ≥ N any labelling can be fitted, so training error means nothing and every method must be regularised. The effective degrees of freedom can never exceed N.
  • Ignoring correlations is a strong, often winning regulariser: diagonal LDA needs only p variances, where LDA's covariance is singular once p > N − K.
  • Nearest shrunken centroids soft-thresholds standardised class differences by Δ, which classifies and selects genes in one step; s0 protects against genes with tiny variances.
  • Quadratic penalties (RDA, ridge logistic, the SVM) keep every gene, but can be fitted on the N×N matrix R from X = R V⊤. L1 penalties select at most N genes; the fused lasso also smooths ordered features.
  • Select features inside cross-validation, never before it. And when features are missing altogether, kernels and distances still support nearest neighbours, centroids, SVMs and kernel PCA.

Check your understanding

A colleague fits logistic regression to 50 patients and 5,000 genes and reports zero training errors. Is that impressive?

No. With p > N the two classes are almost surely linearly separable, whatever the labels are, so zero training error is guaranteed. (Unpenalised logistic regression would not even converge: its coefficients run to infinity.) Only error on samples that played no part in fitting, or an honest cross-validation, says anything.

Why can LDA not be used directly when p = 1000 and N = 60, and what does diagonal LDA assume instead?

The pooled covariance has rank at most N − K = 58, so the 1000×1000 matrix is singular and cannot be inverted. Diagonal LDA assumes the genes are independent within each class, so it only needs the 1000 variances sj2, each estimated from all 60 samples.

In nearest shrunken centroids with two classes, why do genes drop out of both classes at the same Δ?

With two classes, x̄1j − x̄j and x̄2j − x̄j have opposite signs and sizes in the ratio N2 : N1, and m1 and m2 scale them by exactly the same factors. Both standardised differences come out as ±√(N1N2/N) times the same quantity. Equal sizes mean the soft threshold zeroes them together.

You choose the 20 genes with the largest t-statistics using all 40 samples, then run 10-fold cross-validation of diagonal LDA on those 20 genes. What is wrong?

The held-out samples in each fold already helped choose the genes, so the genes fit them better than they would fit new data. The CV error is optimistic, and with pure-noise labels it can look excellent. The selection has to be repeated within every fold, using only that fold's training samples.

Why can you not run the lasso when all you have is a string kernel between protein sequences?

The lasso penalises individual coefficients, one per feature, so it needs the features themselves. A kernel only gives inner products between whole objects. Methods that depend on the data only through inner products, such as the SVM, nearest centroids and kernel PCA, still work; feature-by-feature methods do not.
