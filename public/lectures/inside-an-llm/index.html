The Transformer, Inside an LLM

One stack of masked attention, one job: the next token

GPT-2, GPT-3 and Llama all share one design. A text goes in as tokens; a stack of transformer blocks, each looking only backwards, turns every token into a vector; and a final layer turns each vector into a probability for every possible next token. That is the whole machine. Training teaches it to guess well; writing is just guessing, choosing, and guessing again. The model in the figure is a real one, small enough to run in your browser: 213,056 weights, trained offline on five Jane Austen novels, one character at a time. Type a beginning and press Write, or click a bar to choose the next character yourself.

The job

One job: predict the next token, everywhere at once

Chapter 4.1 split a text’s probability into a chain of next-word guesses. A large language model is a machine for exactly that chain. It reads tokens x1, …, xt and outputs a distribution over the token xt+1. Our model’s tokens are single characters, 75 of them (letters, digits, punctuation, space and new line); real models use tens of thousands of sub-word tokens (chapter 4.2), but nothing else in this chapter depends on that choice. The design that makes it trainable is that one pass over a text of n tokens makes a guess at every position at once, so every token of the training text is a lesson. Pick a passage and hover any character to see what the model expected there.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(x_1,\dots,x_n) &= p(x_1) \\ &\quad \times \prod_{t=1}^{n-1} p_\theta(x_{t+1} \mid x_{\le t}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the probability of a whole text is the product of the model’s probability for each token given everything before it. pθ is the network with weights θ. Nothing is assumed about how far back the dependence goes, unlike an n-gram, which sees only the last n − 1 tokens; our model sees up to 128 characters.
Say it“The probability of the text is p of x-one times the product over t of p-theta of x-t-plus-one given x up to t.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \ell_t &= -\log p_\theta(x_{t+1}\mid x_{\le t}) \\ \mathcal{L} &= \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n-1} \ell_t \\ \text{bits per char} &= \mathcal{L}/\ln 2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ℓt is the model’s surprise at the true next token; the training loss ℒ is its average over the text. Measured in base 2 it is the number of bits the model would need, on average, to encode each character. A model that guessed uniformly among 75 characters would need log2 75 ≈ 6.23 bits; 2bits is the perplexity, the number of equally likely choices that would be as surprising.
Say it“Ell-t is minus log p-theta of the next token; the loss is the average of the ell-t; divide by log two for bits per character.”

The architecture

The decoder-only stack

The original transformer of chapter 2.6 had two halves: an encoder that read the input and a decoder that wrote the output while looking at it. A language model has no separate input to read, so GPT (Radford et al., 2018) kept only the decoder and dropped its cross-attention. What is left is a straight pipe. Each token becomes a vector (its embedding plus a learned vector for its position), passes up through L identical blocks, each made of masked self-attention and a small per-token network (the MLP), and the vector at the top is scored against every token in the vocabulary. Our model has L = 4 blocks of width d = 64 with 4 heads each, and a 128-character context; GPT-2 small has 12 blocks of width 768, and GPT-3 has 96 blocks of width 12,288. Slide along the text to follow one character’s vector up the stack.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{h}^{(0)}_t &= E[x_t] + P_t \\ \mathbf{u}^{(\ell)} &= \mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)} + \operatorname{Attn}_\ell\big(\operatorname{LN}(\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)})\big) \\ \mathbf{h}^{(\ell)} &= \mathbf{u}^{(\ell)} + \operatorname{MLP}_\ell\big(\operatorname{LN}(\mathbf{u}^{(\ell)})\big) \\ \mathbf{z}_t &= \operatorname{LN}_f\big(\mathbf{h}^{(L)}_t\big)\,E^{\top} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: E is the 75 × 64 token-embedding table and P the 128 × 64 table of learned position vectors. Each block ℓ reads a layer-normalised copy of the stream, attends (each position looks at itself and earlier positions only), and adds the result back; then its MLP (64 → 256 → 64 with a GELU, as in GPT-2) does the same. After L blocks a final layer norm and the transposed embedding table turn the 64 numbers into 75 scores, one per character: the logits zt.
Say it“h-zero is the token’s embedding plus its position’s; each block adds attention, then an MLP; the logits are the final layer norm of h-L times E-transpose.”

The rule

No peeking: the causal mask

Predicting every position in one pass has a catch. Position t’s job is to guess xt+1, and xt+1 is sitting right there in the input, one place to the right. If attention could reach it, training would teach the model to copy the answer, the loss would fall to almost nothing, and the model would be useless the moment it had to write a text whose next token does not exist yet. The causal mask forbids it: before the softmax, every score from a position to a later one is set to −∞, so its weight is exactly 0. Because attention is the only place where positions exchange information, nothing at position t can depend on anything after it. Choose a position, then rewrite everything after it, with the mask on and off.

\[ \begin{aligned} M_{ij} &= \begin{cases} 0 & j \le i \\ -\infty & j > i \end{cases} \\ A &= \operatorname{softmax}\Big(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: row i of the attention matrix holds the weights position i gives to every position j. Adding −∞ above the diagonal makes e−∞ = 0 there, so each row spreads its weight over positions 1 to i only, and still sums to 1. In code, −∞ is a large negative number such as −109.
Say it“M-i-j is zero when j is at most i and minus infinity when j is after i; A is the softmax of Q K-transpose over root d-k, plus M.”
\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{\partial\, \mathbf{z}_t}{\partial x_s} = 0 \quad \text{for every } s > t \\ \text{scores per layer and head} = \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2} \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: the logits at position t do not change at all, however you change any later token. That is what makes one pass equal to n separate predictions, each from its own prefix, and it is also what lets a model reuse its past work when it writes (the KV cache, below). Only the lower triangle of scores is needed: about half of n².
Say it“The change in z-t with respect to any later token is zero; each head computes n times n plus one over two scores.”

The output

From logits to words

The vector at the top of the stack is 64 numbers; the model must turn it into one probability per token. It does so by comparing it, with a dot product, with every token’s own embedding: the token whose embedding points most nearly the same way gets the largest logit. Using the input table E again at the output is called weight tying (Press & Wolf, 2017; Inan et al., 2017); GPT-2 does it, and many newer models keep a separate output matrix instead. A softmax then turns logits into probabilities. Only differences between logits matter, and dividing them all by a temperature τ sharpens (τ < 1) or flattens (τ > 1) the distribution; chapter 4.5 is about how to choose from it. Type a text, then change the temperature.

\[ \begin{aligned} z_i &= \operatorname{LN}_f\big(\mathbf{h}^{(L)}_t\big)\cdot \mathbf{e}_i \\ p_i &= \frac{e^{\,z_i/\tau}}{\sum_{j=1}^{75} e^{\,z_j/\tau}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ei is row i of the embedding table, the same vector that represents character i on the way in. Its dot product with the final vector is the logit zi. The softmax exponentiates and normalises. τ = 1 gives the model’s own probabilities, the ones its loss was trained on.
Say it“z-i is the final vector dotted with e-i; p-i is e to the z-i over tau, over the sum of the same for every token.”
\[ \begin{gathered} \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z} + c\,\mathbf{1}) = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z}) \\ H = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: adding the same number c to every logit multiplies every ez by ec, which cancels in the fraction, so logits have no absolute meaning; only gaps between them do. The entropy H, in bits, measures how torn the model is: 0 when it is certain, log2 75 ≈ 6.23 when every character is equally likely.
Say it“Softmax of z plus a constant equals softmax of z; the entropy is minus the sum of p-i log-two p-i.”

The highway

The residual stream

Look again at the stack: no sub-layer ever replaces the vector. Each one reads a normalised copy and adds its output. So the vector at the top is a plain sum: the token’s embedding, its position vector, and one write from every attention layer and every MLP. Elhage et al. (2021) call this running sum the residual stream and think of it as a shared bus: a 64-lane channel that each layer reads from and writes to. Because the final layer norm only centres and rescales, each write’s share of a logit can be read off exactly (direct logit attribution). That shows what each part says directly; a write can also matter indirectly, by changing what later layers read. To see that, switch parts off. Pick a character and switch layers off.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{h}^{(L)}_t = {}& \underbrace{E[x_t]}_{\text{token}} + \underbrace{P_t}_{\text{position}} \\ &+ \sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\big(\mathbf{a}^{(\ell)}_t + \mathbf{m}^{(\ell)}_t\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a(ℓ) is what the attention of block ℓ wrote at this position, m(ℓ) what its MLP wrote. The sum is exact: the stream is nothing but ten vectors added together, the token, the position and two writes per block (eight, for L = 4).
Say it“h-L at t is the token embedding plus the position vector plus the sum over layers of what attention and the MLP wrote.”
\[ \begin{aligned} z_w - \bar z &= \sum_{c} s_c + \boldsymbol\beta\cdot\Delta\mathbf{e}_w \\ s_c &= \frac{\boldsymbol\gamma \odot (\mathbf{c} - \bar c\,\mathbf{1})}{\sigma}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{e}_w \\ \Delta\mathbf{e}_w &= \mathbf{e}_w - \bar{\mathbf{e}} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: how far the logit of character w stands above the average logit z̄, split into one share sc per write c. c̄ is the mean of c’s 64 numbers, σ the standard deviation of the whole stream h(L)t, γ and β the final layer norm’s scale and shift, ē the average embedding, so Δew is how ew differs from a typical character. Since σ is shared by all parts, the shares add up exactly.
Say it“z-w minus the mean logit is the sum of the shares s-c, plus beta dotted with delta-e-w; each share is gamma times the centred write over sigma, dotted with delta-e-w.”

Reading the stream

The logit lens: what does it think so far?

If the stream is a sum that is only decoded at the very end, nothing stops us decoding it early. Apply the final layer norm and the unembedding to the stream after block 1, 2 or 3, and you get the model’s “guess so far”. This trick, the logit lens, was introduced by the pseudonymous blogger nostalgebraist (2020) on GPT-2; it works because every layer writes into the same space the final layer reads. It is only a lens, not the truth: the middle layers were never trained to be read this way (Belrose et al., 2023, train a small correction for each layer, the tuned lens). Type a text and click any cell to see the guesses at that layer.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}_t &= \operatorname{LN}_f\big(\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}_t\big)\,E^{\top} \\ \mathbf{p}^{(\ell)}_t &= \operatorname{softmax}\big(\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}_t\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: for ℓ = 0, 1, …, L, decode the stream after block ℓ exactly as if it were the last one. ℓ = 0 reads the raw embedding plus position: it can only know the current character, so it behaves like a bigram model. ℓ = L is the real output. Scoring each layer’s guesses with the loss shows how much each block adds.
Say it“z at layer ell is the final layer norm of h-ell times E-transpose; p at layer ell is its softmax.”

Moving information

What the heads learned

Attention is the only part of the model that moves information from one position to another; the MLPs work on each position alone. So the 16 heads (4 per block) are where the model decides which earlier characters matter, and nobody tells them what to look for. In large trained transformers, Anthropic’s researchers keep finding two kinds of head (Elhage et al., 2021; Olsson et al., 2022): previous-token heads, which look one step back, and induction heads, which find the earlier place where the current token appeared and look at what came next there, so the model can continue a pattern it has only just seen. Does our small model have them? The fair test for copying is a string of random letters written twice: nothing learned from Austen can predict it, so any saving on the second copy has to come from the context. Click the heads, then switch to random letters.

\[ \text{prev}(h) = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{t=2}^{n} A^{h}_{t,\,t-1} \]
Read it as: the share of its attention that head h puts on the position just before, averaged over the text. A perfect previous-token head scores 1. Combined with the previous character’s identity, it lets a later layer know “what came before me”.
Say it“The previous-token score of head h is the average attention from each position to the one before it.”
\[ \text{ind}(h) = \frac{1}{R-1}\sum_{t=R+1}^{2R-1} A^{h}_{t,\,t-R+1} \]
Read it as: on a random string of R letters written twice, position t in the second copy is the letter from position t − R; the letter that followed it then sits at t − R + 1. The induction score is the attention head h puts exactly there. Such a head turns “…AB…A” into a confident “B”.
Say it“The induction score is the average attention from each position in the second copy to the position just after its twin in the first.”

What we found. Our heads are mostly local: they spread their attention over the current character and the two or three before it, and a few of them reach back to the space that starts the current word. That is what a character model most needs, to know which word it is spelling. But there is no induction head. The second copy of random letters costs nearly as much as the first, and no head attends along the induction diagonal. We even tried to coax one out: for the last 3,500 of the 6,000 training steps, 40% of the training windows contained a short passage (a phrase from the novels, or random letters) written twice. It did not happen. A circuit that would lower the loss is not guaranteed to be found: in Olsson et al.’s models, trained on billions of tokens, induction heads appeared suddenly, in a “phase change”, and our model has read 25 million characters. This is an honest negative result, and a useful one: what a transformer can represent and what training actually finds are different questions.

Writing

Writing one token at a time, and the KV cache

To write, the model predicts, a token is chosen, appended, and the longer text goes back in. Done naively, every new token re-runs the whole stack on the whole text. But the causal mask guarantees that nothing at an old position can change when a token is added, so its keys and values in every layer are the same as last time. A real implementation keeps them: the KV cache. Each new token then needs one pass for one position, and its query is compared with the cached keys. The price is memory: with long contexts and many users at once, the cache can take more memory than the weights themselves; grouped-query attention (Ainslie et al., 2023), used in Llama 2 70B, shrinks it by sharing each key and value head among several query heads. Step the model forward one character at a time, then compare real models.

\[ \begin{gathered} x_{t+1} \sim p_\theta(\,\cdot \mid x_{\le t}) \\ \mathbf{k}^{(\ell)}_{s},\ \mathbf{v}^{(\ell)}_{s}\ \ (s \le t): \\ \text{reused, not recomputed} \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: draw the next token from the model’s distribution (here: the top choice, or a draw at temperature 0.8), append it, repeat. In every layer ℓ, the keys and values of the earlier positions do not depend on the new token, thanks to the mask, so only the new position’s query, key and value are computed.
Say it“x-t-plus-one is drawn from p-theta given x up to t; the keys and values of earlier positions are reused.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{cache} &= 2\,L\,n\;n_{kv}\,d_{\text{head}}\;b \\ \text{FLOPs}_{\text{cache}} &\approx 2N + 2Lnd \\ \text{FLOPs}_{\text{no cache}} &\approx n\,(2N + Lnd) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: keys and values (2) for every layer (L), every position so far (n), every key-value head (nkv, each dhead wide), at b bytes a number (2 for 16-bit), gives the cache size in bytes. With a cache a new token costs about two operations per block weight N plus the attention over n positions (Kaplan et al., 2020); without one, all n positions are recomputed (their attention averages about n/2 positions each, hence L·n·d).
Say it“The cache is two times L times n times n-kv d-head times b bytes; a cached step costs about two N plus two L n d.”

Real-world project

Project: a tiny GPT that writes Austen

Everything on this page runs one real model. It was trained on five of Jane Austen’s six finished novels from Project Gutenberg (Pride and Prejudice, Sense and Sensibility, Emma, Mansfield Park and Northanger Abbey: 3.5 million characters after cleaning), and is tested on the sixth, Persuasion, which it never saw. It is a GPT-2-shaped decoder: 4 blocks, width 64, 4 heads, a 128-character context, GELU MLPs, pre-layer-norm, learned positions and a tied output layer, 213,056 weights in all. It was trained with our own numpy code (a forward pass and a hand-written backward pass, checked against finite differences), using AdamW on batches of 32 random 128-character windows; in the last 3,500 batches, 40% of the windows had a short passage written twice inside them (section 7 explains why). The weights embedded in this page are rounded to 16 bits; your browser runs them in 64-bit arithmetic.

3 · The same model in numpy

The reference below loads the very weights embedded in this page and runs them in numpy, in 64-bit floating point. The page computes the same quantities in JavaScript; the table above and the checks below put the two side by side.

import base64, json, math, collections, numpy as np
S = "./"                                                    # il-model.json and train.txt
M = json.load(open(S + "il-model.json"))                    # the float16 weights embedded in this page
W = {k: np.frombuffer(base64.b64decode(v), "<f2").astype(float).reshape(M["shapes"][k]) for k, v in M["w"].items()}
VOC = M["vocab"]; d, L, H = 64, 4, 4; dk = d // H
enc = lambda s: [VOC.index(c) for c in s]

def ln(x, p): return (x - x.mean(-1, keepdims=True)) / np.sqrt(x.var(-1, keepdims=True) + 1e-5) * W[p + ".g"] + W[p + ".b"]
def gelu(u): return 0.5 * u * (1 + np.tanh(math.sqrt(2 / math.pi) * (u + 0.044715 * u ** 3)))
def gpt(ids):
    """logits for every position, and the residual stream after the embedding and after each block"""
    n = len(ids); x = W["E"][ids] + W["P"][:n]; stream = [x]
    mask = np.triu(np.full((n, n), -np.inf), 1)                # position i may not look at j > i
    for l in range(L):
        p = f"blocks.{l}."
        q, k, v = np.split(ln(x, p + "ln1") @ W[p + "qkv.W"] + W[p + "qkv.b"], 3, -1)
        q, k, v = (z.reshape(n, H, dk).transpose(1, 0, 2) for z in (q, k, v))
        s = q @ k.transpose(0, 2, 1) / math.sqrt(dk) + mask
        a = np.exp(s - s.max(-1, keepdims=True)); a /= a.sum(-1, keepdims=True)
        x = x + (a @ v).transpose(1, 0, 2).reshape(n, d) @ W[p + "o.W"] + W[p + "o.b"]
        x = x + gelu(ln(x, p + "ln2") @ W[p + "fc.W"] + W[p + "fc.b"]) @ W[p + "pr.W"] + W[p + "pr.b"]
        stream.append(x)
    return ln(x, "lnf") @ W["E"].T, stream                     # output layer tied to the embedding

def logsm(z): z = z - z.max(-1, keepdims=True); return z - np.log(np.exp(z).sum(-1, keepdims=True))
bits = lambda lp: -lp.mean() / math.log(2)

# 1. Persuasion, a novel the model never saw: 48 windows of 128 predictions each, every layer read through the logit lens
lens = np.zeros(L + 1); final = []
for w in M["test"]:
    ids = enc(w); z, stream = gpt(ids[:-1]); y = ids[1:]
    final.append(logsm(z)[range(128), y])
    for l, h in enumerate(stream): lens[l] += -logsm(ln(h, "lnf") @ W["E"].T)[range(128), y].sum()
print(f"tiny GPT on Persuasion: {bits(np.concatenate(final)):.4f} bits/char over {128 * len(M['test'])} predictions")
print("logit lens, bits/char after embedding, block 1..4:", " ".join(f"{v / 128 / len(M['test']) / math.log(2):.3f}" for v in lens))

# 2. next-character probabilities for one prompt
z, _ = gpt(enc("It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good f"))
p = np.exp(logsm(z[-1])); top = np.argsort(-p)[:5]
print("after '...of a good f':", "  ".join(f"{VOC[i]!r} {p[i]:.4f}" for i in top))

# 3. the copying test: 30 random letters, then the same 30 again
r = np.random.default_rng(4); s = "".join(r.choice(list("abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz"), 30))
ids = enc(s + s); z, _ = gpt(ids[:-1]); lp = logsm(z)[range(59), ids[1:]]
print(f"copy test {s!r}: first copy {bits(lp[:29]):.3f} bits/char, second copy {bits(lp[30:]):.3f} bits/char")

# 4. a character n-gram with interpolated Kneser-Ney (chapter 4.1), trained on the same five novels, same predictions
train = open(S + "train.txt", encoding="utf-8").read(); N = 6; D = 0.75
cnt = [collections.Counter(train[i:i + n] for i in range(len(train) - n + 1)) for n in range(1, N + 1)]
cont = [collections.Counter(g[1:] for g in cnt[n]) for n in range(1, N)]          # N1+(. g): distinct left neighbours
def stats(C):
    tot, typ = collections.Counter(), collections.Counter()
    for g, c in C.items(): tot[g[:-1]] += c; typ[g[:-1]] += 1
    return tot, typ
ST = [stats(c) for c in cnt]; CT = [stats(c) for c in cont]
def p_kn(h, c, top=True):
    if not h: n1 = cont[0]; return 0.98 * n1[c] / sum(n1.values()) + 0.02 / len(VOC) if n1 else 1 / len(VOC)
    n = len(h); C, (tot, typ) = (cnt[n], ST[n]) if top else (cont[n], CT[n])
    if tot[h] == 0: return p_kn(h[1:], c, False)
    return max(C[h + c] - D, 0) / tot[h] + D * typ[h] / tot[h] * p_kn(h[1:], c, False)
print(f"n-gram tables (orders 1-6): {sum(map(len, cnt)):,} distinct n-grams counted")
for n in (2, 4, 6):
    lp = [math.log(p_kn(w[max(0, t - n + 2):t + 1], w[t + 1])) for w in M["test"] for t in range(128)]
    print(f"Kneser-Ney {n}-gram on the same predictions: {bits(np.array(lp)):.4f} bits/char")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A decoder-only LLM is one stack: embed the tokens and their positions, pass them through L blocks of masked self-attention and MLPs, and score the top vector against every token’s embedding.
  • It is trained to predict the next token at every position at once; the loss is the average surprise, −log p, and in bits per token it says how well the model compresses text.
  • The causal mask sets every score towards the future to −∞, so position t never depends on later tokens. That makes one pass equal to n honest predictions, and makes the KV cache possible.
  • The residual stream is a sum: every block reads it and adds its write. Decoding it early (the logit lens) or splitting a logit into each write’s share shows where a prediction is formed.
  • Heads learn jobs nobody assigned. Ours look at the last few characters and the start of the current word; large models also grow induction heads that copy from context, but ours, after 25 million characters, never did. Writing reuses cached keys and values, trading memory for speed.

Check your understanding

A model is trained on 1,000 windows of 128 tokens. How many next-token predictions does each training pass learn from, and why can they all be made at once?

1,000 × 128 = 128,000 (one per position, if each window also holds its 129th token as the last target). The causal mask makes each position’s prediction depend only on its own prefix, so the single pass is exactly equivalent to 128,000 separate predictions.

Someone removes the causal mask during training. The training loss drops to nearly zero. Is the model better?

No. Each position can now attend to the next token, which is its own answer, and learns to copy it. When writing, the next token does not exist yet, so the copying skill is useless and the model’s predictions are poor.

Adding 5 to every logit, or dividing every logit by 2: which changes the probabilities?

Adding 5 changes nothing, because softmax depends only on differences between logits. Dividing by 2 is temperature τ = 2: the gaps halve and the distribution becomes flatter.

Why does the logit lens work at all, even though the middle layers were never trained to produce outputs?

Because of the residual stream: every layer adds its output to the same vector space that the final layer norm and unembedding read. The middle of the stream is a partial sum of the final one, so decoding it gives a rough early version of the answer. It is approximate; the tuned lens corrects it with a small trained map per layer.

Llama 2 7B has 32 layers of width 4,096 and stores keys and values in 16 bits. How much KV cache does one 4,096-token conversation need?

2 × 32 × 4,096 × 4,096 numbers × 2 bytes = 2,147,483,648 bytes = 2 GiB: half a mebibyte per token. That is why long contexts are memory-hungry, and why larger models share key-value heads (grouped-query attention).
