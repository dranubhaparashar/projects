Conditional Independence & d-Separation

Which facts can change which beliefs? The arrows already know.

Chapter 6.1 built a Bayesian network for rain, a sprinkler and wet grass. Here it grows into a whole garden: a cloudy morning, muddy shoes, an umbrella, a traffic jam and being late for work. Before computing a single probability, the shape of the graph tells you which pieces of evidence can move which beliefs, and which never can. The rule that reads this off the arrows is called d-separation. Tap nodes to observe them and pick the node you learn about: the glowing trails are the routes by which news about it can travel, and the bars show how much each other belief really moves.

Bishop 8.2 · the definition

What conditional independence means

Two variables are independent when learning one tells you nothing about the other. They are conditionally independent given c when, once you already know c, learning one tells you nothing more about the other. The two ideas are different, and most of this chapter lives in the gap between them.

Take three variables from the garden: Rain, whether people carry an Umbrella, and whether there is a Traffic jam. Umbrellas and traffic jams go together: see a crowd of umbrellas and you should expect slow roads. But both are caused by the rain. If you already know it is raining, the umbrellas add nothing: the traffic is as likely as it was a moment ago. Umbrella and Traffic are dependent, yet conditionally independent given Rain. Bishop writes this a ⊥ b | c.

The pictures below are mosaic plots. Each one is a unit square cut first into columns (Umbrella out or not) and then each column into a stack (traffic or not), with areas equal to probabilities. If the horizontal cut sits at the same height in both columns, the umbrella does not change the chance of traffic: that is independence, and you can see it. Move the sliders and watch the cuts: inside each rain level they line up; pooled together, they do not. Then add a direct link and break the conditional independence too.

\[ a \perp\!\!\!\perp b \mid c \iff p(a \mid b, c) = p(a \mid c) \]
Read it as: for every value of c (with p(b, c) > 0), knowing b as well as c leaves the distribution of a unchanged. The symbol ⊥⊥, often typed ⊥, means “is independent of”; the bar means “given”.
Say it“a is independent of b given c: once c is known, b tells you nothing more about a.”
\[ p(a, b \mid c) = p(a \mid c)\, p(b \mid c) \]
Read it as: the same statement, written symmetrically. Multiply both sides of the first form by p(b | c) and use p(a, b | c) = p(a | b, c) p(b | c). In each slice where c is fixed, the joint table of a and b is the product of its margins: the mosaic’s cuts line up.

Bishop 8.2.1 · three example graphs

Three ways to join three nodes

Every question about a big network comes down to how its nodes meet in threes. Take a path a — c — b. The middle node c can sit in only three kinds of junction, and Bishop names them by which end of each arrow touches c:

  • Chain (head-to-tail), a → c → b: Rain → Traffic → Late. Rain makes lateness likely, but only through the traffic. Know the traffic, and the rain adds nothing.
  • Fork (tail-to-tail), a ← c → b: Umbrella ← Rain → Traffic, the case you just met. A common cause makes its effects dependent, until you know the cause.
  • Collider (head-to-head), a → c ← b: Sprinkler → Wet grass ← Rain. The two causes are independent of each other. Know the effect, and they become dependent.

In the first two, observing c blocks the flow of information between a and b. In the collider it is the other way round: an unobserved collider blocks, and observing it opens the path. Choose a junction and toggle “observe c”; the bars compare P(b | a) for the two values of a, so a gap between them is dependence you can see.

I(a; b), bits
I(a; b | c), bits

The algebra for all three uses nothing but the factorisation of chapter 6.1 and the definition of conditional probability. For the fork, p(a, b, c) = p(c) p(a | c) p(b | c); divide by p(c) and the product of the two conditionals is left, so a ⊥ b | c. Summing c out instead leaves Σc p(c) p(a | c) p(b | c), a mixture that does not split into a function of a times a function of b. For the chain, p(a, b, c) = p(a) p(c | a) p(b | c); dividing by p(c) and using Bayes’ rule, p(a) p(c | a) / p(c) = p(a | c), gives the same product. For the collider, p(a, b, c) = p(a) p(b) p(c | a, b); summing over c removes the last factor, because it adds up to 1, so a ⊥ b with nothing observed. Condition on c and that factor stays, gluing a and b together.

The bars also show the qualifier every rule on this page carries: the graph says when independence is guaranteed. When the graph leaves a path open, the numbers usually show dependence, but they can hide it if an arrow is given no strength. Set either slider to 0 and the open path carries nothing.

Bishop 8.2.1 · the collider in action

Explaining away

Chapter 6.1 ended on a puzzle. The grass is wet, so rain becomes more likely. Then you learn the sprinkler was on, and rain becomes less likely again, even below where it started. Nothing about the sprinkler touches the rain directly. What happened is the collider: Sprinkler → Wet grass ← Rain. Once the wet grass is known, the two causes compete to explain it. Confirm one, and the other is no longer needed. This is explaining away. Bishop tells the same story with a car whose fuel gauge reads empty: learning the battery is flat makes an empty tank less likely, because a flat battery could make the gauge misread.

There is a second, sneakier way to open a collider: observe one of its descendants. Muddy shoes are a noisy report of wet grass. Seeing them raises your belief in wet grass, which is enough to set rain and sprinkler competing again, more weakly. Pick what you have seen, and try the gardener of chapter 6.1, who seldom runs the sprinkler on a rainy day, against a gardener who never checks the weather.

Read it as: R, S, W stand for “rain”, “sprinkler on”, “wet grass” (all true). P(R, W) is the probability that it rains and the grass is wet. With only the wet grass known, the sprinkler is summed out. Once it is known, its value is plugged in, and because the sprinkler alone explains wet grass well, the rain’s share of the explanation shrinks. When the gardener ignores the weather, P(s | r) is the same for both r.
Say it“Rain given wet grass sums over the sprinkler; rain given wet grass and sprinkler plugs it in.”

d-separation: the rule for any graph

A big graph is just many junctions strung together. To ask whether X ⊥ Y | Z, list every path between X and Y: a route along the edges that ignores their direction and never visits a node twice. Walk along each path, and at every node in the middle look at the junction it forms with its two neighbours on that path.

A path is blocked by Z if it has at least one middle node where

  • the arrows meet head-to-tail (chain) or tail-to-tail (fork), and the node is in Z; or
  • the arrows meet head-to-head (collider), and neither the node nor any of its descendants is in Z.

A path that is not blocked is active (or open). If every path between X and Y is blocked, Z d-separates them, and then X ⊥ Y | Z holds in every distribution that factorises over the graph, whatever numbers its tables contain. The “d” stands for directed. Pick X and Y, observe some nodes, and read the verdict on every path; then check it against the conditional mutual information computed from the garden’s tables.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\pi \text{ is blocked by } Z \iff \text{some middle} \\ &\quad \text{node } m \text{ is a chain or fork node} \\ &\qquad \text{with } m \in Z, \\ &\quad \text{or a collider with} \\ &\qquad (\{m\} \cup \mathrm{de}(m)) \cap Z = \varnothing \\[4pt] &X \perp_{d} Y \mid Z \iff \text{all paths} \\ &\qquad \text{are blocked} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: de(m) is the set of descendants of m (its children, their children, and so on). One blocking node is enough to block a path; one active path is enough to connect X and Y. The subscript d marks a statement about the graph, which then implies the statement X ⊥ Y | Z about the distribution.
Say it“X and Y are d-separated by Z when every path between them is blocked: by an observed chain or fork node, or by a collider with nothing observed at or below it.”

    Two machines that check it for you

    Listing paths works by hand, but a graph can have exponentially many of them. Two procedures give the same answer without listing any.

    The Bayes ball (Shachter, 1998; it appears as the Reachable procedure in Koller and Friedman) sends a ball out from X and lets it bounce. It remembers whether it arrived at a node going up (from a child) or down (from a parent), because the junction rules depend on that. Arriving up at an unobserved node, it may go on to the parents and the children. Arriving down at an unobserved node, it may only continue down to the children: going back up would mean passing through a collider. Arriving down at a node that is observed or has an observed descendant, it may turn back up to the parents: that is a collider being opened. Every node the ball reaches is d-connected to X. Each (node, direction) pair is visited at most once, so the work grows only with the number of edges.

    The moral ancestral graph is the test Russell and Norvig give. (1) Keep only X, Y, Z and their ancestors. (2) Marry the parents: join every pair of nodes that share a child. (3) Forget the directions. (4) Delete the observed nodes Z. X and Y are d-separated exactly when no path between them is left. Step 4 is the test for undirected graphs that Goodfellow et al. call plain separation: in an undirected model, Z separates X from Y when every path between them passes through Z. Marrying the parents is what turns the collider rule into that simpler one. Step through both machines on the question you set up above; they always agree.

    \[ \begin{aligned} &X \perp_d Y \mid Z \iff \\ &\quad Z \text{ separates } X \text{ and } Y \\ &\quad \text{in } \big(G_{\mathrm{An}(X \cup Y \cup Z)}\big)^{m} \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: An(·) is the set together with all its ancestors, G restricted to it is the ancestral subgraph, and the superscript m means moralised: parents married, directions dropped. “Separates” is the undirected test: every path from X to Y passes through Z.
    Say it“d-separation in the graph is plain separation in the moral graph of the ancestors.”
    \[ \begin{aligned} (v, \uparrow),\ v \notin Z &\;\Rightarrow\; \mathrm{pa}(v)\!\uparrow,\ \mathrm{ch}(v)\!\downarrow \\ (v, \downarrow),\ v \notin Z &\;\Rightarrow\; \mathrm{ch}(v)\!\downarrow \\ (v, \downarrow),\ v \in \mathrm{An}(Z) &\;\Rightarrow\; \mathrm{pa}(v)\!\uparrow \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: the ball’s three moves. ↑ means it arrived from a child, ↓ from a parent. An(Z) is Z and its ancestors, the nodes with an observed descendant (or observed themselves). An observed node arrived at going up stops the ball: that is the chain and fork rule.
    Say it“Up at a free node, go anywhere; down at a free node, keep going down; down at an observed node or above one, bounce back up.”

    The graph as a filter

    Chapter 6.1 built networks from one assumption, the local Markov property: each node is conditionally independent of its non-descendants given its parents. Russell and Norvig call this the topological semantics of a network, and show it is equivalent to the numerical one, the factorisation into one table per node. d-separation is the global version: it lists every independence that the local statements imply, through chains of reasoning you would never do by hand.

    Bishop pictures a graph as a filter. Pour in every distribution over the variables; let through only those that satisfy every d-separation statement of the graph. Separately, collect every distribution that factorises over the graph. The two sets are the same. So “built from these tables” and “obeys these independences” are two descriptions of one family.

    The lab checks this on the garden network. It takes all 1,792 statements X ⊥ Y | Z (every pair of nodes, every set Z of the other six), asks d-separation about each, and computes the true conditional mutual information from a set of tables drawn at random. Draw new tables a few times: the d-separated statements always sit at zero (to rounding error), whatever the numbers. Then add the accident, a sprinkler table that ignores the clouds, and see the other half of the story: a distribution can have more independences than its graph shows.

    \[ x_i \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathrm{nd}(x_i) \setminus \mathrm{pa}(x_i) \;\big|\; \mathrm{pa}(x_i) \]
    Read it as: nd(xi) are the non-descendants of xi: every node except xi and the nodes below it. Once the parents are known, nothing else above or beside xi tells you anything more about it.
    Say it“Each node is independent of its non-descendants given its parents.”
    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{DF} &= \Big\{ p : p(\mathbf{x}) = \textstyle\prod_i p(x_i \mid \mathrm{pa}_i) \Big\} \\ \mathcal{DI} &= \big\{ p : p \text{ obeys every} \\ &\qquad\quad \text{d-separation of } G \big\} \\ \mathcal{DF} &= \mathcal{DI} \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: DF is the set of distributions that factorise over G, DI the set that pass the filter. Their equality is a theorem, proved in 1990 by Lauritzen and colleagues and by Geiger, Verma and Pearl. The graph promises each independence it implies; it never promises that these are all the independences a particular distribution has.
    Say it“Factorising over the graph and passing its d-separation filter are the same thing.”

    The Markov blanket: all a node needs to know

    Suppose you know the value of every other node. Which of them actually matter for xi? Write the conditional as a ratio of two joints. Every factor of the joint that does not mention xi appears on the top and on the bottom and cancels. The factors left are xi’s own table and the tables of its children. Those mention xi’s parents, its children, and its children’s other parents (the co-parents). That set is the Markov blanket of xi. Given its blanket, a node is independent of everything else in the network.

    The co-parents are the surprise, and they are explaining away again: Sprinkler’s child Wet grass is known, so Rain, the other cause of the wet grass, matters for the sprinkler. Tap a node to make it the target, then flip the values of the others with the switches: flips outside the blanket never change the target’s probability. This is the fact that makes Gibbs sampling cheap: to resample one node you only ever look at its blanket.

    \[ \begin{aligned} p(x_i \mid \mathbf{x}_{\setminus i}) &= \frac{p(\mathbf{x})}{\sum_{x_i} p(\mathbf{x})} \\ &\propto p(x_i \mid \mathrm{pa}_i) \\ &\quad \times \textstyle\prod_{k \in \mathrm{ch}(i)} p(x_k \mid \mathrm{pa}_k) \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: x∖i is every variable except xi. ch(i) are the children of xi, and ∝ means “proportional to”: divide by the same product summed over both values of xi. Only these factors survive the cancellation, so only the parents, the children and the children’s other parents appear on the right.
    Say it“A node given everything else depends only on its own table and its children’s tables.”

    Bishop 8.2.2 · independence inside familiar models

    Independence you have already been using

    Several models from earlier chapters are d-separation statements in disguise. Bishop picks out three.

    Independent, identically distributed data. “The data points are i.i.d.” really means they are independent given the parameter. Draw a graph with a parameter θ at the top and the observations x1, …, xN as its children. Every path between two observations goes through θ, a fork; so given θ they are independent. But if θ is unknown, the fork is open, and each observation tells you about the next one, through what it says about θ. That is exactly why Bayesian prediction learns from data.

    Naive Bayes. The class y is the parent of every feature. Given the class, the features are independent; marginally they are not, because they share a cause. Chapter 1.5’s spam filter assumed the first statement, never the second.

    Bayesian regression. With weights w, training targets t and a new target t̂, all paths from t to t̂ pass through w, so t̂ ⊥ t | w. Knowing the weights exactly would make the training data useless for prediction; it is only because w is uncertain that the posterior over w carries the data’s information forward. Feed the coin some flips, then switch to naive Bayes and compare a feature’s probability with and without the class known.

    \[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta) &= \textstyle\prod_{n=1}^{N} p(x_n \mid \theta) \\ p(\mathcal{D}) &= \textstyle\sum_{\theta} p(\theta) \prod_{n=1}^{N} p(x_n \mid \theta) \\ &\ne \textstyle\prod_{n} p(x_n) \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: given the parameter the likelihood is a product: the observations are conditionally independent. Summing θ out mixes the products, and the mixture is not a product, so marginally the observations depend on each other. Here θ takes two values, fair or trick; for a continuous θ the sum becomes an integral.
    Say it“Independent given theta, dependent once theta is summed out.”
    \[ \begin{aligned} &p(x_{N+1} \mid \mathcal{D}) \\ &\quad = \sum_{\theta} p(x_{N+1} \mid \theta)\; p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: the next flip depends on the past flips only through the posterior over θ. If θ were observed, the past would drop out: p(xN+1 | θ, D) = p(xN+1 | θ). For naive Bayes the same shape reads p(x2 | x1) = Σy p(x2 | y) p(y | x1).
    Say it“Predict by averaging over theta, weighted by what the data say about theta.”
    Real-world project

    Project: put a network’s claims to a census

    A Bayesian network is a list of promises: every d-separation in its graph is an independence the data must show. So a network you draw for real data can be tested, claim by claim, before you fit a single table. The data here is the Adult census extract (B. Becker and R. Kohavi, 1996; UCI Machine Learning Repository, doi:10.24432/C5XW20, licensed CC BY 4.0): 48,842 adults extracted from the 1994 US Census database, the training and test files together. Six of its columns are used, cut into a few levels each: Sex (female, male), Age (17–29, 30–44, 45–59, 60+), Education (no high-school diploma, high-school graduate, some college or associate degree, bachelor’s or higher, from the file’s education-num 1–8, 9, 10–12, 13–16), Marital status (married, never married, divorced, separated, widowed or spouse absent), weekly Hours worked (under 40, exactly 40, over 40) and Income (above $50,000 a year or not). None of these six has missing values. The page embeds one cell code per person, in a fixed random order, so a sample of the first N people is the same in your browser and in Python.

    Each claim is the local Markov statement of one node, X ⊥ (non-descendants) | (parents), tested with the G² likelihood-ratio test. A small p-value says the data would be very surprising if the claim were true. Because 48,842 people can detect almost any dependence, the page also reports the conditional mutual information, which says how much the claim is wrong by. Start from a preset, then tap a node and another node to add an arrow (tap the same pair again to reverse it, a third time to remove it) and watch which claims fall.

    \[ \begin{aligned} G^2 &= 2 \sum_{x,y,z} n_{xyz} \ln \frac{n_{xyz}\, n_{z}}{n_{xz}\, n_{yz}} \\ &= 2N \ln 2 \cdot \hat I(X; Y \mid Z) \\ \mathrm{df} &= \textstyle\sum_{z} (r_z - 1)(c_z - 1) \\ p &= P\big(\chi^2_{\mathrm{df}} \ge G^2\big) \end{aligned} \]
    Read it as: nxyz counts the people with X = x, Y = y and Z = z; nz, nxz, nyz are the matching margins. G² compares each count with what the claim predicts from the margins, and equals 2N ln 2 times the conditional mutual information of the sample, in bits. If the claim is true, G² follows roughly a chi-squared distribution; rz and cz count the levels of X and of Y that actually occur in stratum z.

    The same tests in Python

    One script checks the garden network (two d-separation algorithms on all 1,792 statements, and the zero-information test with random tables) and runs every census test, using scipy for the chi-squared tail and scikit-learn’s mutual_info_score as an independent check of the information.

    import itertools, json, math, sys
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import chi2

# ---------- the garden network: every probability on the page comes from these tables ----------
NODES = ["Cloudy", "Sprinkler", "Rain", "WetGrass", "MuddyShoes", "Umbrella", "Traffic", "Late"]
PA = [[], [0], [0], [1, 2], [3], [2], [2], [6]]
CPT = [[0.5], [0.5, 0.1], [0.2, 0.8], [0.0, 0.9, 0.8, 0.99], [0.1, 0.7], [0.15, 0.85], [0.2, 0.6], [0.1, 0.7]]

def joint(pa, cpt):                                # P(world) for all 2^n worlds, by the factorisation
    m = len(pa); P = np.ones(2 ** m)
    for w in range(2 ** m):
        for i in range(m):
            k = sum(((w >> p) & 1) << j for j, p in enumerate(pa[i])); q = cpt[i][k]
            P[w] *= q if (w >> i) & 1 else 1 - q
    return P

def prob(P, ev):                                   # P(evidence), evidence = {node: 0/1}
    w = np.arange(len(P)); m = np.ones(len(P), bool)
    for i, v in ev.items(): m &= ((w >> i) & 1) == v
    return P[m].sum()

def cmi(P, x, y, z):                               # I(X;Y|Z) in bits, from the full joint
    w = np.arange(len(P)); tot = 0.0
    for zv in itertools.product([0, 1], repeat=len(z)):
        ez = dict(zip(z, zv)); pz = prob(P, ez)
        if pz <= 0: continue
        for a in (0, 1):
            for b in (0, 1):
                pxyz = prob(P, {**ez, x: a, y: b}); pxz = prob(P, {**ez, x: a}); pyz = prob(P, {**ez, y: b})
                if pxyz > 0: tot += pxyz * math.log2(pxyz * pz / (pxz * pyz))
    return tot

def kids(pa): return [[j for j in range(len(pa)) if i in pa[j]] for i in range(len(pa))]
def ancestors(pa, S):
    out, st = set(S), list(S)
    while st:
        for p in pa[st.pop()]:
            if p not in out: out.add(p); st.append(p)
    return out

def bayes_ball(pa, x, z):                          # nodes reachable from x by an active trail given z
    ch, A = kids(pa), ancestors(pa, z); seen, reach, todo = set(), set(), [(x, "up")]
    while todo:
        v, d = todo.pop()
        if (v, d) in seen: continue
        seen.add((v, d))
        if v not in z: reach.add(v)
        if d == "up" and v not in z: todo += [(p, "up") for p in pa[v]] + [(c, "down") for c in ch[v]]
        elif d == "down":
            if v not in z: todo += [(c, "down") for c in ch[v]]
            if v in A: todo += [(p, "up") for p in pa[v]]
    return reach - {x}

def moral_separated(pa, x, y, z):                  # Russell & Norvig: ancestral graph, moralise, cut z
    An = ancestors(pa, {x, y} | set(z)); adj = {v: set() for v in An}
    for v in An:
        for p in pa[v]: adj[v].add(p); adj[p].add(v)
        for p, q in itertools.combinations(pa[v], 2): adj[p].add(q); adj[q].add(p)
    seen, st = {x}, [x]
    while st:
        for u in adj[st.pop()]:
            if u not in seen and u not in z: seen.add(u); st.append(u)
    return y not in seen

def random_cpts(pa, seed):
    r = rng(seed); return [[0.05 + 0.9 * r() for _ in range(2 ** len(p))] for p in pa]

def code(X, cols):                              # several columns -> one joint category
    v = np.zeros(len(X), dtype=int)
    for c in cols: v = v * K[c] + X[:, c]
    return v

def g2_test(X, a, b, z):
    """G-squared test of  a _||_ b | z  (a, b, z lists of column numbers)."""
    A, B, Z = code(X, a), code(X, b), code(X, z)
    G, df, cmi = 0.0, 0, 0.0
    for zv in np.unique(Z):
        m = Z == zv
        t = np.zeros((A.max() + 1, B.max() + 1)); np.add.at(t, (A[m], B[m]), 1)
        t = t[t.sum(1) > 0][:, t.sum(0) > 0]   # drop empty rows and columns of this stratum
        e = t.sum(1, keepdims=True) * t.sum(0, keepdims=True) / t.sum()
        nz = t > 0
        G += 2 * (t[nz] * np.log(t[nz] / e[nz])).sum()
        df += (t.shape[0] - 1) * (t.shape[1] - 1)
        cmi += m.mean() * mutual_info_score(A[m], B[m])  # independent check, in nats
    p = chi2.sf(G, df) if df > 0 else 1.0
    return G, df, p, G / (2 * len(X) * np.log(2)), cmi / np.log(2)

def local_markov(parents):                      # X _||_ non-descendants(X) \ pa(X) | pa(X)
    n = len(parents); kids = [[j for j in range(n) if i in parents[j]] for i in range(n)]
    out = []
    for x in range(n):
        desc, st = set(), list(kids[x])
        while st:
            d = st.pop()
            if d not in desc: desc.add(d); st += kids[d]
        rest = [y for y in range(n) if y != x and y not in desc and y not in parents[x]]
        if rest: out.append(([x], rest, sorted(parents[x])))
    return out

def entropy(X, cols):                           # plug-in entropy of a set of columns, bits
    if not cols: return 0.0
    _, n = np.unique(code(X, cols), return_counts=True); p = n / n.sum()
    return -(p * np.log2(p)).sum()
def bits_lost(X, par):                          # KL(data || best network with this graph), bits per person
    return sum(entropy(X, [i] + par[i]) - entropy(X, par[i]) for i in range(len(par))) - entropy(X, list(range(len(par))))
def n_params(par): return sum((K[i] - 1) * int(np.prod([K[p] for p in par[i]])) for i in range(len(par)))

    Wrap-up

    Five lines to remember

    • a ⊥ b | c means that once c is known, b tells you nothing more about a: p(a | b, c) = p(a | c), or p(a, b | c) = p(a | c) p(b | c). It neither implies nor follows from plain independence.
    • Three junctions decide everything. A chain or a fork carries information until its middle node is observed; a collider blocks it until the collider, or one of its descendants, is observed.
    • Observing a common effect makes its causes compete: explaining away. Confirming the sprinkler lowers the chance of rain, even though neither causes the other.
    • d-separation: if every path between X and Y is blocked by Z, then X ⊥ Y | Z in every distribution that factorises over the graph. The Bayes ball and the moral ancestral graph check it without listing paths. The converse is not guaranteed: tuned numbers can add independences the graph does not show.
    • A node’s Markov blanket (parents, children, co-parents) shields it from the rest of the network. Real data can test a graph’s claims; with large samples, judge them by how much information leaks, not only by p-values.

    Check your understanding

    In the garden, are Sprinkler and Late independent? Does observing Wet grass change that?

    With nothing observed, the path Sprinkler ← Cloudy → Rain → Traffic → Late is active (a fork at Cloudy, chains elsewhere), so they are dependent. Observing Wet grass opens a second path, Sprinkler → Wet grass ← Rain → Traffic → Late, and the first stays open, so they are still dependent. Observing Rain would block both paths at once.

    Why does observing muddy shoes open the path between rain and sprinkler, when the shoes are not on the path?

    Muddy shoes are evidence about wet grass. Seeing them makes wet grass more likely, and wet grass is the collider. Once the effect becomes more likely, its causes compete to explain it, so learning one of them changes the belief in the other. This is why the collider rule mentions descendants.

    A node’s Markov blanket includes its children’s other parents. Give the reason in one sentence.

    When everything else is known, the node’s children are observed, and each observed child is a collider between the node and that child’s other parents, so those co-parents are d-connected to the node through the child.

    Two variables are not d-separated, yet their conditional mutual information is exactly zero. Is the graph wrong?

    No. d-separation promises independence; it does not promise dependence. A particular set of tables can make an open path carry nothing, as when the sprinkler’s table ignores the clouds although the arrow is there. Such a distribution is called unfaithful to the graph. It is still a member of the family the graph describes.

    On the full census every claim of every network is rejected. Does that make all of them useless?

    Not necessarily. With 48,842 people even a dependence worth a hundredth of a bit gives a tiny p-value. The question for modelling is how much is lost by the claim, which the conditional mutual information measures; a network whose claims leak a few hundredths of a bit may be perfectly useful, while one whose claims leak tenths of a bit is not.
