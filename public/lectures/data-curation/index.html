Data for LLMs

Quality, coverage, deduplication and filtering: the pipeline between a web crawl and a training set

The previous chapter treated the training set as a number: so many trillion tokens. This one opens the box. Raw material

Where the text comes from

Nobody writes a trillion tokens for a language model. The text is found, and almost all of it is found in one place: web crawls. Common Crawl, a non-profit that has been saving snapshots of the public web since 2008, releases a few billion pages every month, and most open pretraining sets (C4, the Pile’s web portion, MassiveText, RefinedWeb, Dolma, FineWeb) start from it. A crawl is wide but shallow: it has every topic, every register and every language, but the typical page is a shop listing, a forum thread with the navigation bar scraped in, or a page that exists only to rank in a search engine. The other sources are narrow and deep. Books give long, edited, coherent prose; encyclopaedias give dense facts in a steady register; source code teaches syntax, logic and the habit of being exact; forums and question-and-answer sites give dialogue, instructions and the way people actually ask things; academic archives give technical vocabulary; licensed data (news archives, publishers’ catalogues, transcripts) is bought where crawling is not allowed or not enough.

The job of data curation is to combine them, and the combination is a trade between two things that pull in opposite directions. Quality is how much a document teaches per token: edited, informative, well-formed text teaches more than boilerplate. Coverage is how much of the world the data mentions at all: dialects, minority languages, niche hobbies, local news, informal registers, the ways people write when nobody is editing them. Almost every filter that raises the first lowers the second, because the text that scores badly on quality measures is not spread evenly over topics; it is concentrated in exactly the corners that only the web covers. The toy model below makes the trade visible. Six sources contribute documents to forty topics; each document has a quality score drawn from its source’s distribution. Raise the quality threshold and watch how many topics drop out of the training set entirely before the retained share of tokens has fallen very far.

Web crawls widest

Billions of pages; every topic and register, but dominated by boilerplate, duplicates and search-engine bait. Needs the most filtering and gives the most coverage.

Source code exact

Public repositories, with licences that must be checked. Teaches structure, naming and step-by-step reasoning; its own quality problems are generated files, minified bundles and copied vendored libraries.

Books longest

Edited prose with long-range structure: plot, argument, chapters. Scarce, copyright-laden and the single most contested source; a few hundred billion tokens exist in total.

Encyclopaedias densest

Wikipedia and its relatives: facts per token are high, the register is uniform, and that uniformity is also why “looks like Wikipedia” became a proxy for quality.

Forums and Q&A conversational

Questions, answers, arguments, corrections. The place where how-to knowledge and informal language live; also the place where toxicity and personal information concentrate.

Licensed and curated paid for

News archives, scientific publishers, transcripts, textbooks. Clean and well documented, but expensive, narrow in register, and the terms often restrict how the data may be shared or re-used.

Two further words belong in this vocabulary. Quantity is the third corner of the triangle: a pipeline that keeps only the best tenth of a crawl needs a crawl ten times as large to reach the token count a compute-optimal model wants, and for the largest models the limit on good text is no longer hypothetical. Acquisition and annotation cover everything outside the crawl: negotiating licences, crawling specific sites with their permission, transcribing speech, and paying people to write or label examples. Pretraining data is almost never annotated, but the filters in this chapter are themselves trained on annotated samples, from the handful of sentences per language in the language identifier to the preference labels behind a toxicity classifier, so the choices made during annotation flow straight into what the model reads.

The shape of the pipeline

The pipeline as a funnel

Every published pipeline is a sequence of filters, and the order is nearly always the same, because each stage is cheapest when the ones before it have already shrunk the data. First the HTML is turned into text (a boilerplate remover such as trafilatura or jusText strips menus, adverts and footers; this stage alone can halve the token count). Then language identification keeps the languages the model is meant to speak, usually the single largest cut in an English-only pipeline, since roughly half of the crawl is in other languages. Then quality filtering, by rules or by a classifier, removes documents that are too short, too repetitive, too full of symbols, or that do not look like the text one wants. Then deduplication, exact and near, removes the same page saved under many addresses, the same licence text on a million repositories, the same product description on a thousand shops. Then toxicity and personal-information filters remove or mask what should not be learned. Last, decontamination removes anything that overlaps with the evaluation sets the model will be judged on. Gopher’s MassiveText, RefinedWeb, Dolma and FineWeb all follow this order with their own thresholds, and all report that the whole funnel keeps on the order of a tenth of the crawl they started from.

The arithmetic is a product. If stage i keeps a fraction yi of what reaches it, the survivors are the product of the yields, and the crawl you need for a target training set is the target divided by that product. Set each yield to see both numbers. The defaults are in the range the published pipelines report; they are not measurements of any one of them.

\[ N_{\text{out}} = N_{\text{in}} \prod_{i=1}^{5} y_i, \qquad N_{\text{in}}^{\text{needed}} = \frac{T}{\prod_i y_i} \]
Read it as: yi is the fraction of tokens that pass stage i. Multiplying five yields of about one half gives about one thirtieth; a few yields near one barely matter. T is the number of training tokens you want; the crawl you must start from is T divided by the overall yield. The stages interact (deduplication removes a larger share of a corpus that quality filtering has not already cleaned), so treat the product as a planning estimate.

Stage one

Language identification with character n-grams

The first real decision about a document is what language it is in. The classic answer is a character n-gram model: count sequences of two, three or four characters in known text of each language, then score a new document by how well each language’s counts predict its own sequences. The trigrams “the”, “ th” and “ing” shout English; “sch”, “ung” and “ de” lean German; “ción” is Spanish and “zione” Italian. Production tools do this at scale: Common Crawl’s own language column uses CLD2, and most LLM pipelines use fastText’s lid.176 model (Joulin et al., 2017), a linear classifier over character n-grams and words trained on 176 languages that labels thousands of documents per second per core. The document is kept if the top language is the one wanted and its score is above a threshold (RefinedWeb used 0.65; Dolma and FineWeb similar values), and dropped otherwise.

The identifier below is trained in your browser on twelve sentences per language for six languages, written for this page. It is a multinomial naive Bayes model: each language has a bag of character trigrams, and a document’s log-probability under a language is the sum of the log-frequencies of its trigrams, with a small additive smoothing so that an unseen trigram costs a lot but not everything. The figure plots the running margin between the best and second-best language as the text is read from left to right. Type anything, or pick a sample, and notice two things: on a full paragraph the decision is made within a few dozen characters, and on a short string, a menu, a list of names or a table, the margin never grows, which is why real pipelines treat short documents and low scores as a separate problem. Try bigrams as well: with only twelve sentences per language they generalise better than trigrams on single sentences, because far fewer of them are unseen at test time, and that is the usual sparsity trade-off in miniature (fastText sidesteps it with hundreds of millions of training characters and hashed n-gram embeddings).

\[ \log P(L \mid d) \;\propto\; \log \pi_L + \sum_{g \in d} c_g \log \frac{n_{g,L} + \alpha}{N_L + \alpha (V + 1)} \]
Read it as: d is the document as a multiset of character n-grams g with counts cg; ng,L is how often g appeared in language L’s training text, NL the total number of n-grams seen for L, V the number of distinct n-grams seen across all languages, and α the smoothing count added to every n-gram, seen or not. The priors πL are equal here and drop out. The language with the largest sum wins; the difference between the top two sums, divided by the length of the document, is the per-character margin used as a confidence.

Three honest limits. First, the identifier is only as good as its training languages: a document in a language it has never seen is forced into the nearest one it knows, and the usual remedy, a minimum score, drops the long tail of languages and dialects wholesale, which is a coverage decision made silently at stage one. Second, closely related languages (Spanish and Portuguese, Danish and Norwegian, Hindi and Urdu in transliteration) are confused at rates that matter, and code-mixed documents are assigned to whichever language dominates by character count. Third, the model has no notion of “not language at all”: a table of numbers or a navigation bar still gets a top language, usually with a small margin. The quality rules in the next section exist partly to catch what this stage lets through with a shrug.

Stage two, by hand

Heuristic quality rules

The cheapest quality filter is a list of rules that any programmer could write in an afternoon and that catches an astonishing share of the junk. The best-known list is the one Rae et al. (2021) used to build MassiveText for Gopher, and most later pipelines (RefinedWeb, Dolma, FineWeb) adopted it with small changes. A document is removed if it has fewer than 50 or more than 100,000 words; if its mean word length is under 3 or over 10 characters (gibberish, or words run together); if more than a tenth of its words are hash signs or ellipses; if more than 90% of its lines begin with a bullet or more than 30% end with an ellipsis (lists and truncated teasers); if fewer than 80% of its words contain a letter (tables, code dumps, number lists); and if it contains fewer than two of eight common English stop words (the, be, to, of, and, that, have, with), a rule that doubles as a crude second language check. A second group targets repetition: too many duplicate lines or paragraphs, or too large a share of the characters belonging to the single most frequent 2-, 3- or 4-gram, or to 5- to 10-grams that occur more than once.

Below, every one of these rules runs live on the 41-document corpus that the project at the end of the chapter will push through the whole pipeline. The corpus was written for this page: twenty ordinary paragraphs, some copies and near-copies, some boilerplate, a few documents in other languages, one classified advertisement with a made-up phone number, and one pub quiz that happens to contain our benchmark questions. Pick a document to see its numbers against each threshold, and edit the thresholds to see which documents change sides. The rules are deliberately blunt; the point of the exercise is to feel what blunt means.

\[ \text{symbol ratio} = \frac{\#\{\texttt{\#}\} + \#\{\ldots\}}{\#\text{words}}, \qquad \text{top-}n\text{ share} = \frac{c_{\max}\,(|g_{\max}| - (n-1))}{\sum_w |w|}, \qquad \text{dup-}n\text{ share} = \frac{\sum_{w \in \text{repeated } n\text{-grams}} |w|}{\sum_w |w|} \]
Read it as: the symbol ratio counts hash signs and ellipses per word. The top-n share takes the most frequent word n-gram gmax, which occurs cmax times, and asks what fraction of all the document’s letters it accounts for (its length in letters, without the spaces, times its count). The dup-n share is the fraction of letters that sit inside any n-gram occurring more than once. Gopher’s thresholds are 0.20, 0.18 and 0.16 for the top 2-, 3- and 4-grams and from 0.15 (n = 5) down to 0.10 (n = 10) for the duplicated ones; this page implements n = 5 and n = 10 as representatives.

That gap is what classifiers are for.

Stage two, by model

Model-based quality classifiers and what they learn to dislike

GPT-3’s training set (Brown et al., 2020) introduced the move that most pipelines since have copied: train a classifier to tell reference text (Wikipedia, books, the curated WebText) from random crawl pages, score every crawled document, and keep it with a probability that rises steeply with the score. The classifier does not know what quality is; it knows what the curated sources look like, and it keeps whatever resembles them. CCNet did the same with a language model’s perplexity under Wikipedia, keeping the low-perplexity third of each language. Newer pipelines (FineWeb-Edu, DCLM) train on labels produced by a large language model asked to rate “educational value”, which moves the arbitrariness one step back without removing it. GPT-3 kept a document when np.random.pareto(9) > 1 − score, a soft rule that admits a trickle of low-scoring pages so that the model still sees them.

The classifier below is trained in your browser, on twenty short paragraphs written for this page: ten in an encyclopaedic register and ten in the register of social media and forums. It is a logistic regression on six style features, so that its reasons are visible, and it is then asked to score the 41 corpus documents. Look at the weights, then at the ranking. The model learns, correctly for its training data, that long words, long sentences, no “you” or “I” and no exclamation marks mean “reference”. Then it applies that lesson to the keyword-stuffed page and the legal footers, which it ranks near the top of the corpus, and to the forum post explaining how to fix a dripping tap, which it rejects. (The cookie banners score low too, but for the wrong reason: they are full of “you” and “your”.) Nothing about this toy is unfair to the real thing; the real thing has more features and the same logic.

\[ s(d) = \sigma\!\big(\mathbf w^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf x}(d) + b\big), \qquad P(\text{keep} \mid s) = P\big(\text{Pareto}(a) > 1 - s\big) = (2 - s)^{-a} \]
Read it as: x̃(d) is the vector of standardised style features of document d, w the learned weights, σ the logistic function, so s is the model’s probability that d is reference-like. GPT-3’s keep rule draws a Pareto (Lomax) variable with shape a = 9 and keeps the document if the draw exceeds 1 − s; since P(Pareto(a) > x) = (1 + x)−a, the keep probability is (2 − s)−a: certain at s = 1, about 2.6% at s = 0.5 and 0.2% at s = 0. Lower a makes the rule gentler.

The biases are not hypothetical. Dodge et al. (2021), documenting C4, found that its blocklist filter disproportionately removed text in African American English and text about, and by, LGBTQ+ people, including innocuous text. Gururangan et al. (2022) scored school newspapers from across the United States with a GPT-3-style quality classifier and found that it preferred articles from wealthier, more educated, more urban areas; the classifier was, in their phrase, encoding a language ideology about whose writing counts. Perplexity filters prefer the register of whatever the reference model was trained on, so they penalise dialect, slang, transcribed speech and most fiction. The honest description of a “looks like Wikipedia” filter is that it selects for a formal written standard produced mostly by educated people in a few countries, and that everything it filters out, the model will later be worse at understanding and worse at producing. The remedies are partial: softer keep rules (the Pareto trickle), quality scores that are balanced per domain or per dialect, audits of what was removed, and keeping the removed text around so that the decision can be revisited.

Stage three

Deduplication: Jaccard, MinHash and locality-sensitive hashing

The web repeats itself. The same article appears under a dozen URLs; the same licence text sits in a million repositories; the same product description is copied across hundreds of shops, each with a different price at the bottom. Exact duplicates are easy: normalise the text, hash it (SHA-1 or a 64-bit fingerprint) and keep the first document per hash. Near-duplicates are the real problem, because “the same but for a date, a name and a footer” cannot be caught by any hash of the whole document, and comparing every pair of a billion documents is a billion squared operations, which is out of the question. Broder (1997) solved both halves. Represent a document by its set of shingles, overlapping runs of k consecutive words (or characters). Measure the resemblance of two documents by the Jaccard similarity of their shingle sets: the size of the intersection over the size of the union. Then estimate that similarity, without ever forming the sets side by side, from a short MinHash signature.

The MinHash trick is one line of probability. Take a random hash function h that maps shingles to numbers and apply it to every shingle in A and every shingle in B; write down the smallest value in each set. The two minima are equal exactly when the shingle that achieves the minimum over A ∪ B lies in A ∩ B, and since h is random that shingle is a uniform draw from the union, so the probability of agreement is |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B| = J. Do this with n independent hash functions and the fraction of agreements is an unbiased estimate of J with standard deviation √(J(1 − J)/n); with 100 hashes a document of any length becomes 100 integers, and the similarity of two documents is the fraction of positions in which their signatures agree. Edit the two texts and watch the exact Jaccard and the MinHash estimate.

\[ J(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}, \qquad P\big[\min_{a \in A} h(a) = \min_{b \in B} h(b)\big] = J(A, B) \]
Read it as: A and B are the shingle sets. The minimum of a random hash over a set is a random member of that set; the two minima coincide precisely when the overall minimum over the union falls in the intersection, which happens with probability J. One hash function gives one coin flip with bias J; n functions give n flips.
Say it“The chance that two sets share their minimum hash equals their Jaccard similarity.”
\[ \hat J = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb 1\big[\sigma_i(A) = \sigma_i(B)\big], \qquad \operatorname{sd}(\hat J) = \sqrt{\frac{J(1 - J)}{n}} \]
Read it as: σi(A) is the i-th signature value of A, the minimum of hash hi over A’s shingles. The estimate is the fraction of matching positions. Its error shrinks like one over the square root of n: 100 hashes give about ±0.04 near J = 0.8, which is plenty for a yes/no decision about duplication. Locality-sensitive hashing: finding the pairs without looking at the pairs

Signatures make each comparison cheap, but there are still a billion squared pairs. Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) finds the similar pairs without enumerating the rest. Cut each signature of n = b · r values into b bands of r rows. Hash each band to a bucket; two documents become a candidate pair if they land in the same bucket for any band, which means all r values of that band agree. Since each position agrees with probability s (the true similarity), one band agrees with probability sr, and at least one of the b bands agrees with probability 1 − (1 − sr)b. This is an S-shaped curve of s whose steep part sits near the threshold (1/b)1/r: pairs far above it are almost always found, pairs far below almost never, and only the pairs near the threshold are a gamble. Candidate pairs are then checked exactly (or by comparing full signatures), and one member of each duplicate cluster is kept. Move b and r to see how the threshold and the steepness trade off; more bands with fewer rows lowers the threshold, more rows with fewer bands raises it, and more hashes in total sharpen the curve.

\[ P(\text{candidate} \mid s) = 1 - \big(1 - s^{r}\big)^{b}, \qquad s^{*} \approx \Big(\frac{1}{b}\Big)^{1/r} \]
Read it as: s is the Jaccard similarity of a pair (the probability that any one signature position agrees). sr is the chance that all r rows of a band agree; (1 − sr)b is the chance that no band agrees; one minus that is the chance the pair is flagged. s* is where the curve is steepest, roughly where flagged becomes more likely than not. Lee et al. (2022) used 9,000 hashes in 450 bands of 20 rows (threshold near 0.8) on 50-token shingles; RefinedWeb used 20 bands of 450 rows for a similar threshold; this page uses 20 bands of 5 rows, which puts s* near 0.55.

Why near-duplicates hurt. Lee et al. (2022) deduplicated C4 and other corpora with exactly this machinery (plus an exact-substring pass with a suffix array) and trained models on the results. Three findings matter. Models trained on deduplicated data regurgitated memorised training text about ten times less often when sampled. Perplexity on held-out data was the same or better, with fewer training steps, because the model stopped spending capacity on the hundredth copy of a licence agreement. And a surprising share of the validation sets overlapped with training data, so that the usual measure of generalisation was partly a measure of memorisation; after deduplication the measured perplexities shifted. Carlini et al. (2021) had already shown that memorised sequences can be extracted from a trained model by prompting, including personal information; duplicated data is the single strongest predictor of what gets memorised. Deduplication is therefore not tidying. It is the difference between a model that has read a thing and a model that has learned it by heart.

When good text runs out

What repeated data is worth

Deduplication and the appetite of large models point in opposite directions: one removes tokens, the other wants ever more. A pipeline that keeps a tenth of a crawl can run out of fresh text before a compute budget runs out, and then the only option is to read the same tokens again. Duplicates are the accidental version of this: a document that survives in a hundred copies is, for the model, a document trained on for a hundred epochs. How much is a repeated token worth? Muennighoff et al. (2023) trained over 400 models to answer that, varying how many unique tokens were available and how many times they were repeated, and fitted a simple law: the first few repetitions are worth almost as much as new data, and the value decays exponentially after that, with an effective ceiling of about sixteen passes. Hernandez et al. (2022) found the same shape from the other side, showing that a small fraction of data repeated many times degrades a model far more than its token share would predict, and that the degradation shows up first in exactly the capabilities that look like reasoning. The pretraining chapter plotted the effective-data formula; here it is wired into a loss curve, so that you can see the plateau a data-constrained run hits.

\[ D' = U + U R^{*}\big(1 - e^{-R/R^{*}}\big), \quad R = E - 1,\quad R^{*} = 15.4; \qquad L(N, D') = E_\infty + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D'^{\beta}} \]
Read it as: U is the number of unique tokens, E the number of epochs, R the number of repeats, and D′ the number of fresh tokens the repeated data is worth. The first pass is worth U; each further pass adds a shrinking amount, and the total can never exceed U(1 + R*) ≈ 16.4 U. R* = 15.4 is Muennighoff et al.’s fitted constant. The loss formula is the Chinchilla fit (Hoffmann et al., 2022: E∞ = 1.69, A = 406.4, B = 410.7, α = 0.34, β = 0.28) with D replaced by D′, an illustration of the shape rather than their exact fit, which used its own constants. N is the number of parameters.

Two practical consequences. First, the formula justifies the common rule of thumb that up to about four epochs over a well-deduplicated corpus is nearly free, that eight to sixteen is a real but acceptable loss, and that beyond that the compute would have been better spent on a smaller model trained on fewer passes. Second, it changes what deduplication is for. The near-duplicate pairs removed in the previous section are repeats the pipeline did not choose; removing them and then deliberately repeating the clean corpus two or three times gives the model the same number of tokens with a far flatter distribution of repetition, no document seen a hundred times and none seen once by accident. Stages four and five

Targeted filters: personal information, toxicity and benchmark contamination

The last filters do not ask whether a document is good; they ask whether it contains particular things that must not be learned. Three kinds matter. Personally identifiable information (PII): e-mail addresses, phone numbers, IP addresses, physical addresses, identity numbers, names in some contexts. A language model that has seen a phone number next to a name a few times will reproduce it, so pipelines mask or remove such strings; the StarCoder code dataset, for example, replaced e-mails, IP addresses and secret keys with stand-in tokens using a classifier trained on annotated code. Toxicity: slurs, harassment, sexual content, instructions for violence. Contamination: text from the evaluation sets the model will be scored on, which turns a test of generalisation into a test of recall. Each has a cheap pass (regular expressions, word lists, n-gram lookups) and an expensive pass (a classifier), and each has a false-positive problem that is worth seeing up close.

Patterns

Toxicity: word lists, classifiers and the dialect problem

C4 (Raffel et al., 2020) removed any page containing a word from a public list of “bad words”. It is the simplest possible toxicity filter and it illustrates every failure mode of the genre. A list matches words, not meanings: a medical page about breast cancer, a town whose name contains an offensive substring (the Scunthorpe problem, named after an English town that early profanity filters would not let people type), a history of the drug trade, a crime report, all go the way of the spam they were meant to catch. Dodge et al. (2021) measured this on C4 and found the filter had removed a disproportionate amount of text in African American English and text mentioning sexual orientation, most of it entirely benign. Classifier-based filters such as the Perspective API do better on meaning and inherit a different bias: Sap et al. (2019) showed that tweets in African American English were about twice as likely to be labelled offensive by widely used hate-speech classifiers, and by the annotators who trained them, as the same content in other dialects. A pipeline that drops documents above a toxicity score therefore drops a dialect along with the toxicity, and the model is later worse at understanding speakers of it. Decontamination against evaluation sets

If the benchmark is on the web, it is in the crawl. Decontamination removes training documents that overlap with the test sets before training, since afterwards nothing can be done but to flag the affected scores. The standard check is n-gram overlap: build the set of all word (or token) n-grams in every evaluation example, then drop or clip any training document that contains one. GPT-3 used 13-grams and removed the overlapping spans with a margin on either side; Llama 2 counted a document contaminated when more than 80% of its tokens sat inside 10-grams shared with a benchmark; GPT-4 used 50-character substrings. The choice of n is a trade: short n-grams match by chance (every document contains “of the”), long ones miss paraphrases and reformatted copies. Assembling the set

Mixing domains, counting epochs, and writing it all down

After filtering, the sources are not simply concatenated. Each is given a sampling weight, the share of training tokens drawn from it, and the weights are chosen to be very different from the sources’ sizes. The LLaMA recipe (Touvron et al., 2023) is typical: filtered Common Crawl and C4 together supplied 82% of the 1.4 trillion training tokens, but Wikipedia, which is tiny, was given 4.5% and therefore read 2.45 times over, and books likewise 2.23 times, while GitHub was read only 0.64 times. The Pile (Gao et al., 2020) upweighted Wikipedia and academic text in the same spirit. The logic is the previous section’s: a domain whose tokens are worth more can be repeated a few times at little cost, and a domain that is huge contributes diminishing returns beyond the share that represents its register. Choosing the weights was long a matter of taste; DoReMi (Xie et al., 2023) learns them by training a small proxy model and upweighting the domains where it lags a reference, and finds weights that transfer to large models. Set the weights and the budget below; the widget reports, for each domain, the tokens the model will see, the epochs that implies given the domain’s unique size, and the fresh tokens that is worth under the repetition formula.

Documentation: datasheets and data cards

Every decision in this chapter is a threshold someone chose, and most of them are invisible in the finished corpus. Gebru et al. (2021) proposed that every dataset ship with a datasheet, modelled on the specification sheets of electronic components: a fixed set of questions about why the data was made, what is in it, how it was collected and cleaned, what it should and should not be used for, how it is distributed and who maintains it. Bender and Friedman (2018) proposed data statements for language data in particular, with fields for the speakers, their varieties of the language and the situation of the writing; Pushkarna et al. C4 was released in 2019 as a cleaned crawl with a one-paragraph description; it took Dodge et al. A datasheet written at release would have made each of those a sentence rather than a paper.

        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return nxt

P = 2**31 - 1                                   # a Mersenne prime; all hash values live in [0, P)
def fnv1a(s):                                    # 32-bit FNV-1a over the UTF-8 bytes of a shingle
    h = 0x811C9DC5
    for b in s.encode('utf-8'):
        h = ((h ^ b) * 0x01000193) & 0xFFFFFFFF
    return h

def words(text):                                 # lower-case, keep letters and digits only
    return re.sub(r'[^\w]+', ' ', text.lower(), flags=re.UNICODE).replace('_', ' ').split()

def shingles(text, k=3):                         # the set of word k-grams
    w = words(text)
    return {' '.join(w[i:i + k]) for i in range(len(w) - k + 1)}

def jaccard(A, B):
    return len(A & B) / len(A | B) if A | B else 1.0

def hash_params(n, seed):                        # n random affine hash functions h_i(x) = (a_i x + b_i) mod P
    rng, A, B = mulberry32(seed), [], []
    for _ in range(n):                           # drawn in the same order as the page: a_0, b_0, a_1, b_1, ...
        A.append(1 + int(rng() * (P - 1))); B.append(int(rng() * P))
    return np.array(A, dtype=np.int64), np.array(B, dtype=np.int64)

def minhash(S, A, B):                            # signature: for each hash function, the smallest value over the set
    x = np.array([fnv1a(s) % P for s in S], dtype=np.int64)          # one 31-bit integer per shingle
    H = (A[:, None] * x[None, :] + B[:, None]) % P                   # n_hashes x n_shingles, exact in int64
    return H.min(axis=1)

def lsh_pairs(sigs, bands, rows):                # candidate pairs: any band in which two signatures agree exactly
    pairs = set()
    for b in range(bands):
        buckets = {}
        for i, s in enumerate(sigs):
            key = tuple(s[b * rows:(b + 1) * rows])
            buckets.setdefault(key, []).append(i)
        for ids in buckets.values():
            for u in range(len(ids)):
                for v in range(u + 1, len(ids)):
                    pairs.add((ids[u], ids[v]))
    return sorted(pairs)

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Pretraining text is found, not written: web crawls give coverage, books, encyclopaedias, code, forums and licensed archives give depth, and every filter that raises quality lowers coverage, because the text that scores badly is concentrated in the corners only the web reaches.
  • The pipeline is a funnel whose yields multiply: language identification, quality rules, deduplication, PII and toxicity filters and decontamination together keep about a tenth of a crawl, so a trillion good tokens need a crawl of ten.
  • Character n-gram models identify languages well on paragraphs and badly on fragments; Gopher-style rules measure the shape of text, not its worth; model-based quality scores measure resemblance to a reference, and inherit its register, dialect and class.
  • Deduplication rests on three facts: Jaccard similarity of shingle sets measures near-duplication, the minimum of a random hash agrees across two sets with probability equal to their Jaccard, and banding signatures turns an all-pairs search into bucket look-ups with an S-shaped detection curve 1 − (1 − sr)b.
  • Repeated tokens are worth less each pass, with a ceiling near sixteen passes; duplicates are accidental repeats, mixing weights are deliberate ones, and a datasheet is how the next person finds out which thresholds decided what the model would never see.

Check your understanding

A pipeline keeps 50% at language identification, 55% at quality filtering and 60% at deduplication. Why is the overall yield not “about 55%”, and roughly what is it?

The stages are applied in sequence, so their yields multiply rather than average: 0.5 × 0.55 × 0.6 = 0.165, so about one token in six survives before the small later stages. Three moderate filters compound into a severe one, which is why pipelines report overall yields near a tenth even though no single stage looks dramatic.

Why does the character-trigram identifier decide a paragraph within a few dozen characters but never become confident about a navigation menu?

The per-character margin between the best and second-best language grows roughly linearly with the number of characters of ordinary text, because each trigram adds a small vote. A menu has few characters, many of them capitalised names and symbols that the model has barely seen in any language, so the votes are weak and conflicting; the margin stays small and the threshold rule drops the document, or assigns it to whichever language its few recognisable fragments resemble.

Two cookie banners pass every Gopher rule and the forum post about a dripping tap fails the style classifier. What does that pair of outcomes tell you about the two kinds of filter?

Rules measure form (length, symbols, repetition, stop words), so fluent boilerplate passes and fluent informal text passes. Classifiers measure resemblance to a reference register, so boilerplate written in a formal register scores well and useful text written informally scores badly. Neither measures how much a document would teach a model; they measure correlates of it that fail in opposite directions, which is why pipelines use both and still audit what was removed.

Explain in one sentence why the probability that two sets share their minimum hash value equals their Jaccard similarity.

The shingle achieving the overall minimum of a random hash over A ∪ B is a uniformly random element of the union, and the two minima coincide exactly when that element lies in A ∩ B, which happens with probability |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B|.

You want to catch pairs with Jaccard above 0.8 and ignore pairs below 0.5. You have 100 hashes. Would you pick b = 50, r = 2 or b = 10, r = 10?

b = 50, r = 2 puts the threshold at (1/50)1/2 = 0.14, so almost every pair above 0.5 becomes a candidate and the verification step drowns. b = 10, r = 10 puts it at (1/10)1/10 = 0.79, which is about right: a pair at 0.8 is flagged with probability 1 − (1 − 0.810)10 ≈ 0.68, which is low, so a better split is around b = 20, r = 5 (threshold 0.55, catching 0.8 pairs with probability 0.9997) with the exact Jaccard check rejecting the pairs between 0.5 and 0.8. The answer depends on how cheap verification is.

A document appears 120 times in the corpus. In the language of the repeated-data section, what has happened to it, and what does deduplication change?

The model trains on that document for 120 epochs while most documents get one. Under the repetition law the passes beyond the first few are worth almost nothing, so the compute is wasted, and the document is memorised nearly verbatim, which is the failure mode Lee et al. and Carlini et al. measured. Deduplication reduces it to one copy; if the corpus is then deliberately repeated two or three times, every document is seen two or three times, which is both more useful and much less memorised.

Why can decontamination not be done after training, and why is a very small n in the n-gram check a bad idea?

Once a model has trained on a test item, the only remedy is to discard the score or the model; nothing removes the memory. A small n flags documents that share common phrases with the benchmark by chance (the corpus plot shows most pairs sharing a 3-gram), so almost everything would be removed; a large n misses paraphrased or reformatted copies. Values from 8 to 13 words are the usual compromise, often combined with a token-fraction rule.
