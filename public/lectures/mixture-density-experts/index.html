Mixture Density Networks & Mixtures of Experts

When one input has several right answers

A regression network trained on squared error gives one number for each input: the average of everything that could happen. That is fine when there is one right answer and badly wrong when there are several. The dots on the right follow a simple rule, x = t + 0.3 sin(2πt) plus noise, but we ask the reverse question: which t produced this x? For many x there are three answers. Least squares (dashed) threads between them, through places where there is no data at all. A mixture density network predicts a whole distribution instead: a few Gaussians whose weights, centres and widths are all outputs of the network. Move across the plot to slice it at any x.

The idea

When the answer is multimodal

Many problems run in two directions. The forward direction is usually a function: set a robot arm’s joint angles and the hand ends up in exactly one place. The inverse direction often isn’t: to put the hand at a given spot, the elbow can bend up or down, so there are two good answers. The toy problem here is the same in one dimension: pick t between 0 and 1, compute x = t + 0.3 sin(2πt) plus a little noise. Going from t to x is easy. Going from x back to t, the middle range of x has three answers.

A network trained by least squares cannot say “three answers”. Whatever its shape, the squared error is smallest when it predicts the conditional mean, the average of the possible t at that x. Flip between the two directions and slide x: least squares is excellent forwards and misleading backwards.

\[ \begin{aligned} y^\star(x) &= \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{y}\ \mathbb{E}\big[(t-y)^2 \mid x\big] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[t \mid x] = \int t\, p(t\mid x)\, dt \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: among all possible predictions y at a given x, the one with the smallest expected squared error is the mean of t at that x. A flexible enough network trained on squared error ends up close to this curve. If p(t | x) has several separate bumps, the mean can fall between them, in a place where no data lives.
Say it“The best squared-error prediction is the expected value of t given x: the integral of t times p of t given x.”

Conditional mixtures · 1

Mixtures of linear regressions

The simplest way to allow several answers: suppose the data come from K straight-line relationships, but nobody tells us which point follows which line. Give each point a hidden label z saying which line made it. If we knew the labels, we would fit each line by least squares. We don’t, so we use the EM algorithm from the EM chapter. The E-step asks each line how well it explains each point and turns the answers into responsibilities γnk. The M-step refits each line by least squares in which every point counts with weight γnk. Following Bishop, all lines share one noise level β−1. Press EM step a few times, then try a new random start and the other data sets.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(t \mid \boldsymbol{\phi}) &= \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, \mathcal{N}\!\big(t \,\big|\, \mathbf{w}_k^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\phi},\ \beta^{-1}\big) \\ \boldsymbol{\phi} &= (1,\ x) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: to produce t, first pick line k with probability πk, then add Gaussian noise of variance β−1 around that line’s value wkTφ = wk0 + wk1x. At any x the prediction is a set of K bumps, one on each line.
Say it“p of t given phi is the sum over k of pi-k times a normal in t with mean w-k transpose phi and variance beta inverse.”
\[ \gamma_{nk} = \frac{\pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(t_n \mid \mathbf{w}_k^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_n, \beta^{-1})}{\sum_j \pi_j\, \mathcal{N}(t_n \mid \mathbf{w}_j^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_n, \beta^{-1})} \]
Read it as: the E-step. Point n’s responsibility to line k is line k’s share of the total probability of tn. Each point’s responsibilities add up to 1; on the canvas they set the point’s colour.
Say it“Gamma n-k is pi-k times line k’s likelihood of t-n, divided by the sum of that over all lines.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \pi_k &= \tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_n \gamma_{nk} \\ \mathbf{w}_k &= (\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{R}_k\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{R}_k\mathbf{t} \\ \beta^{-1} &= \tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_n\sum_k \gamma_{nk}\,(t_n - \mathbf{w}_k^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_n)^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the M-step. A line’s weight is its average responsibility. Its coefficients come from weighted least squares, where Rk is the diagonal matrix of the γnk. The shared noise variance is the responsibility-weighted mean squared miss.
Say it“Pi-k is the average gamma; w-k is weighted least squares with weights gamma; one over beta is the weighted average squared residual.”

Conditional mixtures · 2

Mixtures of logistic models

The same trick works for yes/no targets. Imagine a population with two hidden groups: in one, “yes” becomes likely as x grows; in the other, as x falls. A single logistic regression must pick one direction and can only draw an S-shaped curve. A mixture of K logistic models adds K S-curves, weighted by πk, and can bend into a U. EM works as before, with one change: the M-step for each component is now a weighted logistic regression, which has no closed form, so we take Newton steps (iteratively reweighted least squares) with weights γnk. One snag: when a component can split its points perfectly, the likelihood keeps creeping up as its S-curve sharpens towards a vertical step, so each M-step here carries a tiny ridge penalty (0.0005 ‖w‖²) that keeps the weights finite. Run EM with K = 2, then press New random start a few times.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(t \mid \boldsymbol{\phi}) &= \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, y_k^{\,t}\,(1-y_k)^{1-t} \\ y_k &= \sigma(\mathbf{w}_k^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\phi}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: t is 1 or 0. Component k says “yes” with probability yk, a logistic curve in x. The mixture’s chance of yes is Σk πkyk, a weighted sum of S-curves.
Say it“p of t is the sum over k of pi-k times y-k to the t times one minus y-k to the one minus t, where y-k is the sigmoid of w-k transpose phi.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \gamma_{nk} &= \frac{\pi_k\, y_{nk}^{\,t_n}(1-y_{nk})^{1-t_n}}{\sum_j \pi_j\, y_{nj}^{\,t_n}(1-y_{nj})^{1-t_n}} \\ \nabla_{\mathbf{w}_k} Q &= \sum_n \gamma_{nk}\,(t_n - y_{nk})\,\boldsymbol{\phi}_n \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the E-step is unchanged in spirit: each component’s share of the probability of what actually happened. In the M-step the gradient is ordinary logistic regression’s, with each point weighted by its responsibility; Newton’s method follows it to the maximum.
Say it“The gradient for component k is the sum over points of gamma n-k times t-n minus y n-k, times phi-n.”

The network

Mixture density networks

Straight lines with fixed weights are too rigid for the inverse problem: its branches curve, and the number of answers changes with x. Bishop’s mixture density network (1994) keeps the mixture but lets an ordinary neural network produce every parameter of it. With K components and a one-dimensional target the network has 3K outputs: K become mixing weights through a softmax (positive, summing to 1), K are used directly as means, and K become widths through an exponential (always positive). Training minimises the negative log-likelihood of the data, and backpropagation needs only the three derivatives below. Press Train and watch the components find the branches; change K and the hidden units and train again. Click any data point to see its gradients.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(t \mid x) &= \sum_{k} \pi_k(x) \\ &\quad\times \mathcal{N}\big(t \mid \mu_k(x), \sigma_k^2(x)\big) \\ \pi_k &= \frac{e^{a^{\pi}_k}}{\sum_l e^{a^{\pi}_l}} \\ \mu_k &= a^{\mu}_k, \quad \sigma_k = e^{a^{\sigma}_k} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the a’s are the network’s raw outputs for input x. Softmax turns K of them into weights, K are the centres, and the exponential turns K into widths. All three now change with x, which a mixture of straight lines could not do.
Say it“p of t given x is the sum of pi-k of x times a normal with mean mu-k of x and variance sigma-k of x squared; pi is a softmax, mu is the raw output, sigma is e to the raw output.”
\[ \begin{aligned} E(\mathbf{w}) &= -\sum_{n} \ln p(t_n \mid x_n) \\ p(t_n \mid x_n) &= \sum_{k} \pi_{nk}\, \mathcal{N}_{nk} \\ \mathcal{N}_{nk} &= \mathcal{N}\big(t_n \mid \mu_{nk}, \sigma_{nk}^2\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: πnk = πk(xn), and likewise μnk, σnk: the network’s outputs for input xn. The error is minus the log-likelihood: for every training pair, how much probability the predicted mixture puts on the t that really occurred. With K = 1 and a fixed width it reduces to squared error.
Say it“E is minus the sum over points of the log of the mixture density at t-n.”
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial E_n}{\partial a^{\pi}_k} &= \pi_k - \gamma_{nk} \\ \frac{\partial E_n}{\partial a^{\mu}_k} &= \gamma_{nk}\,\frac{\mu_k - t_n}{\sigma_k^2} \\ \frac{\partial E_n}{\partial a^{\sigma}_k} &= \gamma_{nk}\Big(1 - \frac{(t_n-\mu_k)^2}{\sigma_k^2}\Big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: γnk is again a responsibility: component k’s share of the density at tn. A component that explains the point better than its weight suggests gets its weight raised; its centre is pulled toward tn in proportion to γ; its width grows if the miss is more than one σ, and shrinks if less.
Say it“Weights: pi-k minus gamma. Means: gamma times mu minus t over sigma squared. Widths: gamma times one minus the squared miss over sigma squared.”

Using the output

Reading the density: mean, spread and mode

An MDN hands you a whole distribution at every x, so you must decide what to report. Its mean is the conditional mean, the quantity a least-squares network tries to predict. Its variance has two parts: the width of each component, and how far the components sit from each other. When the density has several separate bumps, the most useful single answer is usually the most probable value. Finding the exact peak needs a small search; Bishop suggests a cheap stand-in, the centre of the component with the largest weight. Slide x through the three-answer zone and compare the four markers. (This uses the network from the hero: 3 components, 8 hidden units, trained when the page loaded.)

\[ \mathbb{E}[t \mid x] = \sum_{k} \pi_k(x)\, \mu_k(x) \]
Read it as: the weighted average of the centres. It equals the conditional mean from the first section, so the MDN contains the least-squares answer as one of its summaries.
Say it“The expected t is the sum of pi-k times mu-k.”
\[ \begin{aligned} s^2(x) &= \sum_{k} \pi_k \Big\{ \sigma_k^2 + \big(\mu_k - \bar{\mu}\big)^{2} \Big\} \\ \bar{\mu} &= \sum_l \pi_l\, \mu_l \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: every π, μ and σ here is evaluated at x, and μ̄ is the mean from the left. Total variance = the average width of the components (noise within a branch) + the spread of their centres around the mean (uncertainty about which branch). In the three-answer zone the second part dominates.
Say it“s squared is the sum of pi-k times sigma-k squared plus the squared distance of mu-k from the mean.”

Letting x choose

Mixtures of experts and gating

In a mixture of regressions the weights πk are the same everywhere, so every line claims a share of every x. Jacobs, Jordan, Nowlan and Hinton (1991) made the weights depend on the input: a gating network looks at x and decides how much to trust each expert. Each expert can then specialise in its own region. Here the experts are straight lines and the gate is a softmax of straight lines, so the whole model is still fitted by EM. The E-step uses the gate’s weights at each point; the M-step refits the experts by weighted least squares and the gate by a weighted softmax regression whose targets are the responsibilities. An MDN is a close cousin: there one network produces every parameter, while here each expert is its own model and the gate decides who speaks. Run EM with and without the gate and compare.

\[ \begin{aligned} p(t \mid x) &= \sum_{k} \pi_k(x)\, \mathcal{N}\big(t \mid \mathbf{w}_k^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\phi}, \sigma_k^2\big) \\ \pi_k(x) &= \frac{\exp(\mathbf{v}_k^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\phi})}{\sum_j \exp(\mathbf{v}_j^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\phi})} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the same mixture of lines as before, except that the weights are now a softmax of the input. Where πk(x) ≈ 1, expert k alone answers.
Say it“p of t given x is the sum of pi-k of x times expert k’s normal; pi-k of x is a softmax of v-k transpose phi.”
\[ \begin{aligned} Q_{\text{gate}} &= \sum_n \sum_k \gamma_{nk} \ln \pi_k(x_n) \\ \nabla_{\mathbf{v}_k} Q_{\text{gate}} &= \sum_n \big(\gamma_{nk} - \pi_k(x_n)\big)\,\boldsymbol{\phi}_n \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the gate’s M-step is a softmax regression whose “labels” are soft: the responsibilities from the E-step. The gate learns to send each x to the experts that explained the points near it.
Say it“The gate maximises the sum of gamma times log pi; its gradient is gamma minus pi, times phi.”

Trees of experts

Hierarchical mixtures of experts

Jordan and Jacobs (1994) stacked gates into a tree. The top gate splits the input space softly between two branches; inside each branch a second gate splits again; experts sit at the four leaves. Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman present it as a smooth cousin of a regression tree: CART asks a yes/no question at each node and puts a constant in each leaf, while the HME’s questions are probabilities that change gradually with x, and its leaves hold whole linear models. EM fits it, with a hidden variable for each branch choice. The HME’s splits can also use combinations of inputs rather than one input at a time. One practical difference: the tree’s shape (here, two levels of two branches) is chosen before fitting, whereas CART grows and prunes its tree from the data. Run EM, then hover the plot (or use the slider) to follow one input down the tree. Compare with a depth-2 CART tree on the “Steps” data too.

\[ \begin{aligned} \Pr(t \mid x) &= \sum_{j} g_j(x) \\ &\quad\times \sum_{\ell} g_{\ell\mid j}(x)\,P_{j\ell}(t) \\ P_{j\ell}(t) &= \mathcal{N}\big(t \mid \mathbf{w}_{j\ell}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\phi},\ \sigma_{j\ell}^2\big) \\ g_1(x) &= \sigma(\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\phi}) \\ g_{1\mid j}(x) &= \sigma(\mathbf{b}_j^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\phi}) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: to reach leaf (j, ℓ), go down branch j with probability gj(x), then down branch ℓ with probability gℓ|j(x); the second branch at each node gets the rest, g2 = 1 − g1. Multiply along the path and you get the leaf’s weight; the four weights add up to 1. With two branches per node each gate is a logistic regression (a softmax over two). It is a mixture of experts whose gate happens to be a tree.
Say it“The probability of t is the sum over branches j of g-j times the sum over leaves l of g l-given-j times expert j-l’s normal.”
\[ \begin{aligned} h_{j\ell} &= \frac{g_j\, g_{\ell\mid j}\, P_{j\ell}(t)}{\sum_{j'\ell'} g_{j'}\, g_{\ell'\mid j'}\, P_{j'\ell'}(t)} \\ h_j &= \sum_\ell h_{j\ell}, \qquad h_{\ell\mid j} = \frac{h_{j\ell}}{h_j} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the E-step gives each point a posterior over paths. The M-step then fits three kinds of weighted regression: each expert by least squares with weights hjℓ; the top gate by logistic regression with soft targets hj; each lower gate by logistic regression with targets hℓ|j, weighted by hj (a point that went the other way at the top has little to say about this branch).
Say it“h j-l is the path’s gate product times its expert’s likelihood, normalised; h-j sums over leaves; h l-given-j is the ratio.”

At scale

From mixtures of experts to sparse expert layers in LLMs

The gate idea returned in 2017 with a new purpose: not to model several answers, but to save compute. Shazeer and colleagues placed a layer of thousands of small expert networks between the layers of a language model, with a gate that keeps only the top k experts for each token. The model has the parameters of all the experts but pays for only k of them per token. GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2021) put such layers inside transformers with k = 2; the Switch Transformer (Fedus et al., 2022) used k = 1; Mixtral 8×7B (2024) has 8 experts per layer and uses 2 per token, so of about 47 billion parameters only about 13 billion are used for any one token.

Two things differ from the classical models above. The gate’s weights now mix the experts’ outputs (vectors passed on to the next layer), not probability densities, and everything is trained by backpropagation rather than EM. And a gate left alone tends to overload a few experts, so training adds a load-balancing loss and gives each expert a fixed capacity; tokens beyond it are dropped from that expert and skip ahead through the residual connection. Below, 256 toy “tokens” (2-D points, standing in for real hidden states) meet a randomly initialised router. Press Balance to take gradient steps on the balancing loss alone, and watch the loads even out.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{y} &= \sum_{i \in \mathcal{T}_k(\mathbf{x})} G_i(\mathbf{x})\, E_i(\mathbf{x}) \\ G(\mathbf{x}) &= \operatorname{softmax}\big(\operatorname{TopK}(\mathbf{W}_r\mathbf{x},\ k)\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the router scores every expert with one matrix product Wrx, keeps the k highest scores (the set Tk), and softmaxes over just those. Only the chosen experts Ei are run; their outputs are blended with the weights Gi. The others cost nothing for this token.
Say it“y is the sum over the top-k experts of the gate weight G-i times expert i’s output; G is a softmax of the k largest router scores.”
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} = \alpha\, N \sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i\, P_i \]
Read it as: N experts. fi is the fraction of tokens whose first choice is expert i; Pi is the router’s average probability for expert i. Their dot product, times N, is 1 when both are uniform and grows when the same experts are both popular and favoured. f is a count and has no gradient, so the loss pushes through P (Switch Transformer).
Say it“The auxiliary loss is alpha times N times the sum over experts of f-i times P-i.”
\[ C = \Big\lceil c \cdot \frac{k\, T}{N} \Big\rceil \]
Read it as: T tokens in the batch make kT expert assignments; shared equally, each expert would get kT/N. The capacity factor c adds headroom (with k = 1 this is the Switch Transformer’s rule). Assignments beyond C are dropped. A bigger c drops less but reserves (and wastes) more memory and compute.
Say it“Capacity is c times k T over N, rounded up.”

Real-world project

Project: how late will my flight land?

The US Bureau of Transportation Statistics publishes every domestic flight flown by the larger US airlines, with its scheduled times and its delays (US government data, public domain). We take July 2024: 634,613 scheduled flights, of which 20,675 were cancelled or diverted. Of the 613,938 that landed, 608,861 (99.2%) arrived between 60 minutes early and 300 minutes late; we keep those, sample 30,000 at random, and use 20,000 to train and 10,000 to test. The input is the scheduled departure time (local clock time at the origin airport); the target is the arrival delay in minutes (negative means early).

Delays are a textbook case for a mixture. Most flights land a little early, in a tight peak; a minority hit trouble and spread out over hours. The share of troubled flights changes through the day, because delays pile up as aircraft and crews carry them from flight to flight. A single Gaussian, however its mean moves, cannot describe a narrow peak and a long tail at the same time. Press Train: the page fits seven models, from one Gaussian to a 3-component MDN, and scores each by its average negative log-likelihood on the test flights, in nats per flight (lower is better; a drop of 0.1 means the model gives the real delays about 10% more probability, on average per flight).

What a traveller wants: the chance of a late arrival

Airlines count a flight as late when it arrives 15 or more minutes behind schedule. A density model answers that directly: P(delay ≥ 15) is the area of p(t | x) beyond 15 minutes, which for a mixture is a weighted sum of Gaussian tail areas. Dots are the test flights, grouped by scheduled hour (dot area ∝ number of flights). Hover the plot or move the slider above.

\[ \begin{aligned} P(t \ge 15 \mid x) &= \sum_k \pi_k\,\big[1 - \Phi(z_k)\big] \\ z_k &= \frac{15 - \mu_k}{\sigma_k} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: πk, μk and σk are the model’s outputs at x, and Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution. Each component contributes its weight times the share of its bell curve that lies beyond 15 minutes. (Delays are whole minutes; we treat them as continuous here.)
Say it“The chance of 15 minutes or more is the sum of pi-k times one minus Phi of 15 minus mu-k over sigma-k.”

The same project in Python

import json, warnings
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import logsumexp
from scipy.stats import norm
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")

F = json.load(open("flights.json"))                  # 30,000 BTS flights, July 2024
x = (np.array(F["dep"]) / 60 - 12) / 6               # scheduled departure, scaled: -2 = midnight, 0 = noon
t = np.array(F["delay"], float)                      # arrival delay in minutes, -60 .. 300
tr, te = slice(0, 20000), slice(20000, None)
nll = lambda lp: -lp.mean()                          # nats per flight
res = {}

# one Gaussian for everyone; linear regression on the hour with Gaussian noise
res["one Gaussian"] = [nll(norm.logpdf(t[s], t[tr].mean(), t[tr].std())) for s in (tr, te)]
lr = LinearRegression().fit(x[tr, None], t[tr]); sd_lin = np.sqrt(np.mean((t[tr] - lr.predict(x[tr, None])) ** 2))
res["linear regression"] = [nll(norm.logpdf(t[s], lr.predict(x[s, None]), sd_lin)) for s in (tr, te)]

# a mixture of 3 Gaussians that ignores the hour (same start as the page)
m0, v0, p0 = np.array([-12., 10, 80]), np.array([100., 625, 6400]), np.array([.6, .25, .15])
gm = GaussianMixture(3, means_init=m0[:, None], weights_init=p0, precisions_init=1 / v0[:, None, None],
                     max_iter=100, tol=0, reg_covar=1e-6).fit(t[tr, None])
res["mixture, no input"] = [-gm.score(t[s, None]) for s in (tr, te)]

def em(gated, iters=100):
    """EM for 3 linear-Gaussian experts on (1, x). gated=False: fixed weights (PRML 14.5.1);
    gated=True: softmax gate on (1, x), fitted by Newton steps (a mixture of experts, 14.5.3)."""
    X, T = np.c_[np.ones(20000), x[tr]], t[tr]
    W, var, V, pi = np.c_[m0, np.zeros(3)], v0.copy(), np.zeros((3, 2)), p0.copy()
    logg = lambda X: (X @ V.T) - logsumexp(X @ V.T, 1, keepdims=True) if gated else np.log(pi)
    for _ in range(iters):
        lp = logg(X) + norm.logpdf(T[:, None], X @ W.T, np.sqrt(var))          # E-step
        R = np.exp(lp - logsumexp(lp, 1, keepdims=True))
        for k in range(3):                                                    # M-step: experts
            W[k] = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ (R[:, k, None] * X), X.T @ (R[:, k] * T))
            var[k] = R[:, k] @ (T - X @ W[k]) ** 2 / R[:, k].sum() + 1e-6
        pi = R.mean(0)
        for _ in range(6 if gated else 0):     # M-step: gate, Newton on sum r ln g - 1e-3/2 |V|^2
            G = np.exp(logg(X)); g = ((R - G)[:, :2].T @ X).ravel() - 1e-3 * V[:2].ravel()
            H = np.zeros((4, 4))
            for a in range(2):
                for b in range(2):
                    H[2*a:2*a+2, 2*b:2*b+2] = (X * (G[:, a] * ((a == b) - G[:, b]))[:, None]).T @ X
            V[:2] += np.linalg.solve(H + 1e-3 * np.eye(4), g).reshape(2, 2)
    def score(s):
        Xs = np.c_[np.ones(len(x[s])), x[s]]
        return nll(logsumexp(logg(Xs) + norm.logpdf(t[s, None], Xs @ W.T, np.sqrt(var)), 1))
    return score
for name, gated in [("mixture of 3 regressions", False), ("mixture of 3 experts", True)]:
    f = em(gated); res[name] = [f(tr), f(te)]

def mdn_out(P, K, X, H=8):
    """1 input -> 8 tanh units -> K mixing logits, K means, K log-sigmas (targets in hours)."""
    W1, b1, W2, b2 = P[:H], P[H:2*H], P[2*H:2*H+3*K*H].reshape(3*K, H), P[2*H+3*K*H:]
    h = np.tanh(np.outer(X, W1) + b1); a = h @ W2.T + b2
    return h, a, a[:, :K] - logsumexp(a[:, :K], 1, keepdims=True), a[:, K:2*K], a[:, 2*K:]

def mdn(P, K, H=8, steps=800, lr=0.01):
    """Full-batch Adam on the negative log-likelihood, from the page's own starting weights."""
    P = np.array(P); m = np.zeros_like(P); v = np.zeros_like(P)
    def loss(P, X, T, grad=False):
        h, a, lpi, mu, ls = mdn_out(P, K, X)
        lp = lpi - ls - 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi) - 0.5 * ((T[:, None] - mu) / np.exp(ls)) ** 2
        L = logsumexp(lp, 1)
        if not grad: return -L.mean()
        gam, z = np.exp(lp - L[:, None]), (T[:, None] - mu) / np.exp(ls)
        dA = np.c_[np.exp(lpi) - gam, -gam * z / np.exp(ls), gam * (1 - z ** 2)] / len(T)
        dh = (dA @ P[2*H:2*H+3*K*H].reshape(3*K, H)) * (1 - h ** 2)
        return -L.mean(), np.r_[dh.T @ X, dh.sum(0), (dA.T @ h).ravel(), dA.sum(0)]
    for i in range(1, steps + 1):
        _, g = loss(P, x[tr], t[tr] / 60, True); m = .9 * m + .1 * g; v = .999 * v + .001 * g * g
        P = P - lr * (m / (1 - .9 ** i)) / (np.sqrt(v / (1 - .999 ** i)) + 1e-8)
    return [loss(P, x[s], t[s] / 60) + np.log(60) for s in (tr, te)], P   # + ln 60: nats per minute

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • Squared error makes a network predict the conditional mean E[t | x]. When p(t | x) has several bumps, that mean can sit where no data lives.
  • A conditional mixture predicts a whole density. Mixtures of linear or logistic regressions are fitted by EM: responsibilities in the E-step, weighted regressions in the M-step.
  • A mixture density network lets a neural network output the weights (softmax), centres and widths (exp) of a mixture, trained by minimising the negative log-likelihood. Report the mode, not just the mean, when the density is multimodal.
  • A mixture of experts makes the weights depend on x through a gating network, so each expert specialises; a hierarchical MoE stacks gates into a soft tree, a smooth cousin of CART.
  • Sparse MoE layers in large language models route each token to the top k of N experts to save compute, and need a balancing loss and a capacity limit to keep the load even.

Check your understanding

A network trained with squared error predicts 0.5 for an input whose true answers are 0.2 and 0.8, equally often. Is the network broken?

No. It is doing exactly what squared error asks: 0.5 is the conditional mean, the single number with the smallest expected squared error. The problem is the loss, not the training. A density model such as an MDN would put two bumps at 0.2 and 0.8.

Why does an MDN pass its mixing outputs through a softmax and its width outputs through an exponential?

The weights must be positive and add up to 1, which a softmax guarantees; the widths must be positive, which the exponential guarantees. The network’s raw outputs can then be any real numbers, and gradient descent needs no constraints.

In a mixture of linear regressions the M-step for each line is a weighted least-squares fit. Where do the weights come from?

From the E-step: they are the responsibilities γnk, each line’s share of the probability of point n under the current parameters.

What can a mixture of experts do that a mixture of regressions with fixed πk cannot?

Change the weights with x. With fixed weights every line claims the same share everywhere, so the predicted density keeps bumps on every line at every x. A gate lets one expert own each region and switch off elsewhere.

A sparse MoE layer has 64 experts and routes each token to 2. Roughly what fraction of the layer’s expert parameters does one token use, and why is a balancing loss needed?

2/64, about 3%. Without the balancing loss the router tends to send most tokens to a few experts; those overflow their capacity and drop tokens, while the others sit idle and learn little.
