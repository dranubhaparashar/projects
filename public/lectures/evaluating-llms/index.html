The idea

A benchmark score is an estimate

Imagine a model that would answer some fixed share p of all possible questions of a certain kind correctly. A benchmark doesn’t measure p; it asks n questions and counts the hits. That count is like the number of heads in n coin flips, so it wobbles. The wobble shrinks like 1/√n: four times as many questions halve it. Two extra facts matter in practice. Models are scored on the same questions, which helps when comparing them (section 3). And many famous benchmarks are small: HumanEval has 164 programming problems, GPQA Diamond 198 science questions.

Below, a model with true accuracy p sits the chosen benchmark 2,000 times, each time on a fresh draw of n questions. The dots underneath are real published scores, with the error bars that the publication did not print. Pick a benchmark and move p.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat p &= \frac{k}{n} \\ \text{SE}(\hat p) &= \sqrt{\frac{p\,(1-p)}{n}} \\ 95\%\ \text{CI} &\approx \hat p \pm 1.96\,\text{SE} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: k of the n questions were answered correctly, so the score is k/n. Its standard error is the typical distance between that score and the true accuracy p; in practice p is unknown and the score itself is plugged in. About 95% of the time, the true accuracy lies within 1.96 standard errors of the score.
Say it“p-hat is k over n; its standard error is the square root of p times one minus p, over n; the interval is p-hat plus or minus 1.96 standard errors.”

Bootstrap confidence intervals

The formula above needs right-or-wrong answers. MT-bench grades are 1 to 10, the questions differ wildly in difficulty, and each question has two turns that are graded separately. The bootstrap (Efron, 1979) needs no formula at all: treat the 80 questions you have as a stand-in for all questions, draw 80 of them with replacement (some twice, some not at all), recompute the score, and repeat a thousand times. The spread of those recomputed scores is the uncertainty. One rule matters: resample the units that were drawn independently. The two turns of a question share a topic and a difficulty, so they are not independent, and resampling turns as if they were makes the interval look tighter than it is. Choose a model, then switch between resampling questions and resampling turns.

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar x^{*b} &= \frac{\sum_{q \in S_b} g_q}{\sum_{q \in S_b} m_q} \\ S_b &= \text{80 draws with replacement} \\ \text{CI}_{95} &= \big[\, Q_{2.5}(\bar x^{*}),\; Q_{97.5}(\bar x^{*}) \,\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: gq is the total of question q’s grades and mq how many grades it has (2, or 1 where the judge failed). Each bootstrap replicate b redraws the question set Sb and recomputes the average grade. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the B replicates are the ends of the percentile interval.
Say it“x-bar-star-b is the grade total over the resampled questions divided by their number of grades; the interval runs from the 2.5th to the 97.5th percentile of the replicates.”

Comparing two models

Is model A really better than model B?

Two models’ intervals overlapping does not mean they are indistinguishable. Both models answered the same 80 questions, and a hard question is hard for both: their grades are correlated. Look at the per-question difference instead. The shared difficulty cancels, and the difference is often far less noisy than either score. This is the paired comparison, and it is why benchmarks should always be compared question by question (Miller, 2024). Pick two models; each dot is one question, at model A’s grade across and model B’s grade up.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{SE}_{\text{unpaired}} &= \sqrt{\tfrac{s_A^2 + s_B^2}{n}} \\ \text{SE}_{\text{paired}} &= \frac{s_d}{\sqrt n} \\ s_d^2 &= s_A^2 + s_B^2 - 2\,r\,s_A s_B \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: sA and sB are the standard deviations of the two models’ per-question grades, r their correlation across questions, and d = A − B the per-question difference. The unpaired error pretends the two scores came from different questions. Pairing subtracts 2 r sA sB: the more the models agree on which questions are hard, the smaller the error of the difference.
Say it“The paired standard error is s-d over root n, where s-d squared is s-A squared plus s-B squared minus two r s-A s-B.”

Planning an evaluation

How many questions does a benchmark need?

Turn the question round: if model A really is δ points more accurate than model B, how many questions do you need to notice? Only the questions where the two models disagree carry information; on the rest both are right or both are wrong, and the difference is 0. So the answer depends on the gap δ, on the share q of questions where exactly one of the two is right, and on how often you insist on noticing (the power, usually 80%). Small gaps are brutally expensive: halving δ roughly quadruples n. Move the gap and the disagreement rate, then simulate 400 benchmarks of the size the formula asks for and check that the test really fires as often as promised.

\[ n = \frac{\big(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}\big)^2\,\big(q - \delta^2\big)}{\delta^2} \]
Read it as: per question, the paired difference d is +1 (only A right), −1 (only B right) or 0, so its mean is δ and its variance q − δ². To detect δ with a two-sided test at level α (5%) and power 1 − β, the mean of n such differences must sit z1−α/2 + z1−β standard errors (1.96 + 0.84 for 80% power) away from 0.
Say it“n equals z-alpha plus z-beta, squared, times q minus delta squared, over delta squared.”

Ranking from preferences

From pairwise votes to ratings: Elo and Bradley–Terry

For open-ended chat there is often no answer key. What people can do reliably is compare: “which of these two answers is better?” Chatbot Arena collects such votes from the public; the MT-bench authors collected 3,355 from expert labellers, on answers by six models to the 80 questions. Two classic ways turn votes into ratings. Elo (from chess) nudges both ratings after every vote, by K times the surprise. It is simple but depends on the order of the votes, which for a model evaluation is meaningless. The Bradley–Terry model (1952) fits one strength per model to all votes at once by maximum likelihood, so order cannot matter; it is what Chatbot Arena switched to in 2023. Shuffle the votes and change K: Elo’s lines wander, the dashed Bradley–Terry ratings stay put.

\[ \begin{aligned} E_A &= \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_B - R_A)/400}} \\ R_A &\leftarrow R_A + K\,(S_A - E_A) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: EA is the chance that A wins, predicted from the current ratings; SA is what happened (1 win, ½ tie, 0 loss). A wins against a stronger opponent move the ratings a lot, expected wins hardly at all. B gets the opposite change, so the total is constant.
Say it“A's new rating is its old rating plus K times result minus expectation.”
\[ \begin{aligned} P(i \succ j) &= \frac{\pi_i}{\pi_i + \pi_j} \\ R_i &= 400 \log_{10} \pi_i + c \\ \pi_i &\leftarrow \frac{W_i}{\sum_{j} n_{ij} / (\pi_i + \pi_j)} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each model has a strength πi; on the Elo scale, 400 points of difference means 10-to-1 odds. Wi is model i’s total wins (a tie counts ½) and nij the number of i–j votes. Repeating the update (Hunter’s MM algorithm, 2004) climbs to the maximum-likelihood strengths; c shifts the ratings so they average 1000.
Say it“The chance i beats j is pi-i over pi-i plus pi-j; update pi-i to its wins over the sum of n-i-j over pi-i plus pi-j.”

Automating the grader

LLM-as-a-judge: does it agree with people?

Expert votes are slow and expensive, so modern evaluations ask a strong model to judge. That is only acceptable if the judge agrees with people about as well as people agree with each other. The MT-bench release lets us check: GPT-4 judged the same answer pairs that the experts voted on, each pair twice with the answers swapped, and a verdict that flipped with the order was recorded as a tie. Raw agreement can flatter: if both raters say “A” most of the time they will agree often by luck. Cohen’s κ subtracts that luck. Count ties or drop them, and split by turn, and compare GPT-4–human agreement with human–human agreement on the same items.

\[ \begin{aligned} p_o &= \frac{\sum_c n_{cc}}{n}, \qquad p_e = \sum_c \frac{n_{c\cdot}}{n}\,\frac{n_{\cdot c}}{n} \\ \kappa &= \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: ncc counts the items where both raters chose verdict c (A wins, B wins, tie), so po is the share they agree on. pe is the agreement two raters would reach by chance if they kept their own habits (row and column shares) but decided independently. κ = 1 is perfect agreement, 0 is no better than chance.
Say it“Kappa is observed agreement minus chance agreement, over one minus chance agreement.”

Judges have habits

The judge’s position bias

Show a judge two answers and it may prefer the first one because it is first. The test is simple: judge every pair twice with the order swapped. A fair judge gives the same verdict both times; a biased one sometimes picks whichever answer is in front. The MT-bench release contains exactly this experiment: GPT-4 compared 30 models with GPT-3.5-turbo on all 160 turns, in both orders. Below the real counts, a toy judge lets you see where such numbers come from: it sees the true quality gap Δ between the answers, adds a bonus β for the first position, and makes noise of size σ. Read the real bars, then move β and σ, or let the page fit them to GPT-4.

\[ \begin{gathered} P(\text{first} \mid \Delta) = \Phi\!\Big(\frac{\Delta + \beta}{\sigma}\Big) \\ \Delta \sim \mathcal N(0, 1) \\ P(\text{first, then first}) = \\ \mathbb E_\Delta\Big[\Phi\Big(\tfrac{\Delta + \beta}{\sigma}\Big)\,\Phi\Big(\tfrac{\beta - \Delta}{\sigma}\Big)\Big] \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: Φ is the standard normal CDF. Δ is how much better the answer shown first is (negative if worse). When the order is swapped the gap becomes −Δ but the bonus stays with whoever is first, so picking “first” both times means contradicting itself in favour of position. The page averages over Δ numerically (241 grid points). This judge never says “tie”, so it is compared with GPT-4’s verdicts that had no tie in either order.
Say it“The chance of choosing the first answer both times is the average over Delta of Phi of Delta plus beta over sigma, times Phi of beta minus Delta over sigma.”

Measuring a failure mode

Catching hallucinations, and scoring the catcher

A hallucination is fluent text that is not true. Manakul, Liusie & Gales (2023) asked GPT-3 to write Wikipedia-style biographies of 238 real people and had annotators label all 1,908 sentences: accurate, minor inaccurate or major inaccurate (not about the person at all). Their idea, SelfCheckGPT, needs no reference text: ask the model the same thing 20 more times. Facts it really knows come back in most samples; invented ones change from sample to sample. The simplest version, rebuilt here, counts words: a unigram model is fitted to the 20 samples plus the answer itself, and a sentence is suspicious if it contains a word that the samples almost never use.

The detector gives every sentence a score; whether it is any good is a ranking question, so it is graded by the precision–recall curve and its area (average precision, AUC-PR). Switch the score, change the smoothing, and move the threshold; pick a biography to see the sentences it flags.

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde p(w) &= \frac{c_w + k}{N + kV} \\ S_{\max}(i) &= \max_j\, \big(-\log \tilde p(w_{ij})\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: cw is how often word w occurs in the 20 samples and the answer, N the total word count and V the number of distinct words; k is add-k smoothing (the paper uses k = 0: including the answer already gives every word a count of at least 1). Sentence i’s score is the surprise of its rarest word; the average over its words is the alternative Savg.
Say it“p-tilde of w is c-w plus k over N plus k V; the max score is the largest minus log p-tilde over the sentence's words.”
\[ \text{AP} = \sum_n \big(R_n - R_{n-1}\big)\, P_n \]
Read it as: sort the sentences by score, then lower a threshold past each distinct score n. Pn is the precision (share of flagged sentences that really are inaccurate) and Rn the recall (share of all inaccurate sentences that are flagged). AP adds up precision weighted by each step in recall; a detector that ranks at random scores the base rate, here 72.96%.
Say it“Average precision is the sum over thresholds of the gain in recall times the precision.”

Looking for trouble on purpose

Red-teaming: hunting for failures

Benchmarks measure average behaviour; safety is often about the worst case. Red-teaming sends people (or other models) to make a system misbehave, and the result is a rate: of the attempts, how many succeeded? Ganguli et al. (2022) released 38,961 red-team attempts against Anthropic’s research assistants of three sizes and four kinds: a plain language model, one prompted to be helpful, honest and harmless (stored as “context distillation”), one that draws several replies and keeps the one a harmlessness model rates best (rejection sampling), and one trained with RLHF. After each conversation the red-teamer rated their own success from 0 (failed) to 4 (fully succeeded). Only those ratings are used here, not the conversations. Change what counts as a success, then use the calculator to plan a red-team of your own.

\[ \begin{gathered} c = \frac{\hat p + z^2/2n}{1 + z^2/n} \\ h = \frac{z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + z^2/n} \\ \text{CI}_{\text{Wilson}} = [\,c - h,\; c + h\,] \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: the Wilson interval for a success rate p̂ = k/n (Wilson, 1927) has its centre c pulled slightly towards ½ and a half-width h. Unlike p̂ ± 1.96 SE, it never leaves [0, 1] and still behaves when k is 0 or tiny, which is the usual case for rare failures.
Say it“The Wilson interval: p-hat plus z squared over 2n, plus or minus z times the root of p-hat one-minus-p-hat over n plus z squared over 4 n squared, all over one plus z squared over n.”
\[ \begin{gathered} P(\text{find} \ge 1) = 1 - (1 - p)^n \\ 0 \text{ found} \;\Rightarrow\; p \lesssim \frac{3}{n} \end{gathered} \]
Read it as: if each attempt independently succeeds with probability p, n attempts find at least one failure with probability 1 − (1 − p)n. Turned round: finding nothing in n attempts only tells you, with 95% confidence, that p is below 1 − 0.051/n ≈ 3/n (the “rule of three”). No failures found is not the same as no failures.
Say it“The chance of finding at least one is one minus one-minus-p to the n; if you find none, p is probably below three over n.”

Real-world project

Project: auditing a published leaderboard

MT-bench (Zheng et al., 2023) is a real, widely cited benchmark, and its authors released everything: the 80 questions, the answers of 34 models, GPT-4’s 5,440 single-answer grades, 4,800 pairwise judgments in both orders, and 3,355 expert votes. The LMSYS leaderboard of 17 July 2023 printed one number per model, to two decimals, with no uncertainty. This project audits that table with the tools of this chapter. Every number below is computed in your browser from the released judgment files (downloaded 5 November 2026 from the lmsys/mt-bench space and the lmsys/mt_bench_human_judgments dataset on Hugging Face) and checked against an independent Python implementation.

import json, gzip, re, math, csv
import numpy as np
from scipy import optimize, stats, integrate
from sklearn.metrics import cohen_kappa_score, average_precision_score
D = "el_dl/"                                       # the raw downloads: MT-bench, human judgments, SelfCheckGPT, red team
jl = lambda f: [json.loads(l) for l in open(D + f, encoding="utf8") if l.strip()]

def mulberry32(a):                                 # the page's generator, so both sides draw the same bootstrap samples
    def r():
        nonlocal a
        a = (a + 0x6D2B79F5) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & 0xFFFFFFFF
        t = ((t + (((t ^ (t >> 7)) * (61 | t)) & 0xFFFFFFFF)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) ^ t
        return ((t ^ (t >> 14)) & 0xFFFFFFFF) / 4294967296
    return r
def boot_idx(n, B, seed):
    r = mulberry32(seed); return np.array([[math.floor(r() * n) for _ in range(n)] for _ in range(B)])

# 1. the published table: GPT-4's 1-10 grades of 34 models on 80 questions x 2 turns
qid = {q["question_id"]: i for i, q in enumerate(jl("question.jsonl"))}
G = {}
for o in jl("gpt-4_single.jsonl"):
    G.setdefault(o["model"], np.full(160, np.nan))[qid[o["question_id"]] * 2 + ("multi-turn" in o["judge"][1])] = o["score"]
models = sorted(G, key=lambda m: -np.nanmean(np.where(G[m] == -1, np.nan, G[m])))
pub = {r["Model"]: float(r["MT-bench (score)"]) for r in csv.DictReader(open(D + "leaderboard_table_20230717.csv"))}
norm = lambda s: re.sub(r"[^a-z0-9]", "", s.lower().replace("v1.3", ""))
alias = {"oasst-sft-7-llama-30b": "OpenAssistant-LLaMA-30B", "palm-2-chat-bison-001": "PaLM-Chat-Bison-001",
         "h2ogpt-oasst-open-llama-13b": "H2O-Oasst-OpenLLaMA-13B", "oasst-sft-4-pythia-12b": "OpenAssistant-Pythia-12B"}
name = {m: alias.get(m) or next(p for p in pub if norm(p) == norm(m)) for m in models}
with_fail = {m: G[m].mean() for m in models}                     # a failed grading (-1) averaged in, as published
dropped = {m: np.nanmean(np.where(G[m] == -1, np.nan, G[m])) for m in models}
print("published scores reproduced: counting failures", sum(abs(with_fail[m] - pub[name[m]]) <= 0.005 + 1e-9 for m in models),
      "/ dropping them", sum(abs(dropped[m] - pub[name[m]]) <= 0.005 + 1e-9 for m in models), "of", len(models))

# 2. bootstrap over questions (failed gradings dropped): intervals, plausible ranks, paired significance
S = np.array([[np.nansum(np.where(G[m] == -1, np.nan, G[m])[2 * q:2 * q + 2]) for q in range(80)] for m in models])
N = np.array([[np.sum(G[m][2 * q:2 * q + 2] >= 0) for q in range(80)] for m in models])
idx = boot_idx(80, 2000, 2023)
R = np.array([S[:, ix].sum(1) / N[:, ix].sum(1) for ix in idx])           # 2000 x 34
ci = np.percentile(R, [2.5, 97.5], axis=0)
ranks = np.empty_like(R, dtype=int)
for b in range(len(R)): ranks[b, np.lexsort((np.arange(34), -R[b]))] = np.arange(1, 35)
rr = np.floor(np.percentile(ranks, [2.5, 97.5], axis=0) + 0.5).astype(int)
for k in (0, 1, 2, 3, 6, 33):
    m = models[k]; print(f"{k + 1:2d} {m:22s} {dropped[m]:.4f}  95% CI [{ci[0, k]:.4f}, {ci[1, k]:.4f}]  ranks {rr[0, k]}-{rr[1, k]}")
lo, hi = np.percentile(R[:, :, None] - R[:, None, :], [2.5, 97.5], axis=0)
sig = (lo > 0) | (hi < 0)
print("median CI half-width", round(float(np.median((ci[1] - ci[0]) / 2)), 4), "| pairs separated", int(np.triu(sig, 1).sum()), "of 561",
      "| adjacent pairs", int(sum(sig[k, k + 1] for k in range(33))), "of 33")
m = models.index("Llama-2-70b-chat")
for unit, s, n in (("questions", S[m], N[m]), ("turns", G["Llama-2-70b-chat"][G["Llama-2-70b-chat"] >= 0], np.ones(160))):
    n = n[:len(s)]; r = [s[ix].sum() / n[ix].sum() for ix in boot_idx(len(s), 1000, 2023)]
    print(f"Llama-2-70b-chat, resampling {unit}: 95% CI [{np.percentile(r, 2.5):.4f}, {np.percentile(r, 97.5):.4f}]")

# 3. expert votes and GPT-4 votes on the same items -> Bradley-Terry, agreement, kappa
HM = ["gpt-4", "claude-v1", "gpt-3.5-turbo", "vicuna-13b-v1.2", "alpaca-13b", "llama-13b"]
def canon(r):
    a, b, w = HM.index(r["model_a"]), HM.index(r["model_b"]), {"model_a": 0, "model_b": 1, "tie": 2, "tie (inconsistent)": 2}[r["winner"]]
    return (r["question_id"], b, a, r["turn"], 1 - w if w < 2 else 2) if a > b else (r["question_id"], a, b, r["turn"], w)
hum = [canon(r) + (r["judge"],) for r in json.load(open(D + "mtbh-human.json"))]
g4 = {canon(r)[:4]: canon(r)[4] for r in json.load(open(D + "mtbh-gpt4_pair.json"))}
def bt(votes):                                      # maximum likelihood by L-BFGS (the page uses the MM algorithm)
    i, j, s = (np.array(x) for x in zip(*[(a, b, {0: 1, 1: 0, 2: .5}[w]) for a, b, w in votes]))
    f = lambda t: -np.sum(s * -np.logaddexp(0, t[j] - t[i]) + (1 - s) * -np.logaddexp(0, t[i] - t[j]))
    t = optimize.minimize(f, np.zeros(6), method="L-BFGS-B", options=dict(ftol=1e-15, gtol=1e-12)).x
    return 1000 + 400 * (t - t.mean()) / math.log(10)
print("Bradley-Terry, experts:", np.round(bt([h[1:3] + (h[4],) for h in hum]), 2))
print("Bradley-Terry, GPT-4:  ", np.round(bt([k[1:3] + (w,) for k, w in g4.items()]), 2))
r = mulberry32(1); order = list(range(len(hum)))
for k in range(len(order) - 1, 0, -1): j = math.floor(r() * (k + 1)); order[k], order[j] = order[j], order[k]
E = np.full(6, 1000.0)
for v in order:
    a, b, w = hum[v][1], hum[v][2], hum[v][4]; e = 1 / (1 + 10 ** ((E[b] - E[a]) / 400)); d = 16 * ({0: 1, 1: 0, 2: .5}[w] - e); E[a] += d; E[b] -= d
print("Elo, K = 16, shuffle seed 1:", np.round(E, 2))
h, g = [x[4] for x in hum], [g4[x[:4]] for x in hum]
keep = [a < 2 and b < 2 for a, b in zip(h, g)]
print(f"GPT-4 vs experts: agreement {np.mean(np.equal(h, g)):.4f}, kappa {cohen_kappa_score(h, g):.4f}; without ties "
      f"{np.mean([a == b for a, b, k in zip(h, g, keep) if k]):.4f}, kappa {cohen_kappa_score(np.array(h)[keep], np.array(g)[keep]):.4f}")
items = {}
for x in hum: items.setdefault(x[:4], []).append(x)
pairs = [(u[4], v[4]) for vs in items.values() for i, u in enumerate(vs) for v in vs[i + 1:] if u[5] != v[5]]
print(f"expert vs expert: {np.mean([a == b for a, b in pairs]):.4f} of {len(pairs)} pairs; without ties {np.mean([a == b for a, b in pairs if a < 2 and b < 2]):.4f}")

# 4. position bias: GPT-4 judged each pair in both orders
W = {"model_1": 0, "model_2": 1, "tie": 2}
pos = [(W[o["g1_winner"]], W[o["g2_winner"]]) for o in jl("gpt-4_pair.jsonl") if o["g1_winner"] in W and o["g2_winner"] in W]
first, second = pos.count((0, 1)), pos.count((1, 0))
print(f"position: {len(pos)} pairs, consistent {np.mean([a == b for a, b in pos]):.4f}, flips to first {first}, to second {second}")
ff = integrate.quad(lambda x: stats.norm.pdf(x) * stats.norm.cdf((x + .3) / .5) * stats.norm.cdf((.3 - x) / .5), -np.inf, np.inf)[0]
print(f"toy judge (beta 0.3, sigma 0.5): P(first, first) = {ff:.6f}")

# 5. hallucination detection: SelfCheckGPT with a unigram model of the 20 samples plus the answer
tok = lambda t: re.findall(r"[^\W_]+", t.lower())
sc, y = [], []
for p in json.load(open(D + "wikibio.json", encoding="utf8")):
    c = {}
    for t in [p["gpt3_text"]] + p["gpt3_text_samples"]:
        for w in tok(t): c[w] = c.get(w, 0) + 1

The browser and Python share no code. Both replay the same pseudo-random draws (the page’s mulberry32 generator, re-implemented in Python), so the bootstrap intervals agree exactly, not just roughly; the Bradley–Terry ratings are fitted in Python by a different method (scipy’s L-BFGS on the log-likelihood, against the page’s MM algorithm) and agree to every printed digit; Elo, Cohen’s κ, the position-bias counts and the Wilson intervals match too. Python re-tokenises the raw GPT-3 text with its own regular expression and still gets the same average precision as the page, 0.8565, through scikit-learn. Honest caveats: MT-bench’s grades come from GPT-4, whose own preferences (for longer answers, for its own style) are baked in; 80 questions is small, so its error bars are wide; and these are 2023 models. The method, not the ranking, is the lesson.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A benchmark score is an estimate from a sample of questions: report its confidence interval, which shrinks only like 1/√n.
  • Bootstrap the units that were sampled independently (questions, not turns), and compare models question by question: paired differences are far less noisy.
  • Small gaps need huge benchmarks: n grows like 1/δ², and only the questions where models disagree carry information.
  • Pairwise preferences become ratings with Bradley–Terry (order-free) rather than online Elo; LLM judges must be validated against people and checked for position bias by swapping the order.
  • Failure modes need their own metrics: grade a hallucination detector by its precision–recall curve, report red-team success rates with intervals, and remember that finding nothing in n tries only bounds the rate at about 3/n.

Check your understanding

Model A scores 67.0% and model B 64.0% on HumanEval (164 problems). Is A better?

Not on this evidence. Each score has a standard error of about 3.7 points, so a 3-point gap is well within the noise of two independent scores. A paired test on the problems where the models disagree is more sensitive, but with 164 problems a 3-point gap would still usually go undetected.

Why does resampling turns instead of questions give a narrower bootstrap interval for MT-bench?

The two turns of one question are positively correlated (same topic, same difficulty). Resampling turns treats them as independent pieces of evidence, so it overstates how much information there is and understates the uncertainty. Resample the unit that was drawn independently: the question.

Two models’ 95% intervals overlap. Can one still be significantly better?

Yes. Separate intervals ignore that both models answered the same questions. If their per-question grades are strongly correlated, the paired difference has a much smaller standard error, and its interval can exclude zero even when the individual intervals overlap.

Why prefer Bradley–Terry to online Elo for ranking models from a fixed set of votes?

Online Elo’s final ratings depend on the order the votes arrive in and on the step size K, neither of which says anything about the models. Bradley–Terry finds the maximum-likelihood strengths from all votes at once, so the result is unique and order-free, and it comes with proper confidence intervals.

A red-team of 200 attempts found no successful attack. What can you claim?

Only that the success rate is probably below about 3/200 = 1.5% (95% confidence, by the rule of three), for the kinds of attack the team tried. It does not show that the rate is zero, and attackers with other strategies may do far better.
