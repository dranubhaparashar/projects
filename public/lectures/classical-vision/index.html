Image Formation & Classical Vision

How light becomes pixels, and how pixels give back edges, texture and motion

A camera squashes a three-dimensional world onto a flat grid of numbers. Vision runs that process backwards: from the numbers, recover what is out there and how it moves. Before networks learned their own filters, people built this by hand, and the hand-built pieces (projection, shading, edges, texture, optical flow, segmentation) are still what every vision system rests on. On the right is a real time-lapse frame taken from the International Space Station. The arrows show how the ground below moved in the next four seconds, measured from the pixels alone. Touch the photo to look through an edge-finding lens.

Image formation

From world to image: the pinhole camera

Vision is passive sensing: the camera sends nothing out (unlike a sonar or a lidar, which are active) and simply records light that the world reflects. The image is full of information, but most of it is irrelevant to any one task, so vision systems first compute features, simple quantities such as edges or motion that are cheap to compute and useful. There are two broad ways to use them. One builds an object model (what a cup looks like) and a rendering model (how the world, light and camera make an image) and then inverts the rendering. The other learns the mapping from images to answers directly, as the networks in the previous chapters do. Either way, the inversion is hard, because many different worlds give the same picture.

The simplest camera is a box with a pinhole. Light from a point in the scene passes through the hole and lands on the back wall, upside down. Put the hole at the origin, let Z be the distance in front of it and f the distance from the hole to the back wall. Similar triangles give the whole of perspective projection. Drag the tips of the two arrows, and try to make the tall far arrow and the short near arrow cast exactly the same image.

\[ x = -\,\frac{f\,X}{Z}, \qquad y = -\,\frac{f\,Y}{Z} \]
Read it as: a scene point at height Y and distance Z lands on the back wall at height −fY/Z: flipped (the minus sign), shrunk in proportion to its distance. Double the distance and the image halves. Only the ratio Y/Z survives, so a tree twice as tall and twice as far away gives exactly the same image: depth is lost.
Say it“x is minus f X over Z, and y is minus f Y over Z.”

Parallel lines meet at a vanishing point

Perspective has a famous signature. Follow a line in the scene further and further away: its points get divided by an ever larger Z, and the image of the line runs into a single point that depends only on the line's direction. Every line with that direction, wherever it starts, heads for the same point. Railway tracks meet at the horizon even though they never meet on the ground. Turn the track and watch the vanishing point slide along the horizon.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{P}(t) = \mathbf{P}_0 + t\,\mathbf{U} \\ &x(t) = \frac{f\,(X_0 + tU_x)}{Z_0 + tU_z} \;\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}\; \frac{f\,U_x}{U_z} \\ &y(t) = \frac{f\,(Y_0 + tU_y)}{Z_0 + tU_z} \;\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}\; \frac{f\,U_y}{U_z} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: a line starts at P0 and runs in direction U. Far along it, the starting point stops mattering and the image converges to fUx/Uz, fUy/Uz: the vanishing point of that direction. (We draw the image upright from here on, as camera software does, so the minus signs go.) Directions along the ground plane have Uy = 0, so with a level camera all their vanishing points lie on one line, the horizon.
Say it“As t goes to infinity, the image of P-naught plus t U tends to f U-x over U-z, f U-y over U-z.”

Lenses: more light, at a price

A pinhole has to be tiny to keep the image sharp, so it lets in very little light; widen it and every scene point becomes a blob. Real eyes and cameras use a lens, which gathers light over a wide opening and bends all the rays from one scene point back to one image point. The catch is that this only works for points at one distance. Points nearer or further focus in front of or behind the sensor and spread into a blur circle. The range of distances whose blur is too small to notice is the depth of field. Move the object and the focus, then open and close the aperture.

\[ \frac{1}{Z} + \frac{1}{z'} = \frac{1}{f} \]
Read it as: the thin-lens law. A point at distance Z in front of a lens of focal length f comes to a sharp focus at distance z' behind it. Far points (Z → ∞) focus at z' = f; close points focus further back.
Say it“One over Z plus one over z-prime equals one over f.”
\[ b = A\,\frac{\lvert z' - z_s \rvert}{z'} \]
Read it as: the sensor sits at zs. Rays filling an aperture of width A converge at z'; at the sensor they have spread to a disc of width b. The blur grows with the aperture and with how far the sensor is from the true focus. Shrink A towards a pinhole and the blur vanishes, along with the light.
Say it“The blur b is A times the gap between z-prime and z-s, over z-prime.”

Scaled orthographic projection: the far-away shortcut

Perspective divides every point by its own depth Z, which makes the maths nonlinear. But if an object is far away compared with its own depth, every point on it has nearly the same Z, call it Z0, and the division becomes one fixed scale factor. That is scaled orthographic projection. It is the right model for a satellite image of gently rolling ground, and the wrong one for a corridor or a railway line. Below, the camera zooms as it backs away, so the box keeps the same size in the image (a “dolly zoom”). Back the camera away and watch perspective (solid) settle onto the orthographic shortcut (dashed).

\[ x = s\,X, \quad y = s\,Y, \qquad s = \frac{f}{Z_0} \]
Read it as: drop the division by each point's own depth and use one scale s for the whole object. The error is roughly the object's depth range ΔZ over its distance Z0: a 1 m box seen from 50 m is off by about 2%, so far objects really are flattened copies of themselves.
Say it“x is s X and y is s Y, where s is f over Z-naught.”

Light and shading

How bright a pixel is depends on three things: the light falling on the surface, how the surface reflects it, and the angle between them. A matt surface such as chalk or dry soil is diffuse (Lambertian): it scatters light equally in every direction, so it looks equally bright from anywhere, and its brightness depends only on how squarely the light hits it. A shiny surface adds specular reflection: a mirror-like highlight that moves when you move. Where the surface faces away from the light it is in attached shadow; light bouncing off other surfaces still reaches it, which the crude ambient term stands in for. These rules are why shading is a cue to shape. Move the light around the sphere and hover over the sphere to read off any pixel.

\[ \begin{aligned} I = {}&\rho\,\big(I_a + I_0 \max(0,\ \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l})\big) \\ &+ k_s I_0 \max(0,\ \mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{v})^{\alpha} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: n is the surface normal, l the direction to the light, and n·l = cos θ the cosine of the angle between them. A Lambertian surface of albedo ρ (its reflectance, between 0 and 1) returns ρ I0 cos θ, plus a little ambient light Ia. The last term is a simple specular model: r is the light's mirror direction, v points to the viewer, and the exponent α sets how tight the highlight is.
Say it“Brightness is rho times ambient plus I-naught times the cosine of the angle to the light, plus k-s I-naught times r dot v to the alpha.”
of the disc in attached shadow

Colour: three numbers from a whole spectrum

Light is a mixture of wavelengths, described by a spectrum: energy at every wavelength from violet (about 400 nm) to red (about 700 nm). Neither your eye nor a camera records the spectrum. Each has three kinds of sensor, each sensitive to a broad band, and each reports one number. This is the principle of trichromacy: for human observers, almost any colour can be matched by mixing just three suitable primaries. A consequence is metamers: physically different spectra that produce identical responses and so look identical. Pick a pure wavelength and watch three primaries match it.

\[ \begin{aligned} &R_k = \int E(\lambda)\, \rho(\lambda)\, S_k(\lambda)\, d\lambda \\ &k \in \{\text{long},\ \text{medium},\ \text{short}\} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the light source emits spectrum E(λ), the surface reflects a fraction ρ(λ) of each wavelength, and sensor k weights what arrives by its sensitivity Sk(λ) and adds it all up. A whole curve goes in; three numbers come out. Any two spectra with the same three integrals are indistinguishable to this sensor.
Say it“Response k is the integral over wavelength of E times rho times S-k.”
sensor responses (L, M, S)

Colour constancy: which colour is the light?

Under a warm lamp a white page sends a yellowish spectrum to your eye, yet you still see it as white. People discount the colour of the light: that is colour constancy. A camera has to do it in software, and it can only do so by guessing the illuminant. The simplest model treats the light as one gain per channel, so a change of light multiplies each channel by its own number. The grey-world guess assumes the scene averages to grey. Tint the light, then press grey-world, and see when the guess works and when it fails on this real ISS photo.

\[ \begin{aligned} &c_k = e_k\,\rho_k, \qquad \hat{\rho}_k = \frac{c_k}{\hat e_k} \\ &\hat e_k \propto \operatorname{mean}(c_k) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: each channel's value ck is the illuminant's strength ek in that channel times the surface's reflectance ρk. Divide by an estimate of ek and you recover the surface. Grey world estimates ek from the channel's average, which is right only if the true reflectances average to grey.
Say it“The camera sees e-k times rho-k; divide by your guess of e-k, which grey world takes from the channel mean.”

Image features

Edges: where brightness jumps

Edges are curves in the image across which brightness changes sharply. They matter because they usually mark something real in the scene: a depth discontinuity (one object in front of another), a change of surface orientation (the fold of a box), a change of reflectance (a coastline, a printed letter), or a change of illumination (the boundary of a shadow). Finding them sounds easy: look for large derivatives. But differentiating amplifies noise, so every detector first smooths with a Gaussian. Because convolution and differentiation commute, smoothing then differentiating is the same as convolving once with the derivative of a Gaussian. Raise the noise, then raise σ, and watch the false peaks die.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(G_\sigma * I\big) = \Big(\frac{\partial G_\sigma}{\partial x}\Big) * I \\ &G_\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\, e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: blur the signal with a Gaussian of width σ, then take the slope; or, equivalently, slide the slope of the Gaussian along the signal in one pass. Small σ keeps edges sharp and lets noise through; large σ kills noise and blurs nearby edges together.
Say it“The derivative of G-sigma convolved with I equals the derivative of G-sigma, convolved with I.”

peaks above half-max

In two dimensions: gradient, thinning, hysteresis

In an image the derivative becomes a gradient, a little arrow at every pixel pointing uphill in brightness. Its length says how strong the edge is and its direction is perpendicular to the edge. Canny's detector (1986) then does two clever things. Non-maximum suppression keeps a pixel only if it is stronger than both neighbours along the gradient, which thins thick ridges to one-pixel curves. Hysteresis keeps every pixel above a high threshold, plus weaker pixels (above 0.4 of it) only if they connect to a strong one, so edges are not broken into dashes. Step through the stages and tune σ and the threshold on the real ISS frame; hover to read any pixel's gradient.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\lVert \nabla I \rVert = \sqrt{I_x^2 + I_y^2} \\ &\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(I_y,\ I_x) \\ &I_x \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\big(I(x{+}1,y) - I(x{-}1,y)\big) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Ix and Iy are the slopes of the smoothed image across and down, estimated by central differences. The gradient's length is the edge strength; its angle θ is the direction of steepest brightening, at right angles to the edge itself.
Say it“The gradient magnitude is the square root of I-x squared plus I-y squared; its angle is atan2 of I-y and I-x.”

Image features

Texture: the statistics of a patch

Texture is a pattern that repeats roughly over a surface: ripples on water, fields seen from orbit, the gridded cells of a solar array. One pixel says nothing about texture; it is a property of a window of pixels. A simple, effective description is a histogram of gradient orientations inside the window, each gradient voting with its strength. Smooth sea gives an almost empty histogram; a gridded panel gives sharp spikes at two angles; land and cloud give broad, mixed votes. Because the votes are normalised, the description barely changes when the light gets brighter, which is exactly what you want of a texture descriptor. Hover over the photo (or tap a preset), then change the brightness.

\[ \begin{aligned} &h_k = \tfrac{1}{Z}\!\sum_{p \in \mathcal{W}} \lVert \nabla I(p) \rVert\ \mathbf{1}\big[\theta(p) \in \text{bin}_k\big] \\ &Z = \sum_{p \in \mathcal{W}} \lVert \nabla I(p) \rVert \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: split orientations from 0° to 180° into 8 bins. Every pixel p in the window adds its gradient strength to the bin its edge direction falls into. Divide by the total so the bins sum to 1. Multiply the image by any brightness factor and every vote, and the total, scale together: h does not change. The total Z itself, per pixel, measures how busy the texture is.
Say it“Bin k collects the gradient strength of every pixel in the window whose orientation lands in bin k, divided by the total.”

Image features

Optical flow: how the picture moves

When the camera or the scene moves, the pattern of brightness in the image moves too. Optical flow is that apparent motion: a little displacement vector at every pixel. It tells a fly how fast it is approaching a wall and tells the ISS camera's software how fast the ground slides by. The direct way to measure it is to take a small patch from one frame and search the next frame for the shift that matches best, scoring each shift by the sum of squared differences (SSD). Hover over the frame to choose a patch and look at its SSD surface: a sharp bowl means a confident match, a long valley means the patch only constrains motion across an edge, and a flat plain means there is nothing to match.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\operatorname{SSD}(\Delta x, \Delta y) = \sum_{(x,y) \in \mathcal{W}} e(x, y)^2 \\ &e = I_t(x, y) - I_{t+1}(x{+}\Delta x,\, y{+}\Delta y) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: slide the next frame It+1 by (Δx, Δy), subtract it from this frame It pixel by pixel over the window to get the error e, square and add. The shift with the smallest total is the estimated flow. It needs texture: on a blank patch every shift scores the same.
Say it“SSD of delta-x, delta-y is the sum over the window of the squared difference between this frame and the next frame shifted by delta-x, delta-y.”

Brightness constancy and the aperture problem

Searching is slow, and it only gives whole pixels. Lucas and Kanade (1981) solved for the motion instead. Assume a point keeps its brightness as it moves, and expand to first order: the change in time must be explained by the slope times the motion. That is one equation per pixel with two unknowns, so pool every pixel in a window and solve by least squares. The 2 × 2 matrix G that appears is the window's structure tensor. Its smaller eigenvalue says how well the motion is pinned down: large for a corner, almost zero along a straight edge. That second case is the aperture problem: through a small window an edge's motion along itself is invisible, so only the component across the edge can be measured. Pick a pattern and a true motion, and compare the estimate with the truth.

\[ I_x\,u + I_y\,v + I_t = 0 \]
Read it as: the brightness-constancy constraint. (u, v) is the motion, Ix, Iy the spatial slopes and It the change between frames. One line of possible motions per pixel.
Say it“I-x u plus I-y v plus I-t equals zero.”
\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \Sigma I_x^2 & \Sigma I_x I_y \\ \Sigma I_x I_y & \Sigma I_y^2 \end{bmatrix}}_{G} \!\begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix} \!=\! -\!\begin{bmatrix} \Sigma I_x I_t \\ \Sigma I_y I_t \end{bmatrix} \]
Read it as: least squares over the window. Solvable when G is well conditioned, that is when the window has gradients in two different directions. Big motions are handled by repeating this on a coarse-to-fine image pyramid.
Say it“G times u, v equals minus the sums of I-x I-t and I-y I-t.”

Segmenting natural images

Segmentation splits an image into regions that each belong to one thing. There are two strategies. Boundary-based methods ask, for each pixel and direction, whether a boundary runs through it: they compare brightness, colour and texture on the two sides of a small disc and let a classifier, trained on boundaries that people drew by hand (the Berkeley segmentation dataset), turn the differences into a probability. Region-based methods group pixels that are alike. In practice one first over-segments into superpixels, small compact patches that rarely straddle a true boundary, and then reasons about a few hundred patches instead of tens of thousands of pixels. SLIC (2012) does this with k-means on colour plus position. Change the spacing and compactness, then merge the superpixels into a handful of regions.

\[ \begin{aligned} D^2 = {}&\lVert \mathbf{c}_p - \mathbf{c}_j \rVert^2 \\ &+ \Big(\frac{m}{S}\Big)^2 \lVert \mathbf{x}_p - \mathbf{x}_j \rVert^2 \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the distance from pixel p to superpixel centre j mixes a colour difference (RGB) with a position difference. S is the grid spacing the centres start on and m the compactness: large m makes position dominate (square, tidy patches), small m lets colour dominate (patches that hug boundaries but go ragged). Each pixel joins the nearest centre within 2S; centres move to their members' means; repeat 10 times.
Say it“D squared is the colour distance squared plus m over S, squared, times the position distance squared.”

Normalised cuts: segmentation as graph partitioning

Another view treats every pixel (or superpixel) as a node in a graph, with an edge between two nodes weighted by how alike they are. Segmenting means cutting the graph into pieces with little weight between them. Simply minimising the weight cut is a trap: it is cheapest to snip off one lonely node. Shi and Malik (2000) divide the cut by how strongly each side is tied to the whole graph, which punishes tiny slivers. The exact minimum is NP-hard for big graphs, but relaxing it gives an eigenvector problem. Drag the points and compare the three answers.

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Ncut}(A, B) &= \frac{\operatorname{cut}(A,B)}{\operatorname{assoc}(A, V)} \\ &\quad + \frac{\operatorname{cut}(A,B)}{\operatorname{assoc}(B, V)} \\ w_{ij} &= e^{-\lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j \rVert^2 / 2\sigma^2} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: cut(A, B) adds the weights of edges running between the two sides; assoc(A, V) adds the weights of all edges touching A. A sliver has a tiny assoc, so its Ncut is large. The spectral relaxation takes the eigenvector with the second-smallest eigenvalue of (D − W)y = λDy, where D holds each node's total weight, and splits the nodes by thresholding it.
Say it“Ncut is the cut over A's association plus the cut over B's association.”

Project: watching Earth move under the ISS

On 10 August 2019 the Expedition 60 crew left a camera on the International Space Station shooting one frame a second as it crossed the Mediterranean. NASA's caption says the sequence sweeps from the coast of Tunisia across Sicily and Italy. We take five consecutive frames, ISS060-E-34378 to 34382 (EXIF times 10:13:01 to 10:13:05 GMT), downscale them from 640 × 427 to 320 × 213 and convert to grey levels. Everything below is computed in your browser from those pixels: Canny edges, and pyramidal Lucas–Kanade flow on a grid of 228 points (every 16 pixels), 3 pyramid levels, 15 × 15 windows, 6 iterations per level. A point counts as trackable when λmin of its window's G exceeds 100. Change the time gap and watch the arrows grow.

The same project in Python

import json, base64, numpy as np
from scipy import ndimage as ndi

D = json.load(open("x058-data.json"))           # five ISS frames, 320 x 213, one second apart
W, H = D["W"], D["H"]
F = [np.frombuffer(base64.b64decode(g), np.uint8).reshape(H, W).astype(float) for g in D["grey"]]

def blur(I, s):  # Gaussian, radius int(3s + 0.5), edges replicated
    return ndi.gaussian_filter(I, s, mode="nearest", truncate=3.0)

def grad(S):     # central differences: (I[x+1] - I[x-1]) / 2
    k = [-0.5, 0, 0.5]
    return ndi.correlate1d(S, k, axis=1, mode="nearest"), ndi.correlate1d(S, k, axis=0, mode="nearest")

def canny(I, s, hi, lo_frac=0.4):
    gx, gy = grad(blur(I, s)); mag = np.hypot(gx, gy)
    a = np.degrees(np.arctan2(gy, gx)) % 180
    d = np.select([(a < 22.5) | (a >= 157.5), a < 67.5, a < 112.5], [0, 1, 2], 3)
    off = {0: (0, 1), 1: (1, 1), 2: (1, 0), 3: (1, -1)}         # (dy, dx) along the gradient
    nms = np.zeros_like(mag)
    for k, (dy, dx) in off.items():
        n1 = np.roll(mag, (-dy, -dx), (0, 1)); n2 = np.roll(mag, (dy, dx), (0, 1))
        keep = (d == k) & (mag >= n1) & (mag >= n2) & (mag > 0)
        nms[keep] = mag[keep]
    nms[0, :] = nms[-1, :] = nms[:, 0] = nms[:, -1] = 0
    lab, _ = ndi.label(nms >= hi * lo_frac, structure=np.ones((3, 3)))   # hysteresis
    return np.isin(lab, np.unique(lab[nms >= hi])) & (lab > 0)

def pyramid(I, levels=3):
    P = [blur(I, 1)]
    for _ in range(levels - 1):
        S = blur(P[-1], 1); h, w = S.shape[0] // 2, S.shape[1] // 2
        P.append(S[:2*h, :2*w].reshape(h, 2, w, 2).mean(axis=(1, 3)))
    return [(p, *grad(p)) for p in P]

def bilinear(I, x, y):
    h, w = I.shape; x = np.clip(x, 0, w - 1); y = np.clip(y, 0, h - 1)
    x0 = np.minimum(np.floor(x).astype(int), w - 2); y0 = np.minimum(np.floor(y).astype(int), h - 2)
    fx, fy = x - x0, y - y0
    return (1-fy)*((1-fx)*I[y0, x0] + fx*I[y0, x0+1]) + fy*((1-fx)*I[y0+1, x0] + fx*I[y0+1, x0+1])

def lucas_kanade(PA, PB, x, y, w=7, iters=6):   # coarse-to-fine, 15 x 15 window
    u = v = 0.0
    for l in range(len(PA) - 1, -1, -1):
        (A, gx, gy), (B, _, _) = PA[l], PB[l]
        if l < len(PA) - 1: u, v = 2 * u, 2 * v
        xs = np.clip((x >> l) + np.arange(-w, w + 1), 0, A.shape[1] - 1)
        Y, X = np.meshgrid(np.clip((y >> l) + np.arange(-w, w + 1), 0, A.shape[0] - 1), xs, indexing="ij")
        Ix, Iy, Ia = gx[Y, X], gy[Y, X], A[Y, X]
        G = np.array([[np.sum(Ix * Ix), np.sum(Ix * Iy)], [np.sum(Ix * Iy), np.sum(Iy * Iy)]])
        for _ in range(iters):
            e = Ia - bilinear(B, X + u, Y + v)
            if np.linalg.det(G) > 1e-9:
                du, dv = np.linalg.solve(G, [np.sum(e * Ix), np.sum(e * Iy)]); u, v = u + du, v + dv
    return u, v, np.linalg.eigvalsh(G)[0]

for s, hi in [(1.0, 8), (1.5, 6), (2.5, 4)]:
    E = canny(F[0], s, hi)
    print(f"Canny sigma={s} high={hi} low={0.4*hi:.1f}: {E.sum():5d} edge pixels ({100*E.mean():.2f}% of the frame)")
pts = [(x, y) for y in range(16, H - 15, 16) for x in range(16, W - 15, 16)]    # 19 x 12 grid
panel = np.array([x >= 240 and y <= 64 for x, y in pts]); Y = np.array([y for x, y in pts])
PA = pyramid(F[0])
for gap in (1, 2, 4):
    PB = pyramid(F[gap]); R = np.array([lucas_kanade(PA, PB, x, y) for x, y in pts])
    ok = R[:, 2] > 100                                   # enough texture to track
    earth = ok & ~panel
    print(f"gap {gap} s: {ok.sum()} of {len(pts)} points trackable; Earth median flow "
          f"({np.median(R[earth, 0]):+.3f}, {np.median(R[earth, 1]):+.3f}) px; "
          f"solar-array max |flow| {np.abs(R[panel & ok, :2]).max():.3f} px")
    for lo, hi in [(80, 128), (128, 176), (176, 213)]:
        m = earth & (Y >= lo) & (Y < hi)
        print(f"   rows {lo:3d}-{hi:3d}: median speed {np.median(np.hypot(R[m, 0], R[m, 1])):.3f} px over {m.sum():2d} points")

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A pinhole camera maps (X, Y, Z) to (fX/Z, fY/Z): far things shrink, parallel lines meet at vanishing points, and depth is lost. When an object's depth range is small next to its distance, one scale s = f/Z0 will do.
  • Lenses gather far more light than a pinhole but focus only one distance sharply (1/Z + 1/z' = 1/f); the blur circle grows with the aperture, so depth of field shrinks as the lens opens.
  • Pixel brightness is light × reflectance × geometry: a Lambertian surface gives ρI0cos θ. Colour is three integrals of a spectrum, which is why metamers exist and why colour constancy needs a guess about the light.
  • Edges, texture and optical flow are all built from image gradients: smooth, differentiate, then thin and link (Canny); pool orientations over a window (texture); pool brightness-constancy constraints over a window (Lucas–Kanade), which fails along straight edges (the aperture problem).
  • Segmentation groups pixels by boundaries or by similarity: superpixels by k-means on colour and position, normalised cuts by an eigenvector. On real ISS frames, gradients alone measure the ground sliding by at about 1 to 2 px a second, faster where the ground is nearer.

Check your understanding

A 2 m person stands 10 m from a pinhole camera with f = 50 mm. How tall is their image? And a 4 m statue at 20 m?

fY/Z = 50 mm × 2/10 = 10 mm. The statue gives 50 × 4/20 = 10 mm too: the image keeps only the ratio Y/Z, so size and distance cannot be separated from one view.

Why does stopping a lens down from f/2 to f/8 deepen the depth of field, and what does it cost?

The aperture width A = f/N shrinks by 4, so every blur circle b = A|z' − zs|/z' shrinks by 4 and a wider range of distances stays under the acceptable blur. The light gathered falls with the aperture's area: by a factor of 16.

Why does every practical edge detector smooth before differentiating?

Differentiation amplifies high-frequency noise: neighbouring noisy pixels can differ as much as the two sides of a real edge. Smoothing with a Gaussian first (equivalently, convolving with the derivative of a Gaussian) suppresses the noise, at the cost of blurring fine detail; σ sets that trade-off.

A window contains one long straight edge. What can Lucas–Kanade tell you about its motion, and how does G show it?

Only the component across the edge. All gradients point the same way, so G has one large eigenvalue and one near zero; the brightness-constancy lines are all parallel and their intersection is undetermined along the edge. That is the aperture problem.

Why is the plain minimum cut a bad segmentation criterion, and how does Ncut fix it?

Cutting off a single weakly connected node costs very little, so the minimum cut favours tiny slivers. Ncut divides the cut by each side's total association with the graph; a sliver has a small association, so its Ncut is large, and balanced partitions win.
