Animal Learning

Classical and instrumental conditioning, the Rescorla–Wagner rule, and the TD model that turned a learning algorithm into a theory of what animals expect

Reinforcement learning borrowed its two central ideas from the psychology of animal learning: that an animal predicts what is about to happen to it, and that it repeats what has worked. This chapter runs the borrowing in reverse. The TD algorithm you already know, applied moment by moment inside a single conditioning trial, becomes a model of Pavlov’s dogs, Kamin’s blocked rats and Thorndike’s cats. The two experiments · 14.1

Prediction and control, in animals

Psychologists sort animal learning into two experimental traditions, and the sorting rule is exactly the one that separates the two halves of this part of the book. In classical (or Pavlovian) conditioning the animal learns that one event predicts another, and nothing it does changes whether the second event arrives. Pavlov’s dog hears a metronome and, a few seconds later, gets meat powder; it comes to salivate at the metronome, but the meat powder would have come whether it salivated or not. In instrumental (or operant) conditioning what the animal does determines what it gets. Thorndike’s cat is shut in a puzzle box with a fish outside; the door opens only when it pulls a loop of string, and over trials the loop gets pulled sooner and sooner.

Translate into the language of the previous chapters and the two traditions are prediction and control. Classical conditioning is policy evaluation: learn how much of something good or bad is coming, given what you currently see, with no action to choose. That is the job of TD(λ), and the stimulus the animal learns about plays the part of the state. Instrumental conditioning is policy improvement: make the actions that led to good outcomes more likely. That is the job of Sarsa, Q-learning and the actor in an actor–critic. The vocabulary collides in one place. In psychology reinforcement is any event that strengthens learning, and in classical conditioning the reinforcer (the food) arrives regardless of behaviour; in reinforcement learning the word belongs to the reward signal and to its effect on the policy. We will use US (unconditioned stimulus) for the psychologist’s reinforcer in a classical experiment and reward for the algorithm’s signal, and say explicitly when they coincide.

Why should a course on algorithms spend a chapter on rats and pigeons? Because the traffic has run both ways. The TD algorithm was not derived from dynamic programming and then found to resemble animal learning; Sutton and Barto arrived at it in the early 1980s while trying to build a mechanistic account of classical conditioning that could explain blocking and second-order conditioning at once, and only later connected it to Bellman’s equations. Eligibility traces came from Klopf’s neuron, not from the λ-return. The law of effect became the actor; secondary reinforcement became the critic. Reading the psychology, then, is reading the design notes of the algorithms you already know, and the places where the models fail the animals (there are several, listed at the end of section 6) are where the next ideas are likely to come from.

How can you tell, from an animal, which kind of learning you are looking at? With an omission schedule. When a lit key predicts grain, pigeons start pecking the key even though pecking is not required (Brown and Jenkins, 1968, called it autoshaping). Williams and Williams (1969) then made the peck cancel the grain: a trial on which the bird pecked ended with nothing. If the pecking were instrumental the bird would stop, since every peck costs a meal. The birds kept pecking, at a reduced rate, trial after trial. The peck is a Pavlovian response, driven by what the key predicts, not by what the peck produces. Classical conditioning · 14.2

Acquisition, extinction and the shape of the learning curve

Pavlov’s vocabulary first. Food in the mouth makes a dog salivate without any training: the food is the unconditioned stimulus (US) and salivation its unconditioned response (UR). A metronome does nothing until it has been followed by food a number of times; then the dog salivates to the metronome alone. The metronome has become a conditioned stimulus (CS) and the salivation it produces is a conditioned response (CR). Two arrangements of time matter. In delay conditioning the CS stays on until the US arrives; in trace conditioning it switches off first and the animal must bridge a gap. Either way the time from CS onset to US onset is the interstimulus interval, ISI.

The CR grows over trials along a curve that rises quickly and then flattens: negatively accelerated. Present the CS without the US and the CR fades again; that is extinction. Pavlov also noticed that a rested dog whose response had been extinguished the day before responds again the next morning (spontaneous recovery), which tells us that extinction is not simply the erasure of what was learned. Neither model in this chapter has anything to say about spontaneous recovery; it is one of the honest limits we will list.

The shape of the acquisition curve is the first clue to the mechanism. If each pairing simply added a fixed amount to the association, the curve would be a straight line. If instead each trial closes a fixed fraction of the gap between what the animal predicts and what it receives, the increments shrink as the prediction approaches the US and the curve bends over. That gap is an error, the US minus the prediction, and learning in proportion to it is the idea every model below shares. The simulator draws the trial-by-trial prediction of the two models you are about to meet, Rescorla–Wagner and TD, together with a simulated animal that responds on each trial with probability equal to its prediction. Fear · Estes & Skinner, 1941; Kamin, 1969

Taste aversion · Garcia & Koelling, 1966

Rats, flavour → illness hours later

One pairing can suffice, across delays of hours, and only flavours (not lights or sounds) condition to illness. A standing reminder that the stimuli an animal can associate, and over what delays, are prepared by its biology.

The unit of analysis

Trials, and moments within trials

Rescorla–Wagner speaks only of trials: how many, with which stimuli, with or without the US. The TD model speaks of moments: when each stimulus comes on and off, when the US arrives, and what the animal expects at each instant. Both are needed to read the preparations above.

\[ V_n = V_{n-1} + \alpha\,(\lambda - V_{n-1}) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_n = \lambda\,\big(1 - (1-\alpha)^n\big) \]
Read it as: Vn is the associative strength after n paired trials, λ the strength the US can support (its magnitude, in the psychologists’ notation; it is not the trace-decay λ of TD(λ)), and α a learning rate between 0 and 1. The gap λ − V shrinks by the factor (1 − α) every trial, so V approaches λ geometrically: the negatively accelerated curve. On an extinction trial λ = 0 and the same rule shrinks V by the same factor.

Compound stimuli · 14.2.1

Blocking, overshadowing and higher-order conditioning

Pairing is not enough. Leon Kamin’s 1969 experiment is the cleanest demonstration, and it changed the field. In phase one, rats heard a noise (call it A) followed by a mild shock until they feared the noise. In phase two the noise and a light (B) were presented together and again followed by shock. Then the light was tested alone. Rats that had been through phase one barely reacted to the light. Control rats, given only phase two, feared it strongly. The light had been paired with shock exactly as often in both groups; in the pre-trained group it learned almost nothing, because the shock was already predicted by the noise. There was no surprise left for the light to explain. This is blocking, and any theory in which learning depends only on CS–US contiguity fails it.

Overshadowing is the two-stimulus case without a first phase. Train A and B together from the start and each ends up with less strength than it would have earned alone; the more intense or salient of the two takes the larger share. Both effects say the same thing: stimuli presented together compete for a limited amount of learning, and what limits it is how much of the US is still unexplained.

Higher-order conditioning runs the other way. Once A reliably predicts food, present a new stimulus B followed by A, with no food at all. B becomes a CS too: this is second-order conditioning (Pavlov described it; Rizley and Rescorla, 1972, studied it carefully). B was never paired with the US. It was paired with a predictor of the US, which has become what reinforcement learning calls a secondary reinforcer: its value does the job of a reward. The effect is real but fragile. Because A is now being presented without food, A extinguishes, and B’s support goes with it; with many trials of A alone followed by food interleaved with AB followed by nothing, B turns into a conditioned inhibitor, a signal that the expected US will not come. The model · 14.2.1

The Rescorla–Wagner model

Robert Rescorla and Allan Wagner wrote Kamin’s lesson down as an equation in 1972, and it has been the reference point for every theory of conditioning since. Two assumptions. First, the change in a stimulus’s associative strength on a trial is proportional to the discrepancy between the US that occurred, λ, and the total prediction made by all the stimuli present on that trial, the sum of their strengths. Second, the constant of proportionality is a product of a salience αi belonging to the stimulus and a learning rate β belonging to the US. Every stimulus present is updated with the same discrepancy; that single word, total, is what makes stimuli compete.

Blocking falls out immediately. After phase one VA is close to λ, so on the first compound trial the discrepancy λ − (VA + VB) is already close to zero and B receives almost nothing. Overshadowing: two stimuli trained together share one λ between them, in proportion to their saliences. Conditioned inhibition: on an AB trial with no US the total VA + VB exceeds λ = 0, so the discrepancy is negative and B is pushed below zero. Extinction is the same rule with λ = 0. The model also made a new prediction, overexpectation: train A and B separately to predict the US, then present AB followed by the same US. The total prediction 2λ exceeds λ, so both stimuli should lose strength even though each is still being paired with the US. Rescorla confirmed it.

Rescorla and Wagner did not know it at the time, but their rule is the least-mean-square (LMS) rule of Widrow and Hoff (1960), the delta rule of neural networks, in a different notation: a linear prediction V = Σi Vixi over binary presence features xi, adjusted by stochastic gradient descent on the squared error (λ − V)². Sutton and Barto made the connection explicit in 1981. It means the model inherits everything known about LMS: it converges to the least-squares predictor of the US given the pattern of stimuli, and stimuli that are present together are credited by how much each one adds to the prediction, not by how often each one was paired. Work through blocking by hand below, then push the saliences around in the simulator.

\[ \Delta V_i = \alpha_i\,\beta\,\Big(\lambda - \sum_{j \in \text{trial}} V_j\Big) \qquad \text{for every stimulus } i \text{ present on the trial} \]
Read it as: λ is the strength the US on this trial can support (0 on a trial with no US); ΣjVj is the total prediction of all stimuli present, the model’s expectation of the US; the bracket is the surprise. αi (0 to 1) is how salient stimulus i is and β how fast this US teaches. Absent stimuli change nothing. With one stimulus and β = 1 this is the learning-curve rule of section 2.
Say it“Delta V-i equals alpha-i beta times lambda minus the sum of the V’s present.”

Why the arithmetic works. The rule never asks how often B was paired with the US; it asks how much of the US was still unexplained on each trial B was present. In the blocked group A had already explained 89% of it, so B competes for the remaining 11% and, with equal saliences, gets half of that. Change the saliences and the shares change: a very salient B (αB = 0.5) grabs more of the residue, but the residue is still 0.107, so blocking survives. Only a shorter first phase leaves B more to learn.

Rescorla and Wagner wrote the model with separate α and β; because they only ever appear as a product, the simulations here fold β into α. Psychologists usually also let β differ between US and no-US trials; we keep one value.

What it gets right

  • Acquisition and extinction curves
  • Blocking and overshadowing
  • Conditioned inhibition
  • Overexpectation (a new prediction, confirmed)
  • Contingency: a US that comes without the CS as often as with it produces no CR, because the context learns it

What it misses

  • Time inside the trial: no ISI, no CR timing
  • Second-order conditioning (it predicts inhibition instead)
  • Latent inhibition: pre-exposure to a CS alone slows later learning, but V = 0 either way
  • Spontaneous recovery after extinction
  • Trial order: the model does not care whether the US came before or after the CS

The TD model · 14.2.2

The TD model of classical conditioning

Two of Rescorla–Wagner’s failures share a root. It cannot speak about the interstimulus interval or about when in the trial the CR appears, because it treats a trial as a single step with no inside; and it cannot produce second-order conditioning, because the only teacher it knows is the US delivered on that trial. Sutton and Barto’s proposal (1981, then in its present form in 1987 and 1990) removes both limits with one move: compute the prediction and the error at every moment, and let the prediction at one moment be taught by the prediction at the next. That is temporal-difference learning, and when it is applied inside a conditioning trial it is called the TD model of classical conditioning.

Time runs in small steps t within a trial (think of a step as a few tens of milliseconds for an eyeblink, or a second for a hungry rat). At each step the animal’s situation is a feature vector xt describing which stimuli are on and for how long they have been on, and the prediction is linear in it, Vt = w·xt. What is it a prediction of? Not just the next US, but the discounted sum of all the US to come: a return. The error that drives learning is then the TD error δt: what arrived plus the discounted new prediction, minus the old prediction. Rescorla–Wagner’s surprise λ − ΣV is the special case in which the trial is one step and nothing is predicted beyond it. Credit for the error is spread backwards over recent features by an eligibility trace zt, so that a feature active a few steps before the US is still corrected when the US arrives.

\[ \begin{aligned} V_t &= \mathbf w_t^{\top}\mathbf x_t \;\approx\; \mathbb E\Big[\sum_{k\ge 0}\gamma^k\,R_{t+k+1}\Big] \\ \delta_t &= R_{t+1} + \gamma\, V_{t+1} - V_t \\ \mathbf z_t &= \gamma\lambda\,\mathbf z_{t-1} + \mathbf x_t \\ \mathbf w_{t+1} &= \mathbf w_t + \alpha\,\delta_t\,\mathbf z_t \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Rt+1 is the US arriving at step t+1 (its magnitude, 0 otherwise). Vt predicts the discounted US still to come; γ (here 0.95 per step) says how much a US one step later is worth. δt is the moment-to-moment surprise: positive when the US arrives unpredicted, or when a prediction rises unexpectedly, as when a well-trained CS comes on. zt is the eligibility of each feature, an exponentially fading memory of when it was active, decaying by γλ per step with λ the trace parameter (0.9 here). Features that were recently active are moved most. Between trials nothing is on, so V = 0 and the traces die away.
Say it“Delta is reward plus gamma times the next prediction minus this one; move every weight by alpha delta times its eligibility.”
\[ \text{one step per trial},\ \gamma = 0,\ \mathbf x = \text{presence}\ \Longrightarrow\ \delta = \lambda - \sum_{j\in\text{trial}} V_j,\quad \Delta V_i = \alpha\,\delta\,x_i \]
Read it as: with a single step per trial, no discounting and one feature per stimulus that equals 1 when it is present, the TD error is Rescorla–Wagner’s surprise and the update is Rescorla–Wagner’s rule with αiβ = α. The TD model is a generalisation, so it keeps acquisition, extinction, blocking, overshadowing, inhibition and overexpectation, and adds everything that comes from time and from bootstrapping. The chapter’s Python checks this: TD with γ = 0 and presence features reproduces the 0.2, 0.36, 0.488, 0.590 of the hand calculation exactly.

The one thing the equations leave open is the stimulus representation: what the feature vector xt looks like while a CS is on. Three choices are drawn below for a CS that comes on at step 5 and lasts 10 steps. The presence representation has one feature per CS, equal to 1 while it is on; it knows nothing about how long the CS has been on, so the prediction is flat across the CS. The complete serial compound (CSC) has a separate feature for every time step since onset, a perfect clock, so each moment of the CS can carry its own prediction. Microstimuli (Ludvig, Sutton and Kehoe, 2008, 2012) sit in between: the CS leaves a memory trace that decays after onset, and a bank of Gaussian receptive fields reads the trace, so early moments are represented by sharp, strong features and later moments by broad, weak ones. Time is represented, but with precision that falls with elapsed time, like the animal’s own sense of it. The memory trace outlives the stimulus, which lets microstimuli bridge the gap in trace conditioning.

  4. 4 · Trial 1 again, with traces (λ = 0.9)
    \( \mathbf z_2 = (0.81^2,\ 0.81,\ 1) \Rightarrow \mathbf w = (0.0656,\ 0.081,\ 0.1) \)
    how exactly?
    γλ = 0.81. The trace at step 2 remembers step 1 with weight 0.81 and step 0 with 0.81² = 0.6561. The single error δ2 = 1 is applied to all three: w0 = 0.1×0.6561 = 0.0656, w1 = 0.081, w2 = 0.1. After three trials the weights are (0.182, 0.222, 0.271): the whole CS has learned at once, and the profile already slopes up towards the US.

What the hand calculation shows. The last moment before the US learns first and most, directly from the US. Earlier moments learn from the moments after them, so the prediction grows into a ramp that rises towards the time the US is due, with slope set by γ: a step earlier is worth γ times less. The TD error itself migrates: on trial 1 it is all at the US; as training proceeds it shrinks there and appears at the earlier transitions, finally settling at the CS onset, where nothing predicts the CS. Hold that picture: in the next chapter it is the firing pattern of dopamine neurons.

Traces change the speed, not the destination. With λ = 0 the ramp takes as many trials as there are steps to form; with λ near 1 every eligible moment learns from the US at once, like a Monte Carlo update, and the discount still shapes the result because the trace decays by γλ per step. Simulations · 14.2.3

What Rescorla–Wagner cannot do: four simulations

The interstimulus interval. Conditioning depends sharply on the time between CS onset and US onset. In the rabbit’s nictitating-membrane preparation (Smith, Coleman and Gormezano, 1969) almost no CRs develop with an ISI of 50 ms, acquisition is fastest and strongest around 200 to 400 ms, and it falls away again towards one or two seconds; a US that comes before the CS teaches nothing. Rescorla–Wagner has no ISI to depend on. In the TD model the dependence comes from the discount and from the representation. With presence features a longer ISI means more steps on which the single feature is told “nothing yet” for each step on which it is told “US”, so its asymptote falls with ISI, but nothing stops it learning at the shortest ISIs. With the CSC the feature just before the US always learns the full US, whatever the ISI; what falls with ISI is how much, and how fast, the CS onset learns. With microstimuli the empirical shape appears: at the shortest ISI there is too little representation at the US time, at long ISIs the late microstimuli are weak and broad, and the strength after a fixed number of trials peaks in between. The first simulation sweeps the ISI for all three.

Second-order conditioning. After A has been paired with the US, run trials in which B comes on, then A, and no US ever arrives. At the moment A comes on, the prediction jumps from B’s small value to A’s large one. That jump is a positive TD error, and B’s features, still eligible, are strengthened by it: B learns from a predictor, exactly as the animal does. Rescorla–Wagner sees only λ = 0 on these trials and drives B negative. Both the model and the animal find the effect transient: with no US, A extinguishes, and the jump that taught B disappears.

The timing of the prediction. A rabbit trained with a 250 ms ISI closes its eyelid just before 250 ms; move the air puff to 500 ms and over trials the closure moves with it. The TD model with a temporal representation does the same: the ramp is built to peak at the expected US time, and if the US moves, the old peak is extinguished and a new one grows. The third simulation trains at one ISI and then shifts the US.

Temporal primacy overriding blocking. Kehoe, Schreurs and Graham (1987) trained rabbits with B followed by the US, then added A before B in a serial compound. Rescorla–Wagner predicts that B, already predicting the US, blocks A. The rabbits learned A strongly. In the TD model, when B comes on the prediction leaps to B’s value, and that leap is a positive TD error for A, which was on just before: A is learning that B is coming, and B is as good as the US. Instrumental conditioning · 14.3

Instrumental conditioning: the law of effect and shaping

Edward Thorndike’s cats (1898; the book is Animal Intelligence, 1911) were shut in boxes that opened when a loop was pulled or a lever pressed. The first escape took minutes of clawing and squeezing. Over repeated trials the time to escape fell, gradually, with no sudden moment of insight: the useless movements dropped out and the useful one came earlier. Thorndike’s summary is the Law of Effect: of the responses made in a situation, those followed by satisfaction become more firmly connected to the situation and more likely to recur; those followed by discomfort, less likely. Sutton and Barto point out that this packs two ideas together. Learning is selectional: alternatives are tried and the good ones kept, as in evolution. And it is associative: what is kept is tied to the situation in which it worked, so the result is a policy rather than a single habit. Search plus memory is the whole of reinforcement learning in one sentence from 1911.

B. F. Skinner (1938) replaced discrete trials with free responding in a chamber with a lever or a key, made the rate of responding the measure, and catalogued how different schedules of reinforcement shape it. He also found, in 1943, while trying to teach a pigeon to bowl, the technique that every animal trainer now uses (Peterson, 2004, tells the story). The pigeon would not swipe the ball, so he reinforced any movement towards it, then only closer ones, then only swipes; within minutes it was bowling. This is shaping by successive approximations: reinforce the best of what the animal currently does, move the criterion as the behaviour moves, and a response that would never have occurred spontaneously is built in stages. Reward design in robotics borrows it directly, and so does any curriculum that starts an agent on easy versions of a task.

The RL version of shaping goes by the same name and needs the same care. Reward shaping adds intermediate rewards to a sparse problem so that an agent is reinforced for approaching the goal, not only for reaching it; Ng, Harada and Russell (1999) showed that if the added reward is a difference of potentials, Φ(s′) − Φ(s), the optimal policy is unchanged, and that almost any other shaping can be gamed, with the agent circling through the intermediate rewards instead of finishing. Skinner’s pigeon can be gamed the same way: reinforce “closer to the ball” for too long and the bird learns to hover near the ball rather than to swipe it. A curriculum, in which the task itself is made easier and then harder, is the other half of shaping: moving the criterion rather than adding rewards.

Why does shaping work when waiting does not? The law of effect can only strengthen responses that occur. If the target is six standard deviations away from what the animal does now, the chance of seeing it on any trial is about one in a billion; no amount of patience will do. But behaviour varies from trial to trial, and reinforcing the top of the current distribution shifts the whole distribution upwards; extinction, meanwhile, increases variability, which widens the search. Each small criterion is reachable, and the sum of small steps is the impossible target. Run the simulator both ways: wait for the full response, or shape it.

Credit assignment · 14.4

Delayed reinforcement and the credit-assignment problem

Thorndike noticed that his law worked best when the satisfaction came right after the response, and Clark Hull (1932, 1943) made the observation a principle: the goal gradient, by which the strength of a response falls off with the time between it and the reinforcer. Grice (1948) measured it cleanly. Rats learned to choose the brighter of two alleys for food that arrived 0, 0.5, 1.2, 2, 5 or 10 seconds after the choice; learning slowed with every added second, and at 10 seconds it barely happened at all. The reason is not that rats forget in ten seconds. It is that in ten seconds a rat does a great many things, and the reward, when it comes, has to be credited to the right one. This is the credit-assignment problem, and Minsky (1961) named it as the central difficulty for any learning machine.

Psychology proposed two mechanisms, and TD(λ) contains both. One is secondary reinforcement: a stimulus that reliably intervenes between the response and the reward becomes a conditioned reinforcer and rewards the response immediately. Grice found that his rats tolerated the delay far better when a distinctive cue filled it. In TD terms the cue acquires value and the bootstrapped target γv̂(S′) carries the credit back one step per episode. The other is a trace: a fading record of what was just done persists for a while and can be strengthened when the reward arrives. Hull and Pavlov both spoke of stimulus traces; Klopf (1972, 1982) made the idea mechanistic by proposing that a synapse that has just contributed to firing stays eligible for modification for a short time, so that a later reinforcing signal can change it. That is the eligibility trace zt, and it carries credit back several steps within a single episode.

The two mechanisms are not rivals; TD(λ) is a dial between them. At λ = 0 all the credit travels by bootstrapping, one step per update, which is cheap and low-variance but slow over long delays. At λ = 1 the trace carries the whole return back to every step of the episode at once, which is Monte Carlo credit assignment: immediate, but noisy when the path to the reward is long and chancy. Animals appear to sit in between, and so does most practical RL. Hull’s goal gradient in this reading is not a single curve but a family: how steeply learning falls with delay depends on how far the trace reaches, and a cue that fills the gap flattens it by giving bootstrapping a stepping stone.

The simulator strips the problem to its bones. At the first step the learner chooses left or right. Then come d filler steps on which nothing happens, and then a reward of 1 if the choice was left and nothing if it was right. A Sarsa(λ) learner with γ = 1 runs 100 episodes for four delays. With λ = 0 the reward’s error reaches only the last filler step on the first rewarded episode, the step before it on the next, and the choice only after d + 1 lucky episodes: a goal gradient in trials. With λ close to 1 the trace of the choice is still (γλ)d when the reward arrives and the choice learns on the spot. Slide λ and watch the four curves pull together; the lower panel shows the trace that does the carrying.

  • Stimulus and response tracesHull and Pavlov: a decaying internal record of what was just perceived or done, available to be strengthened when the reinforcer arrives. In TD(λ) this is the eligibility trace zt, decaying by γλ per step. It solves credit assignment within an episode, for delays up to the trace’s reach.
  • Conditioned (secondary) reinforcementA stimulus that reliably precedes the reinforcer acquires reinforcing power of its own and rewards the response at once: the click of a clicker, the sound of the food hopper. In TD this is the bootstrapped term γv̂(S′); it solves credit assignment across episodes, one step per update, for any delay.
  • ChainingLong sequences are learned backwards from the goal: the last link first, because it is reinforced directly; then the link before it, reinforced by the now-valuable last link; and so on. This is the migration of the TD error from the reward to the start of the chain, and the order in which the Q-values in the simulation below become positive.

Two systems · 14.5

Habits and goals: model-free and model-based

A rat presses a lever for sugar pellets. Now devalue the pellets: feed the rat pellets until it is sated, or pair them with lithium chloride so that they make it ill. Then put it back with the lever, in extinction, so that no pellets are delivered and nothing it learns in the test can confound the result. Anthony Dickinson’s 1985 paper drew the line that organises this literature. Rats given moderate training press much less after devaluation: they know what the lever produces and they no longer want it. Their behaviour is goal-directed, defined by two criteria: it is sensitive to the current value of the outcome, and to whether the action still produces it. Rats given extended training (Adams, 1982) keep pressing at nearly the trained rate, as though the pellets were still worth having. Their behaviour has become a habit: triggered by the situation, blind to the outcome’s value until they experience it again.

Reinforcement learning has two kinds of control that map onto these with unusual precision (Daw, Niv and Dayan, 2005; Sutton and Barto, section 14.5). A model-based agent computes the value of pressing from a model of what pressing leads to and the current worth of that outcome; devalue the pellet and the computed value of pressing drops before the lever is ever touched again. A model-free agent stores the value of pressing directly, stamped in by past reward; it can only revise it by pressing and failing to get anything worth having, which the extinction test denies it by design. Why would an animal run both? The model-based system is flexible but costly, and its predictions are noisy when the model is uncertain or the chain of consequences long. The model-free system is cheap and, after enough experience, accurate. Daw and colleagues proposed that each system carries an estimate of its own uncertainty and the less uncertain one controls behaviour. Early in training the cached values are unreliable and the goal-directed system is in charge; after overtraining the cache is trustworthy and the habit takes over. That is Adams’s result, predicted rather than described.

import numpy as np

# ---------- Rescorla-Wagner: one update per trial ----------
def rescorla_wagner(phases, alpha=0.2, beta=1.0):
    """phases: list of (n_trials, stimuli, lam). Every stimulus present in a
    trial is updated with the same total prediction. Returns per-trial V."""
    V, hist = {}, []
    for n, stims, lam in phases:
        for _ in range(n):
            for s in stims:
                V.setdefault(s, 0.0)
            total = sum(V[s] for s in stims)
            for s in stims:
                V[s] += alpha * beta * (lam - total)
            hist.append(dict(V))
    return hist

# ---------- TD model: linear TD(lambda) within and across trials ----------
class TDModel:
    """rep = 'csc' (one feature per CS per time step, while the CS is on),
    'presence' (one feature per CS) or 'micro' (Gaussian microstimuli on a
    decaying memory trace that starts at CS onset and outlives the CS)."""
    def __init__(self, stimuli, T=40, rep='csc', alpha=0.1, gamma=0.95, lam=0.9,
                 m=10, sigma=0.1, decay=0.9):
        self.stimuli, self.T, self.rep = list(stimuli), T, rep
        self.alpha, self.gamma, self.lam = alpha, gamma, lam
        self.m, self.sigma, self.decay = m, sigma, decay
        self.k = {'csc': T, 'presence': 1, 'micro': m}[rep]
        self.w = np.zeros(len(self.stimuli) * self.k)

    def features(self, t, stims):
        x = np.zeros_like(self.w)
        for s, (on, dur) in stims.items():
            i, age = self.stimuli.index(s) * self.k, t - on
            if age < 0:
                continue
            if self.rep == 'csc' and age < dur:
                x[i + age] = 1.0
            elif self.rep == 'presence' and age < dur:
                x[i] = 1.0
            elif self.rep == 'micro':
                y = self.decay ** age
                c = (np.arange(self.m) + 1) / self.m
                x[i:i + self.m] = y * np.exp(-(y - c) ** 2 / (2 * self.sigma ** 2))
        return x

    def trial(self, stims, us_time=None, us=0.0, learn=True):
        """stims: {'A': (onset, duration)}; the US of size `us` arrives at us_time.
        Returns the prediction V(t) for t = 0..T-1 as the animal experienced it."""
        w, g, lam, a = self.w, self.gamma, self.lam, self.alpha
        z = np.zeros_like(w)
        x_prev = self.features(0, stims)
        V_prev = float(w @ x_prev)
        profile = [V_prev]
        for t in range(1, self.T + 1):
            x = self.features(t, stims) if t < self.T else np.zeros_like(w)
            V = float(w @ x)
            R = us if t == us_time else 0.0
            delta = R + g * V - V_prev          # TD error
            z = g * lam * z + x_prev            # accumulating eligibility trace
            if learn:
                w += a * delta * z
            x_prev, V_prev = x, float(w @ x)
            if t < self.T:
                profile.append(V)
        return np.array(profile)

    def probe(self, stims):
        """Present stimuli alone, no US, no learning: the conditioned response."""
        return self.trial(stims, None, 0.0, learn=False)

def peak(profile, until):
    return float(profile[:until].max())

def run(model, n, stims, us_time, us=1.0):
    return [peak(model.trial(stims, us_time, us), us_time) for _ in range(n)]

Wrap-up · 14.6

Five lines to remember

  • Classical conditioning is prediction and instrumental conditioning is control: the two experimental traditions of animal learning are the two halves of reinforcement learning, and an omission schedule tells you which one you are looking at.
  • Learning is driven by surprise, not pairing. Rescorla–Wagner’s ΔVi = αiβ(λ − ΣV) makes stimuli compete for a fixed amount of US to explain, which gives blocking, overshadowing, inhibition and overexpectation, and it is the LMS rule in disguise.
  • The TD model computes that surprise at every moment and bootstraps on its own next prediction: Rescorla–Wagner is its γ = 0, one-step special case. The extra machinery buys the ISI function, the timing of the CR at the expected US, second-order conditioning and temporal primacy; what it buys depends on the stimulus representation, with microstimuli the most faithful.
  • Thorndike’s law of effect is search plus memory, and shaping works because reinforcement can only select what occurs. Delayed reinforcement is the credit-assignment problem; secondary reinforcement (bootstrapping) and eligibility traces are the two mechanisms, and TD(λ) has both.
  • Goal-directed behaviour is model-based and habits are model-free. Outcome devaluation separates them, and an uncertainty-based arbitration between the two systems predicts that habits take over with extended training.

Check your understanding

In the omission experiment the Pavlovian learner settles at about half its usual peck rate. Why half, and not zero or one?

Its prediction V tracks the probability that grain follows the key, and it pecks with probability V. Under omission, grain comes only on trials without a peck, so the grain probability is 1 − V. The fixed point is V = 1 − V, so V = ½: it pecks on half the trials and loses half its grain. An instrumental learner, by contrast, credits the peck with the missing grain and stops.

Rescorla–Wagner predicts that B becomes a conditioned inhibitor in the second-order protocol, yet animals show excitation at first. What does the TD model change to get this right, and why is the effect transient in both animal and model?

TD replaces “the US on this trial” with “the US on this trial plus γ times the next prediction”. When A comes on after B, the prediction jumps to A’s value; that jump is a positive error for B’s eligible features, so B gains strength although no US arrives. The effect fades because the same trials present A without the US, so A extinguishes and the jump that taught B shrinks; with enough trials B’s own extinction updates win and it ends up inhibitory, which is also what Rescorla–Wagner says and what animals eventually show.

With CSC features the peak of the TD prediction reaches the full US size for every ISI, yet animals condition poorly at long ISIs. What is missing, and how do microstimuli supply it?

The CSC gives every time step since onset its own feature, so the step just before the US always learns the US directly and the clock is perfect at any delay. Real temporal representations lose precision with elapsed time. Microstimuli encode time on a decaying trace read by Gaussian receptive fields: late moments are represented by weak, broad features, so a US far from CS onset is predicted weakly and smeared in time, and at the shortest ISI too little representation has built up. The prediction after a fixed number of trials then rises and falls with ISI, as in the data.

Why does an eligibility trace let a response learn from a reward delivered d steps later within a single episode, while bootstrapping alone needs about d episodes?

Bootstrapping passes credit one step back per update: the last filler step learns on the first rewarded episode, the one before it on the next, and the choice only after the chain has been traversed. The trace of the choice is still (γλ)d when the reward arrives, so that fraction of the reward’s TD error reaches the choice immediately, in the same episode. Hull’s stimulus trace and Klopf’s eligibility are this idea; secondary reinforcement is the other.

A rat trained for 300 trials keeps pressing after the pellets have been devalued. In the model-based / model-free account, what has and has not changed in the animal’s head?

Its model has changed: it knows what the lever produces and that the pellet is now worthless, so the model-based value of pressing is low. Its cached, model-free value of pressing has not changed, because it was stamped in by past rewards and can only be revised by pressing and experiencing the new outcome, which extinction prevents. After extensive training the cache is the less uncertain system and controls behaviour, so the rat presses; with moderate training the model would have been in charge and it would not.

In what precise sense is Rescorla–Wagner a special case of the TD model?

Take one time step per trial, no discounting (γ = 0), and presence features. The TD error becomes R − V, with R the US magnitude λ and V the sum of the strengths of the stimuli present, and the update Δwi = αδxi moves every present stimulus by α(λ − ΣV): the Rescorla–Wagner rule with αiβ = α. The page’s Python checks that the two produce 0.2, 0.36, 0.488, 0.590 on four trials.
