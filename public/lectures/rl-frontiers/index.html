RL Frontiers

Options, general value functions, reward design, inverse RL and safe exploration: what an agent can ask, how it can act in chunks, and where its goal comes from

Every earlier chapter took three things for granted: the agent predicts one signal (reward), acts one step at a time, and someone has already written down the reward. This chapter loosens all three. General value functions let an agent predict any signal it can sense, at any time scale, about any way of behaving. Options let it act in extended chunks and plan over them. Reward design, shaping and inverse RL ask where the reward comes from and how to tell whether the one you wrote says what you meant. Safe exploration asks how to learn without breaking things on the way. The figure is the classic four-rooms world: switch the explorer from single steps to hallway options and watch how much of the building the same number of steps covers.

Predictive knowledge · 17.1

General value functions: one stream, many questions

A value function answers one question: if I follow π from here, how much reward will I collect, discounted by γ? Look at the three ingredients of that sentence. The thing being summed is the reward. The weighting over time is a constant discount. The behaviour assumed is the policy being learned. None of the three is forced on us by the mathematics of TD learning. The TD update only cares that the quantity it predicts obeys a Bellman-style recursion: what I get now, plus a discounted version of what I predict next. Any signal the agent can sense obeys that recursion. So does any time scale. So does any hypothetical way of behaving.

A general value function (GVF) makes the three ingredients explicit parameters. The signal to be summed is called the cumulant Ct; it can be reward, but also a light-sensor reading, the current drawn by a motor, a bump, a pixel, or a “1” that simply counts time. The discount becomes a state-dependent continuation function γ(s), so that a prediction can run until some event happens (γ drops to 0 at the event) rather than fading at a fixed rate. The policy becomes any target policy π of interest, whether or not the agent is actually following it. A GVF is therefore a question: if I behaved according to π from this state, what would the γ-weighted sum of C be? The answer, once learned, is a piece of knowledge about the world that is grounded in the agent’s own sensors and can be checked against experience, which is exactly what makes it worth learning.

\[ v_{\pi,\gamma,C}(s) \doteq \mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{k=t}^{\infty} \Big(\prod_{i=t+1}^{k} \gamma(S_i)\Big) C_{k+1} \;\middle|\; S_t = s,\ A_{t:\infty}\sim\pi \right] \]
Read it as: start in s, behave according to π, and add up the cumulants Ct+1, Ct+2, … where each one is weighted by the product of the continuation values of the states passed through on the way (the empty product for k = t is 1). With C = R and γ constant this is the ordinary vπ. With C = 1 and γ = 1 except at some event, where it is 0, it is the expected number of steps until the event. The subscript (π, γ, C) is the question; the function is the answer.
Say it“A GVF is the expected discounted sum of a cumulant under a target policy, where the discount can change from state to state.”
\[ \delta_t = C_{t+1} + \gamma(S_{t+1})\,\hat v(S_{t+1}) - \hat v(S_t), \qquad \hat v(S_t) \leftarrow \hat v(S_t) + \alpha\,\delta_t \]
Read it as: the TD(0) update for a GVF is the ordinary one with the reward replaced by the cumulant and the discount evaluated at the next state. The same stream of states feeds every GVF the agent is learning; each one just reads a different cumulant and continuation out of it. Prediction is cheap, which is why an agent can afford to ask many questions at once.
Say it“TD error equals cumulant plus continuation times next prediction minus current prediction.”

Its sensors see three things: a lamp whose brightness peaks at cell 12 and falls off like a bell, a carpet covering cells 24 to 31, and three doors at cells 5, 20 and 33. Questions an agent might ask

  • How far to the wall?Cumulant 1; γ = 0 when the bump sensor fires, 1 otherwise; target policy “keep driving forward”. The answer is a distance measured in the robot’s own steps, learned without a ruler.
  • Will this light stay on?Cumulant: the light sensor; γ = 0.9; target policy: “stop”. A prediction about the world rather than the agent; useful as a feature for anything that depends on the light.
  • How much battery will this cost?Cumulant: motor current; γ = 0 at the end of the manoeuvre; target policy: the manoeuvre itself. Predicting the cost of a behaviour is the first step towards choosing between behaviours.
  • How long until I am picked up?Cumulant 1; γ = 0 at the pick-up event. One of the long-horizon predictions Horde demons learned on a real robot; the answer is a time-to-event in seconds.
  • What is my value?Cumulant: reward; γ constant; target policy: the one being learned. The ordinary value function is the GVF the rest of the book is about; here it is one demon among many.

Three things are worth noticing. First, the two lamp demons learn from identical data yet end up with different shapes: the short-horizon one is a slightly smeared copy of the lamp, the long-horizon one is broad and shifted left of the lamp, because a robot that drifts rightwards from cell 5 will spend the next thirty steps passing through the bright region. A prediction is always about a way of behaving. Second, the door demon’s answer is a saw-tooth that resets to 0 at each door: the continuation function made a time-to-event question out of the same machinery. Third, nothing here needed a reward. The robot has no task; it is simply building a sensorimotor description of its corridor, and that description is what predictive knowledge means: knowledge stated as verifiable predictions about the agent’s own experience.

Parallel off-policy learning · 17.1

Horde and auxiliary tasks

The demons above asked about the policy the robot was actually following. The interesting questions are usually about policies it is not following: “if I kept going right, how far is the next door?”, “if I stopped here, would the light stay on?” Those are off-policy questions, and the robot cannot follow all of the target policies at once. It follows one behaviour policy and must learn about the others from the resulting data, which is the problem of the previous two chapters. In the tabular case the fix is the importance-sampling ratio ρt = π(At|St)/b(At|St): multiply the TD update by it, so that actions the target policy would not have taken teach nothing and actions it favours teach more. With function approximation the same idea needs Gradient-TD or Emphatic-TD to stay stable.

The Horde architecture (Sutton, Modayil, Delp, Degris, Pilarski, White and Precup, 2011) built exactly this on a real robot. One behaviour policy drove a small wheeled robot around; thousands of demons, each a GVF with its own cumulant, continuation and target policy, updated in parallel from the shared stream of sensor readings, using GTD(λ) with tile-coded features, in real time. Questions included how long various sensor readings would persist, whether a bump was coming, how much the motors would heat up, all under hypothetical policies the robot was not executing. The point was not any single prediction but the demonstration that an agent can maintain a large body of grounded, continually-checked knowledge about its world at no more cost than one pass of TD per demon per step, and can do so about behaviours it is not currently engaged in.

\[ \rho_t = \frac{\pi(A_t \mid S_t)}{b(A_t \mid S_t)}, \qquad \hat v(S_t) \leftarrow \hat v(S_t) + \alpha\,\rho_t\,\big(C_{t+1} + \gamma(S_{t+1})\hat v(S_{t+1}) - \hat v(S_t)\big) \]
Read it as: the off-policy TD(0) update for a demon whose target policy is π. With a deterministic target (“always go right”) and a behaviour that goes right half the time, ρ is 2 when the robot happened to go right and 0 when it went left: the demon learns from exactly the steps that agree with its hypothetical policy, and compensates for seeing only half of them. For the on-policy demon, ρ = 1 always.
Say it“Rho is pi over b of the action taken; it scales the cumulant-based TD update.”

Auxiliary tasks: predictions as a scaffold for representation

Why would an agent that only cares about reward bother predicting the light sensor? Two reasons. The first is that GVF answers make good features: “steps to the next door going right” is a far more useful input to a value function than raw sensor readings, and a layer of such predictions is a learned, grounded state representation. The second is that in deep RL the predictions are not even used directly; they are trained as auxiliary tasks that share a network with the main value function, so that the gradients from many cheap, dense prediction problems shape the hidden layers that the sparse reward signal alone would shape slowly. The UNREAL agent (Jaderberg et al., 2017) added pixel-change control, reward prediction and value-function replay to an actor–critic learner and roughly doubled its performance and sample efficiency on 3-D navigation tasks; the Horde view says those auxiliary heads are GVFs with particular cumulants. Choosing which questions to ask, so that the answers are useful for the task the agent is finally judged on, is one of the open problems Sutton and Barto list for the field.

Cumulant

Anything sensed

Reward, a sensor reading, a feature of the state, an indicator of an event, the change in a group of pixels, even another GVF’s prediction. The cumulant turns a signal into a question.

Continuation γ(s)

Any time scale

Constant: an exponentially weighted horizon. Zero at an event: a prediction that runs until the event. Near 1 almost everywhere: a long-run forecast. Different demons can share a stream with different horizons.

Target policy π

Any way of behaving

The behaviour itself (on-policy, ρ = 1), a fixed hypothetical (“keep going right”), or a policy that is itself being learned. Off-policy targets are what make a Horde knowledgeable about more than it does.

Temporal abstraction · 17.2

Options: closed-loop behaviours that take their time

People do not plan a journey in muscle twitches. “Walk to the door, take the lift down, cross the road” is a plan over actions that each take many primitive steps and end when something is achieved. Reinforcement learning on primitive actions alone has no such vocabulary: every decision is one tick of the clock, every plan has to be rediscovered a step at a time, and the deeper the goal the harder credit is to assign. Options (Sutton, Precup and Singh, 1999) give an agent that vocabulary without leaving the MDP framework. An option is a closed-loop behaviour: it observes the state while it runs and stops when the state says so, which is what distinguishes it from a fixed macro-action (a scripted sequence that cannot react).

Formally an option ω is a triple. The initiation set I ⊆ S lists the states in which the option may be started. The internal policy πω(a | s) chooses primitive actions while it runs. The termination function βω(s) ∈ [0, 1] gives the probability that the option stops on arrival in s. A primitive action is the special case that may start anywhere, always takes itself, and terminates with probability 1 after one step, so a set of options can contain the primitives and nothing is lost. A policy over options μ(ω | s) then chooses which option to launch whenever the current one terminates; between launches the agent is committed. The result is a semi-Markov decision process: the time between decisions is random, but the state at a decision point still summarises everything that matters.

\[ \omega = \langle I_\omega,\ \pi_\omega,\ \beta_\omega\rangle, \qquad \text{available in } s \iff s \in I_\omega, \qquad \Pr(\text{stop on arriving in } s') = \beta_\omega(s') \]
Read it as: where it may begin, what it does, when it ends. In the four-rooms world each hallway option can be started anywhere in its room (plus the room’s other doorway), follows the shortest route to its target doorway, and terminates with probability 1 on reaching it or on being knocked out of the room; inside the room β = 0.
Say it“An option is an initiation set, a policy and a termination function.”

Why the abstraction pays

The hero figure already showed the first benefit: exploration. A random walk over primitives dithers inside a room for hundreds of steps; a random walk over hallway options crosses the building, because each decision commits the agent to a purposeful stretch of behaviour. Options are to exploration what long strides are to walking. The second benefit is credit assignment: a reward obtained after taking an option is credited to the decision to take it, however many primitive steps that was ago, so values propagate back through decisions rather than through ticks of the clock. The third, which the next section is about, is planning: an option has a model, in exactly the sense a primitive action has one, and the Bellman equations go through unchanged with options in place of actions. The price is that options have to come from somewhere. Here they are hand-designed; discovering them is a research area of its own.

Where options come from

Hand-designed

Sub-goals you can name

Doorways, charging stations, “pick up the object”. The option’s policy is learned or planned for the sub-goal (a GVF-style pseudo-reward), its termination is the sub-goal itself. The hallway options are this. Cheap and reliable when a designer knows the task’s structure.

Bottlenecks

States many paths cross

Doorways can be found rather than named: cells visited by many successful trajectories but few failed ones, or vertices whose removal disconnects the state graph (graph cuts, betweenness). Each bottleneck becomes a sub-goal and an option is learned to reach it.

McGovern & Barto (2001); Şimşek & Barto (2004)
Geometry

Eigenoptions

The eigenvectors of the state graph’s Laplacian are smooth functions that vary slowly within rooms and change sign across doorways. Treating each one as a pseudo-reward gives options that traverse the environment along its principal directions, without any reward at all.

Machado, Bellemare & Bowling (2017)
End to end

Option-critic

Hierarchical RL · 17.2

Planning and learning with options

A primitive action a has a model: the expected reward r(s, a) and the transition probabilities p(s' | s, a). An option has a model of the same shape, except that the transition part must account for the option taking a variable number of steps. The reward model r(s, ω) is the expected discounted reward collected while ω runs from s until it terminates. The transition model p(s' | s, ω) is the probability of ending in s', discounted by γk for a run of k steps, summed over all k. Folding the discount into the model is what makes the Bellman equation look exactly as it always did: the value of launching ω in s is the reward model plus the model-weighted value of where you end up. Because the model’s rows sum to E[γk] rather than to 1, a long option automatically counts for less.

\[ r(s,\omega) = \mathbb E\big[R_1 + \gamma R_2 + \cdots + \gamma^{k-1} R_k\big], \qquad p(s'\mid s,\omega) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \gamma^{k}\,\Pr(\text{terminate in } s' \text{ after } k \text{ steps}) \]
Read it as: the two halves of an option’s model. Both can be computed by a Bellman-like recursion over the option’s own policy: r(s, ω) = Σaπω(a|s)Σs''p(s''|s,a)[r + (1 − β(s''))γ r(s'', ω)], and likewise for p, which is how the page computes them. They can also be learned from experience by TD methods (intra-option model learning).
Say it“The option’s transition model is the discounted probability of where it ends; the reward model is the discounted reward on the way.”
\[ v_{\mu}(s) = \sum_{\omega \in \mathcal O(s)} \mu(\omega\mid s)\Big[r(s,\omega) + \sum_{s'} p(s'\mid s,\omega)\, v_{\mu}(s')\Big], \qquad v^*_{\mathcal O}(s) = \max_{\omega\in\mathcal O(s)}\Big[r(s,\omega) + \sum_{s'} p(s'\mid s,\omega)\, v^*_{\mathcal O}(s')\Big] \]
Read it as: the Bellman equations for a policy over options and for the optimal policy over a given option set O. There is no γ in front of the sum because it is already inside p. Value iteration with these equations is SMDP value iteration. If O contains the primitives, v*O equals the ordinary v*; if it contains only extended options, it is the best that can be done using those options alone, which may be worse.
Say it“Value equals the option’s reward model plus the model-weighted value of the terminating state, maximised over the options available.”

The payoff is in how fast values spread. With primitive actions, one sweep of value iteration pushes information one cell. With hallway options, one sweep pushes it across a whole room. Learning at both levels at once

Everything above assumed the options and their models were known. They can be learned. SMDP Q-learning treats each option execution as one big step: on termination after k steps with discounted return G, update Q(s, ω) towards G + γk maxω' Q(s', ω'). It is simple but wasteful, because an option that ran twenty steps produced twenty transitions and only one update. Intra-option learning uses every step: for each option whose policy would have taken the action just taken, update that option’s value towards r + γ[(1 − β(s'))Q(s', ω) + β(s') maxω'Q(s', ω')], continuing if the option would continue and re-deciding if it would stop. Many options learn from one trajectory, off-policy, which is Horde’s trick again at the level of behaviour. The same idea learns option models from single steps. And the options themselves can be learned: MAXQ (Dietterich, 2000) decomposes a task into a hierarchy of sub-tasks with their own value functions; the option-critic (Bacon, Harb and Precup, 2017) differentiates the SMDP return with respect to the internal policies and termination functions and learns both by gradient ascent, so that the option set itself is shaped by the reward. Hierarchical RL is the general name for all of this: decisions at more than one time scale, each level treating the one below as its primitive.

Reward design · 17.4

The reward hypothesis and the art of saying what you want

Everything in this book runs on one assumption, which Sutton calls the reward hypothesis: all of what we mean by goals and purposes can be well thought of as the maximisation of the expected value of the cumulative sum of a received scalar signal. It is a strong claim (Silver, Singh, Precup and Sutton argued in 2021 that reward alone is enough to account for intelligence; others doubt that a scalar can carry everything we care about), but as an engineering stance it has a direct consequence: the reward is the specification. The agent will do whatever maximises it. Not what you meant, not what a reasonable person would infer, but what the number says. Reward design is the discipline of writing that number so that the two coincide.

The first rule is that reward should say what to achieve, not how. A chess agent rewarded for winning will learn to take pieces when that helps; an agent rewarded for taking pieces may learn to trade down into lost endgames. Rewarding sub-goals is tempting because it is dense and informative, and it is dangerous for exactly that reason: the agent can satisfy the sub-goal without the goal. The second rule is that every term interacts with every other. A per-step cost is not a detail: with a positive “living reward” a finite task becomes one the agent would rather never finish; with a very negative one the agent will take any exit, including a bad one. The discount sets how far ahead a reward can be seen, so a sparse goal behind a long corridor can be invisible at γ = 0.9 and obvious at 0.99. And every one of these choices is checked by the same test: solve the MDP and look at the policy.

\[ \pi^* = \arg\max_\pi\ \mathbb E_\pi\Big[\sum_{t} \gamma^t R_{t+1}\Big] \quad\text{with}\quad R_{t+1} = r_{\text{step}} + r_{\text{goal}}\,\mathbb 1[S_{t+1}=G] + r_{\text{pit}}\,\mathbb 1[S_{t+1}=P] \]
Read it as: the agent maximises the discounted sum of a reward that is the sum of a few designed terms: a per-step living reward rstep (usually negative), a bonus for reaching the goal and a penalty for falling in the pit, both of which end the episode. The policy that comes out depends on the ratios between these and on γ; scaling all of them by the same positive constant changes nothing, adding a constant to all of them (which is what rstep does) changes a great deal in an episodic task.
Say it“The optimal policy maximises expected discounted return; the return is whatever the reward terms add up to.”

How rewards go wrong in practice

Proxy ≠ goal

Reward hacking

The agent finds a way to score that does not deliver what the designer wanted: a boat-racing agent that circles a lagoon collecting respawning power-ups instead of finishing the race, a simulated robot that learns to appear to grasp an object by placing its hand between the object and the camera. The proxy was easier to maximise than the goal it stood for.

Amodei et al. (2016) §3; Krakovna et al. (2020) list dozens
Dense but wrong

Sub-goal farming

Rewarding intermediate achievements (distance covered, pieces taken, a checkpoint visited) lets the agent collect them repeatedly without ever finishing. The cure is a reward for the outcome and, if dense guidance is needed, shaping of a form that cannot change the optimum (next section).

Ng, Harada & Russell (1999) opened with exactly this failure
Missing terms

Negative side effects

A reward that mentions only the task is silent about everything else, so the agent is indifferent to knocking over the vase on the way. One cannot enumerate every vase; proposals include penalising deviation from a baseline state, or learning what people care about from their behaviour (the IRL section).

Amodei et al. (2016) §2
Scale and horizon

Invisible goals

A sparse +1 twenty steps away is worth 0.920 ≈ 0.12 at the start under γ = 0.9 and is never found by random exploration. Raising γ, adding a per-step cost, shaping, or options (longer strides) are the standard remedies; each changes the problem in a way that should be checked.

Reward shaping · 17.4

Shaping that cannot hurt: potential-based rewards

Sparse rewards are honest but slow. Shaping, a term borrowed from animal training, adds extra reward along the way to guide the learner towards the goal. The question is which extra rewards are safe. Ng, Harada and Russell (1999) answered it exactly. Write the shaping term as a function F(s, a, s') added to the true reward, so that the agent learns in a modified MDP M' with reward R + F. Then the optimal policies of M' coincide with those of M for every choice of true reward and transition function if and only if F is a potential-based shaping function: the discounted difference of a real-valued potential Φ over states.

\[ F(s,a,s') = \gamma\,\Phi(s') - \Phi(s) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad Q'^*(s,a) = Q^*(s,a) - \Phi(s),\quad V'^*(s) = V^*(s) - \Phi(s) \]
Read it as: if every shaping reward is “γ times the potential where you arrive, minus the potential where you were”, then the shaped action values are the original ones shifted by a quantity that depends only on the state, not on the action. A shift that is the same for all actions in a state cannot change which action is best there, so the greedy policy is unchanged, and so is every optimal policy. (For episodic tasks, take Φ = 0 at the terminal state so that all episodes are shifted alike.) Conversely, if F is not of this form there exists some MDP in which it changes the optimal policy, which is what the next figure demonstrates.
Say it“Potential-based shaping subtracts the potential of the current state from every action value, and nothing else.”
why exactly does the policy survive?

Take any trajectory s0, s1, s2, … and add up its shaping rewards: γ0[γΦ(s1) − Φ(s0)] + γ1[γΦ(s2) − Φ(s1)] + γ2[γΦ(s3) − Φ(s2)] + … Every Φ(sk) for k ≥ 1 appears twice, once as +γkΦ(sk) and once as −γkΦ(sk), and the pair cancels. The sum telescopes to −Φ(s0) (plus a tail γTΦ(sT) that is zero at a terminal state with Φ = 0, or vanishes as T → ∞).

So the shaped return of every trajectory from s is the original return minus the same constant Φ(s). Expectations preserve that: Q'π(s, a) = Qπ(s, a) − Φ(s) for every policy π, hence also for the optimal one. Since the shift does not depend on a, argmaxa Q'*(s, a) = argmaxa Q*(s, a).

Wiewiora (2003) showed the same thing from another angle: Q-learning with potential-based shaping behaves exactly like Q-learning without shaping but with the table initialised at Q0(s, a) = Φ(s). Shaping is advice about where values are high, and the ideal advice is Φ = V*, which makes Q'*(s, a*) = 0 for optimal actions: a zero-initialised table starts out already right about the best moves and only has to learn that the others are worse.

Learning is dramatically faster at k = 1 because the shaped values of optimal actions are already zero, and even a badly scaled k (0.3, or 2) leaves the optimum intact while helping less. With the plain bonus, b = 0.05 looks harmless: the optimum is unchanged and learning is a little faster. At b = 0.1 the first red cells appear next to the goal, and by 0.15 the optimal policy of the shaped problem walks back and forth between two warm cells for ever, since b/(1 − γ) = 10b per step of shuttling beats a one-off +1. The learning curve shows the same thing from the agent’s side: it finds the goal quickly at first, then learns not to. A bonus is not wrong because it is dense; it is wrong because it is not a difference of potentials, and so it adds a different amount to different trajectories.

Apprenticeship & inverse RL

Inverse reinforcement learning: reading the reward off the behaviour

If writing a reward is this error-prone, perhaps the reward should be learned. People are often poor at stating what they want and good at demonstrating it. Inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) turns the usual problem round: given an MDP without its reward function and a set of demonstrations from an expert, find a reward under which the expert’s behaviour is (near-)optimal. Once the reward is in hand the forward problem can be solved as usual, and the result generalises in a way that copying the demonstrated actions (behavioural cloning) does not: a reward is a compact, transferable description of why, while a copied policy only says what, and only in the states that were visited.

Ng and Russell (2000) posed the problem and immediately hit its central difficulty: it is ill-posed. Many rewards explain the same behaviour; the all-zero reward explains every behaviour, since every policy is optimal for it. Abbeel and Ng’s apprenticeship learning (2004) sidestepped the ambiguity by asking for a policy, not a reward: assume the reward is linear in known features, r = θ·φ(s), note that the expected return of any policy is then θ·μ(π) where μ is the policy’s discounted feature expectations, and find a policy whose feature expectations match the expert’s; it is then guaranteed to do as well as the expert for whatever the true θ is. Ziebart, Maas, Bagnell and Dey (2008) resolved the ambiguity about the reward itself with the principle of maximum entropy: among all distributions over trajectories that match the expert’s feature expectations, choose the one with the highest entropy, which commits to nothing the data do not support. The result is a clean probabilistic model in which the expert prefers higher-reward paths exponentially but is allowed to be imperfect.

\[ P(\tau\mid\theta) = \frac{1}{Z(\theta)}\exp\!\Big(\sum_{s_t\in\tau}\theta^{\!\top}\phi(s_t)\Big), \qquad \nabla_\theta \log P(\mathcal D\mid\theta) = \tilde f - \sum_{s} D_\theta(s)\,\phi(s) \]
Read it as: the probability of a trajectory is proportional to the exponential of its total reward (for deterministic dynamics; with stochastic dynamics the transition probabilities multiply in). Maximising the likelihood of the demonstrations gives a gradient that is simply the empirical feature counts of the demonstrations, f̃, minus the expected feature counts under the current model, where Dθ(s) is the expected number of visits to s. At the optimum the two match, which is Abbeel and Ng’s matching condition, now with the ambiguity resolved. Computing Dθ is the one piece of work: a backward pass that gives the soft-optimal policy for θ, and a forward pass that propagates the start distribution through it.
Say it“The gradient is what the expert visited minus what the model would visit; raise the reward where the expert went more than the model does.”
\[ \text{backward: } V_T(s)=0,\quad Q_t(s,a) = \theta^{\!\top}\phi(s) + \sum_{s'}p(s'|s,a)V_{t+1}(s'),\quad V_t(s) = \log\sum_a e^{Q_t(s,a)},\quad \pi_t(a|s) = e^{Q_t(s,a)-V_t(s)} \]
Read it as: value iteration with the maximum replaced by a soft maximum (log-sum-exp) over a finite horizon T. The resulting policy prefers higher-Q actions exponentially instead of picking only the best: that is the maximum-entropy model of a slightly imperfect expert. The forward pass then starts with D0 at the start state and repeatedly applies πt and the dynamics to get the expected visits Dt(s) for each step, which are summed over t.

The inverse-RL family

Feature matching

Apprenticeship learning

Assume r = θ·φ(s), estimate the expert’s feature expectations μE from demonstrations, and find a policy (by a max-margin or projection step followed by an RL solve) whose μ is within ε of μE. Guarantees near-expert return for any θ of bounded norm; returns a policy, not a reward.

Abbeel & Ng (2004)
Probabilistic

Maximum-entropy IRL

Trajectories are exponentially more likely the more reward they collect; match feature counts by gradient ascent on the likelihood. Handles imperfect demonstrators and stochastic dynamics, and gives a unique reward up to a constant. The method on this page.

Ziebart et al. (2008)
Bayesian

Posterior over rewards

Put a prior on θ, a Boltzmann likelihood on the demonstrated actions, and sample the posterior by MCMC. Instead of one reward you get a distribution, which tells you where the data leave the reward undetermined, exactly the ambiguity IRL suffers from.

Ramachandran & Amir (2007)
Adversarial

GAIL and AIRL

Train a discriminator to tell expert state–action pairs from the learner’s, and use its output as the learner’s reward. GAIL imitates without ever naming a reward; AIRL constrains the discriminator so that a transferable reward can be read off it. Both scale to high-dimensional continuous control.

Ho & Ermon (2016); Fu, Luo & Levine (2018)
From comparisons

Preference learning

When demonstrating is hard, people can still say which of two clips is better. A reward model is fitted to those comparisons (Bradley–Terry) and the agent is trained on it; the same recipe, applied to language models, is the reward model in RLHF.

Christiano et al. (2017)

What IRL can and cannot tell you

It can

  • Recover a reward that explains the demonstrations and transfers to new starts, new dynamics, or new horizons, because it describes the goal rather than the route.
  • Report what the demonstrator avoided, by contrasting their paths with those the model would take: a negative reward appears where the shortest path goes and the expert does not.
  • Serve as an audit: feed an agent’s own behaviour back through IRL and read off what reward it is effectively pursuing (the project does exactly this).

It cannot

  • Fix an additive constant (and, with potential-based terms, a potential): any reward plus a constant explains the data equally well, so only differences are meaningful.
  • Say anything about states the expert never visits and the model never expects to: there the gradient is zero and the reward stays at its initial value.
  • Distinguish an expert’s preferences from their limitations: a demonstrator who could not see the shortcut looks like one who did not want it. Later work (Bayesian IRL, adversarial IRL, preference learning from comparisons) addresses parts of this.
Safe exploration · constrained RL

Safe exploration and constrained RL

Trial and error is how reinforcement learning works, and some errors are not acceptable: a robot that learns where the stairs are by falling down them, a recommender that tests the limits of a user’s patience, a chemical plant that explores pressure settings. Amodei, Olah, Steinhardt, Christiano, Schulman and Mané (2016) listed safe exploration as one of five concrete problems in AI safety, alongside avoiding negative side effects, avoiding reward hacking, scalable oversight and robustness to distributional shift. Three of those five are about the reward (this chapter’s preceding sections); safe exploration is about the path to a good policy, not only the destination. A learner can end up with a perfectly safe policy having visited every hazard a thousand times on the way.

The standard formalisation is the constrained MDP (Altman, 1999): alongside the reward there is a cost signal c(s, a, s'), and the task is to maximise return subject to the expected cumulative cost staying below a budget d. The Lagrangian of that problem is an ordinary MDP whose reward is r − λc; for each multiplier λ one can solve it, measure the resulting cost, and adjust λ until the constraint is just met. Constrained Policy Optimisation (Achiam, Held, Tamar and Abbeel, 2017) does the same thing with policy gradients and a trust region so that each update is guaranteed, to first order, not to violate the constraint. Beyond expectations, risk-sensitive criteria (variance, CVaR) bound the bad tail rather than the average; beyond the final policy, shields or known-safe baseline policies restrict exploration itself so that the learner is never allowed to take an action known to be unsafe, which is the only way to protect the learning process rather than its result.

\[ \max_\pi\ J_r(\pi)\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ J_c(\pi) \le d \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \min_{\lambda\ge 0}\ \max_\pi\ \big[J_r(\pi) - \lambda\,(J_c(\pi) - d)\big] \]
Read it as: Jr is the expected discounted return and Jc the expected discounted cost from the start. The multiplier λ is the price of a unit of cost. For a fixed λ the inner problem is an MDP with reward r − λc, solvable by value iteration; if its policy spends more than d, raise λ, if less, lower it. The λ where the constraint is exactly met is the shadow price of safety, and the return given up relative to λ = 0 is what safety costs in this environment. (At the exact budget the constrained optimum may need to randomise between two deterministic policies; the demo shows the cheapest deterministic policy that fits.)

How to read the audit. The recovered reward is only defined up to a constant and is blank wherever neither the agent nor the model ever goes, so look at contrasts. A healthy design shows the goal as the clear maximum. Do not expect the mud to come out negative when the agent’s chosen route runs through it: IRL measures reward relative to the alternatives the agent passed up, and here it passed up the lava-side route, so the two mud cells it walks through look like part of a good road, while the lava stays low. The correlation with your designed reward is modest for the same reason, and because cells nobody visits carry no information. If you add the stone bonus and raise it past the point where shuttling pays (roughly when b exceeds the step cost by more than (1 − γ) times the value of finishing), the trained agent’s rollouts start to loop next to the goal, the behaviour report shows timeouts instead of successes, and the audit puts its highest recovered reward on the stones, not on the goal: the specification said “deliver”, the revealed objective says “collect stones”. Add the potential-based term instead and nothing in the audit should change beyond noise, because the agent’s behaviour has not changed; shaping that preserves the optimal policy also preserves what IRL recovers from it. Cheap lava shows the step-cost trap from the reward-design section in a learned agent: every rollout ends in the lava, because a quick death costs less than the walk. Finally, try Free steps. The planner’s optimum still finishes (V*(start) = +4.08: discounting makes sooner better) but the learner never does: with nothing to lose per step, bumping into the wall at the start is worth exactly 0, the route past the lava and mud has picked up slightly negative values from early accidents, and 0 beats negative, so exploration dies before the goal’s value can travel back fourteen cells. A step cost is also an exploration incentive.

Things to try

  1. Find the stone threshold. With the sensible design, raise the stone bonus in steps of 0.5 and retrain each time. Below about 1.5 the agent passes through the stones on its way; above it the stone visits per rollout jump. Compare with the shuttle value (b − step cost)/(1 − γ) against the value of finishing from a stone, roughly goal reward minus one step.
  2. Make the mud worth it. Raise the mud penalty until the agent switches to the lava-side route, then raise the slip probability and watch the lava endings climb. The design question is which risk you prefer; the reward is where you answer it.
  3. Shape, then audit. With the stone bonus at 2, add the potential term with k = 1. The potential does not rescue the policy (shaping cannot change an optimum, including a bad one); only removing the bonus does. The audit should say the same thing either way.
  4. Paint a bottleneck. Wall off the right-hand gap so every route passes the lava. The highest recovered reward may move to the gap cell at (3, 0): IRL cannot tell a cell every path must cross from a cell the agent wants, which is why the verdict asks you to look closer.

import numpy as np

# ---------- the 8 x 8 design grid (row 0 at the top) ----------
LAYOUT = [".......G",
          "......oo",
          ".LL.....",
          ".#####..",
          "......MM",
          "......MM",
          "........",
          "S......."]
ROWS, COLS = 8, 8
N = ROWS * COLS
GAMMA, SLIP = 0.95, 0.1
cells = {ch: [(r, c) for r in range(ROWS) for c in range(COLS) if LAYOUT[r][c] == ch] for ch in "SG#LMo"}
sid = lambda r, c: r * COLS + c
START, GOAL = sid(*cells["S"][0]), sid(*cells["G"][0])
wall = np.zeros(N, bool); wall[[sid(*rc) for rc in cells["#"]]] = True
terminal = np.zeros(N, bool); terminal[GOAL] = True; terminal[[sid(*rc) for rc in cells["L"]]] = True

def transitions(slip):
    """P[s, a, s'] with 4 actions (up, right, down, left); a slip goes one of the other 3 ways."""
    P = np.zeros((N, 4, N)); d = [(-1, 0), (0, 1), (1, 0), (0, -1)]
    for s in range(N):
        r, c = divmod(s, COLS)
        for a in range(4):
            for b in range(4):
                p = 1 - slip if b == a else slip / 3
                r2, c2 = r + d[b][0], c + d[b][1]
                if not (0 <= r2 < ROWS and 0 <= c2 < COLS) or wall[sid(r2, c2)]: r2, c2 = r, c
                P[s, a, sid(r2, c2)] += p
    return P
P = transitions(SLIP)

def enter_reward(goal=10., mud=2., lava=10., stone=0.):
    e = np.zeros(N); e[GOAL] = goal
    for rc in cells["M"]: e[sid(*rc)] = -mud
    for rc in cells["L"]: e[sid(*rc)] = -lava
    for rc in cells["o"]: e[sid(*rc)] = stone
    return e

def bfs_distance():
    """Steps to the goal around walls (lava is passable for the potential)."""
    d = np.full(N, np.inf); d[GOAL] = 0; frontier = [GOAL]
    while frontier:
        nxt = []
        for s in frontier:
            r, c = divmod(s, COLS)
            for dr, dc in [(-1, 0), (0, 1), (1, 0), (0, -1)]:
                r2, c2 = r + dr, c + dc
                if 0 <= r2 < ROWS and 0 <= c2 < COLS and not wall[sid(r2, c2)] and d[sid(r2, c2)] == np.inf:
                    d[sid(r2, c2)] = d[s] + 1; nxt.append(sid(r2, c2))
        frontier = nxt
    return d
DIST = bfs_distance()

def value_iteration(enter, step_cost=1., phi=None, tol=1e-10):
    """r(s,a,s') = -step_cost + enter[s'] + gamma*phi[s'] - phi[s]; terminal states are absorbing with V = 0."""
    phi = np.zeros(N) if phi is None else phi
    R = -step_cost + enter[None, :] + GAMMA * phi[None, :] - phi[:, None]       # R[s, s']
    V = np.zeros(N)
    while True:
        Q = np.einsum('sat,st->sa', P, R) + GAMMA * np.einsum('sat,t->sa', P, np.where(terminal, 0., V))
        V2 = np.where(terminal | wall, 0., Q.max(1))
        if np.abs(V2 - V).max() < tol: break
        V = V2
    return V2, Q.argmax(1), Q

def policy_map(pi):
    arrows = "^>v<"
    return "\n".join("".join("#" if wall[sid(r, c)] else "G" if sid(r, c) == GOAL else "L" if terminal[sid(r, c)]
                             else arrows[pi[sid(r, c)]] for c in range(COLS)) for r in range(ROWS))

def greedy_rollout(pi, slip=0.0, max_steps=60, rng=None):
    Pr = transitions(slip); s, path = START, [START]
    for _ in range(max_steps):
        s = int(np.argmax(Pr[s, pi[s]])) if rng is None else int(rng.choice(N, p=Pr[s, pi[s]]))
        path.append(s)
        if terminal[s]: break
    return path

# ---------- 1. the designed reward ----------
E0 = enter_reward()
V0, pi0, Q0 = value_iteration(E0)
print("1. designed reward: goal +10, step -1, mud -2, lava -10 (terminal), gamma 0.95, slip 0.1")
print(policy_map(pi0))
print(f"   V*(start) = {V0[START]:.4f};  greedy route (no slip): {len(greedy_rollout(pi0)) - 1} steps, "

# ---------- 4. the audit: maximum-entropy IRL from trajectories ----------
T, SINK = 30, N   # sink state absorbs trajectories after a terminal cell; it has no features
def soft_policy(r_state):
    """Finite-horizon soft (MaxEnt) policy for state rewards r_state over N+1 states."""
    Pt = np.zeros((N + 1, 4, N + 1)); Pt[:N, :, :N] = P
    ti = np.where(terminal)[0]; Pt[ti, :, :] = 0; Pt[ti, :, SINK] = 1; Pt[SINK, :, SINK] = 1
    r = np.append(r_state, 0.); V = np.zeros(N + 1); pols = []
    for t in range(T - 1, -1, -1):
        Q = r[:, None] + Pt @ V
        m = Q.max(1, keepdims=True); Z = np.exp(Q - m).sum(1, keepdims=True)
        pols.append(np.exp(Q - m) / Z); V = (m + np.log(Z))[:, 0]
    return pols[::-1], Pt

def expected_visits(pols, Pt):
    D = np.zeros(N + 1); D[START] = 1; total = np.zeros(N + 1)
    for t in range(T):
        total += D
        D = np.einsum('s,sa,sat->t', D, pols[t], Pt)
    return total[:N]

def sample_demos(pols, Pt, n, rng):
    demos = []
    for _ in range(n):
        s, traj = START, []
        for t in range(T):
            traj.append(s)
            if s == SINK: break
            a = rng.choice(4, p=pols[t][s]); s = rng.choice(N + 1, p=Pt[s, a, :])
        demos.append([x for x in traj if x != SINK])
    return demos

def maxent_irl(demos, iters=300, eta=0.05):
    f_emp = np.zeros(N)
    for d in demos: np.add.at(f_emp, d, 1.0)
    f_emp /= len(demos)
    theta = np.zeros(N)
    for _ in range(iters):
        pols, Pt = soft_policy(theta)
        theta += eta * (f_emp - expected_visits(pols, Pt))
    return theta

    print("   three highest recovered rewards:", ", ".join(f"{divmod(int(s), COLS)} {theta[s]:+.2f}" for s in top))

Honest caveats. The Python demonstrations come from the soft-optimal policy for each reward, which is the generative model MaxEnt assumes; most of them still reach the goal even with the +2 bonus, because a finite-horizon soft policy sometimes wanders off the shuttle, yet the recovered reward already ranks the two stones second and third. The browser audits the greedy learner instead, whose rollouts loop outright under that bonus. The correlation is computed over cells the true behaviour reaches with non-negligible probability; over all 64 cells it would be dominated by cells about which the data say nothing. With one feature per cell the recovered reward can only be trusted where there is data, which is a limitation of the audit as well as of IRL.

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • A general value function is a question (cumulant, continuation, target policy) answered by the same TD machinery as a value function. A Horde of them, learned off-policy from one stream, is grounded knowledge about the world; as auxiliary tasks, the same predictions shape a representation that the sparse reward alone would shape slowly.
  • An option is ⟨I, π, β⟩: where it can start, what it does, when it stops. Options have reward and transition models with the discount folded in, the Bellman equations go through unchanged, and value iteration over options spreads information a room at a time instead of a cell at a time. Options can be learned at both levels at once (intra-option learning, option-critic).
  • The reward is the specification. Say what, not how; remember that every term interacts with every other and with γ; and check a design by solving it and looking at the policy, because the agent will.
  • Shaping with F = γΦ(s′) − Φ(s) shifts every action value by Φ(s) and nothing else, so it can only speed learning, never change the optimum; any other additive bonus can be farmed, and a plain “getting warm” reward makes the agent shuttle for ever once it beats finishing.
  • Inverse RL reads a reward off behaviour: match the demonstrator’s feature counts, resolve the ambiguity with maximum entropy, accept that constants and never-visited states are invisible. Used on your own agent it is an audit. Constrained MDPs, Lagrangian pricing and shields bound the harm of the policy and of the exploration that finds it.

Check your understanding

A demon has cumulant 1 and continuation γ(s) = 0 at doors, 1 elsewhere. What does its value mean, and why does it need no reward?

It is the expected number of steps until the agent next stands in a doorway, under the demon’s target policy: each step contributes 1 until the continuation drops to 0 at a door and cuts the sum off. It is a prediction about the agent’s own future experience, learnable by TD from the stream of states alone; reward plays no part because the cumulant is the counting signal, not the reward.

In the Horde demo, the “keep going right” demon only updates on the steps where the random walk happened to go right, with ρ = 2. Why 2, and what would go wrong with ρ = 1?

ρ = π(right|s)/b(right|s) = 1/0.5 = 2. The demon sees only half of the steps its target policy would have produced, so each one must count double for the expected update to equal the on-policy update under π. With ρ = 1 the updates would be too small by half in expectation; in the tabular case this only slows learning (the fixed point of the update with ρ = 1 on the right-going steps is still the right value, since left steps contribute nothing), but with function approximation or a stochastic target policy the un-weighted update converges to the wrong answer.

Why is the transition model of an option p(s′|s, ω) not a probability distribution, and why does that make the Bellman equation simpler rather than harder?

Each term is multiplied by γk for a termination after k steps, so the row sums to E[γk] < 1. Because the discount is inside the model, the Bellman equation for options needs no explicit γ: v(s) = r(s, ω) + Σs′ p(s′|s, ω) v(s′), exactly the shape of the one-step equation, and an option that takes longer is automatically worth less.

Planning with hallway options alone filled the whole four-rooms world with values in two sweeps for G1 but gave poor values for G2. Why the difference, and what fixes it?

G1 is a doorway, so two options are aimed exactly at it and every other room is one option away from one of those; a sweep moves information a room at a time. G2 is inside a room, so no option terminates there on purpose; options only pass through it by accident and the value they can claim is small. Adding the primitive actions to the option set lets the agent plan precisely in the last room while still using options for the long hops: both sets together converge faster than primitives alone and to the same optimum.

A designer adds +0.1 for every step that reduces the Manhattan distance to the goal and −0.1 for every step that increases it. Is this potential-based? Does the answer depend on γ?

With γ = 1 it is: it equals Φ(s′) − Φ(s) with Φ(s) = −0.1 × distance, so it is harmless. With γ < 1 the potential-based form would be γΦ(s′) − Φ(s), which differs from the designer’s bonus by (1 − γ)Φ(s′), a state-dependent term that is not a difference of potentials. For γ close to 1 the discrepancy is small and usually harmless in practice, but it is not covered by the guarantee; using γΦ(s′) − Φ(s) costs nothing and is.

MaxEnt IRL recovered a negative reward for the mud even though the demonstrations almost never entered it. How?

The gradient is empirical visits minus expected visits under the current model. Starting from zero reward, the model’s soft-optimal policy spreads over all short routes, including those through the mud, so its expected visits there are positive while the empirical count is near zero. The gradient is therefore negative on the mud cells and the reward there is pushed down until the model, too, avoids them. IRL learns from what the expert did not do as much as from what it did, wherever the model would have expected otherwise.

Why does a Lagrangian solution of a constrained MDP sometimes need a randomised policy, and what does the demo do instead?

As λ increases, the optimal deterministic policy for r − λc jumps between a few discrete choices, so the expected cost takes a few discrete values; the budget d typically falls between two of them. The exact constrained optimum then mixes the two neighbouring deterministic policies in the proportion that meets d exactly, gaining a little return over the safer one. The demo reports the cheapest deterministic policy whose cost is within budget, which is the conservative end of that mixture.
