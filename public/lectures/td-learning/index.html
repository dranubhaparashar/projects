Reinforcement learning · chapter 3.1d · temporal-difference learning

Temporal-Difference Learning in Depth

Learn a guess from a guess, one move at a time

Monte Carlo waits until the game is over and then asks how it went. Temporal-difference (TD) learning doesn’t wait: after every move it nudges its estimate for the position it just left toward its estimate for the position it has reached. That one idea, applied 30,000 times to games the program plays against itself, produces the tic-tac-toe player in the figure, which drew every one of its 2,000 test games against a perfect opponent. Here it has opened as X; each empty square shows how good that reply looks to it, from your side of the board (1 = you win, ½ = draw, 0 = you lose). Play it: it keeps learning from your game, and the caption shows the TD update each move causes.

Sutton & Barto 1.5 · the first example

A first TD learner: tic-tac-toe

Suppose you want a program that beats a particular, imperfect tic-tac-toe opponent. The textbook answers are awkward here. Minimax assumes the opponent plays perfectly, so it will never steer toward a position it could lose from, even when this opponent would blunder there. Dynamic programming could compute the best reply to this opponent exactly, but it needs a complete model of how the opponent moves, which you don’t have. An evolutionary method could try whole policies and keep the ones that win most, but it only ever learns from final results and ignores everything that happened during a game.

The value-function approach keeps one number per position: V(s), the program’s current estimate of its chance of winning from s. Positions with three Xs in a row are worth 1; positions with three Os, or a full board, are worth 0 (here a draw counts as not winning); everything else starts at ½. To move, it looks at every position it could create and usually takes the one with the highest value (a greedy move); now and then it plays at random (an exploratory move) to keep seeing new positions. After each greedy move it shifts the value of the position its previous move created a little toward the value of the position its new move created. Exploratory moves are not backed up, because they say nothing about how good the greedy choice was. Pick an opponent and train; then raise ε or α and see what changes.

\[ \begin{aligned} V(S_t) &\leftarrow V(S_t) \\ &\quad + \alpha\,\big[\, V(S_{t+1}) - V(S_t) \,\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: St is the position after the program’s move at step t, St+1 the position after its next move (or the final position). The bracket is the surprise: how much better or worse things look one move later. The step size α decides how far each surprise moves the old estimate. Because the update uses one estimate to correct another, it is a temporal-difference update.
Say it“The value of S-t moves toward the value of S-t-plus-one, by alpha times their difference.”

Sutton & Barto 6 · TD prediction

TD(0): learning a guess from a guess

Chapter 6 of Sutton and Barto calls TD learning the idea most central to reinforcement learning. It sits between the two methods of the previous chapters. Like Monte Carlo, it learns from raw experience, with no model of the environment. Like dynamic programming, it bootstraps: it updates an estimate partly from other estimates instead of waiting for a final outcome. Take the simplest test bed in the book, a random walk: five states A to E in a row, start in the middle, step left or right with equal chance, and the walk ends off either end, with reward +1 only off the right end. With no discounting, the true value of each state is its chance of finishing on the right: 1/6, 2/6, …, 5/6.

Constant-α Monte Carlo waits for the episode to end, then moves each visited state toward the return Gt it actually saw. TD(0) moves the state it has just left toward one reward plus the estimate of where it landed, straight away. Step through a walk one move at a time: TD’s bars move as the walker moves; Monte Carlo’s move only when the episode is over.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{MC:} \\ &\quad V(S_t) \leftarrow V(S_t) + \alpha\,[\,G_t - V(S_t)\,] \\ &\text{TD(0):} \\ &\quad V(S_t) \leftarrow V(S_t) + \alpha\,\delta_t \\ &\quad \delta_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma V(S_{t+1}) - V(S_t) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: both nudge V(St) toward a target. Monte Carlo’s target is the return Gt, known only at the end of the episode. TD(0)’s target is the next reward plus the discounted estimate of the next state (taken as 0 once the episode has ended), available one step later. δt is the TD error.
Say it“TD moves V of S-t toward R-t-plus-one plus gamma V of S-t-plus-one; the difference is the TD error delta-t.”
\[ G_t - V(S_t) = \sum_{k=t}^{T-1} \gamma^{\,k-t}\,\delta_k \]
Read it as: if V is held fixed during the episode, Monte Carlo’s error is exactly the (discounted) sum of the TD errors along the rest of the episode. TD spreads the correction over the steps where the surprises happened; MC applies it all at once at the end. (With TD updating V during the episode the identity is only approximate.)
Say it“The Monte Carlo error is the sum of the TD errors from t to the end.”

Sutton & Barto 6.2

Why TD usually beats Monte Carlo

Three advantages are clear before any experiment. TD needs no model of rewards or transitions, unlike dynamic programming. It is online and fully incremental: it learns after every step, which matters when episodes are long, and is the only option for continuing tasks that never end; Monte Carlo must wait, and in control it has to discard or discount the part of an episode after an exploratory action. And it is sound: for a fixed policy, tabular TD(0) converges to vπ in the mean for a small enough constant step size, and with probability 1 if the step size shrinks according to the usual stochastic-approximation conditions.

Which learns faster is an open question in general (nobody has proved that either always wins), but on stochastic tasks TD is usually ahead in practice. Here are both learners on the random walk, each curve averaged over 100 independent runs of 100 walks, with every state starting at ½. The faint curves are the step sizes the book uses. Find the best α for each method: even then, TD ends lower.

\[ \text{RMS} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{5}\textstyle\sum_{s=A}^{E} \big(V(s) - v_\pi(s)\big)^2} \]
Read it as: the typical size of the error over the five states, averaged here over 100 runs after each episode.
Say it“RMS is the square root of the mean squared gap between estimate and truth.”
\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \alpha_n = \infty, \qquad \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \alpha_n^2 < \infty \]
Read it as: for convergence with probability 1, the step sizes must add up to infinity (so the estimate can travel any distance) but their squares must not (so the noise eventually dies away). αn = 1/n qualifies; a constant α does not, which is why constant-α curves level off at a floor set by the noise instead of reaching zero.
Say it“The step sizes sum to infinity, their squares to something finite.”

Sutton & Barto 6.3 · part 1

Batch updating: what each method converges to

Constant-α curves wobble because each estimate is pushed around by the last few episodes. To see what the two methods are really aiming at, use batch updating: keep every episode seen so far, sweep through all of them computing the updates, apply the summed increments, and repeat until nothing changes; only then take the next episode. With a small enough α, batch TD(0) and batch MC each converge to a single answer that does not depend on α, so the chart below computes those limits directly. Batch MC converges to the average return observed from each state. Batch TD(0) converges to something else: the values that would be exactly right if the world were the Markov process estimated from the data (more on that in the next section). Slide through the episodes of one run and compare the two answers with the truth.

\[ V_{\text{MC}}(s) = \frac{1}{N(s)} \sum_{\text{visits of } s} G \]
Read it as: N(s) is how many times state s was visited in all the stored episodes; batch MC is simply the average of the returns that followed those visits. It fits the training data best in the squared-error sense.
Say it“Batch Monte Carlo is the average return after each visit.”
\[ V_{\text{TD}}(s) = \frac{1}{N(s)} \sum_{s \to s'} \big[\, r + V_{\text{TD}}(s') \,\big] \]
Read it as: batch TD stops moving when, for every state, the average TD target over all its stored transitions s → s′ (reward r, then the next state’s value) equals its own value. That is a small set of linear equations; the page solves them exactly.
Say it“Batch TD is the value that equals its own average one-step target.”

Sutton & Barto 6.3 · part 2

You are the predictor: certainty equivalence

Here is the book’s puzzle in miniature. You observe eight episodes of an unknown process. One starts in A, gets reward 0, moves to B, gets 0 and ends. Six start in B and end with reward 1. One starts in B and ends with 0. What is V(B)? Everyone says ¾: six of eight endings from B paid 1. What is V(A)? Batch Monte Carlo says 0: the one return ever seen from A was 0, and that is the answer with the least squared error on these data. Batch TD says ¾: A always led to B with no reward, so A should be worth whatever B is worth.

TD’s answer is the certainty-equivalence estimate. Build the maximum-likelihood model of the process (transition probabilities are observed fractions, rewards are observed averages) and compute the values that would be exactly correct if that model were the truth. If the process really is Markov, this is the better guess for future data, and that is the underlying reason TD beat MC in the last two sections. Add or remove episodes and watch the model, the two answers and batch TD’s sweeps.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat p(s' \mid s) &= \frac{n(s \to s')}{n(s)} \\ \hat r(s) &= \frac{1}{n(s)}\!\sum_{\text{visits}} r \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the maximum-likelihood model counts. n(s → s′) is how often s was followed by s′, n(s) how often s was left at all; the estimated reward is the average reward collected on leaving s.
Say it“p-hat of s-prime given s is the fraction of exits from s that went to s-prime.”
\[ V_{\text{CE}}(s) = \hat r(s) + \sum_{s'} \hat p(s' \mid s)\, V_{\text{CE}}(s') \]
Read it as: the Bellman equation of the fitted model. For n states, solving it directly needs about n² memory for the model and n³ steps; TD(0) heads toward the same answer with memory for just n values, which is what makes it practical when n is large.
Say it“V-CE is the estimated reward plus the expected V-CE of the next state under the fitted model.”

Make the process real: A always goes to B with reward 0, and B ends with reward 1 with probability p. Draw a batch of n episodes, k of which start in A (the rest start in B), estimate V(A) both ways, and repeat 4,000 times. Monte Carlo can use only the k returns that started in A; certainty equivalence uses every visit to B, n of them. Change n and k and compare the errors with the exact formulas.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{MSE}_{\text{MC}}\big(V(A)\big) &= \frac{p(1-p)}{k} \\ \text{MSE}_{\text{CE}}\big(V(A)\big) &= \frac{p(1-p)}{n} \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: both estimates are unbiased averages of 0/1 outcomes, so their mean squared error is the variance p(1 − p) divided by how many outcomes each one averages. Certainty equivalence pools n outcomes, Monte Carlo only k.
Say it“MC’s error is p times one minus p over k; certainty equivalence divides by n instead.”

Sutton & Barto 6.6

Expected Sarsa: average over the next action

For control, TD learns action values Q(s, a). Sarsa backs up toward the value of the next action it actually picked, Q(St+1, At+1), so its target carries the randomness of that pick. Q-learning backs up toward the best next action, whatever it then does. Expected Sarsa uses the average of all next action values, weighted by how likely the policy is to choose each one. It moves, deterministically, in the direction Sarsa moves on average, so the noise from choosing At+1 is gone; the price is a little more computation per step. If the target policy is greedy, its average is just the maximum, and Expected Sarsa is Q-learning, so it can also be used off-policy.

The test is the book’s cliff walk: a 4 × 12 grid, start bottom-left, goal bottom-right, the cells between them a cliff (reward −100 and back to the start), −1 for every other step, no discounting, all three learners ε-greedy with ε = 0.1. Push α towards 1: Sarsa falls apart over the long run, Expected Sarsa doesn’t, because in this deterministic world its target has no noise left to average away.

\[ \begin{aligned} &Q(S_t,A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t) + \alpha\,\delta_t \\ &\delta_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma\, \bar Q_{t+1} - Q(S_t,A_t) \\ &\bar Q_{t+1} =\textstyle\sum_a \pi(a \mid S_{t+1})\, Q(S_{t+1}, a) \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: the usual TD update for an action value, with the next value replaced by its expectation under the policy π. For ε-greedy π, every action gets weight ε/4 and the greedy action(s) share the remaining 1 − ε. Sarsa would put Q(St+1, At+1) in that slot; Q-learning puts maxa Q(St+1, a).
Say it“Q of S-t, A-t moves toward the reward plus gamma times the policy-weighted average of the next Q values.”

Sutton & Barto 6.8

Afterstates and games

The tic-tac-toe learner of the first section did not really learn state values or action values. It valued the board right after its own move, before the opponent replies. That is an afterstate, and afterstate value functions pay off whenever you know the immediate effect of your action but not what happens next. In a game you know exactly what board your move makes; you do not know the reply. Many (position, move) pairs produce the same board, and an action-value table would have to learn each of them separately, while an afterstate table learns them once. The same trick fits other problems with a known first half of the transition: in queuing tasks, for instance, assigning a customer to a server or turning a customer away has an outcome you can compute; only the next arrivals are random.

Below, both kinds of table learn tic-tac-toe by self-play with identical TD updates, exploration and step size, and both are told the result of a move that ends the game; the only difference is what the table is indexed by. They are scored on all 4,520 positions where the game is not over: does the greedy move keep the value that perfect play would get? Browse the transpositions, then run the race.

\[ \begin{aligned} Q(s,a) &= V\big(f(s,a)\big) \\ V(b_t) &\leftarrow V(b_t) \\ &\quad + \alpha\big[\, V(b_{t+1}) - V(b_t) \,\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: f(s, a) is the known, deterministic effect of move a in position s: the afterstate b. An action value is then just the value of the afterstate it creates, so two pairs with f(s, a) = f(s′, a′) share one entry. In self-play the update runs ply by ply: each afterstate moves toward the value of the next one, chosen by the side to move (X picks the highest value, O the lowest).
Say it“Q of s, a is V of the afterstate f of s, a; each afterstate’s value moves toward the next afterstate’s.”

Sutton & Barto 6.9 · the chapter in one picture

The TD family at a glance

Every method so far builds a target and moves an estimate toward it. Two questions sort them. Does the target use an expectation over what could happen next (which needs a model) or a sample of what did happen? And does it bootstrap from an estimate after one step, or run to the end of the episode? Dynamic programming takes expectations and bootstraps; Monte Carlo samples and runs to the end; TD samples and bootstraps. The price of bootstrapping is bias (the target leans on estimates that may be wrong); the price of a full sampled return is variance. TD’s bet is that a little bias is worth much less variance. Pick a target and a state, then make the current estimates worse or better.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{bias} &= \mathbb{E}[Y] - v_\pi(s) \\ \text{variance} &= \mathbb{E}\big[(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])^2\big] \end{aligned} \]
Read it as: Y is the target. On the random walk from state s, the DP target 0.5·(next value on the left) + 0.5·(on the right) never varies; the TD target is one of those two values at random; the MC target is the return, 0 or 1. With estimates V = (1 − q)·½ + q·vπ, q sets how good the current estimates are.
Say it“Bias is how far the target is from the truth on average; variance is how much it jumps around.”

These are the one-step, tabular, model-free TD methods. The next chapters stretch each word: n-step methods sit between TD(0) and Monte Carlo, Dyna adds a learned model, and function approximation replaces the table. TD is also more general than control: it is a way of learning to make long-term predictions about any signal, from game outcomes to the dopamine responses that later chapters compare it with.

Project: a self-taught tic-tac-toe player, checked against 958 real endgames

Aha, 1991; UCI Machine Learning Repository, doi:10.24432/C5688J, licensed CC BY 4.0) lists every board on which a game of tic-tac-toe can end when X moves first: 958 boards, each labelled “positive” if X has three in a row. This page embeds all 958 rows unchanged. They are used three ways: to check this page’s rules, to build test positions one and two moves before a decisive ending, and as the stopping points for perfect play, which is computed here by minimax over the 5,478 positions that can occur in a game.

The player itself knows only the rules. It starts with every non-final position worth ½ and plays itself; after every greedy move, the previous position’s value moves toward the new one’s (win = 1, draw = ½, loss = 0 for X; X maximises, O minimises). Nothing about perfect play or the endgame file is used in training.

import numpy as np

M32 = 0xFFFFFFFF
def rng(a):                                   # mulberry32, the same generator the page uses
    st = [a & M32]
    def r():
        st[0] = (st[0] + 0x6D2B79F5) & M32; a = st[0]
        t = ((a ^ (a >> 15)) * (1 | a)) & M32

# ---------- the five-state random walk (Example 6.2) ----------
TRUE = [0] + [s / 6 for s in range(1, 6)] + [0]
def walk(r):
    s, x = [3], 3
    while 0 < x < 6: x += -1 if r() < 0.5 else 1; s.append(x)
    return s
def td_ep(V, ep, a):
    for s, s2 in zip(ep, ep[1:]): V[s] += a * ((1 if s2 == 6 else 0) + (0 if s2 in (0, 6) else V[s2]) - V[s])
def mc_ep(V, ep, a):
    G = 1 if ep[-1] == 6 else 0
    for s in ep[:-1]: V[s] += a * (G - V[s])
def rms(V): return math.sqrt(sum((V[s] - TRUE[s]) * (V[s] - TRUE[s]) for s in range(1, 6)) / 5)
def race(a_td, a_mc, runs, n, seed):
    td, mc = np.zeros(n + 1), np.zeros(n + 1)
    for r in range(runs):
        R = rng(seed + r); V1 = [0, .5, .5, .5, .5, .5, 0]; V2 = V1[:]; td[0] += rms(V1); mc[0] += rms(V2)
        for e in range(1, n + 1):
            ep = walk(R); td_ep(V1, ep, a_td); mc_ep(V2, ep, a_mc); td[e] += rms(V1); mc[e] += rms(V2)
    return td / runs, mc / runs
def batch(counts):                            # every-visit MC averages and the certainty-equivalence fixed point
    n, gs, gc = counts; mc = [0, .5, .5, .5, .5, .5, 0]; ce = mc[:]
    vis = [s for s in range(1, 6) if gc[s]]
    for s in vis: mc[s] = gs[s] / gc[s]
    if vis:
        P = np.array([[n[s][u] / gc[s] for u in vis] for s in vis]); b = np.array([n[s][6] / gc[s] for s in vis])
        for s, v in zip(vis, np.linalg.solve(np.eye(len(vis)) - P, b)): ce[s] = v
    return mc, ce
def batch_race(runs, n, seed):
    td, mc = np.zeros(n + 1), np.zeros(n + 1)
    for r in range(runs):
        R = rng(seed + r); cnt = (np.zeros((7, 7)), np.zeros(7), np.zeros(7)); td[0] += rms([0, .5, .5, .5, .5, .5, 0]); mc[0] += rms([0, .5, .5, .5, .5, .5, 0])
        for e in range(1, n + 1):
            ep = walk(R); G = 1 if ep[-1] == 6 else 0
            for s, s2 in zip(ep, ep[1:]): cnt[0][s][s2] += 1; cnt[1][s] += G; cnt[2][s] += 1
            m, c = batch(cnt); td[e] += rms(c); mc[e] += rms(m)
    return td / runs, mc / runs

def ab_sim(n, k, p, reps, seed):
    R = rng(seed); smc = sce = 0.0
    for _ in range(reps):
        a1 = b1 = 0
        for j in range(n):
            w = 1 if R() < p else 0; b1 += w
            if j < k: a1 += w
        mca, cea = a1 / k, b1 / n; smc += (mca - p) * (mca - p); sce += (cea - p) * (cea - p)
    return smc / reps, sce / reps

# ---------- cliff walking (Example 6.6) ----------
def cw_step(s, a):
    r, c = divmod(s, 12); r2 = min(3, max(0, r + (-1, 0, 1, 0)[a])); c2 = min(11, max(0, c + (0, 1, 0, -1)[a]))
    if r2 == 3 and 1 <= c2 <= 10: return 36, -100, False
    s2 = r2 * 12 + c2; return s2, -1, s2 == 47
def argmax_r(Q, s, R):
    q = Q[s * 4:s * 4 + 4]; m = max(q); best = [a for a in range(4) if q[a] == m]
    return best[0] if len(best) == 1 else best[math.floor(R() * len(best))]
def eps_g(Q, s, eps, R): return math.floor(R() * 4) if R() < eps else argmax_r(Q, s, R)
def exp_q(Q, s, eps):
    q = Q[s * 4:s * 4 + 4]; m = max(q); k = sum(1 for x in q if x == m); e = 0.0
    for a in range(4): e += (eps / 4 + ((1 - eps) / k if q[a] == m else 0)) * q[a]
    return e
def cliff_run(method, alpha, eps, n, R):
    Q = [0.0] * 192; ret = []
    for _ in range(n):
        s = 36; a = eps_g(Q, s, eps, R); G = 0; steps = 0
        while True:
            s2, rw, done = cw_step(s, a); G += rw; steps += 1
            if done or steps >= 100000: Q[s * 4 + a] += alpha * (rw - Q[s * 4 + a]); break
            a2 = eps_g(Q, s2, eps, R)
            tgt = Q[s2 * 4 + a2] if method == "sarsa" else exp_q(Q, s2, eps) if method == "esarsa" else max(Q[s2 * 4:s2 * 4 + 4])
            Q[s * 4 + a] += alpha * (rw + tgt - Q[s * 4 + a]); s, a = s2, a2
        ret.append(G)
    return ret, Q
def cliff_mean(method, alpha, eps, n, frm, runs, seed):
    tot = 0.0
    for r in range(runs):
        ret, _ = cliff_run(method, alpha, eps, n, rng(seed + r)); s = 0.0
        for e in range(frm, n): s += ret[e]
        tot += s / (n - frm)
    return tot / runs

    b = decode(k); NX[k] = b.count(1); NO[k] = b.count(2); MOVES[k] = [i for i in range(9) if b[i] == 0]
    w = 0
    for L in LINES:
        v = b[L[0]]
        if v and v == b[L[1]] == b[L[2]]: w = v if w in (0, v) else 4
    TERM[k] = w if w else (3 if NX[k] + NO[k] == 9 else 0)
def to_move(k): return 1 if NX[k] == NO[k] else 2
REACH = [0] * NK
stack = [0]
while stack:
    k = stack.pop()
    if REACH[k]: continue
    REACH[k] = 1
    if not TERM[k]: stack += [k + to_move(k) * P3[i] for i in MOVES[k]]
reachable = [k for k in range(NK) if REACH[k]]; terminals = [k for k in reachable if TERM[k]]
MM = {}
def mm(k):
    if k in MM: return MM[k]
    t = TERM[k]
    if t: v = 1.0 if t == 1 else 0.0 if t == 2 else 0.5
    else:
        p = to_move(k); c = [mm(k + p * P3[i]) for i in MOVES[k]]; v = max(c) if p == 1 else min(c)
    MM[k] = v; return v
mm(0)
def tval(t, d): return 1.0 if t == 1 else 0.0 if t == 2 else d
def pick_best(vals, want_max, R):
    m = max(vals) if want_max else min(vals); idx = [i for i, v in enumerate(vals) if v == m]
    return idx[math.floor(R() * len(idx))] if len(idx) > 1 and R else idx[0]
def opp_move(kind, k, p, R):
    mv = MOVES[k]
    if kind == "care":
        q = 3 - p; c = [i for i in mv if TERM[k + p * P3[i]] == p]
        if not c: c = [i for i in mv if TERM[k + q * P3[i]] == q]
        if not c: c = mv
        return c[math.floor(R() * len(c))]
    if kind == "perfect":
        vs = [MM[k + p * P3[i]] for i in mv]; b = max(vs) if p == 1 else min(vs); c = [i for i, v in zip(mv, vs) if v == b]
        return c[math.floor(R() * len(c))]
    return mv[math.floor(R() * len(mv))]

def sutton_train(games, alpha, eps, opp, seed):       # section 1.5: we are X, V = chance of winning
    R = rng(seed); V = [0.5] * NK; out = []
    val = lambda k: (1.0 if TERM[k] == 1 else 0.0) if TERM[k] else V[k]
    for _ in range(games):
        k, prev = 0, -1
        while True:
            mv = MOVES[k]; greedy = True
            if R() < eps: i = mv[math.floor(R() * len(mv))]; greedy = False
            else: i = mv[pick_best([val(k + P3[c]) for c in mv], True, R)]
            k2 = k + P3[i]
            if greedy and prev >= 0: V[prev] += alpha * (val(k2) - V[prev])
            prev = k = k2
            if TERM[k]: out.append(1 if TERM[k] == 1 else 0); break
            k += 2 * P3[opp_move(opp, k, 2, R)]
            if TERM[k]: V[prev] += alpha * (0 - V[prev]); out.append(-1 if TERM[k] == 2 else 0); break
    return V, out
def play_x(V, opp, games, seed):
    R = rng(seed); w = d = l = 0; val = lambda k: (1.0 if TERM[k] == 1 else 0.0) if TERM[k] else V[k]
    for _ in range(games):
        k = 0
        while True:
            mv = MOVES[k]; k += P3[mv[pick_best([val(k + P3[c]) for c in mv], True, R)]]
            if TERM[k]:
                if TERM[k] == 1: w += 1
                else: d += 1
                break
            k += 2 * P3[opp_move(opp, k, 2, R)]
            if TERM[k]:
                if TERM[k] == 2: l += 1
                else: d += 1
                break
    return w, d, l

def self_td(kind, games, alpha, eps, seed, every=0, on_check=None):   # self-play, afterstates or (position, move) pairs
    R = rng(seed); aft = kind != "q"; V = [0.5] * (NK if aft else NK * 9)
    for g in range(1, games + 1):
        k, p, prev = 0, 1, -1
        while not TERM[k]:
            mv = MOVES[k]; vals = []
            for i in mv:
                k2 = k + p * P3[i]; t = TERM[k2]; vals.append(tval(t, 0.5) if t else (V[k2] if aft else V[k * 9 + i]))
            greedy = True
            if R() < eps: j = math.floor(R() * len(mv)); greedy = False
            else: j = pick_best(vals, p == 1, R)
            if greedy and prev >= 0: V[prev] += alpha * (vals[j] - V[prev])
            k2 = k + p * P3[mv[j]]; prev = -1 if TERM[k2] else (k2 if aft else k * 9 + mv[j]); k = k2; p = 3 - p
        if on_check and g % every == 0: on_check(g, V)
    return V
def ev_of(V, kind):
    def f(k, i, p):
        k2 = k + p * P3[i]; t = TERM[k2]; return tval(t, 0.5) if t else (V[k * 9 + i] if kind == "q" else V[k2])
    return f
def agent_move(V, k, kind="after"):
    p = to_move(k); ev = ev_of(V, kind); return MOVES[k][pick_best([ev(k, i, p) for i in MOVES[k]], p == 1, None)]
def opt_rate(V, kind):
    opt = 0; open_ = 0
    for k in reachable:
        if TERM[k]: continue
        open_ += 1; p = to_move(k)
        if MM[k + p * P3[agent_move(V, k, kind)]] == MM[k]: opt += 1
    return opt, open_
def match(V, side, opp, games, seed):
    R = rng(seed); w = d = l = 0; val = lambda k: tval(TERM[k], 0.5) if TERM[k] else V[k]
    for _ in range(games):
        k, p = 0, 1
        while not TERM[k]:
            if p == side: mv = MOVES[k]; i = mv[pick_best([val(k + p * P3[c]) for c in mv], p == 1, R)]
            else: i = opp_move(opp, k, p, R)
            k += p * P3[i]; p = 3 - p
        t = TERM[k]
        if t == 3: d += 1
        elif t == side: w += 1
        else: l += 1
    return w, d, l

Wrap-up

Five lines to remember

  • TD learning updates an estimate toward the next reward plus the next estimate: V(S) ← V(S) + α[R + γV(S′) − V(S)]. It samples like Monte Carlo and bootstraps like dynamic programming.
  • It needs no model, learns online after every step, converges for a fixed policy, and on stochastic tasks such as the random walk it usually learns faster than constant-α Monte Carlo.
  • Under batch updating, Monte Carlo converges to the average observed returns; TD(0) converges to the certainty-equivalence estimate, the exact values of the maximum-likelihood Markov model, which predicts better when the process is Markov.
  • Expected Sarsa replaces the next action’s value with its expectation under the policy: less variance than Sarsa, stable even at α = 1 on the cliff walk, and Q-learning when the target policy is greedy.
  • When you know the immediate effect of your move, value the afterstate: all (position, move) pairs that make the same board share one entry, which is why self-play TD learns tic-tac-toe faster with afterstates.

Check your understanding

Why does the tic-tac-toe learner not back up after an exploratory move?

Its values are meant to describe greedy play. An exploratory move is deliberately not what the policy would choose, so the value of the position it creates says nothing about how good the previous greedy choice was; backing it up would drag good positions down because of a random move.

On the random walk, a large TD step size makes the RMS error fall fast and then rise again. Why?

With a constant α, each update keeps a fixed fraction of the latest, noisy target, so the estimates never settle; a bigger α gives a higher noise floor. Early on, the fast moves away from the starting values of ½ dominate; later the noise does, and the error climbs back up to that floor.

Batch MC says V(A) = 0 and batch TD says V(A) = ¾ on the eight episodes. When would MC be the better answer?

When the process is not Markov, for example if reaching B by way of A really does change what happens next. Then pooling A’s path with the other visits to B is wrong, and the plain average of the returns from A is the safer estimate. If the process is Markov, the certainty-equivalence answer uses all the evidence about B and predicts better.

Why can Expected Sarsa use α = 1 on the cliff walk while Sarsa cannot?

The grid is deterministic, so the only randomness in Sarsa’s target is the choice of the next action. With α = 1 each update copies that noisy target outright. Expected Sarsa averages over the next action instead, so its target is deterministic and copying it is harmless.

Give two different (position, move) pairs in tic-tac-toe with the same afterstate, and say why that helps.

X at the corner and O at the centre, then X takes the opposite corner; or X at that opposite corner and O at the centre, then X takes the first corner. Both make the same board. An afterstate table learns the value of that board once and uses it from both positions; an action-value table must learn the two pairs separately.
